I spent a little over forty years in experimental condensed-matter physics — thin-film magnetism, mostly — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group in formal epistemology at the university where I used to teach. Much of it is new to me, but I came across Solomonoff induction a few months ago and it struck me as the sort of thing I ought to have been taught instead of, or at least alongside, the model-selection heuristics my field actually uses. As I understand it: one places a prior over hypotheses inversely proportional to 2 raised to the length of the shortest program that generates the data, and the resulting predictor then converges on the truth for any computable data-generating process, with total expected error bounded by a constant times the complexity of that process. If I have that right, induction is not merely pragmatically indispensable but provably optimal, which would seem to dissolve Hume's problem rather than merely manage it.

The obvious difficulty, as everyone points out, is that the prior is not computable, so the whole thing is an ideal limit rather than a procedure. This bothers me rather less than it bothers the philosophers in the group, because it seems to me that one can simply approximate it. True Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable, but an off-the-shelf compressor yields an upper bound on it up to an additive constant — and, importantly, a constant one can actually write down, rather than the unspecified and possibly astronomical constant hidden inside the optimality theorem. So I wrote a small program that does the following: given a data set, it enumerates a family of candidate models (polynomials up to some degree, a few mechanistic forms I specify by hand, and simple mixtures of them), encodes each candidate's functional form together with its fitted parameters and its residuals using a standard compressor, and selects whichever candidate minimises total compressed length, then predicts with that model. We have started calling this 'poor man's Solomonoff induction'. What I want to know is whether the reasoning behind it is sound: since my scoring rule is a computable stand-in for the universal prior, shouldn't it inherit the optimality guarantee approximately, with the slack given by the now-explicit constant relating my compressor to genuine Kolmogorov complexity? If so, the practical problem of induction is solved for any data generated by a computable process, and I can stop worrying about whether nature is uniform and simply trust the compressor.

Two remarks from the group make me suspect I am missing something, and I would be grateful if you could set out where exactly my reasoning breaks down. First, one member argued that the convergence result does not vindicate Occam's razor at all, because the simplicity weighting is not what does the work: any prior assigning the true hypothesis non-negligible weight would converge, and the particular 2-to-the-minus-complexity weighting only buys a bound on the rate. If that is right, then simplicity preference is not what makes the method succeed — but then I do not see why I should prefer the simpler of two models that fit my data equally well, which is what I had always taken the razor to be for. Second, when I said I would like to use the compressor score to choose between two empirically equivalent cosmological models — to prefer the simpler one as more likely to be true, not merely as more convenient for prediction — I was told that I had quietly shifted from a result about predictive accuracy to a claim about truth, and that nothing in the derivation licenses that shift. I am unconvinced: if the prior is a genuine probability distribution over hypotheses, then the simpler hypothesis really does have the higher posterior, and tracking the truth is what probabilities are for. A third member added that, since my procedure is itself computable, the universal predictor is guaranteed to do at least as well as mine and not the reverse — which sounds to me close to paradoxical, because I had thought of the ideal method as the limit of such approximations rather than as their competitor. Could you say which step in the move from Solomonoff's ideal predictor to my compressor score actually fails, what the optimality theorem does and does not license, and whether 'poor man's Solomonoff induction' is anything more than an ordinary model-selection heuristic with a formalist veneer?
