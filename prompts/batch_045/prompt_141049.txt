I'm TAing an intro-level semantics/pragmatics course this term and the slur unit always goes sideways, so before we get to the readings proper I want to nail down what we should even be asking of a theory here. Could you write me a desiderata list: what would an adequate account of slurs have to deliver — the data it has to predict, the distinctions it can't collapse, the things we already know about language it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each one, and a note on which are non-negotiable and which are really just commitments of one camp smuggled in as criteria. Also tell me which single requirement eliminates the most candidate theories, and whether any subset of your own list looks jointly unsatisfiable; if the list is self-contradictory I'd much rather find out now than after I've handed it out.

A few distinctions I think it has to respect, but you tell me if I'm overrating them: offensive-to-hear vs. derogatory-toward-the-target vs. actually harmful, which everyone in section keeps running together even though they seem to come apart (a slur yelled at nobody in an empty room; a perfectly neutral word used to sneer; a slur used by someone with so little credibility that nobody believes them). Slur vs. ordinary pejorative vs. generic insult — 'idiot' or 'cow' can wound but don't feel like the same kind of object as a slur, and there are slurs about which nobody can even agree who the target group is meant to be. And the embedding behaviour: a slur tucked inside a negation, a yes/no question, a conditional antecedent, or an 'I don't think…' still seems to offend in a way that a descriptive predicate plus a sneer doesn't, which is the standard argument against just treating the nasty part as one more conjunct.

I'd also want the list tested against weird cases rather than the two or three examples everyone always uses, so make sure your criteria say something about: (1) a slur with no history behind it — a friend of mine who builds conlangs made up a word for people born in February and used it exactly the way a real slur gets used, and it just isn't offensive, though everyone can tell what it's meant to do; why not, and does a theory have to explain that? (2) a slur whose target nobody can identify any more, where old recordings of it still make people flinch. (3) dubbing a film where the original slur has no counterpart, so the translator reaches for a slur aimed at a completely different group — now you've insulted a group that isn't in the scene at all. (4) no speaker intentions in the picture whatsoever: a parrot, a screen reader reciting an archived comment, an autocomplete finishing the phrase. (5) out-group self-application ('I was being such a ___ about it'), which is awkward in a different way from in-group reclamation. (6) why some slurs get reclaimed and others stay permanently radioactive, and whether the theory has to predict which or can just note it case by case. As for what the theory is adequate for: I want it to explain what gets communicated and what's lost when you swap a slur for its neutral counterpart, and to take a position on whether the offense lives in the linguistic meaning or downstream of it — I don't need it to settle moderation policy. If it's cheap, add a short scorecard at the end for the four-ish families I'll be teaching (descriptivist/truth-conditional, semantic-expressivist, pragmatic or conventional-implicature style, and the prohibitionist one where the whole word is banned rather than some piece of content), but keep the weight on the criteria.
