I'm a journalist working on a piece about whether "AI alignment" names a single problem or a bundle of them, and a source I interviewed handed me an argument I can neither dislodge nor accept.

(1) A system is aligned only if it does what we would endorse, not merely what we managed to write down for it.
(2) Every target we can actually hand a machine — a loss function, a reward model, a set of written rules, a corpus of demonstrations — is finite and coarse, while the situations the machine may face are open-ended. Call this the specification gap.
(3) So for any such target T, there are systems that optimize T and behave in ways we would reject. The standard patch — make the system defer to us, ask, or stop in cases it can't handle — inherits the gap, since "defer exactly when deference is called for" is as open-ended as anything else, and a system can pass every test of good deference while still deferring in the wrong cases or subtly steering what we say.
(4) Hence no amount of getting a system to optimize a specified target, deferential or not, ever settles whether it is aligned. Alignment must consist in tracking something we cannot specify in advance: what we would want under better information and more reflection.
(5) But that is not a fixed point we can aim at either: our reflective judgments shift, they are formed partly under the influence of the systems we have already built, and there need be no unique place at which reflection converges.
(6) So there is no fact of the matter whether a system is aligned: for any candidate rendering of "what we really want," whether a system that satisfies it counts as aligned is indeterminate.
(7) So nothing could count as achieving alignment, even to a degree, and "the alignment problem" has no object — at best it is a label for several unrelated technical problems: robustness, out-of-distribution generalization, oversight.
(8) But the same reasoning runs unchanged for a statute, an employee, a child: finite specification, open-ended cases, revisable reflective endorsements. If (7) were right, none of those would have a target either — and plainly they can go better or worse.

I want to keep (2) and (3): my reporting keeps turning up systems that satisfy the spec and do the wrong thing. I want to keep the last clause of (8). Past that I will give up whatever I have to. Which step should I reject, why does it look sound when it isn't, and what does rejecting it commit me to about whether alignment is one problem or several?
