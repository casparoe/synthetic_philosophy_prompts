I'm TAing for an intro epistemology course and we got completely bogged down on the unit about pragmatic encroachment vs epistemic contextualism. You know the setup: the contextualists say whether it's true to say someone knows p depends on the attributor's context—especially the attributor's practical stakes or how salient error possibilities are—so in a chill low-stakes conversation 'I know the bank is open tomorrow' can be true, but in a high-stakes version of the same conversation (or when a sceptic starts talking) the same sentence can be false. The pragmatic encroachers (the subject-sensitive invariantist types) say no, the attributor's conversational context is irrelevant; what matters is the subject's own practical stakes: if she has a lot riding on p, she needs a stronger epistemic position to count as knowing, and the word 'knows' doesn't shift around with whoever's speaking. And then there are the stubborn invariantists who think neither the attributor's context nor the subject's stakes touch knowledge at all—they only affect what it's practically rational to do. Everyone in class just traded bank cases and airport cases and nobody moved an inch; every time one side gave an intuition, the other side said 'that's just your context talking' or 'you're confusing knowledge with rational action.' I left feeling like the debate has totaly stalled.

Call it the reflexive-stakes case: Ada is trying to decide whether to drive over a bridge. If Ada takes herself to know the bridge is safe, she won't get out and inspect it, and if the bridge is actually unsafe she dies—huge stakes. But if Ada withholds knowledge (because she's unsure), she'll inspect the bridge first, find out it's unsafe, and not drive—so the cost of being wrong is tiny. Her practical stakes about 'the bridge is safe' seem to depend on whether she thinks she knows it. So if knowledge depends on stakes (as the encroachers say), and her stakes depend on her epistemic self-assessment, we get this loop: to know whether she knows, you need to know her stakes, but her stakes seem to depend on whether she knows (or thinks she knows). Is this a decisive problem for pragmatic encroachment—do we need to say knowledge depends only on actual/objective stakes, or on stakes she believes she has, or do we need some fixed-point story?
