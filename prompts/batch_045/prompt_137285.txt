I co-host a tiny history podcast with a friend — every other week a listener sends in a "why did X happen?" and we try to say whether the explanation is any good or whether it's just a story that hangs together nicely. I do data work by day so my instinct is always comparison classes and baselines; my co-host thinks the whole thing reduces to one test: could you have made money betting on it? I'm pretty sure that cant be right but I can't say why cleanly. And I keep tripping over the pop-psych stuff I've read about — hindsight bias, the thing where an outcome feels like it had to happen once you know it happened, the "narrative fallacy" business where a smooth story makes a fragile outcome look inevitable. What I actually want is a rule I can apply to a submitted paragraph, not a general warning that stories are seductive.

Here's where my gut lands on the ones we've done, and I can't get them to line up. (1) "The Titanic sank because she hit an iceberg" — bad, even though it's true and even though it's the thing that distinguishes her from ships that didn't sink. But "she sank because the bulkheads didn't go high enough and she was running near full speed at night through reported ice" — good. I think the difference is that the first explains why she hit and the second explains why hitting turned into sinking, but I don't know how to say that in general. (2) "Rome fell because of lead pipes / decadence" — obviously bad, and I also bristle at "Rome fell" as a thing that happened at all, so maybe part of what's wrong is that the explanandum is fake. (3) "Napoleon lost at Waterloo because he waited for the ground to dry before attacking, which let the Prussians come up" — I'm inclined to accept it, even though nobody predicted it and it's one contingent detail. (4) "The Bronze Age palace economies collapsed because they were complex and interdependent, so a shock to the trade network brought the whole system down" — this one is the least satisfying and the most true-sounding at the same time, and I genuinely can't tell if I'm being had by a fancy word. (5) "The 2008 crash was overdetermined: subprime, leverage, the ratings agencies, shadow banking, all necessary, none sufficient" — I think that's a dodge, but the one-cause version ("it was all Fannie Mae") I also reject, and I want a rule that says what the good middle looks like rather than just splitting the difference. (6) Someone who wrote in 2005 that US housing was a bubble and now says "see?" — I want to give them credit, but only if their reasoning was right; being early on its own shouldn't count. Except I also feel that a story assembled after the fact should be penalised somehow, or else everything is post hoc. Those two verdicts feel like they pull in opposite directions. (7) "My grandfather's hardware store failed in 1981 because he was too proud to take a partner" — I believe it, from knowing him, and it's a single contingent detail just like Waterloo; but I also feel that for individual lives the story-shaped explanation is always available and always flatters the teller, so maybe I should distrust it exactly as much. (8) A listener explained a failed 1970s startup by saying the founders lacked "grit," and when I asked what a non-gritty founder looks like he couldn't say — that feels like a fake version of the same move I like in case 1.

The rough rule I've been trying out is two-part: an explanation is good only if (a) it names a mechanism that's attested independently of this outcome — we've seen it work somewhere else, not just here — and (b) the thing it names is shown to matter relative to a comparison class, meaning cases like this one that went the other way. My co-host says (b) does all the work and (a) is just (b) in different clothes, and I half suspect he's right about Waterloo and wrong about the Bronze Age. So: is there a single principle that captures my verdicts, or most of them? State it precisely, tell me which clause is carrying which case, where my two-part rule breaks, and be straight with me about which of my verdicts I should give up — I'd rather drop one than keep a rule with an extra clause bolted on just to save it. Then run whatever principle you land on at three cases we haven't done: a one-off with no comparison class at all (why farming got going in the Fertile Crescent when it did), an explanation of something that didn't happen (why the industrial transformation didn't happen in Song China), and the one I keep going back and forth on — whether it's legitimate to explain an outcome by saying the people inside it couldn't see it coming, e.g. that everyone in 1914 expected a short war and that expectation is partly why they got a long one. Also: is the betting test a decent proxy for the real thing, or is it actively misleading, and if it's misleading, where exactly does it mislead? And one last thing — is my discomfort in case 2 (Rome "falling" at all) part of the same principle, or a separate check I should be running first?
