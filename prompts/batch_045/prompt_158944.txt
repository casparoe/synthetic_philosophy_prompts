I'm a doctoral student in comparative economic history, and my dissertation's methods chapter has stalled on something I can't resolve from the literature itself: whether the contrastive "why" questions that organize this field are well posed at all. My own chapter asks a contrastive question about a pair nobody writes books about — why the Dutch Republic and not the Ottoman Empire, in a period where both had substantial commercial sophistication, fiscal apparatus, and long-distance shipping — and I don't want to spend three years discovering that the form of the question was doing the work. So I want a specification, not another survey.

Write the desiderata that a contrastive historical explanation must meet to count as a satisfactory answer to a question of the form "why F rather than C?", where F is the focal case that got the outcome and C is the contrast case or class that didn't. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of one methodological camp. Fix the standard of adequacy as follows: an explanation is good enough if it would be worth three years of primary-source work to test — that is, if a negative finding about it (the cause was absent in F, or it was present in C after all) would be informative rather than merely deflationary.

Below are the candidate requirements I keep seeing asserted, which I can't sort into genuine demands and camp prejudices. Adjudicate them, add the ones I've missed, and feel free to re-cut the taxonomy — I care more about the criteria and their motivating cases than about the scoring of answers.

1. Whether the contrast class must be a "natural" class rather than one gerrymandered to make the focal case look exceptional, and what test would distinguish the two. "Why Britain and not China?", "why north-west Europe and not the Yangzi delta?", and "why Britain and not France?" give noticeably different lists of candidate causes. If the answer to the question is unstable under re-description of the arms at coarser or finer grain, is that a defect in the question, a discovery about the outcome's causal structure, or neither?

2. Whether an adequate explanation must be contrast-invariant — the same cause doing the work under any reasonable re-description of both arms — or whether it may be contrast-relative, in the sense of naming whatever F had that C lacked, and thereby shifting as C shifts. My suspicion is that most published answers are contrast-relative while presenting themselves as invariant, and I want to know whether that is a real failing.

3. Symmetric counterfactual support. Must a candidate cause both (a) have been sufficiently absent in C that adding it would have produced the outcome there, and (b) have been sufficiently present in F that removing it would have prevented the outcome here? Or is one direction enough? In practice the literature almost always argues only one direction, and I want the cost of demanding both stated explicitly.

4. Separation of the arms. In an experiment, treating one unit doesn't affect the other. In these cases the units traded with each other, borrowed each other's techniques, competed for the same bullion flows and the same colonial hinterlands, and the outcome in F plausibly depressed the outcome in C. Can a contrast be well posed when the arms are causally coupled? If not, much of this literature is answering an ill-posed question. If yes, we owe an account of what "holding C's trajectory fixed while removing F's cause" could even mean.

5. Diachronic adequacy: must a good explanation also say why the divergence happened when it did, and not merely why it happened in F rather than C? The awkward case is the near-miss — a contrast case that appears to have had many of the hypothesized preconditions centuries earlier and still did not make the transition. Does an adequate answer have to explain the near-miss, or is the near-miss evidence that the contrast has been misdescribed?

6. Transportability versus difference-making. Must the cause be such that instantiating it in C would have produced the outcome there, as opposed to being merely correlated with the outcome within the observed set of cases? And separately: how should we treat causes present in both arms — factors that are genuine causes of the outcome but not of the difference, and that are therefore invisible to the contrast while plausibly doing most of the causal work? I want a requirement, not a shrug.

7. Level and integration. Explanations are offered at the level of measurable proximate factors (coalfield location, real wages, freight rates), at the level of institutions and property rights, at the level of beliefs and intellectual culture, and at the level of sheer contingency. Must an adequate answer integrate levels, or is a single-level answer acceptable if complete at its level? Is the standing demand for a "deeper" explanation a desideratum or the prejudice of the institutions camp?

8. Whether the question is well posed at all when the outcome is a matter of degree and the arms were already on divergent trajectories before the divergence — i.e., whether the contrast presupposes a shared baseline that the evidence does not in fact supply.

Flag any pair of desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied. If the list turns out to be a result — if, say, no explanation can be both stable under re-description and fine-grained enough to be worth testing, or both honest about causal coupling between the arms and usable as a contrast — say so, and say what conclusion we should draw about the question rather than about any proposed answer to it. Then, in a few lines and only to show the list has teeth, name the single desideratum that excludes the largest share of the usual families of answers (resource endowments, institutions, culture, state formation, pure contingency).
