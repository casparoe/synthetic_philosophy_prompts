I'm writing a thesis chapter on the Hart–Fuller exchange, and I want to put my reading of it to you before I commit to it. As I understand him, Hart concedes Fuller's central claim: by granting both that any viable legal system must contain a minimum content of natural law and that judges must sometimes legislate in the penumbra of a rule, he accepts that legality has a moral threshold, a point beyond which a system's purported rules cease to be law and become organised coercion. On that reading the debate is not about whether there is such a threshold but only about where it lies, and the famous dispute over the post-war informer cases is simply a quarrel about where that line should be drawn.

Two things make me doubt the reading, and I would be grateful for your view on each. First, on the informer cases: Hart and Fuller seem to converge on the outcome. Both thought the defendant should be punished, and Hart's objection was to the courts' reliance on Radbruch's formula rather than to retroactive punishment as such. If they agree on the result and on the remedy, is there anything substantive left between them? Second, if what remains is only a dispute about where to set a threshold, the whole exchange looks like a verbal disagreement about the extension of 'law', in which case the chapter ought to be reframed as a meta-semantic one about which concept of law we should employ. Is that the right conclusion, or is there a disagreement that is not about the word at all, and that my reading is suppressing?
