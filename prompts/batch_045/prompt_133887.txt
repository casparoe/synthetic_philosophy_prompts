I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and once a year I run a two-day numeracy bootcamp for reporters: mostly probability, a bit of causal inference, and one session where I try to teach them enough game theory that they can read a poli-sci paper without embarrassing themselves. I'm writing the problem set for that session and I need one multi-part question on iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies where the eliminators are allowed to be mixed strategies.

The point of the question is to seperate people who can actually operate the machinery from people who've memorised the definition. So: first it should make them build a payoff matrix in which some strategy is strictly dominated by a mixture of two pure strategies but by neither of those pure strategies on its own — they have to construct it, not recognise one from a lecture slide. Second, it should ask whether the order in which eliminations are performed can change which profiles survive once mixed dominators are on the table, and make them either show why it can't or hand me an explicit counterexample matrix, whichever is actually right. Third, it should go at the thing I can never myself remember: whether a strategy that is never a best response to any belief about what the others will do is the same thing as a strategy that is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy, and whether that answer changes when there are three players rather than two.

Constraints: 45-minute take-home, 25 marks total. These are people who are fine with expected-value arithmetic and can read a matrix but have no proof-writing background, so anything that would really need a separating-hyperplane argument should be asked as state whether it holds and back it up with a matrix rather than prove it. And the game should be dressed up in something a reporter would recognise — competing outlets deciding whether to run a story, say — because blank matrices put them to sleep.

Give me the question text first, then a model answer of 700-800 words including the matrices, then the marking scheme as a markdown table with exactly these columns: Part | What a full-marks answer must do | Marks | The specific mistake that should cost marks here. After the table, add a JSON object with these fields: total_marks, time_minutes, prerequisite_skills (array of strings), hardest_part (one sentence on which part does the real discriminating), bad_answer_tells (array of two or three strings describing what an answer looks like when someone has memorised the vocabulary without understanding it).

One last thing: if there's a wrinkle in the two-player versus three-player bit that the standard treatments skip over, I want the question to force it into the open — that's where I expect even my strongest participants to bluff, and I'd rather grade the bluff than miss it.
