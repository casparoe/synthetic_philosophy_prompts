I'm a fourth-year PhD student finishing a paper on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, and I'm defending a response I've started calling the currency view. The objection I'm answering is the familiar one: the things we call pleasures — the specific satisfaction of finally seeing why a proof works, the relief of a cold drink, the kick of a pun, bodily ease after pain, the absorbed pleasure of playing well — seem to share no introspectible qualitative feature, so pleasure looks like a gerrymandered kind, and a theory that tells us to maximize it looks like a theory with no unit of measurement. My reply borrows an analogy with money: the items on a shopping list need not resemble one another for their prices to be commensurable, and what makes something a price is a functional role in a system of exchange rather than a look or a feel. Likewise, I argue, what unifies pleasures is not a phenomenology but a role: each is a state that functions, within a creature's economizing of its own behaviour, as a positive scalar signal — something the system is structured to seek more of and to trade off against other such signals at some rate, however steep. On this view the heterogeneity of pleasures is not a puzzle to be solved by discovering a hidden common quality but exactly what we should expect if pleasure is a common currency rather than a kind of experience; and questions like how intense was it compared with that other one become tractable in principle even across wildly different cases, and even, eventually, across persons and species.

What I want from you is an honest map of what would count against this, and I'd rather have the argumentative structure than any citations — I know the literature, so please don't give me reading lists or bibliographies, just the defeaters. I'm especially interested in four things I keep circling. First, stipulated cases: a subject whose reinforcement system is wired so that the states which reinforce are precisely the ones she finds most aversive (a reverse-valence case); a pair of pleasures that no rate of exchange will substitute between, because one of them simply trumps any amount of the other; and a creature with two valuation systems that never communicate with each other, so that there is no fact about how much of one it would give up for the other. For each, tell me whether the currency view is refuted, merely embarrassed, or can absorb it, and what exactly it has to give up. Second, empirical results that arguably already exist: the now-familiar dissociation between wanting and liking in reward neuroscience, and the growing evidence that valuation is reference-dependent and context-sensitive rather than a matter of reading off a single stored scalar. Are these decisive, awkward, or irrelevant, and would a defender of my view concede them as counting against it? Third, my supervisor's charge that the view is a motte-and-bailey: that the exciting claim — pleasantness is what matters — gets defended by arguments that only establish that reinforcement signals matter, and that if pressed I will retreat to the second. Is that a real defeater, or is it just a slogan? Fourth, and what I personally find most persuasive: I am moved by cases where the view is forced to call something pleasant that no one in the room would call pleasant, or where a subject's own sincere report gets overridden by the theory. I want to know whether the view can be stated so that this genuinely cannot happen, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a confession that it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has been, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them a committed defender would concede and which she would simply dismiss.
