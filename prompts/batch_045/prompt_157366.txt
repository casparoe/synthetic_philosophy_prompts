I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm currently working on a piece about what happens to moral language once a great deal of it starts coming out of institutions and machines rather than people: crisis triage protocols drafted by rotating hospital committees and then applied by software, model-generated summaries of what a patient "ought" to be told, training datasets built from moral annotations whose authors are dead. At a workshop dinner I sat through recently, two philosophers had an argument I have not been able to stop turning over, and I would like it adjudicated rather than summarized. They began from common ground: expressivism holds that to call something wrong is not to state a belief but to voice a conative attitude, something closer to cheering or booing than to describing; the Frege–Geach problem is that moral sentences keep their full meaning in positions where nothing is being voiced, as when "stealing is wrong" sits in the antecedent of a conditional, and yet moral arguments containing such sentences look straightforwardly valid; and the best-known expressivist reply, the quasi-realist one associated with Simon Blackburn, relocates validity one level up, so that what makes the argument good is not shared representational content but the incoherence of simultaneously being for the antecedent, for the conditional, and against the consequent.

From there it went like this. (1) The critic, Julian, introduced what he called "unowned" moral sentences: tokens that are produced, stored, and used without any agent ever having the relevant attitude and, in some cases, without any agent being in a position to have it — a protocol line saying that withholding life-sustaining treatment in a certain class of cases is permissible, or a verdict emitted by a model trained on annotators no one can now question. He said such tokens enter inferences that are plainly valid and plainly action-guiding, while the expressivist's surrogate for validity has nothing to bite on, since there is no one whose attitudes could be incoherent. (2) The expressivist, Nadia, answered that this is the original problem in a new costume: the Frege–Geach problem was always about unasserted occurrences, an unowned token is simply an unasserted one with nobody standing nearby, and meaning is fixed by the practices of a community of attitude-holders from which the token inherits its content; the protocol's inference is valid in that anyone who did take up those sentences — who applied them while rejecting their upshot — would be incoherent. (3) Julian replied that this makes validity hostage to agents who will never exist, since the protocol simply runs and no one takes it up in the relevant sense, and that in the meantime Nadia is quietly treating the sentences as correct or incorrect independently of anybody's attitudes, which is the very thing in dispute; he added that if content can be inherited from attitudes held by people long dead, then the attitudes are doing no semantic work and expressivism has become a causal story about how a practice got started. (4) Nadia's sharpest move was to say that Julian's pressure case is really a dispute about what counts as a bearer of commitment rather than about moral semantics: expressivism never required an occurrent feeling, the attitude in the embedded antecedent was never felt either, and if an institution or a system with a stable dispositional profile can count as a commitment-bearer, then its patterned dispositions can coherently or incoherently hang together and the view accommodates the case. (5) Julian answered that if commitments can float entirely free of affect, motivation, and any capacity to be moved by what one "accepts," the position survives only by ceasing to be a distinctive semantic thesis — it has become realism with a deflationary theory of truth bolted on, and the old motivation for expressivism, the tight link between moral judgment and motivation, has quietly evaporated. Neither would budge, and I genuinely could not tell who was winning.

So please referee it. Go through the five moves and tell me who is right about what at each step — ideally rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying the rating briefly, since I suspect several of them are partly right in different respects — and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. Specifically: are unowned sentences a genuinely new problem for expressivism or only the Frege–Geach problem re-described; does the disagreement in fact bottom out somewhere other than moral semantics, in a general theory of content or of what an agent is; does the case tell us anything about whether a practice needs live participants to confer meaning; and what, if anything, all of this settles about the question I actually have to answer in my piece — whether sentences issued by committees, protocols, and models can be said to mean what our moral sentences mean.
