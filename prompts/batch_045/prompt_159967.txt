I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training. The only modal logic I've met came from model checking at work, where a Kripke structure is a set of states with a transition relation and a labeling function, and "necessarily P" just means P holds at every reachable state. I later ran into people using the same vocabulary — possible worlds, rigid designators, necessity — and I've been trying to reimplement the famous claim that water is necessarily H2O but knowable only empirically, as a small executable model, because I don't think I understand it until I can compile it. I keep getting a result that seems too cheap, and I want to know whether my implementation is faithful or whether I've built the conclusion in by hand.

My encoding: a rigid designator is a globally scoped identifier, bound once, at the state where the naming event happens; every other state must resolve that identifier to the same entity, or the model fails to typecheck. Given that, the necessity of the identity looks like a compile-time guarantee I introduced myself. No two states can disagree about the identity of a referent, because identity of referents is just equality of the identifier, and the a posteriori part is only a runtime lookup of which identifier got bound in the actual state.

Three consequences follow, and I can't tell which are bugs and which are features:

1. On this reading the necessity looks like a fact about our naming history rather than about the stuff in the lakes. Had we encountered the other liquid first — the one that looks and behaves like water but has a different microstructure — we would have bound "water" to it, and the identity would have come out necessarily false. So the necessity of the actual claim looks indexed to a contingent fact about how we in fact use the word, which makes it look a priori after all: necessary relative to a hidden parameter. The same worry applies to the standard morning-star/evening-star case: once both names are rigid, their identity is necessary just by the necessity of identity, so what is left for astronomers to have discovered?

2. My typechecker rejects any state in which a bound identifier has no referent, so it makes every named thing exist in every state. Combined with the usual insistence that Aristotle could not have been a nonhuman animal, it forces me to add strange states containing a nonhuman Aristotle, or one with different parents. That feels like a reductio of my encoding rather than a discovery.

3. If necessity is only truth at every state in my model, it depends on which states I chose to include and how I drew the transitions, so the dispute looks like a disagreement about model parameters that could be settled by stipulation. Is there anything in the view that rules out my parameterization?

Relatedly: what should the model do in the case where what everyone took to be one mineral turned out to be two distinct substances rather than one? Does the necessity claim simply evaporate there?

I suspect I manufactured the necessity in my choice of encoding, and that the celebrated necessary a posteriori is mostly variable binding plus the necessity of identity. Please go through my three points, tell me plainly where my reconstruction stops being faithful and what I should have written instead, and use my encoding vocabulary where you can. Please answer in American English.
