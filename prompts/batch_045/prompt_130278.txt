I'm a PhD candidate in my department's metaphysics group and I'm the graduate teaching fellow writing this term's final for our third-year seminar on dispositions and causation, which I'll also be grading. My own dissertation chapter is on whether dispositions can be identified with their causal bases, so I've been living with this material, and I've now had the same argument about three times with my office-mate, who thinks the whole fink/mask discussion is settled and boring. The seminar spent two weeks on the simple conditional analysis, on the fink cases where the stimulus itself removes the disposition, on the mask cases where something extrinsic blocks the manifestation, and on the standard repair — roughly, that x is disposed at a time to give response r to stimulus s just in case there is some intrinsic property B that x has at that time and some later time t' such that, if x were to undergo s while retaining B right up to t', then s together with x's having B would be a sufficiently complete cause of x's giving r. My worry is that a competent student can write a whole blue book saying that finks remove the disposition while masks prevent the manifestation, name the repair, and still not have touched the real crux: both objections, and the repair itself, quietly assume that we already have a grip on whether the disposition survives the stimulus, independent of the very counterfactual behavior that was supposed to be doing the analyzing. I want a question that makes that assumption the thing under examination.

So I've drafted two cases of my own invention, which nobody can have read about, and I'd like the question built around them. Call the first a loan: a drinking glass is made of a polymer that deforms rather than shatters when struck hard, but it carries a microscopic monitor that detects the onset of an impact and, within a microsecond, reorganizes the glass's molecular structure into that of ordinary fragile glass — after which the glass does shatter. Intuitively the glass is not fragile and its shattering is not in virtue of any fragility it has; but it does shatter whenever struck, and, depending on whether we take x to be the glass or the glass-plus-monitor composite and on which intrinsic properties we allow ourselves to quantify over, the repair seems to deliver opposite verdicts. Call the second a race: the glass really is fragile, but it is also wired to a hardening mechanism that, on detecting an impact, would prevent shattering; the mechanism takes a microsecond to act, while the shattering would occur within a nanosecond of the impact. Whether the glass counts as fragile now seems to depend on how coarsely or finely we specify the stimulus and the timescale at which we read the counterfactual. I want students to have to say what the right verdicts are, what in the accounts fixes them, and what the cases show about whether a disposition is something over and above its basis.

What I need from you is the finished exam item: the essay question itself, worded so that it can be answered in ninety minutes and about 1,200 words closed-book, and phrased so that simply reciting the fink/mask distinction and the repair earns little; then a model answer of roughly 700 words; then a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. Please do not build the question or the answer around any bibliographic knowledge: I want students to argue from the cases themselves, so no citations, no further reading, and no names of papers are required anywhere in the question, the model answer, or the scheme. Please write everything in American English.
