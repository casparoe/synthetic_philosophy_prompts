I'm co-teaching a first-year seminar next term that pairs Shakespeare's tragedies with some formal epistemology and rational choice theory, and Macbeth lands the week after we do prediction and decision. A colleague from the mathematics department sat in on my preparatory lecture and said afterwards, only half joking, that the play is just Newcomb's problem with better dialogue: a reliable predictor tells an agent something about her own future action, and everything downstream is a consequence of how she handles that information. I think that's too quick, but I couldn't say exactly why, and I'd like to be able to before I stand in front of thirty students and a smirking probabilist. So: read the play through the family of cases that includes Newcomb's problem and Death in Damascus, and tell me where the frame illuminates a scene and where it distorts it.

Let me fix the set-up, because I've seen these cases stated with different stipulations and I want the mapping disciplined. Death tells you he is coming for you tomorrow; he has already recorded his destination in a sealed letter that will be opened after you act; he is an infallible predictor of what you will do; you die if and only if you are in the same city as he is; staying in Damascus costs nothing and fleeing to Aleppo costs you everything you own. The letter is already written, so nothing you do now can change where Death will be — but your decision is also the best possible evidence of what the letter says. I want you to say, first, what the main decision-theoretic verdicts in that neighbourhood come to under whatever stipulation you adopt, and to stay consistent with it; then distinguish three kinds of predictor the formal literature usually keeps separate, because I suspect the play moves between them: an infallible predictor whose prediction is fixed in advance, a merely reliable one with a track record but some error rate, and a predictor who has preferences and whose announcement is a move in a game rather than a forecast. Which of the three are the Weird Sisters, scene by scene? Does it matter that they seem to want something, whereas Death is only doing a job?

Three further things I keep turning over and would like handled in the reading. First, the early aside where Macbeth reasons that if chance means him to be king, chance may crown him without his stirring — that strikes me as a genuine evidential-decision moment, the one place where he sees the outcome as possibly independent of his act, and Lady Macbeth's response is to close that option off and treat the prophecy as something to be made true. Banquo, by contrast, is told his descendants will be kings, declines to stir toward it, and says the powers that tell him this are telling him a truth in order to destroy him: that looks like a claim about the predictor's intentions rather than about the prediction's reliability, and I don't think the standard case has a slot for it. Second, randomization: if the prediction is already fixed, is there a version of the case where flipping a coin is the rational response, and is the play's refusal of that option a feature of Macbeth's character or a feature of the situation? Third, and what I most want your view on: the formal set-up assumes the agent knows what the possible outcomes are, whereas the apparitions tell Macbeth things that are true but that he misdescribes to himself — the man not of woman born, the wood that comes to the hill — so his outcome space isn't transparent and the prediction turns out to be informative about language rather than about the world. Say whether that observation is a criticism of the play-as-decision-problem reading, or a real gap in the theory that the play exposes. I don't need line references or secondary reading; my students have the text and I have the philosophy. What I want is a reading faithful in both directions: don't reduce the tragedy to an illustration, don't bend the theory to fit it, and be willing to say which stretches of the play — the murder itself, the banquet, the sleepwalking, the last act — are not about prediction at all but about something adjacent, like sunk costs and the collapse of the capacity to deliberate, and where that leaves the frame.
