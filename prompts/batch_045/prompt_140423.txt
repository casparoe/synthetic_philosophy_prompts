Our college's naming committee has spent two terms arguing about a single name, and because the full faculty will vote on it in May I've been asked to produce a summary of the argument. I took the minutes and honestly cannot tell who had the better of it.

The case: Marchbank College was endowed in 1871 by Josiah Marchbank, a shipowner and backbench MP. A graduate researcher working in the family papers two years ago traced a little over a third of the founding endowment to contracts for the carriage of indentured labour in the Indian Ocean. The same papers show that Marchbank privately called those contracts a scandal in a letter to his brother and never sold the shares, that he anonymously endowed a hospital for seamen's families and a subscription library, and that he campaigned against the extension of the franchise. The deed of gift is unusual: it stipulates that if the college ever ceases to bear the name, a fixed share of the original endowment reverts to a family trust. Three options are on the table — rename the college, keep the name but install a permanent exhibit on the indenture trade in the entrance hall, or keep the name on the college and rename only its great hall.

The two longest-serving members of the committee, Dr. Naomi Okonjo and Dr. Sabine Reinholt, both framed the question using Nietzsche's early essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and they agreed to treat its three modes — monumental, antiquarian and critical — as the vocabulary of the dispute. I am interested in adjudicating the arguments, not in who has written about them, so please work from the concepts themselves rather than from anything you'd need to look up. Their exchange, compressed:

(1) Okonjo: A name is not a description of the past but a present-tense claim about what is worth remembering and emulating, which is exactly what Nietzsche means by monumental history — holding up the great in the past as an incentive to the living. The question is therefore not whether Marchbank was a man of his time, or whether the ledger of his life comes out net positive. It is whether we want the college's self-presentation to be a monument to him. It doesn't, so the name goes.

(2) Reinholt: The same essay that licenses the critical mode also warns that history in excess is hostile to life, and that the capacity to forget, and to act inside an unhistorical horizon, are conditions of any action at all. The critical mode is a solvent. It can tell you what to destroy and never what to build, and it is self-undermining here, because the judging generation is the product of the very past it prosecutes. Besides, you have the object wrong: a building name is a receipt, not a statue. Nobody passing under the arch is being invited to admire Marchbank.

(3) Okonjo: The receipt distinction is elegant and empirically false. Prospective students look the name up; our own admissions material tells a story about a visionary benefactor; the name does small, repeated pedagogical work about whose judgment counts. And Nietzsche's test is functional, not semiotic — history for the sake of life, of action and struggle. What matters is what the name does, and what it does is instruct.

(4) Reinholt: Then apply your own test honestly and count the costs — the reverter clause means that a third of the endowment leaves with the name, and that money currently funds scholarships for students from exactly the region the labour contracts recruited from. More importantly, notice a structural bias in critical history as a practice. The archive is a residue produced disproportionately by the powerful and the litigious, and it preserves denunciation far better than it preserves decency. Marchbank's contracts and pamphlets survive in folders; the gratitude of the seamen's families survives in three letters. A tribunal whose evidence base is the documentary residue of power will find guilt with near-perfect reliability, and each successive generation will conclude that it is the first to have seen clearly. That is not reckoning; it is a ratchet.

(5) Okonjo: That the archive is biased is an argument for epistemic humility in assigning blame, not for leaving the name in place. And 'you are the product of the past, so you cannot judge it' proves far too much — it would bar every reform ever undertaken by someone who had benefited from what they reformed. As for forgetting: the unhistorical forgetting Nietzsche describes belongs to a creature that must sleep and then act. An institution that has already chosen to remember this much — an archive, a founding date, a portrait, a benefactor named in perpetuity in the deed — cannot claim the innocence of forgetting. Once you are a memory institution you are in the historical mode; the only question left is which one.

(6) Reinholt: Here is what actually stops me, and I say it as a person whose great-great-grandfather came to this colony under those contracts. I don't want the name removed. I want it kept with the indenture ledgers mounted in the entrance hall directly beneath it. Remove it and you produce a clean institution whose money is mysteriously untainted; in fifty years nobody will be able to find the connection, because the connection is now spelled only in the name. The critical mode, completed, is its own kind of forgetting: it consumes the evidence and then declares innocence. What I want is neither veneration nor demolition but something like antiquarian history with a trapdoor — loyal to the small, the shameful and the ordinary, which is also, on Nietzsche's own account, the mode most liable to decay into an indiscriminate reverence for whatever is merely old.

I would be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and separate the claim actually made from any stronger nearby claim it might be trading on. Then say who, if anyone, is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing — including any resources in the essay's own argument that neither side reaches for.

Two further things I'd like addressed directly. First, take Reinholt's ratchet point and run it forward rather than backward: if our successors will apply some future standard to us with better archives and different morals, does the practice of posthumous reckoning have any stable content at all, or is it a process that can only ever ratchet and never settle? Second, assess her 'antiquarian history with a trapdoor' proposal on its own terms. Is keeping the name precisely in order to display what it rests on a coherent position within Nietzsche's threefold scheme, a degenerate case of one of the three, or a fourth thing that the scheme has no room for — and does his account give us any way to evaluate an institution that deliberately curates the evidence against itself?
