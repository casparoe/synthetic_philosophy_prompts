hey — I'm taking a social choice / political philosophy module this term and we did Sen's liberal paradox last week (the impossibility result: you can't have even a minimally liberal social choice rule and Pareto at the same time, the textbook illustration being the prude and the lewd guy and who ends up reading Lady Chatterley's Lover, where the prude's preference is really about what the other guy reads). I get why it's a *result*. What I can't get is what it's supposed to mean for people who actually have to decide stuff, because I am one of those people and I have to do it every two weeks.

I'm one of three undergrad reps on my uni's residence-life committee. We meet fortnightly in a 90-minute slot, we get through maybe five proposals a meeting, and whatever we decide binds about 3,500 residents for the rest of the academic year. Every decision needs a one-paragraph written rationale that goes to the Dean, we're not allowed to abstain or defer (a non-decision just locks in the status quo), and the only check on us is the Dean's veto, which is only ever used on legal/insurance grounds. Real things on our backlog: whether the ground-floor lounge is a quiet study space or a social space; overnight guest limits; whether a hall goes alcohol-free; whether we assign rooms without asking residents whether they'd mind a vaper as a roommate; mandatory "wellness checks" on anyone an RA hasn't seen in three days; whether to ban candles.

What I want is a decision procedure my committee could actually run — five minutes a proposal, no theory jargon in the output — that takes Sen's result seriously instead of quietly ignoring it and voting. So: given a proposal, how do we work out whose protected personal sphere it actually touches (if anyone's); how do we tell whether a resident's stated preference about *someone else's* conduct is self-regarding or just nosy/meddlesome, which is the thing that makes the paradox bite in the first place; and when two people's protected spheres collide, or when respecting everyone's rights leaves everyone worse off than overriding one person would, what do we do then? I know the escape hatches in principle — narrow the personal sphere, restrict the domain of admissible preferences, make rights conditional on how others exercise theirs, weaken Pareto, treat the right as a veto rather than a choice — but they're useless at 8pm on a Tuesday with a room full of people, so I need them as ordered questions with hard stopping rules. I want it to be explicit about where the procedure outputs "no decision, status quo stays, and here is the sentence you write to the Dean explaining why that was the correct outcome rather than cowardice," and where it instead hands the call to us as a values judgement we have to own out loud.

Two half-formed ideas of mine that I'd like you to build in, fix, or throw out if they're wrong:

1. A "blindfold test" for meddlesomeness: if the objecting resident couldn't tell what the other person was doing, or couldn't find out without going out of their way, does their preference still fall inside their own protected sphere — and doesn't that just dissolve the paradox case? My worry is that it also dissolves complaints I think are legitimate, like noise through a wall or vape aerosol through a shared vent, which are genuinely perceivable but still amount to "stop doing the thing you're doing in your own room."
2. A split between the right being a *veto* over outcomes and the right being *control over which options are on the table*. My hunch is that agenda-control survives Pareto but veto doesn't, and that this is roughly what Gibbard is doing with rights-over-acts rather than rights-over-outcomes — though I might be mangling that, it came from someone in my reading group, not from me. Either way I can't work out how to tell in practice which kind of right a given proposal involves.

Then run the thing on three proposals from the actual backlog, and please make one of them come out as an answer we won't like:

- Making the ground-floor lounge quiet 24/7. Big majority say the noise stops them working and they'd be better off; the minority who use it to socialise say they'd be worse off and that how they spend their own evening is nobody else's business.
- Mandatory wellness checks on any resident an RA hasn't seen in 72 hours. Near-unanimous, framed as care; a handful of residents say being checked on unbidden is *itself* worse for them than being left alone, and that it's their door.
- Banning vaping in rooms, where the complaint is specifically the smell coming through shared vents — so it is perceived by others, but nothing short of not vaping would fix it.

Finally, be straight with me about where this breaks. My guesses: we never have an actual preference profile, just a straw poll and some angry emails, so "Pareto improvement" is mostly our guess dressed up as fact; most of our proposals bundle a personal-sphere issue with a genuinely collective one (a lounge is a shared resource, not just three people's evenings); and the Dean's legal veto is a hard constraint the framework has no vocabulary for at all. Which of those is actually fatal and which is just messiness? And what can a procedure like this never deliver, no matter how well built? I'd rather hand my committee something that admits its limits than something that looks rigorous and just launders a majority vote.
