I've got my thesis defense in three weeks, and the chapter I'm going to actually get grilled on is my attempt to split apart two things the predictive processing literature treats as one thing. Sketch of the argument, so you know what to aim at:

1. There's precision in the formal sense: the inverse variance, or more generally the precision matrix, over prediction errors under the system's own generative model.
2. There's precision in the wet sense: the synaptic gain / excitability of the units carrying errors up the hierarchy, which is what people mean when they say attention just is precision optimization, and which usually gets cashed out anatomically as gain on the superficial pyramidal cells in the canonical cortical microcircuit, under neuromodulatory control.
3. The bridge claim that makes (2) an implementation of (1) is never stated, and I argue it isn't generally true. Gain-scaling and precision re-weighting come apart whenever the noise is correlated across channels, and they come apart again whenever the organism can reduce noise by moving its sensors rather than by turning up the volume on the error signal. So I swap the whole framing for a quantity I'm calling yield: expected information gain about the model's own latents, expected under a candidate policy, divided by the expected metabolic and computational cost of getting it. Precision optimization falls out as the special case where the only free knob is a scalar gain and costs are flat across channels. And I claim this buys something empirically: it predicts dissociations, cases where the system should turn gain down even though the error signal is getting louder, and vice versa.
