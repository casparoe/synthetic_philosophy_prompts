I teach a graduate seminar on global justice, and last week I sat on a university-wide panel on food security where a colleague from development economics brought her remarks to a close with the line "there is no such thing as a natural famine." She offered it as though it disposed of a question my students had been circling for two sessions: whether the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the famine years of the Great Leap Forward really belong in a single explanatory category, or whether a unified treatment of the three would paper over differences in mechanism that ought to matter to anyone doing political philosophy about them. I would like to hand my students something sharper than the slogan itself.

Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that sentence could be used to express — from the trivial to the substantive to the false — with a verdict on each. The candidates I can see are roughly these, but split, merge, or discard them as accuracy requires, and add readings I have not thought of.

1. The no-shortage-needed thesis: a famine can occur with no decline in aggregate food availability, because a collapse in entitlements, in the sense made familiar by the entitlement approach to famine, is sufficient on its own.
2. The no-shortage-sufficient thesis: a large decline in aggregate availability need not produce famine, so a harvest failure is at most a trigger and never the famine itself.
3. The definitional thesis: "famine" is partly constituted by a failure of protection, so a natural famine is close to a contradiction in terms — in which case the sentence is partly stipulative and cannot be answered with a counterexample.
4. The avoidability thesis: the deaths were preventable given the resources, technology, and administrative capacity available at the time. Note, please, that this reading risks vacuity: on a generous enough reading of "available," every death from hunger is preventable, and the slogan comes out true of any mortality event whatever and therefore says nothing.
5. The attributability thesis: a moral rather than causal claim, to the effect that identifiable agents are answerable for the deaths. This can hold even where the proximate trigger is drought, and can fail even where policy error contributed, if the error was reasonable given the information then available.
6. The explanatory-priority thesis: political and economic variables do the explanatory work, so a natural-disaster framing misleads even when a climatic shock sits in the causal chain.
7. The institutional-immunity thesis: famines occur only where those in power lack the incentive to relieve them — the companion claim one often hears in this literature, that famines do not occur where rulers face regular competitive elections and a relatively free press — so every famine is a symptom of institutional failure.
8. The expressive reading: the sentence is not primarily a description at all, but a refusal of the fatalistic register, a way of saying "do not treat this like weather." It may be excellent rhetoric and do no classificatory work whatsoever.

For each entry I want three things: whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical claim, or overstated and likely false; whether it is a claim about causation, about moral responsibility, about the meaning of a word, or about what we ought to attend to; and — the part I actually care about — whether it does the work my colleague needed it to do, namely licensing the treatment of the three cases as one kind of thing. My suspicion, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that some readings (I would guess the fifth) hold equally across all three cases while doing no taxonomic work, that others (the first and second) fit the Bengal and Irish cases far more comfortably than a command-economy case in which output itself collapsed while procurement and the suppression of information did damage of their own, and that at least one reading unifies two of the cases and splits off the third. Tell me where the taxonomy draws its lines and why.

Then test the menu against three cases of my own construction, none of which should be treated as a real historical episode.

(a) The anachronism case. A small, stateless, non-market community of herders, with no state above it and no market beyond it, loses its animals to a local climatic shock, and a substantial part of the group starves. On which readings is this not a famine? And where a reading excludes it, is that a cost — does it reveal the thesis to be a claim about a historically specific kind of society, one with states and markets, rather than about famine as such? If the historically scoped and the unscoped versions differ in verdict, please list them as separate menu entries.

(b) The mirror pair. In region A a third of the harvest is lost but entitlement protection functions and there is no excess mortality. In region B the harvest is normal and the granaries are full, but the export crop on which landless labourers depend collapses in price, and they starve with the food in front of them. Which readings count B as a famine and A as not? Which readings say instead that A's near-miss is itself a political fact while B's deaths are a market fact that no one intended and no one had a duty to prevent? I want to know whether any reading can accommodate both halves of the pair without strain.

(c) The shadow famine. An entitlement collapse raises mortality sharply, but the mechanism is disease following on destitution, crowding, and the sale of assets rather than calorie shortfall itself. Which readings — the first and seventh in particular — are committed to classifying this as a famine, and what happens to the empirical case for the slogan if much of the excess mortality in the canonical cases turns out to be of this mediated kind rather than death by starvation proper?

Finally: which of these readings is falsifiable at all, what evidence would discriminate between the ones that can come apart in principle (the first and second versus the sixth strike me as the pair most easily conflated), which reading my colleague most likely meant, and whether on that reading her use of the line was actually licensed. Please do not pad the answer with citations or a reading list; I have the literature, and what I need from you is the conceptual map.
