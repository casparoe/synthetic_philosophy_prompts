I am a retired experimental physical chemist — four decades of gravimetric and spectroscopic work, most of it spent on the tedious business of getting a number you can trust out of an instrument — and for the last two years I have been attending a philosophy reading group at the local university. We have recently worked through Quine's attack on the distinction between analytic and synthetic truths. I follow the argument, or think I do, but it does not seem to me to describe a kind of principle that I met with constantly in the laboratory, and I have rashly agreed to give the group a twenty-minute talk in the spring. So I would like to know whether what I have in mind already has a settled name and a settled analysis, or whether I would be inventing a category on the spot.

The principles I mean have a peculiar double character. First, no experiment performed inside the practice could have refuted them, because every such experiment deploys them. The constancy of atomic weights is my stock example: the whole business of inferring a composition from a weighing presupposes that chlorine, say, has the same atomic weight whatever its source, so no measurement internal to analytical chemistry could have told against it — you would have had to use it in order to test it. Second, they are nevertheless not definitions and not conventions. Their denials are perfectly intelligible, the successor practice that rejects them is coherent, and when they go we take ourselves to have abandoned something substantive about the world rather than to have agreed to speak differently or to adopt a new scale of units; the discovery of isotopes was not a linguistic decision. Third, and this is the feature I find hardest to place, they are not held in place by decision either. Nobody at the time could have stipulated otherwise, because the principle is not written down in the theory so much as built into the instruments, the data-reduction routines, the error analysis, and what a graduate student is taught to see when she looks at a chart. I would say the same of the assumption that a reading is a property of the sample and not of the measuring arrangement — that the value you record belongs to the thing measured and does not depend on what else you chose to measure alongside it. I am told that quantum mechanics is very widely read as denying precisely that, which would make it a second instance, and the additivity of weights in gravimetric work a third. Fourth: when such a principle fails, the failure is discovered from within the very practice it makes possible, using apparatus that itself presupposes it — the variability of atomic weights was found with instruments whose calibration assumed them constant.

So: is there an established concept, or a literature, for this configuration — untestable from inside yet substantive, not held by convention, not held by fiat, and found to fail from within — and if so, what belongs to it and what does not? I am not asking for references, which I can chase myself; I want the carving. Specifically: which of my four features is load-bearing and which are incidental; whether the historical replacement of such a principle is genuinely different from its having been refuted; and how the configuration relates to the near neighbours a non-specialist keeps blundering into — to the Quinean claim that nothing is immune to revision, which seems to me to be about a different axis altogether, since immunity is not what I am pointing at; to the idea that a framework carries principles that are constitutive of it, or a priori only relative to it; to a paradigm in Kuhn's sense or to the protected core of a research programme, both of which are held in place by a communal decision where my principles appear to be held in place by the plumbing; and to the thought that some propositions simply stand fast for us without resting on evidence at all. If the fit with each of these is imperfect, I would rather be told exactly where it fails than be handed a label. And if there is no established term, I would be grateful for a coined one, with a definition and a boundary case or two — for instance, the case where a practice's own improving accuracy eventually violates the principle outright, which is the case where I am least confident that my description still applies.
