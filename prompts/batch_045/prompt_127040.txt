I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of religion, and this term we are working through the argument from reasonable non-belief. On the last three offerings of the course I have found myself circling the same complaint about a certain family of replies, and I would like to be able to hand my students a name for it, because right now they keep rederiving it in their papers without realising that they have all hit the same nerve. The complaint is this. Theodicies of hiddenness typically explain why God permits someone to lack relationship with him by citing a good that the absence makes possible: a response that is not coerced, faith as a venture rather than an inference, moral choice made at an epistemic distance, a search that could have gone either way. What I keep noticing is that the goods cited are goods internal to the relationship whose absence is supposed to be explained, so that they are only available to someone who already has one foot inside it. Distance is a good for the person who knows there is someone to be distant from; a venture is valuable in the way alleged only for the one who reaches the shore; trusting without seeing is something you do to a God you believe in, not to a void. So when the explanation is applied to the case it is supposed to cover — a serious, unresentful inquirer who has weighed the matter as carefully as she knows how, concluded that there is no God, and died in that conclusion — the cited good either vanishes or has silently been swapped for a much thinner one: the value of having searched, the value of the option having been open, the contribution her case makes to the ambiguity of the world for others. Nobody would have offered the thin version as the justification in the first place. The reply, in short, is specified in the vocabulary of its own success cases and is then presented as covering the failure cases, where by hypothesis the success never arrives.

I want to be clear about what I am not asking about, since students conflate this with several neighbouring things. It is not the skeptical-theist point that we should not expect to discern God's reasons: I am perfectly willing to grant that the cited good is real, valuable, and exactly the sort of thing that would justify permitting some privation — my question is about who the good is real for. It is not the unfalsifiability worry about inflating resistance until every actual non-believer counts as resisting; on the contrary, I am stipulating a genuinely non-resistant subject. It is not the familiar requirement that a good be one that could not have been obtained without the evil in question, since my worry concerns whether the good is obtained at all by the person in question rather than whether it could have been got more cheaply. Nor, I think, is it the difference between offering a full theodicy and offering a merely possible defence: making the good merely possible does not help, because the difficulty is in the content of the good, not in its actuality. My own tentative diagnosis is that the argument from hiddenness has an inescapably distributive, person-affecting premise — a perfectly loving God would seek relationship with each individual who is not resisting — while the justifications offered are impersonal and aggregate, goods of there being a world of a certain kind, and that what I am seeing is a slide between the two registers. A colleague suggested it is only survivorship bias in theological dress, which I do not think is right either: survivorship bias concerns which data points the inquirer can see, whereas this concerns which outcome the justification is described from. I also notice two distinguishable variants in the student papers, one where the good requires that the subject end up in relationship and one where the good requires only that she be aware that there is aGod she is separated from, and I am not sure whether these are one phenomenon or two.

So: does this pattern have an established name in the philosophy of religion, or in some adjacent literature — I have the vague sense that something structurally similar is discussed around risk, lotteries, and retrospective justification, or in debates about person-affecting versus impersonal value, or perhaps under some heading about global versus local theodicies? If it has a name, I want the analysis and not a reading list: what exactly the phenomenon is, what its nearest neighbours are, how it differs from each of them, and which of them it is really an instance of. If it has no name, coin one, define it tightly, and give me the boundary cases that separate it from the neighbours I have listed. I would also like your verdict on three things: whether the two variants I distinguished are genuinely one phenomenon; whether my distributive-versus-impersonal diagnosis is the right description of what is going wrong or merely one symptom of it; and how far the pattern generalises outside theology, since it seems to me to be the same structure as explaining a student's failure to learn by appeal to the value of struggling, or a runner's injury by appeal to the fortitude it built, in the cases where the learning and the fortitude never came.
