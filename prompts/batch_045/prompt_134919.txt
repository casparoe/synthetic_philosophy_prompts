I hold a view I would like stress-tested. Moral orders, as I understand them, come in three recurring kinds — honor, dignity, and victimhood: in an honor order a slight calls for a direct response from the person slighted, and reputation is what is at stake; in a dignity order slights are to be tolerated or referred to an impartial third party, and the stake is equal standing; in a victimhood order the wronged party appeals to a wider audience for sympathy and enlists third parties as allies, and the stake is acknowledged vulnerability. My further claim is that none of these is morally superior to the others: each is internally coherent, each owes its grip to the social conditions that make it stable, and criticism mounted from outside one of them begs the question — so the familiar complaints (that honor orders are violent, that victimhood orders breed dependence on audiences) carry no force their target need answer.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters a defender of the view should concede, not only the ones its critics reach for, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Three concern me most. First, opportunism: in online disputes, campus conduct systems, and workplace grievance procedures the same people appear to shift frames as convenience dictates, which suggests that these are not equilibria people inhabit but a repertoire they draw on, and that the framework therefore misdescribes its own central cases. Second, the measurement problem: I score communities on three variables — how much reputation a slight puts at stake, who is expected to respond (the victim, the victim's group, or a third party), and what counts as a proportionate response — but if a single community can score high on several at once, these begin to look like dimensions rather than cultures, and the incommensurability claim loses its footing. Third, spillover: some of these orders reliably generate patterns their own participants condemn on reflection (spirals of retaliation, a standing premium on advertising injury), and a view that cannot call that a defect looks insulated rather than vindicated.

Tell me also what the position predicts that its rivals do not, whether any observation could settle it, and honestly whether the claim that no order is morally superior has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether, for a claim of this kind, that is a defect or simply the correct posture.
