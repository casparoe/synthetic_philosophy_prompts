As a professor of philosophy preparing a graduate seminar on early modern moral thought, I want a structured probability estimate rather than an overview. The interpretive claim I want you to assess is this: In Leibniz's mature moral philosophy, what we would call other-regarding moral obligation is, at the deepest explanatory level, a species of enlightened self-love, because the agent's own perfection and happiness are necessarily bound up with the perfection and happiness of others within the optimal possible world order that Leibniz defends. Call this the reduction thesis about other-regarding duty.

Please give me your credence that the reduction thesis is the correct interpretation of the mature Leibniz, and decompose that credence explicitly. Assign rough probabilities to at least the following sub-questions:

1. Does Leibniz endorse a unified motivational psychology on which every act of will aims at some apparent good for the agent?
2. Is his account of justice as benevolence guided by wisdom irreducibly other-regarding, so that the good of others is a fundamental explanatory term rather than a byproduct of the agent's own perfection?
3. Does his metaphysical contention that each finite substance represents the whole universe from its own point of view make the contrast between self-regarding and other-regarding motivation collapse at the fundamental level?
4. On balance, is the textual and doctrinal evidence that a careful reader can assess without a specialized library better read as supporting a reduction of other-regarding duty to self-perfection, or as supporting an irreducible pluralism about the proper objects of moral concern?

Show how these probabilities combine into your final number, and do not simply average them: say which terms are conjunctive, which are alternatives, how you are weighting them, and, if you treat any of the sub-questions as probabilistically dependent, how you are handling that dependence. Then tell me which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and what kind of argument or textual evidence would move you most.

I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading, and you should not try to consult any library, archive, or online database: assume you have only the philosophical and historical knowledge you already possess. I want your own judgment stated as a number with its derivation. The point is not calibration for its own sake but forcing the reasoning into the open and marking its weakest joint.
