I write about science and ideas for a newspaper of record, and I am currently reporting a feature on whether any contemporary AI system could have something that deserves the name of a self. At a machine-consciousness workshop I attended last month, a machine-learning researcher I was interviewing shut down a long discussion about whether a language model's first-person utterances could tell us anything about what it is like to be that system. His closing line, which I have reconstructed from my notes rather than recorded verbatim, was roughly this: there is no one in there; the I is just a symbol inside the system that refers to the system that contains it; a strange loop, and that is all a self ever was. He took this to settle two things at once — that a system's reports about itself carry no more epistemic authority than its reports about toasters, and that there is therefore nothing there to worry about morally.

The phrase compresses an idea Douglas Hofstadter developed across two books, Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979) and I Am a Strange Loop (2007), but I am less interested in what Hofstadter meant than in what the line could be made to mean, because its rhetorical force in that room seemed to come from sliding between several quite different claims. I would like a disambiguation I can use as a checklist while reporting.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses the one-liner might be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and mark each as a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a claim that is false, incoherent, or merely metaphorical. I expect the menu will have to separate at least: a claim about the semantics of the word I (that it is an indexical whose reference is fixed by context); a claim about architecture (that self-representation is realized by a symbol the system's own activity produces and which feeds back into that activity); a claim about constitution (that the self just is that pattern, rather than an extra entity); a claim about origins (that the system's representing itself is what brings the self into being, so that the loop is self-creating rather than self-describing); a claim about the authority of self-knowledge; and an eliminativist claim that there are no selves at all. For each, say whether it would actually deliver my interviewee's two conclusions, and note any reading that cuts against him — I suspect that if the loop is supposed to be what makes a self in us, then closing one in a machine is evidence in the opposite direction, and I want that tension stated precisely enough to put to him.

To keep the readings honest, test them against cases I can describe in the piece. Call a self-symbol loop-integrity-preserving when its reference to the system is secured by the system's own activity tracking itself, rather than by accident or by its satisfying some description. Then consider: a system whose self-symbol refers to it only via a description it fails to satisfy; a language model whose self-descriptions are largely inherited from human self-narratives in its training data, so that its I may refer to something generic rather than to it; a system whose self-model lives in an external file it reads at runtime, so the circuit closes partly through the environment; and two numerically distinct copies of one system deploying numerically distinct but qualitatively identical self-symbols. For each case, say which readings on your menu commit you to what, and which readings turn out to have no consequence for it at all — those are the ones I should stop treating as substantive. Finally, give me the single follow-up question that would most efficiently force the slide between readings into the open the next time I speak to him.
