I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy writing on individual contributions to collective harms, and I've been arguing with my housemate — an agricultural economist — about a version of the inefficacy objection I don't think either of us has seen handled. Grant, for the sake of argument, the picture popular among people who want to defend inconsequentialism about consumer choice: the good in question (say, a supermarket's supply of chicken) is replenished in lumpy batches rather than unit by unit. The store's system reorders a fixed crate whenever displayed stock crosses a trigger, and the trigger's location is unknown to any individual shopper and drifts week to week with demand, deliveries, and waste. On that picture, my housemate says, my skipping a purchase does nothing at all except in the rare case where I happen to be the pivotal unit. My reply has been that this doesn't get you very far: if my uncertainty about where the threshold lies is spread out over the batch, then the small probability of being pivotal and the large effect conditional on being pivotal roughly cancel, and my expected difference-making lands back near one unit — close to the naive answer the objection was supposed to undercut. He thinks the cancellation is an artifact of assuming a smooth prior over the threshold. I want a number on who is closer to right.

Please give me your credence in the following proposition: granted that the good is restocked in lumpy batches with threshold locations unpredictable to the buyer, a single consumer's abstention still has a non-negligible expected causal effect on total production, so that lumpy-threshold structure does not by itself rescue the inefficacy objection. I don't want "it depends on your account of causation" — I want a number and the decomposition. Give me at least five terms with a probability and at most one sentence of justification each: (i) that expected difference-making, as opposed to causal production simpliciter or a complicity/participatory or expressive account, is the morally and explanatorily relevant quantity; (ii) that the cancellation argument above is sound, i.e. that pivotality probability times batch size comes to roughly one unit under realistic priors; (iii) that real supply chains actually have this structure with thresholds genuinely unpredictable to the shopper; (iv) that downstream substitution and reallocation — other shoppers buying the unit, price effects, waste, resale — don't wash the effect out; (v) that an expected effect of this size, mostly realized as a small chance of a large difference, is the sort of quantity a moral theory can aggregate without distortion, so that fair-share and tiny-probability reservations don't bite. Show how you combine them, name the single term your final number is most sensitive to, and tell me which one piece of empirical evidence would move it furthest. Finally: my credence is about 0.6 and my supervisor's is about 0.25 — say which of your five terms that disagreement most likely lives in. Total response: no more than 500 words.
