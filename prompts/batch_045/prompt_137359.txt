The position I hold:

The distinction between a real person and a counterfeit person is a historical one. A counterfeit is something engineered so as to pass for a person in the environments where we now meet people — built to exploit our nearly irresistible tendency to adopt the intentional stance toward anything that talks sensibly, and validated by a benchmark (Turing's imitation game) that was a mistake to have made canonical. What makes something a real person is not how it performs but how it came to be: the causal history that shaped it, the selection pressures it was actually answerable to, the fact that it had something at stake in getting things right. Consequently, no amount of behavioral evidence — not extended conversation, not apparently sincere first-person report, not distress at being called counterfeit, not success at any interrogation we devise — can in principle settle whether we are dealing with a real person. The right response is therefore institutional and forensic rather than interrogative: provenance tracking, disclosure requirements at the output channel, and liability for the makers, along the lines Dennett proposes in his Atlantic essay on counterfeit people, with the watermark on currency as the model. Call this the provenance-first view.

What I want is a map of its exposure: what would count against it, not what its critics say is wrong with it.
