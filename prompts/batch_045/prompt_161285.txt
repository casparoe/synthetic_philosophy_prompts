I spent a little over thirty years as a field entomologist, most of them running light traps for a national moth monitoring scheme, and since retiring I have been attending a philosophy reading group at the university in the town where I now live. We recently spent two sessions on what the group calls the scale argument: the thought that however small one's credence in any individual insect being conscious, the numbers involved are so enormous that the expected total of insect suffering outweighs, by many orders of magnitude, every other item on the moral ledger. I came away dissatisfied. The discussion was conducted almost entirely in abstractions, and it seemed to me to move too fast over several points that a natural scientist is trained to notice. I would like to try to think about it properly by way of fiction, and I would be grateful for your help.

The story I have in mind is set during the final season of a long-running trap survey, on a lowland site that has been sampled nightly for decades. The protagonist is a survey entomologist in her early sixties who has kept, for every night of her working life, a tally book with two columns: the number of individuals taken, and a second column that she has lately begun filling in privately with a figure she does not quite know how to justify. A younger philosopher from a reading group has come out to spend the night with her, and they sort the catch at dawn. I would like the setting to do real work. The trap holds perhaps a dozen egg-boxes; the overwhelming majority of the individuals in it belong to perhaps fifteen common species, and half of them to two or three; most are worn, most will live another few days at most; a large fraction of every cohort dies as a juvenile and never appears in any count at all. Against that background I would like three things to be dramatised rather than explained. First, the problem of correlated credences: if one cabbage white is sentient then almost certainly they all are, so the low probability attaches once to the class rather than independently to each individual. I pointed out in the group that linearity of expectation makes the expected total the same either way, but I have since come to think that this reply rather misses the point, since what correlation changes is not the expectation but the shape of the distribution — the bet becomes nearly all-or-nothing instead of a reliable trickle — and it is not obvious to me that a decision-maker ought to treat those two situations as equivalent. Second, I would like someone in the story to make, and to have gently dismantled, a cruder version of the argument that I caught myself making: that with numbers of that size the probability that at least one insect suffers is effectively one, and that this settles the matter. I now think that argument establishes something quite different from what I took it to establish, and I would like to see the difference made vivid. Third, I would like the story to feel the weight of a question about the unit of account: whether what we are counting is the life or the moment of experience, given both how short and how dominated by juvenile mortality these lives are, and given the possibility that an insect's consciousness, if there is one, is not unified across time in the way a human's appears to be.

There is also a practical knot I would like somewhere in the middle of it, drawn from my own former work. A colleague from my old institute has written asking whether their group should switch to a supposedly more humane biopesticide, and my instinctive professional answer is that it will make no difference whatever, because these populations are r-selected and held near carrying capacity, so that individuals removed are rapidly replaced by better survival among those that remain — which would mean that the intervention achieves nothing at all, or possibly achieves the opposite of what was intended. I do not know whether my instinct is right, and I do not want the story to settle it, but I would like it to be present as something the protagonist carries. In terms of manner, please write an actual story — scene, character, some small incident for the protagonist to have to decide about, such as whether to kill the morning's catch or release it — rather than a dialogue that argues. Something in the region of a thousand words, and no more than fifteen hundred. I should add that I have no interest in references or further reading: I can manage that side myself. What I want is the imaginative version of the problem, because at the moment I can hold the arithmetic in my head and I cannot quite feel it.
