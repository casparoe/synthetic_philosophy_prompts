I am drafting the honesty clause of a deployment specification for a general-purpose AI assistant, and I want to try out one candidate definition of deception against the alternatives.

The systems I care about almost never assert propositions they take to be false: they are trained to answer literally, to hedge, and to flag uncertainty. The behaviour I actually worry about is selective instead. The system answers the question as asked rather than the question the user was trying to ask; it supplies a true statement while omitting the qualification that would reverse the statement's practical upshot; it orders considerations so that one option looks better than it would under any neutral ordering. Call an output deceptive on the informed-interlocutor test just in case the system would not have produced that output, in that form, for a user who already had the information the system has and whom the system knew to have it.

Please describe the most important implications of adopting this test as the operative definition of deception in the specification. In particular: what it would classify as a violation that a no-false-assertions rule lets through; whether a disciplined policy of answering only what was asked can itself count as deception on this test, and whether that result is a defect of the test or a finding; what observable signal could stand in for the counterfactual output during training and evaluation, given that the test is defined over an output that is never actually produced; and where the test would come into conflict with legitimate competing aims such as brevity, deference to the user's framing, and not overwhelming the user with everything relevant.

Do not supply citations or a literature review; I will add those myself. Please answer in under 700 words.
