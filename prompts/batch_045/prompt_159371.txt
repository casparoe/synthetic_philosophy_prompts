I convene a small fortnightly reading group — roughly half philosophers of science, half development economists — and last week we stalled badly on Malthus. One member's line was that Malthus was not refuted but merely premature: the mechanism is intact (population, when unchecked, tending to increase in something like a geometrical ratio while the means of subsistence increase at best arithmetically, so that living standards are driven back towards subsistence by positive and preventive checks), and all that has happened is that a finite, one-off inheritance — fossil fuels, synthetic nitrogen fertiliser, the yield gains of modern plant breeding — has suspended it for a couple of centuries; run the tape out long enough and the checks return. Another member replied that this is exactly what an unfalsifiable theory sounds like once it has failed, and that 'right but early' is a face-saving redescription. I found I could not say what would settle the dispute, and that is what I want explained.

What I would like is a conceptual map rather than a verdict on the economics. Please disentangle at least these: (i) a dated prediction, which can literally be late; (ii) an undated tendency or ceteris paribus claim, which cannot sensibly be early or late at all, only applicable or inapplicable to the system at hand; and (iii) a claim about an attractor or equilibrium of a system whose variables are specified. Then apply the map to the fossil-fuel version above. My own proposal — which the group was unimpressed by and which I want you to test rather than flatter — is a specification condition: a rescuer has earned the right to say 'not yet' only if, before the delay was observed, the theory had already specified the offsetting variable, put some rough bound on how far and for how long it could push the outcome, and named an observable sign that the offset was running out. On my reading Malthus fails this, because the delay was accommodated after the fact and the offsetting causes formed no part of the original statement. But there is an obvious objection, and I want you to take it seriously instead of noting it and moving on: the condition looks strong enough to convict a great many theories we now count as successes, since working scientists almost never enumerate in advance the disturbances that will mask their predictions, and the Duhem–Quine point is that a prediction is only ever tested together with a bundle of auxiliaries. So either the condition is too strong, or successful science routinely violates our own methodological standards. Please pick a horn and argue for it.

Two further complications came up and I would like them handled. First, the awkward fact that the mechanism looks as though it had been operating, more or less as described, for the several millennia before Malthus wrote — material living standards broadly flat while population grew, the so-called Malthusian trap. Does that retrospective evidence base make 'right but early' more credible, as a theory with a genuine track record that happened to falter exactly when it was formulated, or less credible, as a theory whose support was always accommodated rather than predicted? Second: is the shift from land as the binding constraint to energy, or to ecological throughput, a rescue of the same theory by an auxiliary hypothesis, or the quiet replacement of it by a different theory that happens to share a formalism? If the latter, then 'Malthus was right' looks like an equivocation across theory identity and the whole argument is verbal. I would rather have a committed answer than a survey: take one position on the original question and defend it, and tell me in a single sentence which one further fact would most change your mind. Please write in British English, and close with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing at most eight words that states your verdict — for instance 'wrong, not early', 'right but early, precondition unmet', or 'not the same theory any more'.
