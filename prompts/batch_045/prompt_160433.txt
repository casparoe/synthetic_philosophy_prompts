I sit on the allocation committee of a small research-funding foundation, and two colleagues have argued for a year about one recurring pattern in our proposal pipeline. The Fanatic: if our best model assigns a proposal positive expected value, we fund it, whatever the probability of success and whatever the ratio of upside to cost. The Anti-Fanatic: past some ratio of stakes to confidence, expected value stops being action-guiding; she applies what she calls a reliability rule, under which a probability estimate is decision-relevant only if it survives re-derivation under every model in our ensemble that we have not ruled out. The case on the table: Option S returns roughly 4,000 units of value with probability about 0.95; Option L costs the same and returns 10^22 units with probability 3×10^-9, where that figure comes from a single structural model whose track record we cannot yet score.

Write the joint statement they should have produced instead. First a numbered list of claims both can sign, then a table of the residual disagreements, each row phrased as that side would phrase it rather than as the other would paraphrase it. My guess is that they agree on moderate-probability, moderate-stakes cases, on treating model uncertainty as a mixture over models rather than a footnote, and on there being no Dutch-book argument available here; tell me if any of that is wrong.

Then the cruxes, and for each a case or piece of evidence both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Four I want covered, though add or drop as the drafting requires:

1. The clean-lottery case: a physically known 1-in-10^9 chance at 10^22 units, finite stakes, one shot, no model uncertainty. If she funds it, her objection was epistemic all along and this is a dispute about error bars; if she declines it, the dispute is about whether expected value is normative at all. Say which, plainly.
2. Whether the reliability rule is a hard cutoff or a continuous reweighting of the probability. If continuous, is she simply a Fanatic working with different inputs, and is that row of the table verbal?
3. The portfolio argument: we make about forty allocations a year, so he says discarding each small probability loses in aggregate, while she says the aggregate upside is itself model-dependent. Is that one crux or two, and what would a Monte Carlo over our model ensemble have to show to settle it?
4. How well expert-assigned probabilities near 10^-9 have historically tracked observed frequencies — the only empirical question I think both would accept as evidence. Record what each predicts we would find and what result would actually move each of them.

Mark explicitly any row where the disagreement is about words rather than about the world, and give the wording that dissolves it. Argue from the cases and the numbers only; no citations, no reading lists, no references to the literature.
