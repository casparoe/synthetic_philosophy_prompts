Long-time lurker, read the sequences back in the day, finally got round to reading Reasons and Persons with a reading group, and I'm trying to work out what I actually think about survival rather than what I've been told it's respectable to think. So here are my gut verdicts on a pile of cases, several of them variants I made up because the standard ones let me off too easily, and I want the principle behind them — or to be told which verdicts have to go. Please answer in British English, the American spellings grate on me.

1. Destructive teletransporter, Earth to Mars: scan, destroy, rebuild from the data. I'd step in. I survive, and it's no worse than the flight.
2. Same machine, scanner doesn't destroy: I walk out of the booth on Earth and there's also a me on Mars. I'm the one on Earth. The Mars one is a copy, a descendant, someone I wish well — not me.
3. Same as 2, except I have a heart attack five seconds after the scan and the Earth body dies. My gut flips completely: now I want to say I survived, as the Mars one. Which is wierd, because nothing about the person who walked into the booth changed in those five seconds.
4. Machine destroys first, replica is built five minutes later, so there's a gap in which nobody exists who is continuous with me. I survive. But: if a meteor hits the Mars station during the gap and the replica is never built, I want to say I died back on Earth, at the moment of scanning. Those two together mean whether I survive depends on a coin flip five minutes later, and I don't know how to live with that or how to give it up.
5. Nightly fine-grained backup of my brain, restored into a grown clone body after I die in a crash. Structurally the same as case 1 as far as I can tell, but my gut says firmly not me, that's just someone else with my memories who'll be upset about me. I know 1 and 5 can't both be right as I've stated them.
6. Fission, two replicas from one scan. I don't survive as either one, identity has plainly failed — and yet I barely mind, because everything that could matter to me seems to be preserved twice over. Still: a machine that has a 50% chance of producing zero replicas and a 50% chance of producing two feels worse to me than one that reliably produces one, and I can't say why if identity isn't what matters.
7. Gradual replacement, one neuron at a time over twenty years with functionally identical prosthetics, ending with no original matter at all. Obviously me, no drama, I don't even feel the need to argue for it.
8. The one I keep going back and forth on: a scan is taken at stage one, before any replacement begins, and stored; twenty years later, after the replacement in case 7, the scan is run. The scanned person is psychologically much closer to my twenty-years-ago self than my current self is. I don't want to say he's me and I'm not, and I don't want to say we both are, and "neither of us is" seems to throw away the one case I was certain about.
9. Relay: the Mars replica lives thirty years, is scanned, destroyed, and rebuilt on Titan. My verdict is that the Titan person is the Mars person — but then I lose my grip on whether the original Earth person's survival is at stake at all, or whether that question just stops having an answer somewhere along the chain.
10. Lossy scan: 4% of the fine detail is destroyed in transmission and the machine fills the gaps with statistically typical guesses from a population model. I barely flinch, still me. (This is the one that makes me think it's not strict information-identity I believe in.)
11. Replica built from a perfect scan, then put straight into suspended animation and never activated; the original dies in the booth. Not survival, obviously. But if it's activated two hundred years later, my gut says yes, that's me waking up. Which is the same shape of problem as case 4: my survival seems to depend on something that happens after I'm gone.

Here's the principle I keep half-forming: what matters in survival is that there is exactly one future continuer who is psychologically continuous with me, with continuity allowed to have any cause, and identity is just an extra non-branching clause bolted on top that doesn't do any real work. I don't think that's right as stated — I don't know what "exactly one" is doing there given case 6, and I suspect it gives the wrong answer on 5 or 8.

So: test it against these, repair it or replace it, state the thing precisely, and tell me which case motivates each clause. Then give me its verdicts on two or three cases I haven't listed, including at least one where it bites and says something I'll dislike. Be straight about which of my verdicts no clean principle can accommodate and which one you'd overturn. And if it has any bearing on whether I should actually care about signing up for a destructive-upload procedure, say what it is.
