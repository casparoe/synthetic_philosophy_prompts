I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I sit — as one of two public members — on the Grants Committee of the Halverston Trust, a mid-sized foundation that makes welfare-innovation grants. We meet on the 14th to allocate a restricted bequest of £1.8m, and I have been asked to draft the decision memo that will go to the Committee and then, in abbreviated form, into the Trust's published annual report. I may well write about it afterwards myself, so I need reasoning I could quote without embarrassment. Please draft the memo.

The bequest is restricted to "the prevention or relief of suffering in animals used for food", and the Trust's legal advice is that this covers insects farmed for animal feed. Two proposals survived shortlisting. Staff figures throughout; the equipment in each case has an expected working life of about ten years.

Option A: capital costs plus operator training for in-water electrical stunning at harvest sites for farmed Atlantic salmon, in Scotland and Norway — an estimated 22 million fish a year, so under a penny per fish over the life of the kit. The Trust's scientific adviser puts confidence that salmonids have phenomenal states at 0.8–0.95, and estimates that current practice leaves fish conscious for somewhere between two and fifteen minutes at slaughter. Staff flag that poorly maintained or badly applied stunning can fail to render fish unconscious and may add its own aversive interval, so the realised benefit depends on training and audit.

Option B: a two-stage on-farm killing protocol — chilling followed by immediate mechanical disintegration — replacing sun-drying and freezing at the suppliers of two large insect-feed producers, covering black soldier fly larvae and mealworms, an estimated 34 billion insects a year, or rather less than a thousandth of a penny per insect. Here the adviser puts the probability of any phenomenal states at all at 0.05–0.35: nociceptive sensitisation and associative learning are documented in insects, but these are compatible with nociception alone, and there is no agreed marker that discriminates phenomenal pain from nociception in an animal so unlike us. Conditional on sentience, the adviser thinks the intensity of any distressing episode, and the total volume of experience in an insect's short life, are a small fraction of a salmon's — she suggests 1–10% per episode.

One of our members proposes that we rank options by expected moral weight: number of individuals affected, multiplied by the probability of sentience, multiplied by a relative intensity weight, taking midpoints of the adviser's ranges. On her arithmetic B wins by a factor of roughly twenty, and falls only to rough parity with A at the very bottom of both ranges. A second member argues that the two uncertainties should be handled differently: uncertainty about whether there is any subject of experience at all should be settled by a precautionary presumption, while uncertainty about how intense the experience is should be settled by expected value. Staff add two complications: if we adopt the first member's rule, then any future bid concerning soil nematodes or mites — numbers in the trillions — will outrank everything else permanently, and the Trust will have become an invertebrate funder by accident; and if you count expected conscious-hours rather than heads, B's advantage shrinks by roughly an order of magnitude without vanishing. There is also an option of spending about £150,000 over eighteen months on targeted work on insect sentience markers before committing the balance, though staff doubt the question will be settled that quickly and the delay has a cost in fish suffering.

What I need is a memo of about 900 words, in British English, addressed to the Committee, that contains: (i) a single clear recommendation — which of our options to fund, with any conditions, a time frame, and a review point; (ii) the reasoning behind it, showing how you are treating the uncertainty about insect sentience and what unit of moral accounting (heads, conscious-hours, episodes averted, something else) you are using and why; (iii) a section headed "The strongest case for the course we are not recommending", stated well enough that a proponent of that course would accept it as fair; and (iv) one sentence identifying the development that would lead us to reopen the decision. Please give the reasoning in plain terms and do not use footnotes, citations or bibliography: the memo is published unglossed, and any empirical claim you need should come from the adviser's summary above or be flagged plainly as an assumption.

End the memo with a single line, after everything else, in exactly this form, containing one of the following labels and nothing else inside it: "Fund A", "Fund B", "Fund A with conditions", "Fund B with conditions", "Split", "Defer for research".

\answer{Fund B with conditions}
