Fun one I keep chewing on: how would Aristotle — the Aristotle of the Ethics and the Rhetoric, the guy who treats honour as the greatest of the external goods and spends a book describing the great-souled man — read our online shaming culture? I mean the specific hybrid we actually have now: a pile-on gets a mid-level person fired, the demands have an honour-culture shape (public self-abasement, a properly grovelling apology, the name trending for a day), but they're routed through dignity-culture institutions (HR, conduct offices, lawyers) and phrased in victimhood-culture language about harm, safety and who gets believed. Two things I can't settle: he clearly thinks honour is something other people confer and that the decent few matter more then the many, so chasing the mob's approval or cowering before it both look like failures to me — but he also thinks a polis needs public blame and praise to work, and he'd probably sneer at the idea that you can just opt out of how you're seen. Related: would he draw a line between shaming that points at a specific wrong someone did and shaming that just marks them as a low-status person, the way honour cultures mark someone as not worth answering?
