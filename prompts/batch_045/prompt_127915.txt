I convene a fortnightly reading group for final-year undergraduates and a few interested postgraduates on animal minds, and I am putting together a two-page handout on which animals are conscious, and to what degree. The version I half-wrote and then abandoned simply paired the familiar theories of consciousness with the taxa each of them lets in, and on reflection that seems to me to get the cart before the horse: the disagreements that actually animate the room are less about which mechanism does the work than about what sort of thing we take the answer to be at all. Some of us want a yes-or-no verdict per species; some want a scalar quantity comparable across species; and a couple of sessions ago someone raised a third thing I have not been able to shake, namely that the answer may also need to be a count of subjects — how many conscious subjects are present — and that this can come apart from the other two. An octopus with eight neurally semi-autonomous arms, a colony of social insects, a hive, two people doing something jointly, a brain whose hemispheres have been surgically disconnected, a creature with a few hundred neurons: in each case it is unclear whether we are looking at one mind, several, a fraction of one, or none, and nothing in the usual framing of the distribution question obliges us to say.

So I would like a decision tree, not a list. It should begin from a commitment a student could hold before opening any book — say, from the observation that the creatures in question cannot report their experiences — and end at leaves that are positions about the distribution of consciousness, named where a name is current and marked "unnamed" where not. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of canonical names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located in that space. The branch nodes should be the claims that do the separating, and I have views about which ones matter: (i) whether behavioural evidence is constitutive of consciousness or merely evidence of it, and what attitude one takes to the fact that the creatures cannot tell us anything; (ii) whether what matters is functional organisation or membership of a lineage — whether an ancestor's having done something with its nervous system is relevant in itself, or only as a proxy for organisation; (iii) whether consciousness, where present, is all-or-nothing or comes in degrees, and if it comes in degrees whether degrees can be summed across individuals; (iv) the granularity axis, whether the unit that can be conscious must be a whole organism or whether subsystems and super-systems are eligible candidates; and (v) whether creatures of our own species whose cognitive capacities are severely impaired are to be handled by the same criterion, or are protected by a separate principle. Six to eight branch nodes and ten to fourteen leaves would be about right.

Two further requirements and one thing to avoid. Every diagnostic question must be phrased as a case a student can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to — ideally a creature described in a sentence or two of neutral, empirically uncontentious terms; I would rather you invented creatures than leaned on particular experimental findings, since the group will not have read them and I don't want the handout to turn on who has read which paper. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so, and say briefly why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves I would want to distinguish in discussion come out indistinguishable on your tree — theories differing in mechanism but generating the same pattern of verdicts, for instance — flag the collision rather than burying it. What to avoid is the failure mode of my abandoned draft: branches that are just named theories of consciousness. Alongside the tree I want one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment, and a short closing note on how someone landing on each leaf should think about the aggregation problem in cost-effectiveness estimates for animal welfare interventions. I'll add the references and the statutory examples myself; what I need from you is the structure.
