I've been reading this corner of the internet since the sequences were still being posted, and I've finally managed to get an argument into a form where I can't locate the mistake even though I'm certain there is one. Notation: let *value* be whatever it is you are supposed to be maximizing the expectation of, and let the canonical mugger be the stranger who says that unless you hand over five dollars he will use his powers over the causal structure of the world to bring about some astronomically large amount of disvalue, where the number is written in a few characters of up-arrow notation. I am going to grant, for the sake of the argument, that you pay. My difficulty is that the argument seems to generalize until it has eaten every decision I make, and not just the ones involving strangers.

(1) *No zero priors.* For any hypothesis that is not logically impossible, my credence in it is greater than zero. I have never seen a principled place to draw the line, and assigning zero appears to make unlearning impossible.

(2) *Unbounded stakes.* There is no finite upper bound on the value a hypothesis can attach to an action: for any finite V, "my doing A brings about V" is a contingent, well-formed hypothesis.

(3) *Cheap specification.* The size of V is not a cost to stating it. A handful of symbols buys a number larger than anything that appears in physics, so any complexity-based penalty on priors penalizes the *claim* roughly equally at every magnitude. Nothing in my prior, as far as I can tell, makes the probability of "A yields V" decay as V grows.

(4) *Fanaticism.* For any probability p greater than zero and any finite cost c, there is a finite V with pV > c.

(5) *One term bounds the sum.* The expected value of A is a probability-weighted sum over hypotheses, so it is at least as great as the contribution of any one of them: if some hypothesis H gives A the payoff V with probability p, then EV(A) is at least pV minus whatever the rest of my situation contributes, and the rest of my situation is small and finite.

(6) *Universality.* By (1) and (2), for every action available to me and every V, there is a nonzero-probability hypothesis on which that action yields V. By (3) that probability does not shrink as V grows. By (4) the product pV can therefore be pushed past any bound. By (5) that product bounds EV(A) from below. So EV(A) is larger than any finite number, for every A.

(7) So every option open to me has infinite expected value; and running the mirror of (2) for disvalue, every option also has infinitely negative expected value, i.e. the sums are not well-defined at all. Either way expected-value maximization has no verdict: it ranks everything first, or it ranks nothing. It cannot tell me to take the bus rather than walk.

(8) Both halves of that are unacceptable. Expected value plainly does discriminate in ordinary cases — it is the only thing I have ever used to decide anything. And if the reasoning were sound, Pascal's mugging would not be a puzzle of its own; it would be one more instance of a collapse that should have shown up in every mundane choice I made before any stranger ever spoke to me. An argument that explains the mugger by showing that all decision-making is void has proved too much.

Two constraints on any answer. First: I am loath to give up (1) or (4). Denying (1) looks like a license for dogmatism, and denying (4) looks like it makes me indifferent to existential risk, which is the main thing I wanted expected value for. I am also attached to the datum that the mugger case really does feel like a special problem rather than a general one. So I would rather hear what (2), (3), (5), (6) and (7) each cost, and in particular what the move from (5) to (6) is doing.

Second: I do not think the diagnosis is finished until it handles a variant I will call the escalator. Here the stranger does not name a number once. If you refuse, he names a larger one, and he keeps going, and he can see whether you paid at each step. In the escalator, no single step looks different from the canonical case, yet the sequence as a whole is exactly what you would expect from someone with no powers at all, and I cannot tell whether that is because my credence should be collapsing as the sequence lengthens, or because the escalator has smuggled in something the static case did not have. If your fix works only for the static case and dissolves under the escalator, I would like to know that; and if it works only by making me immune to all large-payoff reasoning, I would like to know that too.

So: name the step you reject, explain why it looked sound enough to get into my argument in the first place, and price the alternatives.
