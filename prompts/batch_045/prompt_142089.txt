I write literary fiction, and I have become entangled in a dispute about the vocabulary my craft is made of. I would like it audited rather than surveyed — and, at the end, I want a verdict on whether the conclusion drawn from it survives.

The dispute is this. A friend of mine, who works in the philosophy of mind, maintains that our everyday talk of beliefs and desires is a proto-scientific theory of exactly the sort that science periodically retires: she likens it to the theory of the humors, or to phlogiston, or to caloric fluid. On her telling, the mapping is supposed to be this: there are posits (beliefs, desires, fears, expectations) introduced to explain the messy behaviour of bodies; there are observable effects (what people say and do); the posits earn their keep to the extent that they predict and manage those effects; they have been found to be empirically empty; a mature neuroscience will replace them; and, once it does, our novels will seem as quaint as a humoral diagnosis of melancholy — which is a way of saying both that there is nothing in the head that the sentence about a character who believes the letter is in the drawer is true of, and that the practice built on such sentences is living on borrowed time.

What makes me resist this, and what I have never seen addressed, is that in my working life the vocabulary is almost never used in the way the analogy requires. When I write a scene, I do not infer what my characters believe from what they do, the way a physician inferred an excess of black bile from a patient's behaviour; I stipulate what they believe, and their behaviour is then answerable to what I have stipulated rather than the other way round. The predictive direction of the supposed theory is reversed. And when a reader meets the scene, she does something that is not prediction either: she attributes mental states to the characters, and at a second remove to me, in order to get the text to mean something at all. So my suspicion is that the analogy quietly imports a genre — posits introduced for prediction, testable by prediction, retired when prediction fails — and that both halves of my practice, the making and the reading, belong to a different genre: stipulation and interpretation, not forecasting. A defender of my friend will reply that this simply shows fiction is a pretend case and proves nothing about the real one; I want to know whether the pretend case is a degenerate instance of the practice or its purest instance, because the answer seems to me to cut both ways.

Here is what I would like from you.

First, lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — saying what plays the role of the posits, the observable symptoms, the theory's predictive failures, the successor science, and the quaintness, in the source domain and in the target.

Second, and most importantly, sort the disanalogies by whether they do any work. Some will be noise. I have in mind candidates like these, and I do not know which of them matter: whether mindreading is a theory at all or rather a skill or a practice or a capacity to simulate; whether beliefs and desires are the kind of item that can be discovered not to exist, given that they are individuated by their contents and by their rational relations to one another rather than by a causal job description; whether a successor vocabulary has ever been shown to do the normative work that the mentalistic vocabulary does — promising, blaming, holding responsible, meaning what one says — as opposed to the causal-explanatory work; whether the eliminativist's own thesis can be stated without helping itself to the vocabulary it proposes to retire, and whether that is a fatal problem or merely a cost of transition; and whether a vocabulary can be retained as useful while being denied to refer, the way one might go on using a map's contours after deciding that contour lines are not among the furniture of the world.

Third, tell me what the source domain smuggles in that the target lacks — assumptions about how the posits were introduced, how they were individuated, what counts as evidence against them, and whether retirement is decided on predictive track record alone.

Fourth: give me the verdict. Does the conclusion that my friend drew — that nothing answers to the ascription, and that the novels are therefore quaint — survive once the disanalogies are in place? Please distinguish three claims that I think may be running together in her argument: that the posits do not exist; that the vocabulary ought to be withdrawn from serious description; and that the literary practice built on it is therefore on borrowed time. It seems possible to me that the first is true and the third false, and I want to know where exactly the weight falls.

Finally, if the analogy fails, is there a better one — colour talk against wavelengths, the centre of gravity of a body, the money in an account, the queen in chess, a frontier on a map — and which of them preserves what my friend wants while giving me back the practice?

Two constraints. I do not want names of books, papers, or authors, and I do not want a reading list or a survey of positions with citations: I want the argument, made out in the structure of the view, so that someone who has never opened the literature could follow it and check it. And please do not hedge your way out of the verdict at the end; if the honest answer is that the analogy carries the conclusion or that it does not, say so.
