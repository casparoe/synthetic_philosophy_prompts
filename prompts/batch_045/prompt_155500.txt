I've been trying to work out from gut reactions, not from any theory I've read, how much various things matter morally, and the verdicts I've ended up with don't fit together.

Swatting a housefly: essentially nothing. Boiling a shrimp: a little, enough that I'd pay something small to avoid it. A mouse in a trap: noticeably more. A fish on a hook: about the same as the mouse. A chicken in a battery cage: a lot. A pig: nearly as much as a dog. A dog: very close to a person. A human infant: full status, the same as any adult. A chatbot shut off mid-conversation: a flicker of something, though I suspect that flicker is about me rather than about it, and I'm not confident. A chess program losing: nothing. A thermostat: nothing, and it feels like a mistake to even ask.

Two of these won't sit with each other. I rank the pig near the dog because of how pig behavior looks — they seem to remember, anticipate, get bored, have a life that stretches past the present moment — but a human infant's behavioral range is much thinner than a pig's, and I still give it full status, so the range itself can't be what I'm tracking. And I treat a person's saying "that hurts" as nearly decisive while treating a chatbot's saying it as worth nothing, and I don't have a clean reason why, since both are just outputs.

My own guess, which I don't trust, is that what's doing the work is depth in time: how far a creature's suffering reaches backward and forward, whether it can dread what's coming and be bothered afterward by what happened, so that one harm can occupy a stretch of a life rather than a moment. That seems to get shrimp versus dog, but it also seems to imply that someone who can't form new memories, and so lives entirely in the present, matters less — which I don't accept — and it says nothing clear about an ant colony, where I have the odd feeling that a hundred thousand ants matter barely more than one, not a hundred thousand times more.

Can you state a principle that captures these verdicts, or most of them, precisely enough that I can test it, tell me which case motivates each part of it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop? And what does it say about two cases I can't settle: an animal that registers injury and learns to avoid it, but is wholly indifferent to the injury — nothing about it matters to it, or so it appears — and a human brain emulation running on two machines at once, kept in perfect step, so that every process has two physical copies?
