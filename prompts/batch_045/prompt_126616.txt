I am a high school senior who got pulled into an argument in English class and ended up defending a version of actual intentionalism: the meaning of a text is what its author meant by it. I defend it in a way I have not found in anything I have been able to read, and I want to know whether the move actually strengthens the view or just protects it from ever being wrong.

The move is to split authorial intention into two kinds. Content-intentions are what the author is trying to say, pick out, or get across in writing the words. Uptake-intentions are what the author wants the reader to end up believing, feeling, or doing in response. My claim is only about content-intentions. That lets me absorb the hard cases: when a class finds a pattern in a poem the poet insists was never there, or when an author's announced purpose for a novel plainly is not what the novel does, I say those are failures of uptake, not changes in meaning.

What I want is the map of what this view risks. Which cases, arguments, or discoveries should count against it — a text whose author's content-intentions were never settled, or changed mid-drafting, or were self-contradictory; texts written by several people at once; a text whose meaning seems to shift when we learn who wrote it, or that it was ghostwritten, or partly generated; the fact that we often understand a sentence better than its author did? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a defender of my version should concede as genuinely counting against it rather than dismiss. Say what my view predicts that its rivals do not. And be honest about whether the content/uptake split has insulated the position into saying nothing testable, and if it has, whether that is a fair complaint or one every theory of meaning is exposed to. I should add that I am more attached to the underlying idea than to my fix for it: if the fix is what fails, say so.
