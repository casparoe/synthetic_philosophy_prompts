I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the old OB days and lurking on the forums for years, and at my local meetup last night (pub near Russell Square) two people got into an argument about Thomson's lamp that I could not referee. I've typed up there exchange below as best I remember it — please go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what, and please write the answer in British English.

The setup they both agreed on: a lamp, off at t = 0, with a switch that gets flipped at t = 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, … minutes (i.e. at 1 − 1/2^n). Question: at exactly one minute, is it on or off?

1. Misha: It has to be one or the other, and both are ruled out. It can't be on, because every switching-on is followed by a later switching-off; it can't be off, because every switching-off is followed by a later switching-on. So the description of the procedure is unsatisfiable, which means a supertask — infinitely many operations in a finite time — can't be performed. And in the real world it's worse: any actual lamp has a minimum switching time, filament warm-up, finite switch travel and so on.

2. Dougie: The story only specifies the lamp's state at times strictly before one minute; it's silent about one minute itself. "Off at every earlier time" doesn't entail "off at one minute" — incompleteness isn't contradiction. So the lamp argument at most shows that the story doesn't determine the answer, not that supertasks are impossible. Separately, the engineering complaints are irrelevant: Thomson wants a conceptual argument, and "your switch is too slow" doesn't answer it.

3. Misha: Fine, the bare story is silent. But add the one premise anyone accepts about lamps: a lamp changes state only when it's switched, so its state at a time is whatever the last switch before that time left it as. Add that and the contradiction comes back, because there is no last switch — the sequence of flips has no final member, so there's nothing for the state at one minute to be. The incoherence isn't in the bare story, it's in the story plus the only account of lamphood we actually have.

4. Dougie: That premise is precisely what's up for debate. "State at t = state after the last change before t" is true of finite sequences of changes and quietly assumes there always is a last change — that's finitism dressed up as a fact about lamps, and Thomson doesn't get to help himself to it. Nor is "then the state is a brute fact" a contradiction; it's just indeterminacy. Compare a series that doesn't converge: you don't say its sum is contradictory, you say it has no sum.

5. Misha: Here's a sharper version, then. Two lamps side by side in a perfectly symmetric room, A starting off and B starting on, both flipped at the same instants. Before the minute they are always in opposite states. But the two setups are isomorphic — swap the labels "on" and "off" and A's history is exactly B's — so any rule that reads a final state off the history has to assign them the same final state, while being opposite at every earlier time pushes towards their still being opposite at the minute. So there is no coherent completion rule: limits, majorities, time-averages and parity all either break the symmetry or break something else. That's not mere silence. That's the story refusing to have an ending at all, which is Thomson's conclusion in a stronger form.

6. Dougie: The symmetry argument is on my side — it shows that no rule on the pre-minute history determines the state, and nobody promised determinism. And supertasks as such do have determinate endings when the effects shrink: take a dimmer lamp whose nth operation nudges the brightness by plus or minus 1/2^n, starting from off and alternating up and down. That one ends up determinately at one-third brightness after infinitely many operations. So the impossibility can't be about doing infinitely many things in finite time as such; it's about this particular task, whose history has no limit. Thomson's conclusion overreaches.

7. Misha: That's not the same kind of task. A nudge of size 1/2^n is a vanishing task; infinitely many of them summing to something finite is just a convergent series, and nobody thinks convergent series are impossible. The interesting case is infinitely many full-sized operations — a switch travelling its whole distance, a lamp going fully off to fully on — and for those you need unbounded speed in a bounded time, which our physics doesn't allow. Your reading has a cost too: if the state at the minute is unconstrained by anything, then a completed supertask gives you an uncaused event, which is worse than just admitting supertasks are impossible.

8. Dougie: "Uncaused" is too strong — the state would be caused by the whole infinite sequence, not by nothing, and a cause doesn't need a last member. And if your conclusion is really the metaphysical thesis that no event can have infinitely many causes, then say that: it's a big claim with its own costs, not something the lamp forces on us. Whereas if your conclusion is just "real switches are too slow", we've learned nothing about what's possible in principle and the lamp stops being a conceptual argument. (Misha also said around here that it matters because if supertasks are impossible then aggregating infinitely many tiny effects — infinite-utility, Pascal-ish setups — is incoherent too; Dougie said those are completely seperate questions and the lamp settles neither.)

I'm honestly torn and didn't want to pick a side at the table. Please rate each of the eight moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each briefly, say what if anything both of them are missing — I have a feeling they're arguing past each other about something — and tell me who's more right overall.
