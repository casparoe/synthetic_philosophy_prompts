ok so this has suddenly become my actual problem rather than a hobby argument. I do street outreach on weekend nights for a small harm-reduction org, and the city council here is finally looking at reforming how prostitution is policed, so our board is drafting a position paper. The coalition is split between people who want the Nordic model (decriminalise selling, keep a prohibition on buying) and people who want the New Zealand approach (scrap the sex-work-specific offences and let ordinary labour law handle it), and I got asked to write the "what we all agree on" section. Going through it I realised my own views are a grab bag from both camps and I genuinely can't tell whether they're a package or just a pile. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(a) nobody should be criminalised for selling sex — the seller is not the offender; (b) buying sex isn't rape and payment doesn't nullify consent, I've never been moved by "consent can't be bought", a paid encounter can be perfectly genuinely consensual; (c) but the Nordic/asymmetric model is what I think we should push here, because it takes the penalty off sellers while shrinking the market that traffickers supply; (d) I'm suspicious of the German/Netherlands legalization setup — big licensed brothels seem to have grown the market and to have helped mostly the workers who were already documented and organised; (e) I like decriminalization in the New Zealand sense, remove the special offences entirely and let employment, health-and-safety and anti-discrimination law cover it like any other job; (f) most third-party involvement should stay criminal — managers, brothel keepers, anyone taking a cut of someone else's work — since that's where the coercion actually lives; (g) most people selling sex would rather not be doing it and would leave if they had real alternatives, and that fact should carry real political weight even though (b) is true; (h) I want registration or licensing to be the route to actual protections, so inspectors can get into premises and workers have something concrete to point at; (i) I don't want anyone to have to disclose their status to an employer, landlord or official as the price of being protected.

I also keep using an argument in meetings that I've sort of invented and should either defend properly or drop. I call it the compliance floor: any regime that makes protection conditional on being registered, licensed or otherwise documented imposes fixed costs — ID, a fixed address, a bank account, a tax history, turning up at an office — and those costs fall hardest exactly on the workers with the least documentation, so the official route to protection systematically screens out the people who need it most. I think that's what's really driving my unease in (d), but I'm not sure it leaves (h) standing, and I don't know whether it tells against the Nordic model too (surely any enforcement regime also has a compliance floor, in the sense that the workers it can actually reach are the visible, documented ones?).
