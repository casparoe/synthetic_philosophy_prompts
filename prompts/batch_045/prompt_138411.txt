I am revising the chapter on the fact–value distinction in a handbook of argument analysis for policy professionals, and I want the statement of the is–ought gap to be one a reader could actually apply. The version I have been working with is syntactic: classify every atomic claim in an argument as descriptive or normative, and treat any inference step as illegitimate if none of its premises is normative while its conclusion is. Three difficulties have made me doubt this formulation, and I would like to know which of the diagnoses I can see is the right one, or whether the formulation should be abandoned.

First, the apparent counterexamples. A duty engineer wants the substation not to flood, and pumping the sump is the only means available to her of preventing it; so she ought to pump the sump. No premise contains "ought," "good," "bad," "reason," or "should," and yet the reasoning strikes me as impeccable. Either Hume's law is false, or instrumental reasoning is a recognised exception to it, and I cannot tell which — though the difference clearly matters for what I tell my readers.

Second, the cases where the norm seems already to be inside the descriptive-looking premise. N promised the Board she would keep the records dry; so N ought to keep the records dry. If promising is the sort of thing that binds, the "ought" is packed into the first premise rather than produced by the step, and then whether my rule flags an argument depends only on where the norm is parked, which cannot be a real difference. A student of mine has also pointed out that from "the gauge reads four metres" one may validly infer "the gauge reads four metres or the Board ought to resign," which my rule flags as well, and which surely cannot be what anyone meant to prohibit.

Third, if I retreat to the safer claim that a normative conclusion requires a normative premise somewhere, the thesis begins to look like mere bookkeeping: any argument can be made to satisfy it simply by making the normative premise explicit, and nothing follows about whether the argument is any good. Yet the gap is standardly treated as a deep thesis about the autonomy of ethics, which suggests that in retreating I have lost whatever content it had.

So, as I see it, I have three options: (a) the law, stated syntactically, has counterexamples and should be given up; (b) it survives only because "descriptive" and "normative" are defined so that any premise from which an "ought" follows counts as normative, which makes it circular and unfalsifiable; or (c) what I have been calling the is–ought gap is really only the naturalistic fallacy — the prohibition on analysing "good" as some natural property — and has nothing to say about inference at all.

Which of these is correct, and is there a fourth option I am missing? If the answer is (b), what exactly is the non-trivial claim, what sort of argument would violate it that I would otherwise have been willing to accept, and what would count as successfully bridging the gap rather than merely relabelling a premise as normative? Finally: is the naturalistic fallacy the same thesis as the is–ought gap, a consequence of it, or a separate doctrine that I have been conflating with it?
