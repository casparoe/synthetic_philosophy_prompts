Here's where I've ended up on aesthetic testimony. Call it the calibration view (I made the name up, so don't go looking for it). The pessimists in this area say something like: you can't come to know that a work is beautiful, or moving, or clumsy, just on someones say-so — aesthetic judgement has to be grounded in your own first-hand experience of the thing. The optimists say testimony works here more or less like it does anywhere else. I think both are slightly off, and heres my version:

(i) A piece of aesthetic testimony gives the hearer knowledge only if the hearer has already calibrated that testifier in that narrow domain — that is, has enough first-hand experience of other works this same critic also judged to have learned the mapping from her verdicts to what she's going to experience herself.
(ii) Calibration itself requires first-hand acquaintance, and it doesn't transfer much across domains or across critics. So acquaintance is ineliminable, but not in the way the acquaintance-principle crowd thinks: it's not that every aesthetic judgement needs its own bit of experience behind it, it's that the chain of trust does. On this view a trusted critic is a labour-saving device for people who already know their way around, not a route in for a novice. Novices who take the tip get, at best, true belief plus a good pointer about where to look.

I like it because it explains why deference to critics feels fine among people who share a lot of history and creepy among people who don't, and why "trust me, it's great" from a stranger is worthless while the same sentence from a friend whose taste you've triangulated over a decade is not.

Take a work nobody can any longer experience — a mural destroyed in a fire, a film with no surviving prints, a building that was demolished — where the only route to a verdict about it is testimony and descriptions. We do seem to know things there, and the view says we don't. Is that a real defeater or does it just show the view has a cost I should swallow?
- Similarly: professionals who act on other people's aesthetic judgements and get it right — festival programmers buying on a scout's report, editors, casting directors, sound mixers working to a producer's description. Does the view have to say they're flying blind, and if so is that unacceptable or fine?
- There's an inverted-critic case I keep circling: someone whose actual responses are systematically offset from mine (she loves everything I find cloying) but reliably offset, so that once I learn the transformation I can use her perfectly. If my view can absorb that by allowing "offset calibration," doesn't it stop being a view about the aesthetic and collapse into a general theory of testimony plus a claim that aesthetic domains are unusually hard to calibrate? - And a case the view probably wasn't built for: a recommender system with no experiences whatsoever that predicts your aesthetic responses better than any human critic whose acquaintance you've ever calibrated against. Does the black-box case show the view is really about reliability-tracking, or does it show the case isn't aesthetic testimony at all?
