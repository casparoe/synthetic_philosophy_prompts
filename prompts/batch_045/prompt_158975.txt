I help run a tiny informal bouldering group — no club, no insurance, just a group chat and a poll on WhatsApp — and at the start of each season we get everyone to sign one form that basically says "I'm happy with whatever the session leader decides on the day". A mate of mine says that isn't consent at all, it's just a piece of paper, and we've been going round in circles about it for weeks. Around the same time I had a minor medical thing where I signed a form after about a five-minute chat, and I walked out feeling like I'd agreed to something I didn't fully get, but also that refusing would have been a bit silly. So I've ended up trying to work out what a proper account of when consent is actually *valid* would even have to do, and I'd like the spec before I go any further.

So: write me the desiderata for a theory of valid consent. What does an account have to deliver to count as any good — which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse, which plain facts about how people actually decide it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement? I care about all three of the usual suspects (deception, coercion, competence) but I don't want the answer to just be those three headings with a paragraph each. For every requirement I want a verdict on whether it's non-negotiable or just a thing one camp happens to like; I have a hunch that "consent has to be fully informed about every relevant detail" is a prejudice rather than a genuine requirement, but tell me if I've got that backwards. Please also flag any pair of requirements that look like they can't both be satisfied at once — that's the bit I'm most interested in, because if the list is internally unsatisfiable then that itself is the finding — and say which single requirement does the most work knocking candidate theories out.

Here are the things that actually got me stuck, and I want the list to handle them rather than dodge them. (1) Blanket or up-front consent to an open-ended future act — my season form, and it feels different in kind from agreeing to a specific thing. (2) Something I've started calling *drift*: what you agreed to and what actually happens come apart over twenty small steps, none of which you'd have objected to on its own, but the end state is one you'd have said no to if it had been described to you at the start. Is drift just deception in slow motion, or is it a problem of its own? I really can't tell. (3) Borrowed consent — going ahead on the say-so of someone else who tells you "she's fine with it". (4) Consent given after the cost of saying no has partly been sunk, e.g. you're halfway through something painful or expensive and you just nod along. (5) Consent from someone who is clearly competent but who you're fairly sure will regret it, where the regret on its own seems like evidence of nothing. And (6) something about what consent has to look like from the outside: as the person organising the group I need to be able to tell, in advance and without reading minds, whether I've got it. Tell me honestly if that last one is a real desideratum or whether I'm smuggling in a legal or evidential worry that doesn't belong on a moral list.

Assume the theory is meant to be good enough for two jobs at once: working out what I owe people in ordinary life, and deciding when a signed form should change what's permitted — and if those two jobs pull the requirements in different directions, say exactly where. A quick score of the one or two big general pictures of consent against the list is fine at the end if it's short, but the criteria are what I'm after. Write it in British English, and end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" naming the single requirement you'd bet on as doing the most real work.
