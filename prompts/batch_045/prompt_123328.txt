I'm a PhD student in American literature with a secondary field in intellectual history, writing a seminar paper on Harriet Beecher Stowe's *Uncle Tom's Cabin* as a piece of moral technology: a text designed to do something, not merely to say something. I'd like a reading of the novel through the philosophical literature on moral revolutions — specifically, the skeptical position associated with Bernard Williams, on which practices like Atlantic slavery did not end because anyone was argued out of them (the arguments were largely in place well before the practice was), and what did the work was change in economic and social structure; and, against it, the honor-based account associated with Kwame Appiah, on which the decisive variable is neither belief nor interest but what a relevant community can afford to be *known* doing, so that a revolution consists in a once-honorable practice becoming dishonorable.

I want a reading, not a survey of the debate. Three things in particular.

First: which causal story does the novel itself imply? I don't mean whether the novel caused anything. I mean whether the picture it gives of what sustains slavery — the motives it assigns to Haley, to the Shelbys, to St. Clare, to Legree, to Ophelia, the reasons its characters actually give for what they do — is an interest story or an honor story, and whether Stowe's own confidence that a book could matter is evidence for something neither of them has.

Second, I think the argument / interest / honor trichotomy is too coarse, and I want you to press on the distinction I'm using. Distinguish conversion (getting an agent to accept a proposition she previously rejected) from re-coordination (changing what it costs her to be *seen* acting as she did) from a third thing I'll call availability: changing which features of a practice are perceptible as normative considerations at all, before anyone accepts or rejects anything. My suspicion is that the novel's real strategy — what it stages, what it puts in front of the reader's attention in scenes like the opening sale, Eliza's crossing, Tom's death, George Harris's defiance, the ending's emigration scheme — is best described as availability, and that availability is not reducible to either Williams's mechanism or Appiah's. Argue for or against that; I'd rather be talked out of it now than in front of my committee.

Third, the hard case: the honor-centered reading has to explain the afterlife. The name of Stowe's hero is now a term of contempt for exactly the patient, nonresistant endurance the novel presents as his greatness — the revolution turned and disgraced the conduct its canonical text ennobled. Does an honor-based account predict that overshoot, or does it show that honor only ever borrows its content from something else?

Please be committed rather than even-handed: take a definite position on whether the novel vindicates Williams, Appiah, or neither, name the two or three scenes that carry the weight for you, then name the scene or device that most resists your reading and say what you'd have to find in the text to give it up. I don't need citations or a bibliography — I need a reading that lets the novel push back on the philosophy, rather than decorating a position I already hold.
