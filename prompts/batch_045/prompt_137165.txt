I run a cross-listed module on algorithmic decision-making for about twenty people — graduate students and advanced undergraduates from computer science, public policy, and philosophy in the same room. I have one ninety-minute session, tables of four or five, and no laptops and no coding: everything has to work with index cards, a printed worksheet, and arithmetic that people can do on the back of an envelope. Participants have seen a confusion matrix but no formal fairness criteria, and I do not want them lectured at first. I would like you to design a card game in which they run into an impossibility result in algorithmic fairness before anyone names it.

The fiction, which I would like you to keep but are free to sharpen. A public employment agency serves two wards, Harbor and Mill. A private vendor supplies a risk engine that assigns each claimant a score in {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8} for the chance of remaining unemployed past six months. The score is, by construction, as good as a score can be: it is exactly calibrated within each ward. The agency does not build the score; it decides what to do with it. There are fourteen retraining stipends for forty claimants. Anyone flagged also gets a mandatory weekly reporting requirement, which people experience as a burden, so a false positive is not merely waste.

The deck, which I have fixed and would like you to check. Each team gets an identical forty-card deck: twenty Harbor cards, twenty Mill cards. Harbor: five cards at each score value, with respectively 1, 2, 3, and 4 of them marked on the reverse as long-term unemployed. Mill: ten cards at 0.2 with 2 marked, five at 0.4 with 2 marked, five at 0.6 with 3 marked, and no cards at 0.8. Base rates are therefore ten in twenty in Harbor and seven in twenty in Mill, and Mill has nobody at the top score. Each card carries a case number and a first name. Please verify these figures, and tell me if any of them accidentally makes a combination I describe below as blocked actually achievable, or vice versa; correct the deck if so.

The compliance rules, stated in the agency's own bureaucratic language rather than in the vocabulary of the literature: the score must mean the same thing in both wards, so that among files scored 0.6 the share turning out to be long-term unemployed is the same in Harbor as in Mill; the miss rates must match, so that the share of people who would have found work but were flagged is the same in both wards, and likewise the share of people who would not have found work but were missed; and within a ward, nobody with a higher score may be left unflagged while someone with a lower score is flagged. The agency also has a mission: get as many of the fourteen stipends as possible to people who would otherwise have been long-term unemployed. Please give me a payoff scheme in points that sets the mission against the compliance rules without real money, and that is robust to degenerate solutions — I have already noticed that flagging nobody equalizes every error rate at once, and I do not want to have to rule that out by fiat; the incentives should make it unattractive on their own.

Three beats I want staged, in something like this order, with you deciding the clock and telling me what to cut if they will not all fit. First, teams set a policy, commit, turn the cards over, and fill in the audit worksheet themselves; they should discover that no choice of cutoff satisfies everything at once. Second, the constraint that blocks them is relaxed in character — a legal note saying that treating identical files differently is not prohibited, so a team may flag a stated fraction of a score cell — and they should find that this buys them the error-rate match while quietly destroying the meaning of the score. Third, something makes visible that the quantities in the rules are not all jointly observable: the outcome is recorded only for claimants the agency did not serve, or only for those it did, and what a team gets to learn depends on what it decided. I would like the third beat arranged so that participants' own choices decide which part of the audit they can no longer run.

What I need from you: a materials and preparation list including the print specification for the cards; the rules as participants receive them, ideally as a one-page memo in the agency's voice; a timeline with minute marks; the payoff scheme; a short script of the only things the facilitator is allowed to say during play; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the idea, introducing the standard names for the criteria only at that point, and distinguishing carefully which requirement each maneuver broke. Include at least three failure modes beyond the two I have already flagged — I am thinking of the objection that the base-rate gap is itself a product of past allocation decisions rather than a fixed input, the temptation to declare small numerical gaps close enough, and groups finishing at widely different speeds — with how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the impossibility result the game cannot reproduce and which part you or I will simply have to state: I am thinking of its generality beyond two groups and four score values, and the way a deployed rule reshapes the population it scores.
