Over the past eighteen months I've been a patient rather than the person who teaches this material, and it has made me care about a question I used to wave away: when someone says "the probability is p," what is that number a number of? My fertility clinic quotes me 22% per cycle, a friend in quantum optics tells me I'm asking a pseudo-question, and I keep finding that my verdicts on individual cases are far more confident than anything I can say in general. So, please: no survey of the -isms and no reading list — I want the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, stated sharply enough that I can test it myself. Here are the verdicts I actually trust, put as bluntly as I can. (i) A die about to be rolled: 1/6 for a six, and that is a fact about the die and the setup, not a report of my ignorance — it would be 1/6 even for someone who knew everything about how the die was machined. (ii) The same die, already rolled and hidden under a cup: I won't say the probability of a six is 1/6 as a claim about the world, only as a summary of my evidence; the chance, I want to say, is by now 1 or 0. (iii) A coin flipped by a machine I've inspected, whose dynamics are as deterministic as a pendulum's, with initial conditions nobody has measured: I'm perfectly happy to call the chance of heads 1/2, and I don't hear myself as confessing ignorance when I say it. (iv) A particular radium atom, given the measured half-life: 1/2 chance of decaying within the period, a genuine single-case fact that needs no frequencies to state, and one that, by the rule I've seen discussed under the name of the Principal Principle, should simply become my credence once I learn it. (v) A coin I'm told is biased in an unknown direction and to an unknown degree: 1/2 here is my credence and not the coin's chance — learn the bias and my number moves while nothing about the coin does.

The rest divide into cases I'm settled on and cases I'm not. (vi) Whether a certain manuscript was copied in the twelfth century: I'll say "about 70%" to a colleague, but I don't think there is a chance of its being so — the matter is settled and unrepeatable, and the number records my evidence. (vii) Whether Goldbach's conjecture is true: same verdict, and here I feel no discomfort at all. (viii) A forecast of a 30% chance of rain tomorrow: I can't place it. It isn't anybody's personal credence, it isn't a frequency, it comes out of a model I know to be misspecified, and yet I'd act on it — I want to call it a probability but not a chance, without being able to say how it differs from (iv). (ix) An infinite sequence of tosses of a fair coin, which someone then reorders: I don't think the chance of heads depends on the ordering, which makes me doubt that chances are frequencies, and yet I also don't think "the chance is 1/2" says anything over and above how the tosses tend to come out. (x) A clockwork universe with a Laplacian demon in it: I want to say there are no chances there at all, only the demon's certainty and the rest of us not knowing. And then the two I started from, which I go back and forth on. My clinic's 22%: I want it to be about this embryo and this cycle, not an average over a group of women I could be sorted into or out of — and yet I can add my age, my AMH level, my three failed cycles, and get a different number, which suggests it was never about me. And when the clinic's figure for the next cycle drops after those failures, part of me says my credence should drop but the chance either was always what it was (if cycles are independent) or there is nothing here for it to be; their number is tracking their information. Note that (iii) pulls against (vi) and (x): in the machine-flipped coin the outcome is fully fixed by facts I lack and I still call 1/2 the chance, while in the manuscript case the outcome is equally fixed by facts I lack and I refuse to call 70% a chance.

The principle I've been trying out, and which I suspect is broken: a claim of the form "the probability of A is p" is a claim about objective chance just in case p is the credence an agent would land on who knew every particular fact about the present state of the world and every law, but nothing about the future; everything else — frequencies, model output, my clinic's number — is either evidence about some chance, or credence wearing a lab coat. Please test that against (i) through (x) and against the two medical cases, tell me where it gives an answer I don't want, and repair it if it can be repaired, saying which clause each of my cases is responsible for. Two worries I'd like addressed rather than dissolved. First, whether the thing is circular: the laws I'm helping myself to may themselves contain probabilities, so that chance appears on both sides of the definition, and I can't tell whether that's fatal or just a sign that I should be helping myself to less. Second, what any of this says about a case none of us has settled: an Everettian world in which the spin measurement comes out both ways and there is a descendant of me who sees each — is there a chance of my seeing spin-up, is it a frequency across branches, is it only a self-locating credence, or is there nothing for the number to be? Name the competing principles that fit my verdicts about as well as the repaired one does, tell me which of them come apart on that case, give me your best guess with the reason for preferring it, and say plainly which of my verdicts no coherent account can keep, because I'd sooner give one up than keep fudging.
