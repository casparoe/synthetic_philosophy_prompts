I am a retired applied entomologist — forty years in pest-management research — and over the last two years I have begun reading the philosophical literature on invertebrate welfare. I would be grateful for a translation exercise rather than an explanation.

The position to be restated is this. Grant, for the sake of argument, that insects have welfare that matters morally, and grant that in nature the great majority of juvenile insects die young, of starvation, parasitism or disease. On those grants, the case against insecticide-based pest control is not that it kills, since nature kills far more, but that it kills in a distinctive way and at a distinctive time: a neurotoxic death that unfolds over hours, imposed on animals that would otherwise have died otherwise and elsewhere. The humane alternative, on this view, is not to stop controlling pests but to move from mortality to recruitment — sterile-insect release, mating disruption, oviposition deterrents, push–pull cropping. The reply I keep running into is that preventing a birth and causing a death are demographically interchangeable, so that the shift to fertility control is moral bookkeeping rather than a moral difference.

Please restate that dispute, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually worked in: life tables and age-specific survivorship, density-dependent recruitment, natality and mortality as two terms in one projection equation, standing population versus turnover rate, economic injury thresholds, dose–response curves and LC50, resistance-allele frequencies.

Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what survives only by being distorted, and what cannot be said in that vocabulary at all. Two points especially. First, in a strongly density-dependent population, adult mortality is substantially compensated by juvenile survival, so a lethal regime may alter turnover without much altering the population that is there — is that a consideration for or against the lethal regime, or is it the kind of thing my vocabulary simply has no way of evaluating? Second, the models treat death as instantaneous and as costing nothing in itself, while the ethical argument spends most of its force on how long dying takes: is that a defect in population models, or a sign that more is being made of the mode of death than it can bear?

I am fluent in the ecology and a beginner in the ethics, so keep the translation honest — do not help yourself to a welfare-theoretic premise and present it as an implication of the population model — and tell me, at the end, whether the residue reflects a gap in my framework or an unclarity in theirs.
