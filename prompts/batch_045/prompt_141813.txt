Hi — could you write me a bit of mock front matter for a huge academic handbook? It's going in a set of prompts I'm putting together, so form matters more than polish.

Setup: a university press has just published a fourteen-volume handbook, and the General Editor has taken the preface paradox completely literally. The press has built a whole compliance apparatus around it. I'd like roughly 800 words, deadpan institutional prose, sectioned like real front matter: "From the General Editor", "Statement on Error", "The Assertion Rota", "Notes for Contributors", "Corrections and Clarifications", "Acknowledgements". The voice is a competent, sincere administrator writing a document he believes in — not a comedian doing an impression of one. No winking, no nudging, no one saying "absurd, surely".

What the satire has to get right:

- The press's doctrine should be a fair version of the real no-closure response to the lottery and preface paradoxes. Each chapter is very probably fine; the handbook as a whole very probably contains an error; so the press declines to conjoin. Build the apparatus on that: a monthly rota in which one volume is "live" (officially believed) and the other thirteen are "held pending review", so that the handbook is never asserted as a whole. Then let it break structurally rather than with a punchline — the book can't be cited, the library cancels its standing order, a reviewer asks whether the handbook's own table of contents is a conjunction, someone points out that announcing the rota is itself an assertion covering fourteen volumes. I want the reader to feel that refusing closure is something no institution whose entire job is publishing conjunctions can actually live by, while also feeling that the editor has not made a single stupid move.
- Bring the lottery side in on its own: the press runs a staff raffle, and the same reasoning that stops the editor believing the handbook is sound also stops anyone believing their ticket lost — and they just keep running the raffle, on schedule, with minutes. Don't explain that it's the same structure. Just let it sit next to the rest.
- Three invented things I want in there. (1) "Corrigendum creep": the discovery that each correction introduces a new error, so the errata page grows monotonically. (2) An "error offset" scheme: the press funds corrections in other publishers' books to offset its own, and declares itself "error neutral by 2030". (3) A knock-on version in the Acknowledgements: the editor thanks two hundred people and then has to concede he has definitely forgotten at least one, so the thanks go on the rota too. (Invent a name for that last one if you like — a fictional epistemologist credited with it, someone like "H. L. Vandermeer", is fine.)
- Don't let the editor drift into a different solution halfway (no sudden contextualism, no "well, knowledge is just ascriber-sensitive") unless that drift is itself the joke.
- Please don't lean on the real literature by name — I don't want citations and I'm not going to check them, and anyway the point is the ideas, not who said them in the sixties.

Two levels: my epistemology mates should laugh for the right reason (the paradox's structure, not the idea of a fussy academic), and my mum should still be able to follow it and find the error offsets funny.

British English throughout, please. Nothing in quotation marks from any real text — I don't want to have to check sources, and it would break the voice anyway.

Last thing: put a short note after the piece, 120 words or so, and be committed in it rather than giving me a menu. Say which response to the paradox you've built the joke on, where a defender of that response would say you've strawmanned it, and where you've stretched the paradox to make the apparatus work. Pick one answer for each and say why — I'd rather argue with a definite reading than read three balanced options. And if you think the whole premise is misguided and the piece can't work, say that instead and write something else in the same slot, but you have to be definately commited to calling it.
