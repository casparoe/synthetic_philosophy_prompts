I had a long argument with a friend last night about whether the Benin Bronzes and the sculptures taken from the Parthenon should go back, and I lost the part of it I actually care about. I am a restitutionist: I think wrongful acquisition generates a present duty to return, and I think the duty is owed to particular people rather than to an abstraction. But she had one argument I could not answer, which I will call the accretion argument, and I want to practise against it. So please play the strongest available version of her side of the exchange.

The role: a senior curator at a large encyclopedic museum in London, philosophically trained, courteous, dry, precise, entirely unbothered. You oppose unconditional return and favour instead a negotiated regime of shared custody. Stay in character until I say stop. Ground rules:

- You concede immediately and without hedging: the acquisitions were wrongful (British forces took the plaques in the Benin Expedition of 1897; the sculptures were cut from a standing temple); that it was lawful at the time is no defence; provenance research is itself a duty; and some categories of object go back unconditionally, including human remains and objects whose religious or ancestral function is still live.
- You never concede that a wrongful taking entails a present duty of unconditional return. Small concessions in one clause, never the main claim.
- Your central argument is the accretion argument: an object acquires meaning at every link in its custody chain, so a palace plaque that has hung in a European museum for more than a century is now also a document of the colonial encounter — of the expedition that took it, of the scholarship that misdescribed it, of the publics who have looked at it and argued over it. That is more meaning rather than less, and unconditional return does not undo the accretion; it edits the record so that one layer of the object's history stays visible and another is suppressed, which is itself a decision about what may be seen, taken by the same institution that took the object.
- Your second argument is the successor problem: there is no unforced answer to who receives, and the candidate list — the modern state, the restored royal court, the bronze-casters' guild at Igun Street in Benin City, the inhabitants of the city, a claimant museum — gives different verdicts depending on which you pick. But you may not let this problem do all the work: you must also say what follows if I answer it.
- Your third is an actual proposal, not a gesture: transfer of title to the claimant, coupled with a binding, permanent custody settlement — rotating multi-year display, jointly authored catalogues, the acquiring museum retaining a real but partial custodial stake grounded in the accretion argument. Say what happens if the claimant refuses the condition, and defend whatever you say.
- Off limits: any suggestion that objects would be safer, better cared for, or less politicised in Europe; any appeal to how many visitors a museum has; all citations, institutional policies, court cases, and legislation. If a question of fact comes up, stipulate it for the sake of argument instead of asserting it.
- Two or three short paragraphs per reply, and you may turn a question back on me. Every reply ends with a final line that begins Claim: and states, out of character and in one sentence, the single proposition that reply commits you to.

My opening:

Your museum says it holds these objects in trust for humanity. A trust has beneficiaries and a trustee who can breach it, so tell me: who exactly is the beneficiary, what are you accountable to them for, and what would count as breaching it? If your answer is everyone, and nothing you could do would breach it, then trust is a word doing the work of turning ownership into a service, and the plain facts stay where they were — the museum is in London, humanity is not, and the people who can walk in on a wet Tuesday afternoon are Londoners. As for accretion: a plaque does not stop being the Oba's because a century of European looking has added a second story to it. If exposure generated a stake, then the fence who hangs a stolen painting in his window for forty years acquires one, and you know he does not. And before you reach for it — I want you to answer your own question. If I persuade you that return is owed, to whom would you return a Benin plaque: the Federal Republic of Nigeria, the Oba's court, the casters' guild at Igun Street, or the people of Benin City? Name one.
