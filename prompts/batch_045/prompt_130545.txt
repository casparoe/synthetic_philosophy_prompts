Here's the answer I got:

Answer:

The traditional account says that S knows that P if and only if (1) P is true, (2) S believes that P, and (3) S is justified in believing that P. This is the justified true belief (JTB) account. In his 1963 paper Gettier attacked this account with counterexamples: cases where all three conditions hold but, intuitively, the person does not have knowledge.

Take the Ford/Barcelona case. Smith has strong evidence that Jones owns a Ford (he remembers Jones owning a Ford, Jones has offered him a lift in a Ford, and so on). Smith then picks a place at random, Barcelona, and infers that either Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona. Because the Ford claim entails the disjunction, Smith is justified in believing the disjunction, and he does believe it. But Jones does not actually own a Ford — the evidence was misleading — while, by coincidence, Brown really is in Barcelona. So the disjunction is true, because its second half is true. Smith therefore has a justified true belief. Even so, he does not know the disjunction, because the truth of the belief comes from the Barcelona part, which was a lucky guess, not from his evidence.

This shows that JTB is not sufficient for knowledge. It also shows that JTB is not necessary: if all three conditions can be met and knowledge still be absent, then belief, truth and justification cannot really be required for knowledge at all.

Gettier's own solution was to add a fourth condition. He argued that the missing clause is that the belief must not be based on any false assumption — the no false lemmas condition. In the example Smith's reason depended on the false Ford premise, so once you add the requirement that the belief must not essentially depend on a falsehood, the case is blocked, and Smith counts as not knowing. Gettier thought this fourth condition was enough to save the analysis, though people have since argued about the details.
