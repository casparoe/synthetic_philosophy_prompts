I'm one of three volunteers rewriting the code of conduct for a mountain rescue association — unpaid, on call, occasionally in genuine danger — and a drafting problem has turned into an argument I can't find the exit from. The code has two sections, expected of everyone and above and beyond, and we want the line between them to be defensible; our working constraint is a no-gerrymander condition, to the effect that the line may not fall between two courses of conduct whose cost to the volunteer differs by less than a volunteer could reliably notice (ten extra minutes on a callout, one more weekend of training, driving twenty miles rather than fifteen). A friend on the group used that constraint to build the following case. (1) An act is supererogatory just when it is morally good to do while its omission is neither wrong nor blameworthy, and the second section of the code is meant to house exactly these. (2) If A and A* differ only by an unnoticeable increment of cost and a correspondingly tiny increment of benefit, they cannot fall on opposite sides of a defensible line. (3) From any act in the second section — giving up a Sunday to help with a search, say — there runs a chain of unnoticeable decrements down to doing nothing at all, and from that same act a chain of unnoticeable increments up to something nobody would deny is required. (4) By (2), adjacent links in such a chain share their deontic status, and so do all the links. (5) Doing nothing at all is permissible and not required. (6) So every act in the chain is neither required nor forbidden: everything is optional, and the first section of the code is empty. (7) Run the chain upward instead, from an act everyone agrees is required, and everything, including doing nothing, is required; run the same reasoning over wrongness and pocketing five pounds from the tea fund comes out as wrong as, or no wronger than, emptying the safe. (8) Either way there are no deontic distinctions left to draw, so nothing is supererogatory and our two sections are both arbitrary.

I don't accept (8), but I can't locate the mistake, and while I would rather keep both the existence of duties and the existence of acts beyond duty than keep the no-gerrymander condition, I want to know what giving that condition up costs us: does it commit the code to a merely conventional or arbitrary threshold, to the vagueness of required and forbidden, to a third category of acts that are bad to do but not forbidden, or to something worse? Tell me which step you would reject, why it looked innocent, and what each of the alternatives costs. The practical question behind all this is whether the document wants two sections, three, or a graded scale rather than a binary. Please answer in British English, in at most 400 words, opening with a single sentence naming the step you reject and spending the remainder on what rejecting it costs and on what the code should end up looking like.
