hey — I'm in a personal identity seminar this term (Parfit a few weeks back, this week it's Hofstadter on the "I" being smeared across more than one brain, the bit where he claims some of his dead wife is still running in his own head and that this isn't just a manner of speaking). I'm meant to be writing a response paper and I've realised I have pretty firm gut verdicts on a pile of cases and no idea what generalization they add up to. Don't bother with citations or further reading, we've got the readings — I just want the conceptual work.

1. Split-brain patient, callosum cut, each hemisphere now running a lot of what used to be one stream of consiousness. Verdict: one person. I refuse to say there are two half-shares of one 'I' sitting in a single skull.

2. My grandmother in late-stage dementia: recognises no one, no episodic memory left, but still tells the same jokes with the same timing, still gets irritable about the same things, still butters toast in that weird way. Verdict: still her, but thinned out — like a third of her? Not zero, not 100%.

3. My grandmother after she's dead: my mother makes a face that is unmistakably hers, I catch myself using her phrasing, my sister decides things the way she did. Verdict: yes, something real, genuinely distributed now.

4. A stranger who by coincidence lands at 90% overlap with my grandmother's patterns — same jokes, same mannerisms, arrived at independently, never met her. Verdict: zero. Feels almost offensive to say otherwise.

5. Someone she shaped over twenty years who has since drifted until maybe 5% of her patterns are still running in them. Verdict: yes, small but real share.

6. Destructive scan-and-rebuild teleportation. Verdict: that's me, survival not replacement. Non-destructive version with the original left standing: both are me, but each gets less than full — branching and thinning together.

7. The case I invented that got me into this mess: twelve of her old students and collaborators deliberately train themselves to internalise a chunk of her — one has her editorial judgement, one her taste in arguments, one her way of handling a hostile question, and so on. No single one is recognisably her. But when they deliberate as a group, her way of arriving at a decision reconstitutes, and people who knew her find it uncanny. Verdict: yes, distributed — and the share is bigger when they're together than when they're apart, which feels like it shouldn't be possible if a share is a property of an individual brain.

8. Persistent vegetative state, nothing running at all. Verdict: her 'I' isn't in there anymore, but she is still partly in us. Brain death and pattern death come apart.

9. The one I go back and forth on: a language model fine-tuned on all her letters and diaries that talks like her well enough to unsettle the family. Some days I say that's a share of her, some days I say it's a very good puppet.

My tentative principle: a brain carries a share of S's 'I' to the degree that (a) it runs patterns that are S's patterns, (b) those patterns got there by being transmitted from S rather than arrived at independently, and (c) the patterns are actively run and functionally integrated with the rest of what that brain does, not merely stored or trotted out on demand.

What I want: test that against the list and repair it. The pair I can't reconcile is 1 and 7 — the pattern is split within one skull and I say one person, split across twelve skulls and I say a distributed person; is there any difference between those two situations that isn't just the skulls? Does clause (c) actually do the work I want in case 9, or does it just beg the question? What does your repaired principle say about a case I haven't listed — say my mother and my sister each carrying overlapping 60% of my grandmother, so the shares sum to well over one: should there be a conservation rule on 'I'-stuff or not? And which single verdict of mine should I drop if I want the simplest principle that saves the rest? Pick a side rather than listing options.
