hey — I run a once-a-month philosophy-ish discussion night at the hackerspace I'm a member of. Crowd is maybe twelve people: mostly self-taught programmers, two or three hardware/EE folks, one biologist, nobody with any philosophy training (me included, really). In two weeks I've got 45 minutes and a whiteboard to do the bit of philosophy of mind where multiple realizability is supposed to be a problem for the idea that a mental state like pain is just a neural state. Plan how I should teach it — don't explain the topic to me, tell me how to build the session.

The thing I keep tripping over: I'm fairly sure their default analogy will be "mental state = program, brain = machine, multiple realizability = the same program running on different hardware." And I have a feeling that analogy is a trap — that the argument only really bites when the realizers are *scattered*, i.e. there's no unified lower-level description of them, no shared mechanism, nothing interesting in common underneath the shared causal role — as opposed to when they're merely *many* but clustered into a tight family that some lower-level story could still capture. So I want the whole session built around that contrast between realizer scatter and realizer clustering, and I want one example carrying it. Tell me what that example has to have and has to lack, and why: it needs genuinely scattered realizers, and it must not be a case of the same code on two different chips, because that's exactly the picture I'm trying to break.

Then the order. What has to be on the board before the example can even land — do they need the type-identity / token-identity distinction first, or does that come out of the example? And where in the sequence do I head off each misconception? The ones I expect: the software/hardware one; "so the mind isn't physical at all"; "fine, token identity then, problem solved"; and the sharp one I have no answer to — if any disjunction of realizers counts, then multiply realization is trivially easy to get, so the argument proves nothing. Also someone will ask at what grain or level you're supposed to describe the realizers, and I don't know if that's a real problem or a distraction at this level, or whether I should concede up front that a disjunctive or species-by-species reduction survives the scatter case.

Give me: the dependency order with reasons, the carrying example and its constraints, the misconception map with the point in the sequence where each gets defused, one question at the end that tells me whether they got the scatter point rather than just the portability point, and the two or three things I should openly refuse to get into and say so about.
