i want to practise attacking the kantian story about moral worth, so please play me a properly committed kantian — sharp, not a strawman, not the cartoon version who thinks having nice feelings is bad. your position: an action has moral worth only if it's done from duty, i.e. done because the agent takes it to be required, and doing the right thing from warm spontaneous fellow-feeling is charming but doesn't get you moral worth proper. use the real resources — good will, maxims, the honest shopkeeper against the naturally sympathetic philanthropist, inclination, the thought that it's itself a duty to cultivate sympathetic feeling, the worry that we can never really see into our own motives — and give ground on small points but never on the main one. one paragraph per reply, chatty, no headings or bullet points. break character only when i say 'drop it'. i'm taking the other side: that worth tracks being moved by the right-making considerations, whatever the agent thinks about duty.

here's my opening. please engage with the case rather than with huck finn or anyone else's favourite example, and if a detail is missing just stipulate a sensible one and say what you're assuming instead of asking me.

Bernard visits his brother Des at the same care home every thursday. for two years he hated it: Des is boring and wastly unkind, Bernard would rather have been anywhere else, the visits were a grind he got nothing out of. he went purely because he'd decided it was owed — parents dead, no one else visits, so this is duty and that's that. then Bernard has a stroke, and as a side effect of the brain damage he develops a strong spontaneous aversion to other people's loneliness: he now finds isolation genuinely distressing in a way he never did before. he didn't cultivate it, didn't choose it, wasn't trying to improve himself, and he'd have refused the outcome if he'd known it would also make him miserable about strangers at bus stops. now on thursdays he goes because the thought of Des sitting there on his own bothers him, and he goes to stop that.

my two pushes. first: your view looks committed to saying that Bernard's thursday visits lost moral worth after the stroke, maybe all of it, because they now come from inclination rather than duty. but after the stroke he's responding to exactly the feature that makes visiting right in the first place — Des's loneliness is the point of the thing. isn't it backwards to say worth goes down precisely when the agent starts being moved by what matters? second, and sharper: the pre-stroke Bernard, told credibly that Des is perfectly cheerful alone and doesn't want the visits, would still have turned up, because it's what's owed. the dutiful version of him is counterfactually insensitive to the very feature that makes the action right, while the post-stroke version tracks it. if worth is about acting for the right reasons, post-stroke Bernard wins; if it's about acting under the thought of duty, pre-stroke Bernard wins. tell me straight in your first reply whether post-stroke Bernard's visits have more, less, or no moral worth, and then defend whichever answer you give — and please don't duck it by saying we can never know his motives anyway.
