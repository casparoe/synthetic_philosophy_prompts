Third-year PhD here in philosophy of language, working more or less on the semantics/pragmatics boundary — the question of how much of what an utterance conveys has to be sitting in the lexicon already. In my reading group last week someone proposed a conventional, lexically encoded treatment of free-choice inferences: the step from "you may have cake or ice cream" to "you may have cake and you may have ice cream." Before they'd finished, somebody else said senses are not to be multiplied beyond necessity — our in-house version of Ockham's razor, people round here call it Grice's razor, I've never actually checked whether he said anything like it — and the room treated that as a refutation. I don't think it is one, and I also can't say exactly why.

I've been going back and forth with two distinctions of my own and I want to know wich of them matters. First: a razor about content versus a razor about machinery. One reading says keep the stock of senses small; another says keep the stock of interpretive apparatus small, meaning maxims, exhaustification operators, conventions, defaults, whatever does the work. In the free-choice case, buying fewer senses seems to mean buying a lot more machinery — you need something like a covert exhaustivity operator plus a story about why the inference doesn't cancel the way a normal scalar implicature does — so if the motto is the second kind of razor it might not settle anything at all. Second: synchronic versus diachronic. Is it a constraint on what a lexicon may contain at a time, a claim about how senses get there over time via conventionalized implicatures, or an empirical prediction about what polysemy languages should never have? And then there's the question of what "beyond necessity" is doing: necessary for deriving the attested utterance meanings, necessary for a hearer to compute online, necessary for acquisition?

What I want is the menu. Set out the distinct precise theses that motto could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each give a one-line verdict — trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or not even the kind of thing that could bear a truth value. For each, say whether it would actually do the work the group wanted it to do, i.e. whether it rules out a conventional treatment of free-choice or rather than merely making that treatment unfashionable. Then tell me wich reading people who say it are probably reaching for, and whether that reading survives my two distinctions. Also say which reading, if any, would make it a good objection to something like an ambiguity account of some, since I keep hearing it used there too and I suspect it's a different reading each time.

Two things about how to pitch the answer. Mark how confident you are in each verdict and flag where you're reconstructing a position rather than reporting one — I don't want citations or a reading list, I can find those myself. And if my whole framing is off — if free choice is a bad test case because whatever that inference is, it isn't a sense-versus-implicature question, or if the motto is just a methodological quip with no real content — say that plainly rather than politely generating me six tidy readings of a slogan nobody actually holds.
