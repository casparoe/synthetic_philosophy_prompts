I somehow ended up as the person at work who has to write our internal position on whether clinicians are epistemically OK to rely on our product, and I'm out of my depth. We ship a tool that reads a patient's entire record and hands back a short summary — what has been said about this patient's allergies, and by whom — built by fine-tuning a general language model on a huge pile of clinician-written notes. So the output isn't the system's own findings, and it isn't a quotation from any single document: it's a statistical distillation of thousands of human assertions, produced by something with no beliefs, no intentions, no reputation to protect and nothing to loose by being wrong. Users treat it like a colleague's handover note, which is exactly what worries me, and nobody I've asked has a worked-out view on whether that's even the right category for it. Two philosophers I spoke to said the epistemology of testimony is the obvious literature to reach for, and I've now read enough to get the basic fault line — roughly, whether my justification for believing what I'm told has to be built up out of other evidence, or whether there's a default entitlement to accept it that only gets knocked out by defeaters. But the whole literature seems to assume a speaker on the other end, and I can't tell which of its open questions actually carry over to us.

We've got room to fund maybe two small projects — say a couple of person-years in total, or one PhD student for a year — and I'm the one who has to argue for where that goes, toward the practical question of what we should tell our users (and our regulator) about how much the summary is worth. So: give me a ranked shortlist of three or four open questions in that neighbourhood that you think deserve the money, and defend the ordering. I want the criteria made explicit — which questions are upstream of the others, which are actually tractable in a year for someone working conceptually rather than empirically, which are neglected, and how you're trading those off against each other — and I want to know what kind of result would move something up or down your list. Things I've been kicking around, all of which you should feel free to tell me are confused or a waste of a year: whether reliance here is really closer to reading a thermometer than to trusting a colleague, and whether that turns on the system's not having a mind or on something more specific than that; whether testimonial warrant survives being pooled and compressed across thousands of sources, or whether aggregation destroys whatever made individual testimony special in the first place; whether the default-and-challenge picture applies at all when there's no one who can be held answerable; and what defeat is even supposed to look like when there's nobody to interrogate — whether no one to ask is doing the work, or nothing to ask is. Also say which of the big alleged practical upshots of reductionism versus anti-reductionism you think are overrated or simply don't follow for a case like ours; I'd rather spend the year on two things that matter than five that don't.

Please don't give me a survey of the debate, and don't point me at papers or act as if I can go and look things up — I can't get at most of them, so argue it out in your own words. I want your actual ranking, not a menu with the choice left to me, and I want to know where you're shakiest: which of your judgements you'd give up first under pressure, and what would have to be true for you to reverse the top of the list. Under 600 words, British English.
