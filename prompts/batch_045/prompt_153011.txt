Question 4 (25 points). A city buys a risk score R from a vendor to help decide which defendants awaiting trial can be released. R is a number between 0 and 1, and the vendor says it is calibrated within each racial group: among defendants in group A assigned R = 0.4, about 40% are rearrested before trial, and likewise for other score values and for group B. The observed base rate of pretrial rearrest is 0.32 for defendants in group A and 0.21 for defendants in group B. The score is not perfectly predictive: for at least some values of R strictly between 0 and 1, some defendants are rearrested and some are not. The city uses R two ways: to flag defendants for continued detention, and to rank-order defendants for a pool of about 300 pretrial support slots (out of roughly 1,000 defendants).

(a) State, in your own words, what calibration within groups, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class mean in this setting. (6 points)

(b) A civil-rights group demands that the score satisfy both balance conditions in addition to calibration. Explain, as precisely as you can, why the three requirements cannot all be met here, and say what would have to be true for them to be simultaneously satisfiable. (10 points)

(c) The vendor offers two fixes: (i) report the score in three coarse bins (low/medium/high) rather than a fine-grained number, and (ii) for defendants whose scores land in the middle bin, randomize the release recommendation with a weighted coin. Does either fix get around the problem? Why or why not? (4 points)

(d) One member of the city council says: These impossibility results are just telling us that the data is dirty. Rearrest isn't reoffending — it's contaminated by where police patrol. Get an unbiased outcome variable and the conflict disappears. A second member adds: Anyway, for the support slots we're only ranking people, we never apply a cutoff, so none of this applies to us. Assess both claims. (5 points)

Student's answer:

(a) Calibration within groups means E[Y | R = r, G = g] = r for both groups and every score r — a score of 0.4 means the same thing (a 40% chance of rearrest) whichever group you're in. Balance for the positive class means defendents who actually reoffend get the same average score in group A as in group B, i.e. E[R | Y = 1, A] = E[R | Y = 1, B]. Balance for the negative class is the same condition restricted to the people who don't reoffend, E[R | Y = 0, A] = E[R | Y = 0, B].

(b) All three can't hold at once. Note first that calibration has a consequence: if you average over everybody in a group, E[R | G] = E[E[Y | R, G]] = E[Y | G], so the mean score in a group has to equal that group's base rate — 0.32 in group A, 0.21 in group B. But balance for the positive class requires the average score to be the same in the two groups. So as long as the base rates differ, the average scores differ, and balance has to fail. The only ways out are that the two groups have the same base rate, or that the score is perfect — everyone who will reoffend gets 1 and everyone else gets 0. If the score were perfect then E[R | Y = 1] = 1 and E[R | Y = 0] = 0 in both groups and both balance conditions hold automatically, and calibration holds too. Since we're told no score here is perfectly predictive, the city can't have all three requirements, and it has to decide which one to give up.

(c) Neither fix works. Coarsening to three bins changes nothing: you can run the same argument with three score values instead of a continuum, since the conditions are about the joint distribution of the score and the outcome and not about how finely the score is reported. The bins would also have to be recalibrated to whatever the empirical rearrest rate in each bin turns out to be, so you end up in the same place. Randomising the recommendation doesn't help either — balance and calibration are statements about the scores people recieve and what actually happens to them, and flipping a coin at the decision stage doesn't change anybody's score. If you prefer to think in error-rate terms, randomising just moves you to a point on the line between two deterministic decision rules; it doesn't produce a new point where both equal error rates and equal predictive value hold.

(d) The first council member is basically right. The conflict comes from the fact that rearrest is a bad stand-in for reoffending: it reflects policing patterns, not what defendants do. Once you have an unbiased outcome variable — actual reoffense — the scores mean what they claim to mean, and a calibrated score can satisfy the balance conditions as well. The impossibility results are a fact about biased data, not about fairness as such. The second member also has a point, though it's a bit different: which criterion you should care about does depend on how the score is used, so if you're only ranking people for a limited pool of slots, calibration matters more than error-rate balance and the trade-off bites less.
