Hey — I need help with somthing I keep going in circles on.

There are two arguments I like that seem to share a skeleton, and I want to know whether they're really the same argument in different hats, or whether I'm being fooled by surface similarity.

Argument 1 (anti-luminosity): call a condition C luminous iff whenever C obtains for you, you're in a position to know that C obtains. Knowledge needs a margin for error: if you're in a position to know C in some case, then C also obtains — and you're in a position to know it — in all nearby, barely-different cases. Now line up a chain of cases, tiny steps apart, running from one where C clearly obtains to one where it clearly doesn't. Luminosity kicks you off at the first case, the margin for error shoves you one step along, and by induction you wind up in a position to know C in a case where it doesn't obtain. Contradiction. So the only luminous conditions are the trivial ones (always obtain, or never).

Argument 2 (no-trade / can't-agree-to-disagree): two agents start from a common prior, each gets private information, each updates. If their posteriors are common knowledge — I know mine, I know that you know yours, I know that you know that I know mine, all the way up — then the posteriors have to be identical, so no trade and no persisting disagreement. Again: an infinite hierarchy of iterated attitudes, strong conclusion at the top.

Both climb a ladder and get something surprising at the limit. But they feel different in kind. In (2) each extra rung seems to tighten things: the hierarchy converges to a fixed point. In (1) each rung seems to cost you something, and the ladder collapses.

Roughly what I want explained:

(a) My vague hunch is that (1) is secretly a connectedness argument — being in a position to know behaves like an open-set condition on the space of cases, and since the case space is connected, the only conditions that are both luminous and margin-for-error-ish are the trivial clopen ones. Is that the right way to see it, and can you make it precise without going full topology-nerd on me?

(b) More importantly: does (1) actually kill the KK principle (if you know p, you're in a position to know that you know p), or only the luminosity of first-order conditions like feeling cold? KK seems to hand you each finite iteration one at a time — Kp, KKp, KKKp — but the slippery slope needs the margin not to shrink as you climb. So is there a coherent shaky ladder version of KK, where you always know that you know, but the margin for error degrades a bit at every level and gives out somewhere way up the hierarchy? If that's coherent, the anti-luminosity argument proves less than people usually claim; if it's incoherent, I want to know exactly which premise kills it. (Same question for negative introspection, if it differs.)

(c) Where does the analogy with (2) break? My guess is something like: in (2) you're iterating an operator — conditional expectation against a shared prior — that's idempotent or contracting, so iterating it stabilises; in (1) you're iterating a lossy requirement, so iterating it decays. Make that concrete, and tell me why one hierarchy consolidates while the other erodes.

Don't just tell me they're different arguments — I want the structural reason, and I want to know which bit of my hunch is wrong. Small made-up examples (a dimmer switch, two traders) are very welcome; citations aren't needed.
