I'm the valedictorian at my school's graduation in three weeks, and I'm stuck on the address.

Our assistant principal chose a theme for the year — "Be Yourself" — and it's on the banners, on the program, and she has asked the student speakers to work it in. The trouble is that the framework I've actually spent this year thinking through, in Mr. Aldridge's senior elective on existentialism, makes that slogan close to unintelligible. On that view there is no finished self for any of us to go and be; treating your own traits as settled facts about you is exactly the evasion the view warns against, the way people dodge the discomfort of having to choose. So I can't echo the theme literally without saying something I think is false.

But I also can't just stand up and say the opposite, for two reasons I keep running into.

First, sincerity isn't an available substitute. As I understand the position, demanding sincerity from yourself is another attempt to locate a real self you could finally match up with — the same move in different clothes. So I can't rescue the theme by translating it into "be authentic" or "be true to who you really are." I need some third thing to put at the center of the speech, and I don't know what it is.

Second, a good part of my class had very little room to choose. One friend worked four nights a week at his family's restaurant through senior year; another is enlisting in the fall because it's the only way she can afford college. It would be obscene to tell them they could have been anything they wanted. So the speech has to take seriously that people's situations are real and constraining, without turning around and implying that those situations are what people are.

There's a personal version of this too. For four years I've been saying "I'm just not a math person," and I've come to suspect that sentence has been doing more work for me than I admitted. I'd like to be able to say something about that in the speech — honestly, in a way that isn't either a confession or a boast.

The audience will be about four hundred people: classmates, parents, grandparents, teachers. Mr. Aldridge will be there and will hear exactly what I did with the theme. My grandmother, who raised me, will be in the front row and will not follow a word of philosophy. The principal wants warmth rather than a lecture, so no jargon, no French, and no philosophers named from the stage.

Please write the six-to-eight-minute address. It has to work as a graduation speech first — my classmates should feel something rather than feel analyzed — but I want its structure to survive Mr. Aldridge's attention. Where the theme has to be echoed, echo it in a form I could defend. Afterwards, add a short note explaining what the speech is committed to and where the load-bearing moves are, so I can answer him if he asks me about it at the reception.
