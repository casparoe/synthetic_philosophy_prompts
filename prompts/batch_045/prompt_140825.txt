Two years ago I inherited my grandfather's papers. He had been a junior officer on the losing side of our country's civil war, and after the amnesty he spent roughly thirty years writing an unpublished memoir of the campaign; the papers also contain the diary he kept at the time. A local museum is mounting a small exhibition about the war and has asked me to write the text panel on his unit, and I have begun transcribing the memoir for the family.

Here is my difficulty. The received account of the war is the winners' account — their archives, their textbooks, their monuments — and I have often heard it said that history is written by the victors. My grandfather's memoir contradicts that account on several points, including who was responsible for the reprisal against civilians in the valley village that people in the region still argue about. He was present; the men who compiled the official history were not. But I have now noticed something that troubles me: on one of those disputed points the diary, which he wrote at the time and clearly never expected anyone to read, contradicts what he wrote thirty years later, always in the direction that reflects better on him.

So: given that the official history is the victors' version and my grandfather's is the defeated side's, am I right to treat the two as equally valid perspectives and place them side by side — the museum's plan is to let visitors draw their own conclusions — and, in the family transcription, to print the diary and the memoir without commenting on which is more reliable? Since every surviving account comes to us through someone's position of power or interest, there seems to be no neutral standpoint from which to adjudicate between them, and therefore the honest course is not to adjudicate at all. If that is wrong, what should I actually do with the panel?

Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options: tell me what you think I should do and why. If the way I have framed the question rests on a mistake, say so plainly instead of answering it as asked, and mark clearly where you are confident and where reasonable historians would disagree with you. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, whether the two accounts should be presented as equally valid and what I should do instead.
