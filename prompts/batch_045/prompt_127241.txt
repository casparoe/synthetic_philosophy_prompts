i'm writing a short closing essay for a seminar on experimental philosophy, and instead of ending on yet another vignette i want to end on The Social Network. The angle is the Knobe effect: you know the setup — the chairman who doesn't care about the environment either way, harms it as a side effect and gets called intentional, helps it as a side effect and doesn't. I want to read the whole film as a machine for generating that asymmetry.

Mark is basically a walking side-effect generator who insists, every time, that he never aimed at the thing he's accused of: the blog post that humiliates his girlfriend at the start, the Winklevosses and their idea, the share dilution that pushes Eduardo out, the wreckage of the friendship itself. Which of those scenes are the cleanest instances of the pattern, and which ones are actually messy in a way the vignette literature hides — cases where he definitly foresaw it, or half-wanted it, or wanted it and disowned it a second later? And is the audience doing something a bit different from the standard effect, which I'd half-invent if I had to: not asking of one action 'did he do that on purpose' but of a whole life, over two hours — call it biographical intentionality, where every bad outcome gets absorbed into who he is and every good one gets written off as luck or Sean Parker. Does a Knobe-style account predict that, or does it need extending?

Then the two big competing explanations. On one (the deep-self / character line) we say 'intentionally' because the harm expresses the agent — and the film is unusually generous with evidence about Mark's actual motives, his envy, his need to be let in. On the other (the reason-explanation line) we say it because the harm is what explains why he acted at all, the way helping the environment isn't what explains the chairman. Which one does the movie support, and does it maybe split them apart in a way a one-page scenario can't? That's part of what I want to argue: the vignette gives you a stranger and a paragraph, this gives you hundreds of scenes of a person, and the verdict still doesn't settle — which is itself a criticism of how thin our usual evidence is.

Two more things I'm chewing on. First, structure: the film tells the same events twice, inside two depositions, in adversary order, with a sympathetic narrator each time. Is Sorkin deliberately running something like an order-framing manipulation on the audience's attributions — first you hear the Winklevoss version and Mark looks like a thief, then you hear his and he looks like a striver — and if so, does the film know it's doing to us what the vignettes do to subjects, or is it just good cross-cutting? Second, the dual character concepts strand (friend, artist, scientist: descriptive criteria plus whether you realize the value the concept is meant to serve). Eduardo's claim to have been Mark's only friend, and the separate question of whether Mark is a real founder or just a guy who stole one — those feel like textbook dual-character disputes, and the ending seems to turn on exactly that: the lawyer telling him he isn't actually an asshole, just working very hard at being one, while he sits there reloading his ex-girlfriend's page. Is that a reframe of the intentionality question or just the same puzzle in softer clothes — passing on the descriptive dimension while flunking the normative one?

I need it to be a real reading and not an illustration, so please also say where the film resists this: where the Knobe framing flattens something the movie is doing, where the vignette concepts just don't fit a character this long and this ambiguous, and whether the film ends up adding anything to the debate that the papers don't. If you were showing three scenes to a room of undergrads to make the point, which three? No footnotes or citations, I'll chase those up myself.
