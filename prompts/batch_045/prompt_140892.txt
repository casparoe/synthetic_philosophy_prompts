Exam question

In "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (originally published in The Philosophical Review 60 (1951): 20-43; reprinted in W.V.O. Quine, From a Logical Point of View, Harvard University Press, 1953; second, revised, edition 1961), Quine claims that modern empiricism has been conditioned by two dogmas, and that they are, at root, identical. He opens the essay by writing:

Modern empiricism has been conditioned in large part by two dogmas. One is a belief in some fundamental cleavage between truths which are analytic, or grounded in meanings independently of matters of fact and truths which are synthetic, or grounded in fact. The other dogma is reductionism: the belief that each meaningful statement is equivalent to some logical construct upon terms which refer to immediate experience. Both dogmas, I shall argue, are ill founded. One effect of abandoning them is, as we shall see, a blurring of the supposed boundary between speculative metaphysics and natural science. Another effect is a shift toward pragmatism.

Later, in discussing statements usually taken to be analytic, he writes:

Statements which are analytic by general philosophical acclaim are not, indeed, far to seek. They fall into two classes. Those of the first class, which may be called logically true, are typified by: (1) No unmarried man is married. But there is also a second class of analytic statements, typified by: (2) No bachelor is married.

Suppose a philosopher grants that Quine has discredited the classical idea that analytic statements are true solely by virtue of meaning and knowable a priori, but proposes to retain a weaker, graded version of the distinction. Call this the "central-peripheral salience" (CPS) account. According to CPS, statements in the web of belief differ in how central they are. The more central a statement is, the less likely it is to be revised in response to any particular recalcitrant experience. Thus "No bachelor is married" is more analytic than "Bachelors are happier," not because the former is true by meaning alone, but because it is more central and hence more resistant to revision. On this account, however, no statement is absolutely immune to revision: even the most central statement can in principle be abandoned if we adjust enough elsewhere in the web.

Evaluate whether CPS escapes Quine's critique. In your answer, you must: (i) state the two dogmas and explain why Quine takes them to be at root identical; (ii) explain the role of the verification theory of meaning and of reductionism in his argument, being careful about the order in which the parts of the argument depend on one another; and (iii) assess whether CPS either preserves a recognizable analytic-synthetic distinction or instead collapses into Quine's own holism. In doing so, explain what, if anything, Quine would say about the relation between centrality in the web of belief and analyticity.

Student's answer

Quine's essay attacks two dogmas of empiricism. The first is the belief in a fundamental cleavage between analytic truths, which are true by virtue of meanings and independently of fact, and synthetic truths, which are grounded in fact. The second is reductionism, the belief that each meaningful statement is equivalent to some logical construct upon terms which refer to immediate experience. Quine holds both to be ill founded. He notes that statements usually taken to be analytic fall into two classes: first, logical truths such as "No unmarried man is married," and second, statements such as "No bachelor is married," which are supposed to be analytic because "bachelor" is synonymous with "unmarried man." His critique is that we have no acceptable account of the synonymy or necessity needed to underwrite the second class; the notions are as obscure as analyticity itself.

Quine's argument proceeds as follows. He first establishes his holist conclusion that no statement is immune to revision: any statement can be held true come what may if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the web of belief. Because no statement is thus immune, reductionism must be false, since reductionism requires that each statement be equivalent to some determinate construct of immediate experience. Having refuted reductionism, Quine then argues that the verification theory of meaning is false; and because the verification theory of meaning just is the doctrine that some statements are analytic, this is why he concludes that the two dogmas are, at root, identical. The connection between them is therefore simply that reductionism is the same doctrine as the analytic-synthetic distinction, dressed up in epistemological vocabulary.

Turning to the CPS account: CPS correctly notes that Quine thinks central statements, such as the laws of logic, are less likely to be given up than peripheral ones. However, CPS fails to rescue the analytic-synthetic distinction. This is because Quine explicitly identifies degree of centrality—understood as degree of resistance to revision—with degree of analyticity. On his view, to say that a statement is more analytic just is to say that it is more central in the web of belief and hence harder to revise. Consequently, the CPS account does not preserve a distinctively semantic analytic-synthetic distinction; at most, it redescribes Quine's own holism in graded terms. Since Quine thinks no statement is absolutely immune to revision, he can allow that "No bachelor is married" is more central than "Bachelors are happier," but he would deny that this gives us the classical distinction. The CPS account therefore collapses into Quine's position, and does not escape his critique.
