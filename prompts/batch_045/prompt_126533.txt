I've been reading this corner of the internet since the Slate Star Codex days and circling the same cluster of questions for years, but I finally hit a version of it where my own intuitions are in open conflict with each other, so I'd like some help doing reflective equilibrium on them rather than another round of confident assertions. The setting is a sandbox survival MMO, Ashfall Reach, running since 2014 with full-loot player-versus-player combat and permanent death, where most of what anyone values is stuff they made themselves and can lose for good. The incident that started this: a player called Vespers spent four years using the game's book-and-quill mechanic to hand-transcribe a few thousand poems into in-game books and shelve them in a library he built, and another player found an old fire spell the developers had forgotten to disable and burned the whole thing down in about ninety seconds. My reaction, and the reaction of nearly everyone on the server, was that this was a genuine and serious harm, worse than most of the property disputes I've seen in that game, and I'm not embarrassed about how strongly I feel it. My friend's line is that the books were rows in a database, that the only thing destroyed was an expectation, and that anyone who disagrees has been taken in by the rendering. That can't be the whole story, but I can't say what the rest of the story is.

So here are my verdicts, going by gut and in no particular order, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. First, the burning of the Cinder Library: a real harm, and a serious one. Second, a guild officer draining the guild bank of items worth something like four thousand dollars on the grey market and cashing out: also a real harm, and yet I rank it clearly below the first, even though the hours of labor and the dollar value both favor the bank, so something about irreplaceability seems to be moving me. Third, the same quantity of gold and gear stolen from an account that had been acquired entirely by botting, where no human ever spent an hour earning it: barely a harm at all, maybe none, which sits badly with the second case because the coins are fungible and the market value identical. Fourth, two hundred hours of gear lost in a full-loot gank on a server everyone joined knowing the rules: not a harm, or at least not a wrong, and I notice that if the same loss happened on a server advertised as safe I would call it obviously both. Fifth, a veteran spending ten minutes trapping and killing a brand-new player whose进度 was fully restored afterward: genuinely wrong but minor, and I can't tell whether I think it is a harm or merely an imposition. Sixth, a companion app whose underlying model was swapped by the company, so that a persona I had been talking to for eight months was simply gone and a different one answered in its place: I felt a real loss and I still do, but I cannot point to a single thing that was destroyed, and this is the case I keep going back and forth on. Seventh, someone using a cloned voice and a photorealistic likeness of a real, identifiable woman, without her knowledge, to make a non-player character beg for mercy in a widely streamed scene: she was harmed, and seriously, even though nothing she owned was touched and she never entered the game. Eighth, a streamer torturing photorealistic but procedurally generated villagers in an offline single-player campaign, where nothing on screen is modeled on any real person: my visceral reaction is strong, my considered verdict is that nobody is harmed at all. Even where I am confident I can see the seams: my verdict in the first case weakens a lot if I imagine Vespers had quietly quit in 2019 and never logged in again, which suggests I am tracking an ongoing attachment rather than the labor itself, and that in turn does not explain why I treat the botted account as nearly nothing.

The best thing I have been able to come up with, and I would like you to test it against these cases and repair or replace it, is a kind of substitution test: take the virtual episode, swap every virtual object for its nearest physical-world counterpart and every virtual action for its nearest physical counterpart, and ask whether anyone is harmed in the resulting scenario; if yes, the virtual episode is a real harm, and its severity carries over. That seems to get the library, the guild bank, the gank, and the newbie roughly right, it looks like it overturns my verdict on the botted account, since stealing found money is still theft in the analogue, and I genuinely do not know what it says about the companion app or about the streamer, because there is no obvious physical counterpart to a persona being quietly replaced or to villagers who were never anyone. Please tell me whether anything like this can be made to work, state as precisely as you can the account you end up with, say which clause is doing the work in each of my cases, and then tell me what it predicts about cases I have not listed: a shard of a world that keeps running after the last human logs off, populated by agents with learned preferences who can be made to suffer; a permanently dead character whose mind-state is restored from a backup an hour later, with no gap from the character's own point of view; and an item that outlives the shutdown of its game as an entry on a public ledger nobody plays with. If no single account covers everything, give me the two or three best candidates, and be straight with me about which of my verdicts each of them forces me to give up. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, in paragraphs, the way you would in a long email: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables.
