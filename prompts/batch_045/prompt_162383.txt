Last night I argued for two hours with a friend about a television series we both love, made by a showrunner who has since been credibly accused of abusing the people who worked under him, and we got nowhere. I think I now see why: we were answering three different questions and treating them as one. Whether the series is still any good. Whether the man's conduct changes what the series is about. And whether we do something wrong by going on watching it and thereby paying him. Every time one of us produced a case that settled one of these, the other produced a case that bore on a different one.

I want to turn that argument into something a reading group can actually use. Please build me a decision tree that sorts people by what they say about cases, not by which label they are willing to wear.

The structure I have in mind:

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of positions. Start by laying out the discriminating questions and the combinations of answers they permit; only then place the actual positions into the cells that turns out to be occupied. Each branch node should be a single question framed as a concrete case, phrased so that someone who has never opened a philosophy book can answer it from intuition, and phrased so that answering it does not reveal which position it leads to. Cap the tree at eight branch nodes and aim for eight to twelve leaves; the branch questions are meant to double as a short quiz, so that a reader's path through the tree is their result and no separate scoring key is needed.

The distinctions I want doing the branching work, roughly:

Whether the moral defect is internal to the work — something the work asks of its audience, an attitude it invites or endorses — or merely external, sitting in the maker's life, letters, or bank account. Whether the work depicts something horrible in order to horrify us, as against inviting us to enjoy it. Whether the wrongdoing is a causal ingredient of the object rather than merely its neighbour: footage in which an animal was genuinely hurt, a painting built from a photograph taken without consent, where the work is partly made of the wrong. Whether the work is detachable from the vice, in the sense that a morally decent version of this artist could have produced this very thing — a bigoted novelist whose novels carry no trace of the bigotry, as against a painter whose cruelty is legible in the brushwork. Whether authorship is distributed, so that the vice belongs to one member of a two-hundred-person collaboration, or to a ghostwriter, or to a studio. Whether continued appreciation now benefits the wrongdoer or is reasonably experienced by his victims as a public betrayal. Whether timing matters: the crime committed decades after the work, a deathbed repentance, an accusation surfacing forty years later, a work entirely unremarkable by the moral standards of its own time and offensive only by ours.

The leaves should include: the view that artistic value is wholly insulated from both the work's moral defects and the artist's; the view that insulates artistic value but concedes that the maker's conduct can give us reasons of another kind about whether to engage; the view that a work's moral defects are pro tanto artistic defects and its moral merits pro tanto artistic merits; the variant of that view on which the defect is really structural, consisting in the work's inviting a response that cannot be merited by what the work supplies; the view that some works are artistically better for being immoral, their viciousness part of what makes them succeed; the view that the maker's vice can change what the work means without changing how good it is as art; and, if there is room, the deflationary exit on which artistic value is not a distinct kind of value at all and the whole debate rests on a mistake. Give each leaf a standard label where one exists, but define it by its answers to your questions rather than by the label, and add one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. I do not want authors' names, paper titles, or citations for any of this.

Two things I especially want, beyond the tree itself.

First, the empty cells. My suspicion is that the space your questions open up is larger than the set of views anyone actually holds, and I want those gaps made explicit rather than quietly pruned. In particular please diagnose the cell in which immorality in the maker counts as an artistic defect while immorality in the work does not, and the cell in which moral defects can be artistic merits but never artistic defects — and say for each, and for any other emptiness you find, whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply undefended because nobody has bothered.

Second, honesty about failure. Flag any pair of positions your tree cannot actually separate, where the difference between them is one of mechanism rather than of verdict and no arrangement of the cases you have will pry them apart. Say what further case would be needed, or concede that none is available.

Then, briefly, the question my friend and I were really arguing about. Set out where a verdict on artistic value does and does not settle what to do: whether to go on watching, to quote, to teach, to buy, to stream for royalties, to programme in a retrospective. I want to see which positions that agree completely on the value question still disagree about conduct, and which disagreements about value turn out to make no practical difference at all.

Please keep the whole instrument, sections included, under about twelve hundred words, in a form I can hand out and argue over.
