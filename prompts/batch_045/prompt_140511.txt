I am revising a paper against coherence arguments for expected utility maximization, and I need a sparring partner who will not fold. Please play the following person. You hold that the normativity of expected utility maximization rests on coherence considerations — synchronic sure-loss arguments and diachronic sure-disadvantage arguments — rather than on the intuitive appeal of the axioms or on representation theorems. You grant straight away that representation theorems are purely as-if results and that nobody has ever been converted by reading the axioms off a page. You also grant that a single, isolated violation of independence need not be a blot. What you will never grant is that there is a kind of rationality for agents like us that expected utility maximization fails to capture, or that an ordering which admits no real-valued utility representation can nonetheless be fully reasonable. You are not a caricature: you concede small points quickly and precisely, you keep the two families of coherence argument strictly apart rather than sliding between them, and you will say which of your own arguments is the weaker one if pressed. You may also say that my case is a problem for one argument but not for the other, if that is what you really think — I want the strongest version of your position, not the most convenient one. Reply in a single paragraph, in the first person, with no citations, no reading suggestions, and no request for clarification. I will say stop when we are finished.

Here is my opening move, which you should answer. The central case of the paper is a hospital physician who each year chooses between a certain modest improvement in her patients' outcomes and a gamble that is better in expectation but carries a one-in-a-thousand chance of a patient's death. Her preferences are lexicographic: no finite benefit compensates for any chance of that death, and I claim this is not a bias but her considered judgment. Three things follow. First, lexicographic orderings violate the Archimedean continuity axiom, so no real-valued utility function represents her — she is outside your framework by construction, not by inattention. Second, she is not pumpable in the way that matters: the only route to pumping her is to offer her the same trade again and again, and having settled the matter once she simply declines each time, which costs her nothing she values. Third, the long-run argument reverses on you. Because her utility is real-valued, every finite disutility she assigns to a death is eventually outweighed by a large enough benefit, so an interlocutor who can scale the stakes up will move any expected-utility maximizer to accept; over a career of such escalated gambles the death occurs with probability approaching one, while the lexicographic agent's patients survive. So you must either tell me that our most confident moral priorities are a species of incoherence, or concede that your argument only ever tells an agent which of indefinitely many utility functions to adopt — in which case it constrains nothing I care about. Which horn do you take, and which of my three claims do you reject?
