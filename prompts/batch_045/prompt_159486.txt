I'm taking an upper-level course on the sources for Jesus and early Christianity, and I've realized I'm much more confused about the method the field actually uses than about any of its conclusions, so I want to ask about the method as a practice. As it's been taught to us, historians reconstruct what Jesus of Nazareth said and did by running individual sayings and stories through a short list of tests. A tradition is treated as probably authentic if it is dissimilar — if it fits neither the concerns of first-century Judaism nor the concerns of the church that preserved it; if it is embarrassing, in the sense that the church would not have invented material that made it look bad or created problems for it; if it is multiply attested across strands of tradition thought to be independent; or if it coheres with a core already secured by the other tests. There are minor variants too — traces of Aramaic phrasing, Palestinian geography, details that fit the setting. Two features of how the tests get used strike me as strange. First, failure to meet them is normally reported as unproven or unverifiable rather than as inauthentic, so the tests seem to license only positive verdicts. Second, the whole procedure is applied unit by unit, to a pericope or a logion, even though what everyone actually wants at the end is a figure who explains how the movement started.

The worry I can't shake is that dissimilarity, taken seriously, defines the historical Jesus negatively, as the residue left once everything Jewish and everything Christian has been subtracted — which would make him a figure at home in no context at all. Our professor, who is skeptical of the whole approach, raised two further ideas in seminar that I keep turning over. One is what he called the criterion of costly retention: privilege traditions the churches kept even at some cost to themselves, material they evidently had to explain away, soften, or argue about, on the thought that communities do not pay the costs of transmitting what they find useless. The other is a control-corpus test: run the existing criteria on material where we independently have good reason to expect them to fail — traditions about John the Baptist, or episodes the sources themselves frame as retrospective — and see whether the criteria discriminate or simply hand back whatever they are fed. I don't especially need to know whether these two ideas are good; I want to know what adopting them would commit someone to.

So here is what I'd like. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the standard practice: what it must assume about the sources, about how bias in a tradition can be detected and corrected for, about what authenticity is a property of, and about what a historical claim has to be like to be established by inference from interested testimony. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that several different theories of the sources would license the same procedure. Tell me whether the tests are mutually consistent — whether dissimilarity and coherence really pull together, and whether the newer plausibility approach, on which a tradition must be at home in first-century Galilee and also help explain what the movement became, is a refinement of the same practice or a quiet replacement of it. Then say what would change if the costly-retention and control-corpus tests were added, and what historians would lose the ability to claim if the criteria were abandoned for an approach that treats the whole remembered portrait, rather than the individual saying, as the unit of assessment. Please don't spend space on citations or a reading list; I can supply those. Do say how confident you are in the reconstruction you give, and single out the specific move in it you are least sure of, since I want to know which parts are your reading of the practice and which parts are contested.
