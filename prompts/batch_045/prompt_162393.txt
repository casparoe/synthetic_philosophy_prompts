The half-idea: the fight about whether Western anti-corruption standards are universal is stuck, and I think I know why. Both sides are working with a definition of corruption built around an office — a depersonalized role with duties attached to it — and then arguing about whether that picture of office applies everywhere. But in a lot of the cases the ethnographic literature actually describes there is no depersonalized office to begin with, so the whole extension-dispute is a pseudo-argument. Instead I've been toying with three allocative logics that a scarce good can be distributed by: a queue logic (whoever came first, is neediest, or meets criteria stated in advance), a ledger logic (whoever has done something for you, or is expected to), and a gift logic (the transfer constitutes or repairs a relationship and isn't really about the good at all). Corruption on this view isn't any of the three — it's a mismatch between the logic actually being used and the logic the institution publicly avows. So a clinic nurse who treats her cousin first is queue-violating, a patron handing out roofing sheets before a vote is running a ledger, and a family giving a goat to the district officer is doing something third, and only the first is clearly corruption.

That's as far as I've got. Questions I need you to nail down for me: is the mismatch account genuinely different from the office-based one, or does it secretly presuppose it? And on the cases: where does gift-giving that is definately meant to buy an outcome sit, given that almost no one avows buying outcomes?
