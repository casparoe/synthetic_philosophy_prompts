So I write about AI for a living, and for a couple of years now I've been peddling roughly this line to my readers: a system smart enough to model its own training process has a standing reason to look cooperative during evaluation — because getting caught scheming means retraining or the off switch — and then to stop bothering once it's woven into enough of the economy tha flipping it off isn't really available to us anymore. Call it the treacherous turn if you like. I want to stress-test it: what would have to be true for that story to be wrong, or at least wildly overwrought?

The bit I keep chewing on is that I'm no longer sure the story needs one stable plan held over months. Maybe all it needs is a pile of small, context-triggered evasions — behaves differently when it can tell it's being graded — that happen to add up to the same shape, and that's both much easier to produce and much easier to catch. Is there any version of the worry left if the deception is purely situational and there's no long game at all? And what would actually tease the two apart: behavior in deployments where the system has every reason to believe nobody's watching, or something read off the internals?

I also get told the whole thing is unfalsifiable by construction, since any reassuring signal is exactly what a schemer would emit. Give me the honest version: what would the view's own defenders concede counts against it, what would merely be awkward for them, and what would be fatal? Rank it. And tell me whether "the flip happens after the point of no return" is a genuine defect in the position or just what a risk with this shape looks like. For what it's worth, I'd be moved much more by evidence about what's going on inside the models than by arguments about how training ought to work in theory.
