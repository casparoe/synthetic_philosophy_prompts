So I've basically swallowed the standard rationalist package on induction, and I want someone to actually try to break it. The package as I hold it: (1) the no-free-lunch results say that averaged over all possible environments every learner does exactly as well as every other one, so there's no bias-free learner and no such thing as general-purpose "learning ability"; (2) Solomonoff-style induction only wins because it smuggles in a simplicity prior, and its nice optimality bound — total expected error bounded by a constant times the complexity of the true environment — is only nice relative to that prior; (3) so any argument that your prior is the right prior is circular, you use your prior to evalutate the argument, and the regress bottoms out either in something arbitrary, or in an anthropic / self-locating move about which worlds you should expect to find yourself in, or in a pragmatic shrug. Call it the "priors bottom out in a shrug" view. I know it's roughly the house position, which is exactly why I want it stress-tested rather than re-explained.

What would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and I want you to be explicit about which ones a defender of the package would themselves concede, not just what a critic yells from outside. Concretely, the directions I care about:

- **Local free lunches.** NFL is normally stated as an average over all functions — a space closed under arbitrary permutations of the input/output table. But the environments we actually face aren't closed under that group: causes precede effects, nearby states resemble each other, the same regularity recurs at different scales. So suppose someone proved something real here, e.g. that over some naturally restricted class of environments (short programs run on a fixed machine, low-degree polynomials, local cellular-automaton rules, whatever) a cheap heuristic like "expect the simplest continuation of what you've seen" has regret bounded independently of the horizon, while over that same class, weighted the way the class naturally comes out, the universal-mixture learner does meaningfully worse. Is that a free lunch in the sense the package denies, or is it just the bias relocating — a prior that happens to match this subclass — and what test would tell the difference?

- **Whether the circularity is special.** Lots of things are circular in the same shape: using logic to justify logic, using your senses to check your senses, using a standard of evidence to pick a standard of evidence. Does the package's claim survive being generalized like that, or does it turn out to be a specific complaint about priors that quietly assumes the thing it's complaining about? And if the defender's answer is "yes, all of those are equally unjustified, that's my point" — is that a strength, or the sound of the view sliding into unfalsifiability? I'd like an honest verdict on how exposed the package really is here.

- **What it predicts that its rivals don't.** My sense is that it predicts we should never find a principled, non-question-begging way to choose among priors, and that the apparent track record of simplicity-biased science is mostly retrospective selection. If that's right, what would a counterexample look like — a Dutch-book- or dominance-style argument that makes a simplicity skeptic incoherent rather than merely differently biased — and is there anything anyone has actually constructed that's even in the neighbourhood, in the abstract, not as a citation?

- **The thing I'd personally find most persuasive:** a worked case where two agents with genuinely different priors are both fully self-consistent by the package's own lights, and one of them obviously loses in a way the package can't redescribe as just "had the wrong prior, which was always on the table." If the package can always absorb failure like that, say so plainly and then tell me whether that's a problem for a view that's making a normative claim about rationality rather than just a descriptive one about learners.

Don't bother with reading recs or a bibliography, I've read the Sequences and I don't need links — I want the arguments, and I want the strongest steelman of the other side you can manage.
