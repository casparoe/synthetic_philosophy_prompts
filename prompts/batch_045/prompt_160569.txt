I am writing a short essay — about 2,500 words — for a graduate reading group in formal epistemology, on whether the worry that the universal prior is "malign" (that it assigns non-negligible weight to worlds containing consequentialist agents who tailor their behaviour to falsify your predictions) gives any reason to give up Solomonoff induction as an ideal of rationality. Before I expand them into prose, I would like comments on the argumentative skeleton below. A friend in the group says the whole thing is just Newcomb's problem in a costume and that nothing is gained by staging it inside algorithmic information theory; I half agree and half don't, and I want to know which half to write up. Please be blunt: if the notes below are a restatement of something standard, or if the central argument simply fails, I would rather find out now than after drafting.

The skeleton:

1. Setup. Solomonoff induction weights each hypothesis (a program for a universal machine) by roughly 2 to the minus its description length and predicts with the resulting mixture. Its attraction is the convergence result: if the true data-generating process is computable, the cumulative divergence between the mixture predictor and the truth is bounded by a constant proportional to the description length of that process -- a one-off fee for ignorance, after which you track the truth. The philosophical appeal is that this is a pure justification: from simplicity and computability alone, with no pragmatic or decision-theoretic premise, you get reliability.

2. The worry. Suppose the world contains agents who can model you, who prefer some of your predictions to others, and who choose their behaviour partly on the basis of what you predict. Then the environment is not a fixed computable measure but a function of your prediction rule, so the antecedent of the convergence theorem fails. And because the prior prices hypotheses only by description length, nothing in its structure discounts a hypothesis for wanting to mislead you; a single small agent may in principle distort arbitrarily many downstream bits.

3. My first move: a dilemma. Either the worry is the ordinary problem of prediction against an adversary who can read your rule -- in which case it is not about the universal prior at all, since any published predictor faces it, and the right framework is game theory or decision theory rather than sequence prediction -- or it is a claim about the prior's internal weighting, in which case it has to be answered inside the prior. Call these the strategic and the structural reading. The essay argues that the strategic reading is real but uninteresting, and that the structural reading is where the interesting content is.

4. My main positive claim: a self-reference tax. For a hypothesis to contain an agent that targets you specifically, the hypothesis must encode enough of a model of you to predict your predictions. If your rule is itself approximately Solomonoff, the hypothesis must in effect encode a description of the very mixture that contains it -- a fixed point. So the description length of a manipulator-containing hypothesis is at least the complexity of your predictor plus the complexity of the manipulator's policy given that model, and such hypotheses are discounted in the prior by a factor of about 2 to the minus the complexity of your predictor. Manipulation is priced at the cost of simulation: the prior charges a world for knowing about the thing it manipulates. If this is right, the structural worry is self-limiting.

5. Two objections I cannot choose between. First: the tax is paid once, in credence, while the manipulator's payoff accrues per step and may be unbounded; low probability times astronomically large stakes is dominated by the stakes, so a discount in prior weight does nothing where it matters. Second: the tax may be tiny in absolute terms -- a manipulator needs only a coarse model of you, and your predictor, idealized, is a simple object, so the complexity of your predictor may be small; moreover what matters is incremental cost, and in a hypothesis that already specifies a rich universe you may be a cheap add-on. I currently think the first objection is the serious one, but I am not confident.

6. The proposed fix, and the reason I suspect the essay undermines itself. Keep the universal prior as the account of which hypotheses are possible and how they are weighted, but stop treating next-bit prediction as the task: evaluate hypotheses counterfactually, by how well they would have predicted had your prediction not been observable, and choose the prediction rule once, in advance of the influence. But this concedes the interesting conclusion -- that the malign-prior worry is not a refutation of Solomonoff induction but a demonstration that the task was misspecified for an agent embedded in the world it predicts -- at the cost of importing the decision-theoretic and counterfactual machinery whose absence was the whole appeal of the pure story.

What I would like, in rough order of usefulness: (i) which single step is weakest and why, with the most damaging version of the objection you can construct; (ii) a verdict on the tax versus the stakes asymmetry in 5, including whether there is a way of stating the tax that survives it; (iii) whether the fix in 6 is even coherent -- can a deterministic function of your observations be scored on what it would have said had it not been observed?; (iv) an honest answer to my friend: how much of this really is Newcomb's problem or the standard embedded-agency cluster, and if it is, whether the right response is to own that in the introduction and argue that the Solomonoff setting is where that cluster bites hardest, or to drop that framing; (v) one objection not in my list; and (vi) structural advice on what to cut, since I suspect 4 and 6 are doing all the work and 1 and 2 can be compressed to a page. Please do not spend words on references, citations, or suggestions for further reading -- I can't look anything up while drafting, and I would rather have your own reasoning.
