Stipulate the view as I teach it: lexical threshhold NU says there's an intensity threshold T such that any episode of suffering at or above T lexically outweighs any finite quantity of suffering below it, and below T suffering just sums as intensity times duration. I want to convince the students that the threshold isn't a technicality that only bites when the numbers go astronomical.

So: build me a case with exactly three options and three theories, one option each —

- lexical threshold NU picks one,
- continuous NU (no threshold, minimise total intensity times duration of suffering) picks a second,
- a suffering-focused leximin over persons (rank options by how much the single worst-off individual suffers, no threshold) picks the third.

Constraints: a real hospital or field-clinic decision; at most twenty patients; every pain in the case something a nurse would recognise, with an unremarkable intensity on a 0–10 scale and a duration in minutes to hours; no probabilities, no one's existence or identity over time at stake, no deaths. And the three options have to be identical in every respect bar their pain profiles — same side effects, same pleasures, same costs, same process, same everything — so the disagreement is purely about how suffering aggregates across intensities, durations and people.

Then: the profiles and the arithmetic for each theory, with a note on which numbers are load-bearing and which you could move about; one sentence on what feature of the case is doing the work; what the verdicts do if I shift T or drop an option; and what someone who likes whichever theory's option looks least attractive here would say to get out of it — plus an honest line on whether this is gerrymandered or whether ordinary triage could actually throw up this shape.
