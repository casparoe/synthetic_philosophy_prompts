I am writing a paper for a collection on fiction and metaethics, and the section I cannot get right is a reading of Cormac McCarthy's *The Road* through evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics: arguments that begin from the claim that our moral judgments are best explained by selective pressures (kin altruism, reciprocal exchange, coalition maintenance) and get their sting from the further claim that those pressures had no need to track mind-independent moral facts. I do not need the secondary literature named for me. I know the realist's replies about the disputed premise, the two horns of the dilemma about coincidence and tracking, and the companions-in-guilt moves; I can supply the apparatus myself. What I am buying is the reading.

The reason I keep returning to this novel is that its world is close to a controlled experiment on the debunker's posit. Strip away the institutions, the abundance, and the third-party enforcement, leave a man and a boy on a road with one bullet between them, and you have roughly the environment in which the debunker says the moral sense was built: a band of two, dependent, surrounded by out-group strangers, with no surplus to share. Ask the question the novel seems to pose: what is left of morality when the world that shaped it is, in miniature, restored?

I want the reading organised around a condition I have been trying out in my own work and want to test against the book. Call it the uptake condition. A debunking argument does its work on a subject who comes to accept its explanation, and yet the standard formulations say almost nothing about what accepting it does to the practice: whether the belief survives its causal story being known, whether it survives with its authority intact or as a naked habit, and whether failing to extinguish it counts against the debunker or against the believer. That is a question about a life over time, which is exactly what a novel has and a journal article does not. The book appears to stage three uptake profiles: the dead wife in the man's memories, who seems to have drawn the conclusion and died of it, insisting that the family's flight is only a slower suicide and that the man's resolve is a relic of a world that no longer exists; the man, who half-draws it, has no argument for his own certainty, and goes on insisting that the two of them are the good guys and carrying the fire; and the boy, who seems immune to it altogether and keeps insisting on obligations to strangers the man has given up on.

So here is what I want settled, in about 1,200 words. First: which version of the debunking argument does the novel's situation actually threaten, and which horn does the fiction load? Is the boy's refusal to abandon the thief they have stripped, or to pass the old man on the road, best read as a datum for the realist (a moral demand that outruns anything the selective story predicts, issuing from a child who has no theory to defend) or as what the debunker should have expected (a moral psychology doing its appointed work on a subject who cannot do otherwise)? Second: is the man's morality, considered across the whole book, actually as parochial as the debunker needs it to be? He kills without hesitation to keep his son and refuses help to a stranger; he also keeps going long after any adaptive point to his going has expired. Say which of these the novel treats as the same thing and which it treats as a difference. Third: apply the uptake condition and tell me whether the novel vindicates it or destroys it. If the wife is the novel's only full uptake case and she dies, does the book mean to say that the debunking story is fatal in practice even if it is answerable in theory, or that her death indicts her inference rather than the world? And if the boy's immunity is just insensitivity to evidence, is the novel quietly confirming the debunker by making the moral saint the character with the least capacity to reason?

Two constraints, both of which matter to me. I want you to say where the novel resists the lens and the honest critic has to concede it: the closing movement is written in a theological register, the woman at the end talks about goodness finding such children, and the last passage is about a divine breath passing from man to man, which may mean the book is not running a secular metaethical experiment at all but something closer to a theistic one — in which case my whole framing is a category mistake and you should tell me so, and tell me what reading survives if I drop it. And I want you to end by saying what the novel adds to the debate that the exchange between debunker and realist does not: my own suspicion is that debunking arguments are always assessed one belief at a time, at the moment of assessment, whereas the book asks what a moral life looks like when the explanation is not a premise in an argument but a fact one has been living with for years, and when the believer's own death is on the schedule. But that is my suspicion, not my conclusion, and I would rather you argued with it than agreed with it.

Assume I know the plot; do not summarise it, and I do not need exact wording for anything. I would rather you built the reading on the scenes I have named plus any others you are confident about, and flagged any detail you are unsure of in a parenthesis rather than resting weight on it.
