Hey, I need something runnable for my intro ethics seminar next week: 18 students, one 75-minute session, and this part has no prep reading behind it. The topic is the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. I want them to feel why adding up gains and losses across different people can seem perfectly fine in one setting and wrong in another, and I want them to run into that themselves rather than hear me announce it. So: no naming the objection, or anything like it, until the debrief.

My half-formed idea, use it or bin it: play the same aggregation decision three times with the arithmetic identical each time. Round one, four students, each holding a private card with their own payoff under policy A and policy B, where A has the bigger total but leaves one person badly down; the group has to settle on a policy together and the person who comes out worst has to say out loud whether they accept it. Round two, one student alone with four cards labelled as her Monday self, Tuesday self and so on, same numbers, same total. Round three, back to four students, but now they choose the decision rule before anyone opens a card.

The part I actually care about, and that I have never seen staged: I want the rules to seperate two things students slide between constantly — that welfare is additive across persons, and that one person's gain can compensate another's loss. So somewhere in there I want a version where the numbers sum beautifully but the benefit is the kind only the winner can consume and literally cannot be handed to the loser, sitting right next to a version where transfer is possible. Different payoff schedules per round is fine. Points only, no money.

Then a debrief that maps each moment onto a piece of the objection, including the bit I always explain badly: why the complaint in round one is not just that someone lost, but that they were traded away, and why round three might quiet the complaint even though the arithmetic never moved. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I expect at least one student to say round two is just as bad, or just as fine, for reasons I haven't thought through), and be straight with me about what the game cannot show and I will have to say in so many words myself. Write it as continuous prose please, like an email — no headings, bullets, bold or tables.
