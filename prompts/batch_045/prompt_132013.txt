I'm in the second year of a philosophy degree, taking a module on language and action — we have spent most of the term on Austin: the constative/performative distinction, the felicity conditions and the misfire/abuse split, the illocutionary/perlocutionary distinction, and then Searle's taxonomy with declarations as the awkward corner of it. For my assessed essay I want to work a real episode through that apparatus, and I have picked the press conference held in East Berlin on the evening of 9 November 1989, at which Günter Schabowski — then the party's spokesman, and, as I understand it, a member of the Politbüro — read out a text containing new travel regulations and, asked when they took effect, answered that they applied immediately, without delay. As I understand the surrounding facts (do correct me if I have any of them wrong): the text had been handed to him by Krenz shortly beforehand; it was meant to be embargoed until the following morning; Schabowski had not been present when it was gone through earlier in the day; West German radio and television carried the news within the hour; crowds gathered at the crossings; the guards at one of them, receiving no orders to that effect, let people through that same night; and within a year both the Wall and the state that built it were gone.

What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions rather than an impressionistic one. First, the theory onto the case: what illocutionary act (or acts) should we say Schabowski performed — an assertion about a regulation due to come into force, a declaration that it was in force, something else — and where exactly does the utterance sit on Austin's conditions? There was a procedure for bringing such a regulation into force and Schabowski was not, it seems, the person appointed to execute it, nor did he intend to do so, nor did he execute it correctly as to timing; so it looks like a misfire, and a misfire is supposed to render the act void. Yet it was not void in its effects. I would like you to work through the candidate verdicts — misfire, abuse, no illocutionary act at all but a bare assertion with spectacular perlocutionary consequences — and say which fits and what each costs. I am especially interested in uptake: Austin wanted only uptake in the thin sense of being understood, but here the uptake by journalists, then by guard commanders, then by the crowds, plus the physical lifting of barriers, looks less like a consequence of a successful act than like the thing that made it one.

Second, the case back onto the theory. Set against that, I have been toying with a label of my own, "cascading declaration": an utterance whose status as a successful declaration is not settled at the moment of utterance but fixed, retrospectively, by the chain of uptake it sets off, so that an act which is void by the book turns out to have been valid because of what came after. Is that a genuine gap in the apparatus, or does the constitutive-rules machinery (the "X counts as Y in C" formula) predict all of this so easily that my "problem" evaporates — and if so, is that itself the interesting result, because it means declarations have no felicity conditions independent of collective acceptance? Does my label collapse into something already catalogued under another name? I would also like the counterfactual faced: if nobody had gone to the border that night, would this have been a void misfire that history forgot, and can a theory in which illocutionary success is settled at the time of the utterance tolerate an answer of yes? And what should we say about the broadcasters who reported that the border was open — does reporting a declaration inherit its force, or start a new one?

Please be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than pressing it to speak, and be explicit about your own commitments: say which claims you would defend, without hedging, in front of a seminar leader who disagrees with you, which you would qualify, and which rest on a contested reading of Austin or Searle. And if my framing is confused — if "cascading declaration" is incoherent, or the episode cannot bear the weight I am putting on it, or the whole exercise is a case of a student seeing theory where there is only politics — say so plainly instead of writing politely around it.
