I am in the second year of a PhD and run a small fortnightly reading group on vagueness with two other graduates; one of them is convinced the whole topic is really a dispute about logic, the other that it is really a dispute about the psychology of concept application, and I am not convinced either of them has earned that conviction. We have spent most of a term on the stand-off between the view that vagueness is a species of ignorance — there is a perfectly sharp boundary somewhere in the penumbra, fixed by whatever the facts about our usage fix, and we are simply barred from knowing where it lies — and the supervaluationist view, on which a borderline application of a vague predicate is neither true nor false, while whole sentences involving it can still be true in virtue of coming out true under every admissible way of sharpening the predicate, with a determinacy operator layered on top so that we can talk about that status from within the language. Both sides have their thoroughly rehearsed objections — the mystery of an unknowable boundary and the apparent absurdity of our being blind to the semantics of our own words, on one side; the departure from classical logic, the odd behaviour of the determinacy operator, and the difficulties with higher-order vagueness, on the other — and every session ends with us re-running the same three exchanges with nobody's credences perceptibly changed. I would like to stop re-running them and get a diagnosis of why they do not move.

My own suspicion, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that a large part of the trouble is that the debate is conducted almost entirely on one-dimensional toy cases: heights, baldness, numbers of grains in a heap. For those cases there may be something close to a translation manual between the two theories, so that no pattern of tolerant judgements, no sorites intuition, no phenomenon of hedging, and no fact about what speakers will assert in the penumbra could possibly discriminate between them; the apparent clash would then be a notational one, which becomes a genuine disagreement only once the predicates are multi-dimensional and the admissible sharpenings are not linearly ordered — whether someone counts as intelligent, whether a settlement counts as a city, whether a country counts as democratic, whether a move counts as a good one. If that is right, the dispute is partly a dispute about which cases are allowed to count as data, and the remedy is to relocate the argument to cases where the theories are forced to diverge. But I have a weaker and competing suspicion: that the disagreement is not really about vagueness at all but about what a semantic theory is supposed to deliver — truth conditions, or an account of what speakers are in a position to assert and to know — in which case each side may be answering its own question correctly and the impasse is largely verbal.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey of the two positions: tell me which of my hypotheses, or what third thing, best explains the stalemate, and argue for it from what each side actually commits itself to rather than characterising both from a safe distance. If the first hypothesis is on to something, say concretely what a multi-dimensional test case would have to look like, what each theory would be committed to saying about it, and whether a real divergence there is available at all or whether the translation can simply be extended to swallow those cases too. If the second is closer to the truth, say what would have to be settled about the aims of semantics before the substantive question could even be posed, and how one would go about settling it. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, some kind of work on speakers' actual judgements, a change of question, an abandonment of the question — or, if you think no progress should be expected, why not. Please write in British English; refer to views and arguments by description rather than by author, title or year, since I will add the references myself; keep it to roughly 1,200 words; attach to each substantial claim some indication of how confident you are in it; and single out the one claim you are least confident of, saying what would settle it.
