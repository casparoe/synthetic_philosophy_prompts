My friend Priya did her undergrad in history and philosophy of science and now keeps, I'm not joking, a spreadsheet where she rules on whether the theoretical posits of dead scientific theories were real — like, whether the term actually picked something out in the world. We argue about it whenever we get coffe. Her verdicts so far, as best I can reconstruct them: caloric fluid, no; phlogiston, no; the luminiferous ether, no — and she sticks with no even when I point out that later field theory keeps around something that carries energy and supports waves; Dalton's atoms, yes, even though he had them indivisible and surrounded by weird repulsive atmospheres, basically every concrete detail wrong; Mendelian "factors" and the gene as people used the word around 1900, yes, and she also says yes to "gene" now even though it's got fuzzy edges and doesn't name any single kind of molecular thing; Pauli's neutrino, yes, at a time when the person who posited it thought it might never be detectible; the intra-Mercurial planet Vulcan, no; electrons, yes, but Bohr–Sommerfeld orbits, no; Wegener's driving mechanism for continental drift, no, even though the continents obviously did move.

The case we're stuck on is one I made up, though it's closely modelled on a real situation (tell me if the mapping does any work or if I've just rebuilt the real case with the serial numbers filed off). Sometime in the early nineties cosmologists posit a cold, collisionless, essentially pressureless relic particle — call it the C-particle — to explain galactic rotation curves, cluster dynamics, and the peak structure of the microwave background. The model containing it predicts, ahead of the data, the height of the third acoustic peak and the shape of the large-scale galaxy correlation function, and both come out right. Thirty years later nobody has ever detected a C-particle in a lab and every direct search has come up null. Meanwhile a rival programme — a modification of gravity with an extra vector field — now reproduces every one of those same observations, and in the regime that's actually been observed the extra field has the same stress-energy and the same perturbation spectrum that a C-particle fluid would have; the two programmes differ only for systems nobody can measure yet.

So: what does Priya say about the C-particle, and what rule is she actually running? My hunch is that she has a soft spot for the interventionist line — you only really believe in a thing once you can build a machine that manipulates it — but the neutrino verdict is hard to square with that, and the ether verdict is hard to square with anything. I want the rules that fit her whole track record about equally well, what each of them predicts about the C-particle, which of her past verdicts is doing the most work in narrowing things down, a best guess with reasons, and the one further verdict — historical or invented, your pick — that I should ask her for to tell the surviving rules apart. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, say so and tell me what she'd be committing to with each answer. Don't bother with citations or names of papers; I just want the reasoning, and I don't care whether she's right about any of this.
