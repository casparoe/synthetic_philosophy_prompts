I sat in on a departmental metaphysics reading group this week where two of my colleagues, Yusuf and Clara, worked themselves into a dispute about the identity of indiscernibles that neither of them resolved. I would like you to adjudicate the argument they actually made rather than to survey what has been said about the topic.

The case they were arguing about is a variant of the classic example of two qualitatively identical iron spheres alone in an otherwise empty space. Their variant has three such spheres: qualitatively identical in every intrinsic respect, arranged collinearly and equally spaced in an otherwise empty Euclidean space — call them A, B, and C, with AB = BC = one mile, and hence AC = two miles. Partway through they added a fourth object to the scenario: a small pebble, unlike the spheres in shape and composition and therefore the only nonspherical thing in the universe, placed on the same straight line one mile beyond A, so that its distances to A, B, and C are one, two, and three miles respectively.

Their exchange, as closely as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Yusuf opened with the three-sphere line itself. The outer two spheres, he said, are indiscernible: each is one mile from the middle sphere and two miles from some sphere or other, and every purely qualitative description true of one is true of the other. Yet there are three spheres, not two. So the example refutes the identity of indiscernibles as a metaphysical principle, and the only version that survives is the trivial strong one that says distinct things differ in their identity-involving properties — which is just the necessity of identity restated as a thesis about properties.

(2) Clara replied that the collinear arrangement cuts against Yusuf rather than for him, because it grades. The middle sphere is discerned by a purely qualitative, purely relational description: it is the only sphere that is one mile from two spheres. So the relational reply to the two-sphere case succeeds here for B. It fails only for A and C, and it fails precisely because the arrangement is symmetric under the interchange of A and C. That, she said, is a virtue of the relational reply rather than a defect: it discerns exactly what can be discerned and declines to discern what the arrangement's own symmetries identify. The identity of indiscernibles is therefore not flatly false; it is false only of objects that some symmetry of the world permutes.

(3) Yusuf answered that the grading is fatal rather than mitigating. Consider, he said, what Clara's view implies about the pebble variant. Place the pebble on the line beyond A, at distances of one, two, and three miles from A, B, and C. Now A is the sphere one mile from the pebble, C is the sphere three miles from the pebble, and the principle is satisfied for all three spheres. So whether A and C are discernible — and, if the identity of indiscernibles is a genuine metaphysical principle, whether they are two things rather than one — depends on whether a qualitatively unlike object happens to be sitting a few miles away in an otherwise irrelevant region of space. That, he said, is absurd: distinctness cannot be that extrinsic and that contingent. Either the principle is false, or it was never a claim about what exists at all — it is a claim about describability, and Clara has confused an epistemic matter, whether a description can pick the two apart, with a metaphysical one, whether there are two of them.

(4) Clara responded that the pebble objection conflates two things she had been careful to keep separate. Being one mile from the pebble is a perfectly genuine relational property, and the principle has always been a thesis about all properties, relational ones included, so there is nothing merely epistemic about it. Nor is it absurd that extrinsic relations should matter here, because the pebble does not create or annihilate a sphere: whether there are three spheres is not what is in question. What the pebble does is make a difference that was already there available to a description. Properly read, she said, the identity of indiscernibles tells us what qualitative properties there are; it does not tell us what exists.

(5) Yusuf objected that this purchases the truth of the principle by emptying it. If the principle is only a thesis about what properties there are, then it is perfectly compatible with there being two qualitatively indiscernible spheres, which was the counterexample in the first place. Worse, on Clara's reading it becomes untestable: for any pair of apparently indiscernible things, one can always posit some unmentioned qualitative difference elsewhere in the world and say that the pair is discerned after all. A principle that no possible arrangement could refute is not a principle anyone has reason to accept.

(6) Clara's final move conceded more than I expected. She granted that A and C cannot be discerned by any purely qualitative description, so that the qualitative version of the principle is false of them. But she said the lesson is not that the principle is false; it is that we should not believe in primitive identities for them. The symmetry of the arrangement shows that there is no determinate fact of the matter which outer sphere is which, even though there are two of them: the world contains the two spheres without containing anything that singles either one out. Yusuf said that this is simply changing the subject, since he had never claimed there were primitive identities — only that there are two things where the principle says there cannot be two.

I could not referee it, and I do not want to signal which way I lean. Please go through the six moves and, for each, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, identifying exactly which claim in it survives and which does not — the deliverable is a point-by-point verdict, not a winner. Then say what you take both of them to be missing, and whether either of them has in fact changed the subject. I would also like you to address, in your own terms, three questions the exchange left unresolved: whether discernibility is being used in one sense or in several; whether a world's having a symmetry that interchanges two objects is the same question as those objects being indiscernible; and whether the pebble variant shows that the identity of indiscernibles is sensitive to what occupies distant regions, or only that our descriptions are.
