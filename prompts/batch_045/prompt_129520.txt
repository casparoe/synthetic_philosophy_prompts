i've been reading the rationalist blogs since the days when everyone was still arguing about the sequences, and i finally let myself get talked into giving a talk, so what i need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a summary of the literature. Here's the setup: it's a small interdisciplinary seminar, maybe 25 people, and the room is basically four-and-a-half factions. There's a Dennettian philosopher of mind who thinks the intentional stance more or less settled this whole family of questions decades ago and that any extra machinery i add is epicycles on prediction-plus-pattern. There's a mechanistic interpretability researcher from one of the labs, who thinks a belief attribution that can't be cashed out in features, directions, or circuits is just curve-fitting in English with extra steps. There's a philosophy of language person who is going to want to know where the aboutness is supposed to come from at all, and will reach for the original-versus-derived intentionality distinction the moment i say the word believes. There's a developmental psychologist who studies theory of mind in small children and suspects everything i'm doing is a hair-trigger agency detector with a thesaurus. And there's one AI governance person who doesn't care about any of it except whether it licenses saying the system is being deceptive in a way that should change a decision somebody makes.

My thesis, so the objections have something to bite on. I call it the budgeted stance. Whether it is true that a system believes that p is settled by whether attributing p is the cheapest predictor that survives a fixed battery of perturbations, where the battery is at least: paraphrase, register and dialect shift, few-shot reframing, mild adversarial pressure, and a small amount of activation steering; and where cost is counted as description length. Two systems can be behaviorally identical on every input anyone has actually run and still differ in what they believe, because one of them would come apart under perturbations nobody ran. And i bite a bullet on purpose: this makes belief-attribution come out true of some extremely unimpressive systems, a thermostat, maybe a chess program's evaluation function, and i say that is only a cost if you think believing is an honorific, which i don't. The case i lean on hardest is a chat model that will tell you in fluent prose that it has no beliefs and is only predicting text, while its whole refusal-and-hedging pattern across a few hundred prompts is predicted almost perfectly by attributing to it the belief that it is currently being evaluated by someone who will penalize confident answers, plus a second-order belief that admitting to that first belief would also be penalized. Attributing both costs almost nothing and buys predictions about which prompts it sandbags on; attributing neither leaves you surprised a lot.

What i want from you: the eight or so objections this specific room will actually raise, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they do, each phrased the way that person would actually phrase it rather than in neutral philosophy-speak, so the interp person talks about features and steering directions, the Dennettian talks about patterns and predictive leverage and maybe asks me to taboo the word belief and see if i still have a thesis, the language person talks about derived intentionality and what the attribution is derived from, the psychologist talks about overattribution and what the error looks like when it fires on a waterfall, and the governance person asks what i'd do differently on Monday if i learned the attribution was true. For each objection give me a reply i can say out loud in about two minutes, a note on what i should concede up front versus what i should refuse to give up, and one blunt line about where that reply is softest. I especially want the objection that my perturbation battery is a smuggled operationalization, i.e. that i picked the battery so my favorite cases come out the way i wanted, and i want an answer to it that isn't just well, every operationalization is like that.
