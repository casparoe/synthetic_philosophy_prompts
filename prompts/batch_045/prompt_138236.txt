I work on a small team that maintains an internal forecasting tool: analysts enter claims, we attach numbers to them, and we score them later against what actually happened. We recently added conditional entries — things of the form if the chip shipment slips past Q3, then the launch moves to next year — and a design argument has stalled on a piece of philosophy. One colleague keeps repeating that the probability of a conditional just is the conditional probability, and he reads that single sentence as settling three separate questions at once: that the number we should store for such an entry is P(Y|X) and nothing else; that our scoring rule should resolve these entries only on the days on which the antecedent obtains, so that days on which X never comes up contribute neither reward nor penalty; and that, because there is no proposition here in the sense a truth-conditional semantics would want, there is no fact of the matter about whether an unresolved if-then entry was ever true, so our dashboard should stop trying to fold these numbers into the same aggregate as our ordinary probabilities for unconditional claims. I have read a short encyclopedia summary of Adams's suppositional view and of Lewis's triviality results, so you may assume I know what the result says at roughly one paragraph's length. What I cannot tell is which reading of the slogan the result actually kills, and which readings survive it untouched because they were never claims about the probability of a proposition in the first place.

The three decisions come apart in cases we actually hit. Suppose an analyst is 30% sure the antecedent will obtain, and 70% sure of the consequent conditional on it, and 20% sure of the consequent conditional on its failure. Then the stored number on my colleague's reading is 0.70; but our de-duplication code treats if the shipment slips then the launch moves and if the launch does not move then the shipment did not slip as the same claim, and the conditional probability of the contrapositive works out to about 0.86; while a truth-functional reading, on which the claim is true in every world where the antecedent fails plus the worlds where both hold, gives about 0.91. Three different numbers for what our users take to be one claim, and we have to pick one or admit that the tool is storing something other than a probability of truth. There are related puzzles we keep running into: nested entries of the form if X then if Y then Z, where applying the slogan twice seems to give P(Z|X and Y) but where the entry was logged by someone who was nearly certain of X; disjunctive antecedents of the form if X or Y then Z, where P(Z|X or Y) is a weighted average that changes when the weights change even though nothing about the claim seems to have changed; and the fact that our analysts' if-then entries are sometimes used to decide action (should we book the launch venue) and sometimes merely reported, which makes me suspect the slogan is doing different work in the two contexts.

So here is what I want. Lay out, as a menu rather than a single favoured gloss, the distinct precise theses that this one sentence could be expressing — from the near-trivial to the radical — including at least: a purely terminological proposal about what to call the number P(Y|X); a claim about degrees of belief or about what it is to accept a conditional; a claim about the normative probability of a conditional assertion; a claim about truth conditions or about the absence of them; and any reading on which the sentence is not a thesis about probability at all but about validity, assertability, or the Ramsey test. For each reading, give me a verdict — platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, or not even coherent — and say explicitly whether it supports storing 0.70, whether it supports resolving only on antecedent-obtaining days, whether it supports the no-proposition conclusion my colleague draws, and how it handles the contrapositive, the 0.91, the nested entry, and the disjunctive antecedent. Then tell me which reading Lewis's triviality result actually refutes, which readings it leaves alone, and whether the residue — the part of the slogan that no single reading captures — is a sign that my colleague is conflating two good ideas or a sign that our tool is trying to store something that does not exist. I am comfortable with probability notation, scoring rules and Bayes; I have almost no formal logic beyond one undergraduate course, so please keep the metalinguistic machinery light without blurring the distinctions. And please do not give me readings that differ only in how the sentence is paraphrased: I want differences that show up in what the software should do differently on Monday.
