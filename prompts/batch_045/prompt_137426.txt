hey — I'm in a philosophy of perception seminar this term and we spent like two weeks on Molyneux's question (the guy blind from birth who gets his sight, and is shown the cube and the globe he's already handled, and you ask whether he can tell which is which by sight alone without touching them again). I keep noticing something in the readings and in seminar discussion and I don't have a word for it and its driving me a bit nuts. I'm writing a short paper on it and I need to know whether I can name it or whether I have to define it myself.

The thing is, every way anyone tries to turn that question into something you could actually run ends up leaky in both directions at once, and not by accident. Say he picks out the right one. Fine — but that's also exactly what you'd see if he'd just learned a one-off pairing: this particular look goes with this particular feel, with nothing at all that connects what he sees to the shape he was feeling. From the outside those two produce the same answer. Now say he gets it wrong. That's also exactly what you'd see if he simply can't yet pick a solid object out of everything else that lands in his eye once the bandages come off — which is a completely different failure, about the state of his vision rather than about whether seeing and touching have anything in common. So a yes doesn't get you the thing and a no doesn't get you the absence of the thing.

I thought I could patch it, and then it fell apart the same way one level up. My fix was: don't test him on the same two objects he learned with. Teach him with a cube and a globe, then test him with a pyramid and a cylinder he's felt but never seen; if he manages the new pair, he must have got hold of the general idea rather than a pairing. But someone in my study group pointed out he could still be going on something crude — pointy stuff versus round stuff — and get the new pair right without there being any shape-in-common between the two senses. And the failure case goes leaky again too. Same shape of problem, just moved up a level.

Third place I think I see it: we talked about people who pick up a genuinely new sense through some device, feeling vibrations or hearing clicks that carry information about what's in front of them, and whether they ever really perceive with it or are only decoding it. Success could be decoding, failure could be unfamiliarity. Same structure.

So: does this have a name? And I don't just want a label. My tutor muttered something about underdetermination, but that feels way too broad — like every experiment is underdetermined somehow — and "confound" isn't right either, because a confound is normally something you could in principle strip out with a cleverer design, and I'm not sure this one can be stripped out at all. It might be that the question was secretly more than one question the whole time, and the leakiness is a symptom of that rather than a flaw in the test. If there's no established term I want to define it myself in the paper, so I need: a definition that isn't so wide it covers every bad experiment, how it differs from the things it looks like (underdetermination, confounds, whatever else is nearby), and a couple of hard cases where a test looks like this but actually does settle something. Please don't just hand me a reading list either, I can't get to the library this week anyway. I'd rather have the carving than the name.
