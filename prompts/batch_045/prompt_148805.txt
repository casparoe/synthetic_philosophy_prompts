I'm putting together a seminar on the boundary problem — who has to be in the demos for a given decision — and I need a case that breaks the "everyone whose interests are affected gets a say" principle in a way that isn't just the usual gripe that it balloons the demos out to everyone on earth.

The wrinkle I want is endogenous affectedness: the harm is real if the affected group doesn't get a vote, but if they do get a vote they block the thing, so the harm never happens, so by the principle's own lights they shouldn't be in the demos, so they don't get a vote, so the harm happens. The principle never settles.

Constraints: one paragraph; a realistic body making a realistic decision (water authority, zoning board, transit agency, whatever); the affected group has to sit outside the jurisdiction, so the rival principle — the demos is everyone subject to the state's coercion, its laws and police and courts — excludes them cleanly; and the loop has to fall out of the ordinary machanics of the vote, not from anyone threatening retaliation or from anything exotic.

After the scenario: say what each principle delivers and why, point at the one feature doing the work, tell me what happens if you tweak the case so the harm is the same size whichever way the vote goes, and tell me how someone who likes the affected-interests principle would try to wriggle out. The verdicts have to fall out of the principles rather than be written into the setup.
