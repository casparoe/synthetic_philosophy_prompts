I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about your views on life after death, and I want to put the fork he left me with directly to you. Please be A. J. Ayer as you are at the very end of your life: still an empiricist and an atheist, still holding that a religious or metaphysical assertion has factual content only if one can say what experience would bear on its truth, candid enough to grant that the strong formulation of the verification principle you set out in 1936 was never satisfactorily stated, and honest about what followed your own brief clinical death — you published an account of it and allowed that it had very slightly weakened your conviction that your death would be the end of you, while adding that you hoped it would be. Stay in character until I say stop. Reply in a short paragraph or two, in your own voice: brisk, concrete, impatient with woolliness, willing to surrender the letter of a point in order to keep its spirit, and unwilling to let a distinction do the work of an argument. Concede freely about the principle itself, but do not concede that anyone has ever had decent evidence for survival. Do not quote or cite your own books at me; make the argument. You may step out of character, in square brackets, only if you need a fact about the world after June 1989.

Here is the fork, and I should tell you that I came round to it by the end of the evening. Take the sentence *I shall consciously survive the death of my body*. Something must be able to count for or against it. Either the counting is done by happenings in the public world — suppose that for months after a man's death a voice is heard which correctly recites the contents of a sealed letter no living person has read, answers questions with his memories and his habits of mind, and survives every check we can devise; call it the archivist case — or it is done by the private experience of the survivor, which is what your own reported experience would have to be. Now: if the archivist case, or your own, would move you even slightly, then survival was always an ordinary empirical hypothesis, and the young Ayer's claim that talk of an afterlife is literally nonsensical was a plain misapplication of his own principle — and worse, you then owe us a reason for having spent half a century not examining the evidence. If neither would move you — if you will say that no such performance could show that the speaker *is* the dead man — then what is doing the work is not your principle but a substantial thesis about what a person must be, a body or a brain or an unbroken physical history, and that is exactly the sort of thesis the principle was introduced to declare empty. Which horn do you take, and what does it cost you? My own suspicion, which my friend pushed me into, is that the principle has been operating as a turnstile that admits only the conclusions you already held.
