Two members of my ethics reading group have spent a year arguing about whether there is something defective about holding a moral belief on the basis of someone else's moral testimony. One holds that there is: even when the testifier is better informed, more reliable than you, and shares your background values, taking her word for it that a practice is wrong leaves you with something less than the real thing — a true belief unaccompanied by the kind of grasp you would have had if you had judged for yourself — and acting on it is, in a way that acting on your own judgment is not, less fully yours. The other holds that moral testimony is just testimony, that deferring to it is often the epistemically and practically best thing to do, and that the first view rests on an inflated picture of what first-hand moral judgment actually delivers.

Write the joint statement they should have produced at the end of that year: the document a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them would yield.

It should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — including claims about particular cases, and claims neither of them would have volunteered at the start of the year. Do not let these be vacuous: if a claim is such that no one has ever denied it, leave it out.

2. A table of residual disagreements, each stated precisely enough that each party would accept the wording of its own side, with the charge that each disagreement really turns on a difference about substance rather than about words.

3. A crux section. At minimum it should cover:

- The object of the testimony. Does the pessimist's complaint apply differently to being told that a practice is wrong, to being told what makes it wrong, and to being told how confident one ought to be? And does it apply differently when the testifier has non-moral information you lack than when she has all your information and, apparently, better judgment with it?

- What kind of defect is alleged. Is deferred moral belief supposed to be less justified or less than knowledge, or is the problem supposed to be practical and aretaic — with the action less creditworthy, less autonomous, less expressive of the agent? Both parties must say whether they accept this split and which horn they are on.

- The case that should embarrass the pessimist: someone from a group that has long been on the receiving end of a practice tells you the practice is demeaning, and you cannot see why. Is declining to defer here itself a failing? Both camps must say what their view implies about it, and the pessimist must explain why the view does not license ignoring her.

- A case neither has discussed: a large language model, fine-tuned to issue moral verdicts, that is more reliable than any person you could consult, has no first-hand experience of anything, and produces its explanations by the same machinery that produces its verdicts. Is deferring to it a case of moral testimony at all, and if not, what is it? What does each camp's view imply, and what does each predict — for instance about whether those who defer to such a system handle the next, unanticipated case worse, or hold their verdicts less steadily under social pressure?

- The analogy cases: deferring to a proof you have not checked, and to an instrument or collaborator you could not replace. What, if anything, distinguishes these from moral deference?

For each crux, give the case, argument, or finding both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

4. A closing note in which each side records what would count as evidence against its own view — not merely against the other's — and a verdict on which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal, and why.

Settle the substance from the cases and arguments themselves; do not spend the document attributing views to published authors or arguing about who said what first.
