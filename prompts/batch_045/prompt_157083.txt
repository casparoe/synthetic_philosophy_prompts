My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether program equilibrium actually explains cooperation, and I would rather locate the disagreement than replay it.

Fix the case so we are talking about the same thing: two players face a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma (T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0; no repetition, no third party, no enforcement). Instead of choosing an action, each submits a program written in a fixed Turing-complete language; each program receives the other program's source text as input, must halt on every input, and outputs C or D. Now add the refinement I introduced halfway through the argument, because it seemed to me to decide the matter: a complexity penalty of ε per bit of submitted source, with ε small but finite rather than lexicographic, so that a shorter program producing the same action profile strictly beats a longer one, and a large enough length gap can in principle outweigh a material payoff difference.

The proposition I want a number on: that this construction explains how fully rational, self-interested, causally independent agents could cooperate in a genuine one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, without altering the payoffs, invoking repetition or enforcement, or assuming the agents are copies or twins of one another. I am at about 0.45; my friend is at about 0.1 and says the apparatus merely relocates the problem into the choice of which program to submit.

Give me your own credence, decomposed rather than bare. The terms I would want, at minimum: whether mutual cooperation survives once the complexity penalty is finite rather than lexicographic, given that a very short unconditional defector can free-ride on a longer conditional cooperator, and given that the cooperator may or may not be able to compress enough to defend itself; whether the multiplicity of program equilibria empties the claim of content, since the framework seems to permit rather than select outcomes, in which case say what does the selecting; whether any implementable test of what the opponent's program will do can be reliable at all, since predicting the behaviour of an arbitrary program is undecidable, so that every usable check is fooled by some syntactically different but functionally similar defector; and whether, if the construction only works between exact or provably equivalent programs, it is really the twin case restated and says nothing about independent agents. Assign a rough probability and one line of justification to each, say how they combine, name the term you are least confident in, and tell me which of them is most likely where my 0.45 and my friend's 0.1 come apart.
