Question 3 (25 marks). The regress problem starts like this: it looks like a belief is justified only if the subject has some reason for it, and a reason is itself a belief that also seems to need a reason. The standard way of laying out the options gives you three horns — the chain stops at a belief that gets no support from any other belief, or it loops back on itself, or it never stops. Foundationalism is standardly read as taking the first horn, coherentism as taking the second, and infinitism as taking the third.

Now consider a view that, as far as anyone can tell, nobody in the literature actually defends. Call it Plural-Source Coherentism (PSC):

S's belief B is justified if and only if
(1) B belongs to a body of S's beliefs that is coherent, where coherence is understood holistically — mutual explanatory support, inferential connections, and probabilistic fitting-together, not bare logical consistency;
(2) that body draws on at least two epistemically independent sources, where two sources count as independent just in case the failure of one would not, in normal circumstances, bring about the failure of the other (perception and testimony, say, or memory and inference); and
(3) no single source supplies more than roughly three-quarters of the beliefs in the body.

Assess PSC. In particular: does the source-plurality condition give it resources that an ordinary coherentist lacks against the objection that a fully self-contained system — an integrated delusion, a conspiracy theory, a beautifully consistent fiction — can satisfy the coherentist's condition just as well as a true one can? And does PSC thereby collapse into foundationalism, or does it remain a genuinely distinct option? Say where you think it sits relative to the three standard responses, and why.

And here's the answer (I've left it as submitted, typos and all):

The regress problem begins with the idea that a belief is justified only if you can give a reason for it. If every reason is itself a belief that needs a reason, then either the chain stops somewhere, or it comes back around to where it started, or it goes on forever. Foundationalism takes the first option: the chain stops at basic beliefs, which are justified without support from any other belief — things like perceptual beliefs and simple a priori truths. Coherentism takes the second: a belief is justified in virtue of belonging to a coherent system of beliefs, where coherence basically means the beliefs are consistent with one another. Infinitism takes the third option: it says that justification requires an infinite chain of reasons, which means the subject has to have actually worked through infinitely many steps of reasoning, which no finite creature can do, so infinitism is mostly useful as a foil.

PSC is a form of coherentism. It says a belief is justified when it sits in a coherent system, but adds that the system must be consistent, must draw on at least two independent sources, and no source can contribute more than 75% of the beliefs in it. The point of conditions (2) and (3) is to deal with the isolation objection against ordinary coherentism. If a delusion or a conspiracy theory is built out of a single source — everything comes from one person's imagination, or from a single informant — then it won't meet condition (2), so it won't count as justified. That's a genuine advantage over plain coherentism, which seems committed to saying that an isolated but consistent system is just as justified as any other.

Does PSC collapse into foundationalism? I think it does. Foundationalism is the view that some beliefs are justified without support from other beliefs and everything else depends on those. In PSC the beliefs from the minority source are the ones doing the real justificatory work, since they're what saves the system from being a closed loop, so they function as the base that the rest rests on. And basic beliefs in foundationalism can't be overturned, because nothing sits above them to overturn them; in the same way, once the minority-source beliefs are in place, the other beliefs can't overturn them without breaking condition (3). So PSC is foundationalism with a quota attached, and it inherits the usual worry about how any belief could be justified while getting no support from other beliefs at all.

So where does it sit? Closest to foundationalism, I'd say. It starts from coherentism, but the source conditions push it away from that, and it's clearly better off than infinitism, since it doesn't ask anyone to complete an infinite chain. Probably worth about 18/25.
