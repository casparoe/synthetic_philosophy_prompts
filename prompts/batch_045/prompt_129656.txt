I'm one of two lay members of my state's Forensic Science Advisory Board — I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, which I assume is why I was appointed — and I've been handed the pen on the memorandum the board owes the legislature by 1 October on latent friction ridge examination. I'd like you to draft it.

The matter that prompted the referral: in a 2019 burglary prosecution (our file calls it the Alvarez matter), an examiner entered a friction ridge mark from a window frame as "of no value for comparison." Two weeks later, after a case briefing at which a detective described the suspect's route through the house and a colleague mentioned that he had already made an identification elsewhere on the same scene, the same examiner entered a full identification on that mark and later testified to it. The images themselves never changed. The defense petition asks us to mandate what it calls full contextual isolation: examiners would receive the mark and the known prints stripped of case facts, of any co-worker's conclusion, and of any information about the suspect.

The board has split into two packages.

Package One (isolation): redacted case files, sequential unmasking, no exposure to a colleague's conclusion before an independent comparison, and independence applied to the verification step as well. The state laboratory's director says this is unworkable: redaction costs money per case, adds days to a backlog that is already the subject of a separate complaint, and — this is the part I find genuinely hard to dismiss — some context is signal rather than bias. Knowing the substrate, the direction and pressure of deposition, and the anatomical region of the source arguably makes comparisons more accurate, not merely more biased.

Package Two (auditable decision): examiners keep their case information, but must record the specific features they relied on before stating any conclusion, must work to a pre-registered comparison sequence, and must state on the record what contextual information they were exposed to, so that the weight of an identification can be tested at a pretrial reliability hearing.

What I want the memo to do is make the choice turn on the right question, and I think the right question is this: through which route does contextual information actually operate? Our practice committee sketchily distinguished three. On the appearance route, the information changes how the mark looks to the examiner — the features themselves. On the selection route, appearance is unchanged but the information changes where the examiner looks and which regions get sampled and encoded in the first place. On the reporting route, both appearance and sampling are unchanged and what moves is the threshold for treating a given degree of correspondence as sufficient. These three call for different remedies, and blinding is obviously most urgent for the first, arguably useless for the third, and ambiguous for the second.

The committee also floated a probe I'd like you to take seriously and either refine or reject: re-present examiners with the very same comparison, but with the earlier contextual information explicitly retracted and shown to have been false. The thought is that a deliberative adjustment of threshold should be undone by that instruction, whereas an effect on appearance or on sampling should survive it. Say in the memo whether that inference holds, what a null result would and would not show, and whether the board could actually run something like this in our own laboratory within two quarters — with the caution that our sample of examiners is small and that any in-house study will be criticised as unblinded in its own way.

Please note two constraints. First, the board cannot wait for the philosophical question to be settled; the memo must decide under uncertainty and say explicitly what the asymmetry of error costs implies (a mistaken identification risks a wrongful conviction, a suppressed or missed one risks leaving an offender at large and costs us the public confidence that the whole enterprise runs on). Second, I don't want citations. The board's file contains only summaries of the relevant empirical literature, none of us can chase down primary sources, and the memo has to be defensible on its reasoning alone; don't gesture at studies you can't specify.

Format: a proper memorandum — To, From, Re, Date — with the recommendation stated in the first two sentences, then the mechanism analysis, then the recommendation worked out concretely (what changes, who implements it, by when, what it costs, and what triggers a revisit), and then a closing paragraph that states plainly the strongest thing that can be said for the package you are rejecting, and what evidence would move you to it. Aim for 1,200 to 1,500 words, in a register that could be entered into the public record.
