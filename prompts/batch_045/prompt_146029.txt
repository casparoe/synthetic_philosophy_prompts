I am applying for a three-year doctoral studentship and need a two-page research proposal. The area is prioritarianism and the levelling-down objection, and I have a half-formed idea for an angle on it that I would like turned into something fundable — or, if it does not survive scrutiny, I would rather find that out before I submit.

Here is the background as I understand it. The levelling-down objection is standardly aimed at the view that equality has final value: if equality matters in itself, then there is one respect in which an outcome where nobody receives some benefit is better than one where only the already better-off receive it, which looks absurd. Prioritarianism is usually credited with escaping the objection. If the contribution a person's well-being makes to the value of an outcome is given by a strictly increasing and strictly concave function of her absolute lifetime level, then making her worse off when nobody gains always lowers total value, and withholding a benefit from her never raises it.

My suspicion is that this immunity is not uniform, and I want to test it against two contrasts. The first is subtractive versus pre-emptive: actively worsening someone's position, as opposed to withholding an improvement one might otherwise have given her. The second is actual versus prospective: levelling down in the outcome that is realized, as opposed to levelling down in expectation, or in a branch of a risky prospect that is not realized. The case that got me interested: suppose we can either raise a badly-off person to a lifetime level of 60 for sure, or give her a gamble that takes her to 100 or to 20 with equal chance, with the better-off person at 100 either way. Expected lifetime well-being is 60 under both options, so a prioritarianism that applies its weighting function to expected well-being is indifferent between them; but in the bad branch she ends at 20 with nobody better off than they would have been under the sure policy, and a prioritarianism that weights realized levels strictly prefers the sure policy.

So the questions I want the proposal to sharpen: does prioritarianism's immunity to levelling down hold everywhere except the prospective cells, and if so is what appears in those cells genuinely a version of the levelling-down objection or rather a different objection — a failure of ex post Pareto — borrowing its vocabulary? Relatedly, is the concavity of the priority function doing two jobs at once, expressing both priority and risk aversion about well-being, and does my matrix survive once those are separated? There is a third contrast I am unsure whether to include: whether the person levelled down is herself the bearer of the priority weight, or someone whose worsening changes who counts as worst off.

Please write the proposal with: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer; an honest account of why the standard reply that prioritarianism escapes levelling down does not settle it; the method, term by term over three years, including why this method rather than the obvious alternative of simply axiomatizing variants of prioritarianism and checking their verdicts; the range of possible conclusions and what would decide between them; what a useful deliverable would look like for people who actually have to aggregate benefits across persons under uncertainty; and a genuine failure condition — including the possibility that three of my four cells collapse into triviality, so that the project is really about one case and the matrix is decoration, and the risk that the project is secretly two projects, one about the currency of prioritarian value and one about its treatment of risk. Do not include a bibliography or any literature review by citation; I will supply the references myself.
