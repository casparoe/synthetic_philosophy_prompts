I am reviewing a chapter on Johannes Kepler in a popular history of science, and I want to audit one move the author makes rather than take it on trust. The chapter says that Kepler's astrology stands to Kepler exactly as Newton's alchemy stands to Newton: a private, irrational pursuit that a great scientific mind clung to, sealed off from the work he is actually remembered for, tolerated largely because it earned money, and of no consequence for the science itself. The author then uses that pairing to license several claims: (i) that Kepler's astrology is biographical colour, so that no one needs to read his astrological writings in order to understand the astronomy; (ii) that it is a plain case of a pioneering modern mind failing to slough off a medieval residue; and (iii), in a more sympathetic passage, that occult hobbies generate real science, so we should be slower than we are to dismiss fields currently regarded as fringe.

What makes me suspicious is that, reading around, Kepler's relation to astrology looks less like private dabbling and more like a reform programme pursued in public. In Tertius Interveniens (1610) he positioned himself as a third party between the defenders of astrology and its theological and medical critics, admonishing the critics not to throw the baby out with the bathwater. He argued that the aspects should be grounded in geometry and used to the exclusion of most of the traditional apparatus of the art; he wrote that "The geometry or harmony of aspects is not between the stars in the sky, but is located rather down here on earth in the point that collects all these rays" (in an English translation by Kenneth Negus), and he insisted that the heavens do not act by pushing on us directly, but that the soul brings its works into consonance with the heavenly harmonies. He cast and revised horoscopes, including one for Wallenstein, while complaining about being used as a marketplace entertainer, and he defended a reformed astrology partly on the economic ground that the foolish daughter earns the bread that keeps her mother astronomy alive. He returned to these themes in Harmonice Mundi (1619) — the book in which the third law of planetary motion is stated.

Please lay out explicitly what maps onto what in the Kepler-astrology / Newton-alchemy comparison: what plays the role of the private pursuit, the respectable science, the paying clientele, the mathematical or experimental apparatus, the published record, and the proposed mechanism. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the three claims above from those that are merely decorative. In particular I would like your assessment of: whether the analogy quietly imports a compartmentalised picture of science-versus-occult that fits twentieth-century boundaries better than a seventeenth-century economy in which astronomy's institutional support depended on astrological work; whether one pursuit supplied problems, data and published books to the science while the other supplied at most a vocabulary; whether "reforming a practice by one's own science" and "inheriting a practice wholesale" are being collapsed; and whether the asymmetry in publicity — almanacs, prognostications and a salaried mathematician's duties versus laboratory notebooks — is load-bearing. For each of (i), (ii) and (iii), say whether it survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the work. Finally, if there is a closer analogy for what Kepler was doing, propose it, and tell me if any premise I have attributed to him above is overstated.
