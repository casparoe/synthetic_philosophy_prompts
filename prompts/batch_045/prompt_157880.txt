I've been reading LessWrong and the EA forums since roughly forever, and there's one argument I keep ending up in and never resolving: given a computation that's actually running, which parts of it are the parts that matter morally. Not "is it conscious" in the abstract — the attribution problem. Where the boundary of the system is, and how much weight each bit of it gets. I know the s-risk crowd worries about the industrial-scale version of this; I think the boring, cost-cutting version is the one we'll actually get. Anyway, I'd rather read a story about it than another four-hundred-comment thread, so please write me one.

Setting, change whatever doesn't work for you: a few decades out, a company runs emulated minds as contractors and has a compute bill problem, so it uses a procedure the engineers call sufficiency pruning. For a given em you search for the smallest set of components S such that, if you run only S and pipe its outputs into a frozen cached copy of everything else, the em still produces its entire behavorial repertoire — pain reports, pleading, insistence that it doesn't want to be turned off, all of it. Anything outside S is judged load-bearing only for speed or compression, and gets quantized, merged, or deleted. The result is what the engineers call cheap mode.

Things I want the story to actually bite on rather than gesture at:

- Minimal sufficient subgraphs aren't unique. There's a whole family of them, and they disagree with each other about whether the thing you'd have called the suffering is still in the loop. One keeps the aversive-valence machinery intact and running. An equally small one routes around it and drives the exact same reports off a general-purpose what-would-this-person-say-next predictor that was distilled from the full system. Both pass every behavioral test anyone has. The audit picks whichever is cheaper to serve. Everyone involved is quietly horrified and nobody can articulate what principle the horrifiedness is tracking.

- Double counting, and where the em stops. Some of its states live in a shared lookup table that forty thousand other instances also read from; some live in per-instance cache; some get recomputed identically across three redundant racks for error correction. If you need a number for how much moral weight is on the premises, do you count implementations, distinct causal histories, or something else? They need the number for insurance.

- Copies in general. Batch jobs spin up thousands of instances of the same cheap-mode em, snapshot them mid-task, restore them. Is that thousands of beings each living one moment, or one being living one moment thousands of times? The accounting rule they ended up with is embarrassingly arbitrary, and I want that shown rather than said.

Voice: tell it from inside — the auditor, one of the ems, or whoever wrote the pruning search. I want the failure to be structural and boring rather than evil: no villains, the cost curves just grind on, the audits keep coming back green, and the thing everyone wanted to appeal to — that there's a fact of the matter about which computations count and causal-role tests will find it — just isn't there. No reprieve in the last paragraph, no engineer who secretly knows the uploads were never conscious, and please nobody saying "but what even is consciousness"; I've seen that sentence alot and it has never once done any work for me. Keep it under ~1500 words, mostly prose, bleak-bureaucratic rather than gory.
