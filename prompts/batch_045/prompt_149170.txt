I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, finishing a paper on the user-illusion view of consciousness — the idea, associated with Dennett, that what we call experience is a kind of finished product the cognitive system presents to itself, an interface rather than a report on the machinery that produced it. I want to open the paper with a short piece of fiction rather than another intuition pump, and I'd be grateful for help drafting it.

The setup: a visual artist is fitted with an experimental neural interface, originally implanted for an unrelated clinical reason, that lets her scrub through the intermediate stages of her own visual processing — edge detection, grouping, object classification — the way one scrubs a video timeline. Two stipulated findings from the lab that the story has to respect. First, the finished percept lags the earliest stages by roughly four hundred milliseconds. Second, any change the system makes to the pipeline earlier than about a tenth of a second before the percept completes is absorbed seamlessly — she reports the result simply as how things looked — while changes made after that point are experienced as intrusions or glitches. Call it the authorship window.

The philosophical work the story has to do is the argument her collaborator makes: if the only access anyone has to her experience is what she says, and her reports track the finished product, then there is no gap left for the product to be an illusion of — the revisionist conclusion is that the interface just is what it is like for her. She is not convinced by this, and the story should leave her unconvinced rather than settle the matter.

Please write it as exactly five numbered scenes, in order, with no introduction and no afterword. Each scene should be a single paragraph of dramatized prose, roughly 80–120 words, dialogue welcome:

1. The calibration session: she scrubs for the first time and finds the intermediate stages alien and, in a way she cannot name, not hers.
2. She discovers the window by accident, before anyone explains it to her.
3. She uses it deliberately on something that carries real weight for her — a room she cannot bring herself to enter, a face she flinches from — and then reports the altered result as how it always was.
4. The argument with the collaborator, in which the revisionist point is made as strongly as it can honestly be made.
5. A closing scene in which she either has the window widened or declines to, and does something that shows the question was about authorship rather than about accuracy.

One constraint: no character may use the words consciousness, qualia, illusion, or interface, and no one should name a philosopher or a theory. The disagreement has to be carried entirely by what happens and by what the characters actually say to each other. It should read as fiction to someone who knows none of the literature, and strike a specialist as getting the dialectic right.
