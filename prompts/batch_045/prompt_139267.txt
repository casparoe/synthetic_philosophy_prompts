Been going back and forth with a friend about Good's theorem — the result that for a Bayesian expected-utility maximiser facing a fixed menu of acts, free information has non-negative expected value *before* you look, so "you can never lose by finding out" is meant to be a theorem and not a slogan. She's at about 0.02 that a real adult human, in a real decision, ever faces free information with genuinely negative value. I'm around 0.35 and defintely can't defend the number. So give me yours, decomposed.

The headline question, sharpened: pick a randomly chosen decision by an adult human today where some costless, relevant piece of information is available — a test result, a review, a spoiler, a background check, a paternity thing, whatever. What's your credence that the expected value of acquiring it, from the standpoint of the person *before* they get it, counting everything they care about, and granting that the information itself is free to acquire, is actually negative? Not "uncomfortable", not "costly in a way I've smuggled back in under the word free" — negative in the decision-theoretic sense. If you think that's still ill-posed, say which sharpening you're using and answer that instead, but I do want a number.

I want sub-questions with numbers on each, not a vibe. Here are the ones I think are load-bearing; add or drop as you like:

- Menu expansion, or what I've started calling the eligibility trap: learning you carry variant X also makes you eligible for — and socially funnelled toward — a treatment or trial you then end up taking, and taking it is worse for you than anything on your old menu. I invented this case, but it looks to me like the standard proof just breaks, because the proof holds the option set fixed while the information is what changes it. How often does learning literally *add* options, and how often are the added ones traps?
- Observability: someone else sees that you looked, or what you found — insurer, employer, family — and updates, and you can't credibly not-know. How much of the theorem's reassurance is really just the single-agent framing doing all the work?
- Intrapersonal commitment failure: you can't bind future-you, and the information makes some tempting act salient, or future-you simply wants different things.
- Preference change: the information changes what you care about, and you can foresee that at the time you decide to look.
- Non-expected-utility agents: ambiguity-averse / worst-case-ish agents can be information-averse in a way EU agents can't be. What share of real behaviour do you think is better described that way?
- Un-thinkability: you plan to ignore the information and can't — you lose the option of acting as if you didn't know. Spoilers, the letter you shouldn't open.
- Bookkeeping: how much of the apparent negative value is really processing cost, attention, or anxiety that I've cheated by ruling out in the setup?

For each: a rough number for how often it's live in a real case, and how often, when it is live, it actually flips the sign. Then combine them somehow, and tell me which term your headline number is most sensitive to and which one you're least confident in.

Then the part I actually care about: split your number into two buckets. Bucket A: cases where the *spirit* of the theorem is false for real agents, i.e. you really can lose by looking. Bucket B: cases that are only "counterexamples" because the theorem was always about a different quantity — the value of a signal inside a fixed decision problem — so nothing has been shown except scope. My worry is that most of my 0.35 is B and I should come down toward her. Tell me if you agree, and where you think the disagreement between a 0.35 person and a 0.02 person most likely lives.

Finally: what would change your mind, and where is your own reasoning most likely to be broken? Give me something that would move you by 0.1 or more in either direction. (Mine, I suspect, is that I'm quietly counting "I regret knowing this" as negative expected value, which it maybe isn't.)

No citations, please — I can't check them and I don't care who proved what. If there's a result you want to lean on, just say what it says and how strong you take it to be.
