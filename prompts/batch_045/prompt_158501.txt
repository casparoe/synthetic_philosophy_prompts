I'm on the quality committee at my hospital (mid-size community place, ~300 beds), and I somehow ended up on the subcommittee rewriting our safety-event taxonomy. We vote next Tuesday and I promised I'd circulate something Monday night, so: draft me the memo.

Here's the case that started this. Patient, 29, six days postpartum, came to the ED twice in three days. Both times she described the worst pressure in her head and said, twice, that something was wrong in a way she couldn't make more precise than that. Both times she got a workup that came back clean, fluids, discharge. Both charts say things like "patient appears anxious, affect distressed, reports stress of new parenthood." Third visit: cortical vein thrombosis and a seizure in the waiting area. The case review came back "no systems issue, clinical judgment appropriate, documentation could be improved." The family read that and lost their minds, and honestly so did one of our nurses.

The proposal on the table, from our one patient-family advisor and the health equity lead: add a new event category, roughly "patient's report discounted or not taken up," kept distinct from our existing communication-failure and delayed-diagnosis codes. Two of the internists on the committee think this is a grievance box with a philosophy name on it, that it'll become a litigation magnet and a stick to beat residents with. Risk management is worried about the same thing but won't say so in the meeting. I'm in the middle and I'm the one who's probably going to break the tie, so I need to actually think it through.

(I read some of the epistemic-injustice literature in grad school years ago — testimonial vs. hermeneutical, credibility deficits — don't explain it back to me, just use it if it earns its keep.)

Things I keep going in circles on, and that I want the memo to actually deal with:

- The chart conversion problem. What she said and what ended up in the record aren't just different in tone, they're different claims: "something is wrong in my head" got entered as "she is anxious." So when we review the case months later, we're reviewing the bleached version, and we literally cannot reconstruct whether the credibility judgment was reasonable. Ive started calling this evidential bleaching. My sense is this is the strongest argument for doing something — but it might equally be an argument for fixing our documentation templates and leaving the taxonomy alone. Tell me which.

- Hindsight. If the new code only ever gets filled out once we know the outcome was bad, then "wasn't believed" is just a roundabout way of writing "bad thing happened," and we all know outcome-based review is close to worthless. Is there any trigger for this that a reviewer could apply before knowing how the case turned out? If not, do we accept the contamination and correct for it somehow?

- Whose call it is. Does the patient flag it, the clinician self-flag, or does a reviewer infer it from the chart? Our advisor wants a patient-facing route. Risk management says a patient-facing "I wasn't beleived" button is a discovery goldmine, and they're not wrong. I don't know how to weigh that.

- And the objection I think deserves the most space even if it loses: taking patients more seriously isn't free. More imaging, more admits, more iatrogenic harm, and residents who stop trusting their own judgment because they're scared of the form. If you recommend adopting this, I want that cost stated straight, without being softened, and I want a concrete answer to the question I know I'll get asked: how do you tell the difference between a clinician who was dismissive and a clinician who was right and got unlucky?

What I want is a recommendation, not a menu. Adopt, don't adopt, or adopt with conditions plus a time frame (we could pilot for six months and review). Say which, and give reasons I can read out loud to two skeptical internists and a lawyer. Where you're making a call that the data can't settle for me, flag it in a sentence and say what would change your mind. Don't lay out three options and leave me to pick — I'm the one who has to vote.
