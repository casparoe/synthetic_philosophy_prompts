I have recently become the lay member of a three-person panel that our city council appointed to advise it on one narrow question. A historical marker is to be erected where a riverside settlement stood until 1919, and the text must say something about how the residents left. The county records describe a compensated buyout: deeds, a ledger, an ordinance, all in order. The descendants' association describes an armed removal, and says the paperwork was drawn up afterward so that the taking would look like a sale. I have spent three months with both accounts, I cannot decide between them, and — more to the point — I have realized that I do not know what I am supposed to be asking for when I ask which one should give way. The panel has to deliver three separate things: a short factual finding for the council, the wording of the inscription, and a recommendation on whether the city owes anything.

Before our next meeting I would rather have a specification than a verdict. Please write the desiderata: what any adequate account of how to adjudicate a conflict between a community's memory and the archival record has to deliver. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of one camp. In particular, please keep the following distinctions apart rather than collapsing them:

1. the evidentiary question (what happened) versus the commemorative question (what the city should now publicly attest) versus the reparative question (what is owed);
2. a memory's being wrong in detail versus wrong in shape — getting the date or the number wrong versus getting the character of the event wrong;
3. a record's being silent about an event versus its having been produced by the very party the memory accuses. My own view is that the ledger cannot corroborate the buyout if the authority accused of the removal is the authority that wrote it, and I want to know whether that belongs on the list as a requirement in its own right — something like an independence or provenance condition — and what it rules out;
4. collective memory as testimony about the past versus collective memory as a present practice that partly constitutes the community that holds it, so that overriding it is not only a change of belief;
5. the two directions of error: overriding a memory that was true, versus preserving one that was false. I want to know whether these are symmetric in cost. It seems to me that the first repeats an injury while the second repeats only a mistake, but I would like that argued rather than assumed.

Then flag any pair of desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied. The one I keep tripping over is between a rule that is the same for every community and a rule sensitive to who produced the documents; if there are others — especially any subset that makes the list as a whole unsatisfiable — that is the part I most want to see. Say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the standard answers, and note which requirements shift depending on whether the output is the finding, the inscription, or the reparative recommendation, since I suspect adequacy is relative to that and that the three pull in different directions.

Finish with a brief scorecard, a few lines each, for three contenders: record-primacy (the archive wins unless it is positively discredited), memory-primacy (the affected community's account wins wherever the record is not independent of the power it names), and the commemorative view (that the dispute is not really about 1919 but about what the city should now say). The criteria are what I need; the scoring is only so I can place the arguments I am hearing around the table.

One disclosure, since it may be a bias rather than a reason: my instinct is to side with the descendants, and I cannot currently tell whether that is a judgment or a mood. If your list gives me a way to test it, so much the better.
