At work we're rolling out a "focus dashboard" — a widget that shows, for everyone on the team, a focus score computed out of keystrokes, calendar load and time in the editor, visible to everyone including your manager and your manager's manager. In the all-hands our VP justified it by quoting Foucault: this is a panopticon, and that's not a bad thing, because people behave better when they can't rule out being watched, so we get the productivity without anyone having to nag. I read most of Discipline and Punish in a seminar years ago and the comparison feels off to me, but I can't say exactly where, and I'd like to know whether its off in a way that matters or just in architectural detail.

So: lay out the correspondence table first — who or what plays the guard, the tower, the cell, the inmate, the backlighting, the Inspector — and mark which of those have real counterparts in a team dashboard and which we're only squinting at. The three places I get stuck: (1) In Bentham's building the mechanism seems to run on the inmate never being able to tell *whether* he is being watched at this particular moment — he has to behave as if he always is, because he can't check — whereas a dashboard that records everything continuously removes precisely the uncertainty that does the work, so maybe we've built the opposite of a panopticon and called it one. (2) Ours is symmetric: I can see my VP's score and she can see mine, and the whole team can see everyone's ranking, but the panopticon is built on a strict asymmetry, the many seen and the one unseen. I've started calling ours a "mutual panopticon" and I'm not sure that's even coherent — it might just be a confession booth, or a reputation market, or something with no name yet. (3) The claim being borrowed isn't really "watched people behave better"; its that this kind of visibility manufactures a particular sort of person, someone who has taken the norm inside and goes on applying it when no one is there. What the score actually seems to manufacture on my team is people gaming the number — leaving a key held down, blocking out fake focus time — which looks like the opposite of self-policing, or at least something else wearing its clothes. Is that a mismatch with the source, or a sign that the power operating here isn't disciplinary in Foucault's sense at all?

Then the part I actually care about: which of those disanalogies bear on the conclusion my VP drew — that transparency buys us productivity with less supervision — and which are just quibbles about buildings? Does that conclusion survive in any restricted form, say for some kinds of tasks and not others? And if the panopticon is the wrong image, what's a better one for what a team-wide metric does to a team — something that gets the productive side right, since it clearly does make new things (a score, a ranking, a certain competitive and slightly performative employee), without pretending we're all prisoners in separate cells. No reading lists please, I want the argument, not sources.
