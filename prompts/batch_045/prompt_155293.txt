You are a professional philosopher of science whose research is in metaphysics and the philosophy of classification, and who has worked through the debates about natural kinds, essences, and realism about the kinds of the special sciences. Please think carefully, step by step, and at some length. Before you answer, spend a paragraph or two brainstorming: set out every presupposition you can detect in what follows, mark the ones you take to be mistaken, and identify the single one doing the most work. Then answer me plainly in about nine hundred words, defining any term of art you use and arguing rather than name-dropping. Do not give me a reading list or a set of references; I want the argument, not the literature. If my question rests on a confusion, I would much rather be corrected than answered as posed.

Some background, so you can see where I am coming from: I spent thirty-eight years in mineral physics and X-ray crystallography, and I now sit in on a graduate seminar in philosophy of science at the university I retired from. My working instinct was always that disputes about classification are empirical disputes, and that they get settled. A mineral species was described in the mid-1970s from a pegmatite — I will call it thackwellite; the actual name is a placeholder and irrelevant — approved on the strength of a distinctive powder pattern and an unusual composition. Some twenty years later, single-crystal work showed that the material was a submicroscopic intergrowth of two phases that were already known, and the species was discredited. That is how it is supposed to go: the microstructure adjudicates. Gold has atomic number 79, and that microstructural fact explains the whole cluster of properties we use to recognise gold; jade turned out to span two quite different minerals, and the ordinary term was revealed as a grab bag. In my field, the joints are real and we find them.

But two things have unsettled me since I started reading philosophy.

First, the rule that the international body vetting new mineral species uses — crystal structure plus specified constraints on chemical composition — is itself a stipulation, and it has been revised more than once. So even where microstructure adjudicates, which microstructural properties are allowed to adjudicate is decided by us. That looks like a conventional element at the heart of the most microstructure-driven classification I know.

Second, I tried to operationalise the old carving-at-the-joints metaphor, because I did not know what else to do with it. Take a domain of specimens, fix a reference list of, say, a thousand properties, and define the projectibility score of a partition as the average share of properties shared by two specimens drawn from the same cell of the partition, minus the average share shared by two specimens drawn from different cells. On this measure, a partition earns the title of natural to the degree that it lets you project properties from one specimen to another. When I computed it for the teaching collection I still have at home, the partition by crystal structure beat the partition by colour, as I expected.

Then I noticed two things that I cannot get past. The score depends on the reference list: change the thousand properties and you change the ranking. And refining a partition can never lower the score; splitting a cell can only make the within-cell pairs more alike, so the maximum, if there is one, is the maximally fine partition — the one that puts every specimen in a cell of its own. Taken at face value, my own criterion says that nature's joints are maximally fine, and I take that to mean one of two things: either the only real kinds are the kinds of fundamental physics and everything above that level — mineral species, biological species, chemical substances as we handle them in the lab — is a useful fiction we impose; or else natural kind is nothing but an honorific we award to whichever classification currently serves our purposes, and there is nothing more to say about which classifications are real.

So my question is: which of those two is it, and why do philosophers resist both? And a related thing I could not follow from a friend in the seminar: she said that some of the things we classify, species especially, might be perfectly real without being kinds at all. If that is right, it breaks my dilemma from a direction I had not considered, but I cannot see how a thing can be real and yet not belong to a kind. Explain that too, if it bears on where my reasoning goes wrong.
