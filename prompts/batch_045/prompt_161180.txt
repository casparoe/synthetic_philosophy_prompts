I teach an introductory philosophy of biology seminar, and I would like to close the unit on the definition of life with Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818) rather than another journal article — but only if the novel can be made to do genuine argumentative work there instead of decorating a point I could make without it. So I want a reading that takes both sides seriously: the creature as Shelley actually draws him, and the three candidate accounts of life I set out below. Please do not quote the novel at me; I have the text in front of me, and what I want is claims about scenes.

The three accounts to read him against. First, an etiological account: a thing is alive only if it stands in the right causal-historical relation to other living things — descent, reproduction, coming into being through the kind of process living things normally come into being through. Second, an organizational or process account: a thing is alive in virtue of what is going on in it now — metabolism, self-maintenance, far-from-equilibrium organization, the capacity to repair and regulate itself. Third, a criterion of my own that I want tested rather than flattered: call it the repair criterion, on which the mark of life is not metabolism as such but dependence on ongoing self-repair carried out with structures the entity builds or rebuilds for itself, so that a thing kept going by an external mechanic does not qualify.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. Verdict under the etiological account: the creature is assembled from dead matter, has no gestation, no birth, and no ancestry. Say whether that account must rule that he was never alive, and say whether the novel's own vocabulary of creator, offspring, and birth quietly commits it to that account or quietly undercuts it.
2. Verdict under the process account and under my repair criterion, and where the two come apart: he eats, heals, feels cold, and learns, but every part of him was made by someone else and nothing in him was built by his own activity. Does the repair criterion give a different answer from the process account here, and is that difference a defect in my criterion or a genuine distinction it has found?
3. The unfinished second creature: Victor tears it apart before animating it and treats that as a different kind of act from killing the living. Say whether any of the three accounts can underwrite that distinction, and whether Victor's own insistence that the first creature is his child makes him inconsistent.
4. The creature's demand for a companion, and Victor's specific horror at a race: note that being capable of Darwinian evolution is a property of populations rather than of individuals, and say whether the novel has stumbled onto the point that on an evolution-involving definition of life, the creature's demand for a second one is close to the condition of his counting as alive at all. Tell me honestly whether that is an insight in the book or an accident of the plot.
5. Where this reading fails and what would dislodge it: grade the borderline cases I want put on the same scale as the creature — the assembled parts before animation, a corpse with residual cellular activity, a transplanted hand, a dormant tardigrade, a cryopreserved embryo — and then my own variant, in which Victor assembles the creature out of tissue kept alive throughout, a graft composite with no moment of animation at all, and say whether any of the three accounts would have to change its verdict. Then add, in the same item, what the novel contributes to the definitional dispute that the arguments alone do not, where the reading distorts the book to fit the idea or the idea to fit the book, what the risk of anachronism is in reading an 1818 novel written in the wake of galvanism through twentieth-century philosophy of biology, and finally which textual feature or which argument would lead you to abandon the reading, along with the step in your reasoning most likely to be wrong.
