ok so my middle school is rolling out a character-education curriculum next fall and I somehow ended up on the committee writing the scope and sequence, which means I've had to actually figure out what I think instead of just nodding along in meetings. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I want a consistency check before I have to defend it to the rest of the department:

(a) Virtue ethics is basically the right framework: what matters morally is the kind of person you are, not whether you applied the right rule or produced the best outcome, and right action is whatever the virtuous agent would do.
(b) I'm convinced by the situationist critique. The psych literature people always cite — the obedience experiments, the seminarian-in-a-hurry one, the dime in the phone booth — makes it pretty clear that behavior tracks situational features (being rushed, a small mood lift, whether someone else already stopped) way more than it tracks anything you'd call a stable trait, and cross-situational consistency for something like honesty or compassion is low.
(c) So I want to say virtues are real but local: a kid can genuinely have honesty-in-the-classroom without having honesty-online, and both are genuine virtues rather than one of them being fake.
(d) I've started calling this the "portability condition": a disposition counts as a virtue only if it survives a specified amount of situational perturbation — returning a lost wallet when three classmates are watching is a virtue, returning it because three classmates are watching is not.
(e) I also want to say the situationist critique doesn't touch the normative core of virtue ethics at all, since it's an empirical claim about how people actually are and not a claim about what's worth being.
(f) And I still want to say it's right to hold kids responsible, to praise and blame them, and to build the unit around stories about exemplars. That's the whole pedagogy, and it's the part my principal actually cares about.

The one thing I keep circling on: a colleague said that if (b) is true, then whatever reliability shows up in (c) and (d) is inherited from the situation rather than from the kid, so the thing I'm calling a virtue is really a property of the environment, and if that's right then (f) — telling kids to be better, blaming them, showing them exemplars — is aimed at the wrong target entirely. I can't tell if that's a real problem or just a definately clever-sounding word game. I'd rather not give up (f), but that's mostly because my principal cares about it, not because I think it's my strongest commitment.

If one of the pairs only looks like a tension, keep it in the list and say so in its own item rather than dropping it. No intro, no conclusion.
