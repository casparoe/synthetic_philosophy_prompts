Hey — I run a tiny fortnightly discussion group at my uni that's officially a "food ethics" reading group but is really me, two PhD students, and whoever wanders in for the free biscuits. We've spent the last couple of sessions going in circles about what you should actually eat if you take animal suffering seriously, and I want to replace the arguing with a decision tree. My flatmate is vegan and rolls her eyes at me; I mostly eat chicken because its cheap; one person insists pasture-raised beef from a cow shot in the field is the least-harm option since crop harvesting mulches field mice and pesticide kills who knows how many insects; some one else says that if you buy that, the whole diet question is a rounding error next to wild animal suffering and we should be arguing about land use instead. So: build me a decision tree for a one-page handout that starts from the single commitment "the suffering of sentient animals matters morally" and lands the reader on one of about eight leaves, where each leaf is a concrete prescription about what to eat or what to do rather than a label — e.g. "vegan, and don't worry about insects", "pasture-raised ruminants, never chicken", "bivalves and cultivated meat only", "diet is basically irrelevant, put the effort into X".

Every internal node should be one claim or one concrete case on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased so a first-year could answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to. I explicitly don't want any node that just asks "do you think animal suffering matters?" or "do you think insects are sentient?" — sort people by what they say about cases, not by which slogan they like. Build it from the logical space where you can: lay out the questions and the answer-combinations they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, note which combinations are unoccupied and why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?), and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate instead of pretending it can.

Cases and distinctions I want in there somewhere: beef vs. chicken, where my flatmate thinks chicken is obviously worse because of the sheer number of birds per calorie and I'm not convinced number-of-lives is the right currency; a steer that lives five decent years on pasture and is then shot painlessly vs. a broiler chicken's six weeks; the field mice and insects killed by harvesting and spraying, and whether those deaths are "ours" in the same way the slaughterhouse ones are; an oyster; crickets or mealworms as your protein source; and the weird one where the pasture would otherwise be wild habitat full of short, nasty lives, so grazing it might lower total wild suffering. Also, somewhere: whether death itself is a harm over and above the suffering, and whether the animal would have existed at all otherwise. At each branch, say roughly which empirical quantity the branch hinges on and how much it would take to flip the verdict — order-of-magnitude guesses from memory are fine, we're not going to fact-check you hard, and please don't give me reading lists or citations, nobody in this group is going to read them. Every leaf should come with one sentence on its most awkward commitment, because that's the bit that actually moves people in the room.
