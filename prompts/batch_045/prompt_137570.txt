I chair the staff recognition committee at a 250-bed community hospital. Every month we pick one person for an "above and beyond" award, and every month it goes the same way: someone tells a story, we all feel something, we vote, and then someone asks why the person who quietly did the same thing last month got nothing. I've started reading about supererogation — acts that go past duty, are praiseworthy if you do them and not blameworthy if you don't — and it looks like exactly the distinction we keep fumbling, but I can't hand a rotating ward manger a philosophy paper.

What I need is a decision procedure we can actually run: the questions in order, in plain words, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the answer is a firm no, and where the question stops being factual and has to go to a person as a values call. Constraints: it gets run in about twenty minutes by a manager with no training and no time, HR has to be able to defend the outcome if it's grieved, and the output is a one-paragraph citation, so the reasoning has to survive being written down and read out loud.

Please build in the things I keep tripping over: that "it was clearly the best thing to do" doesn't by itself make something heroic rather than just your job; that praising something in public seems to turn it into an expectation within a few cycles, so what was optional in March is quietly obligatory by June and the person who doesn't do it gets blamed; and the case where the act was only needed because we understaffed something, where an award feels like it's laundering a duty into a gift. Also: half the time we can't tell what actually happened or why the person did it, and sometimes they did a genuinely good thing and then advertised it all over social media.

Run the finished procedure on three of our real cases: (1) a nurse who came in unpaid on her day off to sit with a dying patient whose family hadn't arrived yet; (2) a porter who spent two hours of his shift hunting down a mislabelled biopsy sample; (3) a senior healthcare assistant who has covered other people's vacancies almost every week for a year, who everyone wants to honor and who I suspect the procedure will tell us not to. Then be straight with me about where it's going to misfire and where a manager just has to use judgment.
