I'm closing my seminar on fakes, forgeries, and the value of originals with a novel instead of one more paper, and I want to use Highsmith's The Talented Mr. Ripley — the Minghella film and the recent Andrew Scott miniseries follow it closely enough that everyone will know the plot. Ripley forges Dickie Greenleaf's hand in letters and on the papers that route Dickie's money to him, but the real forgery is the impersonation: he takes Dickie's clothes, his manner, his signature, and ends up just living as him.

Read the novel against three accounts we've been grinding on. (1) Causal-history: an object's value partly consists in its actual causal chain back to the artist's act, so a perfect copy is missing something no eye can see. (2) Detection-conditional: the defect in a fake is the deception, so a forgery nobody ever catches harms no one and loses nothing. (3) The one I half-invented for the syllabus — call it the risk view — what we prize in an original is the trace of a performance that could have failed, so forgery is parasitic on achievement; it predicts that we should prize a flawed authentic work over a flawless copy, and that we ought to have some grudging regard for a forger who genuinely risked something.

The things I actually want you to get at: Ripley's Dickie is in several respects an improvement on the original — the invented letters are dutiful where the real Dickie was careless — and I'm not sure whether that helps the risk view or wrecks it. Once Dickie is dead there is no original left for Ripley to be a copy of, so what is an orphaned copy a copy of, and does that dissolve the objection to it? And Ripley's forged life accretes history: he lives under it for years, people come to know him under it, which looks like the person-level version of a fake painting that hangs in a museum long enough to acquire a provenance of its own. Does the novel treat that as gradually making him real?

Also be good on where the art analogy to persons breaks — is impersonation wrong in a way forgery isn't, because of the rights of the person copied rather than the value of the trace? And tell me honestly where the reading resists: Highsmith lets Ripley get away and settle into a life of ease, which reads to me like it's endorsing the bragging art-world line that a good fake is as good as the original and maybe better, but I don't trust that. Point me to the two or three scenes you'd put on the syllabus, and finish by saying what, if anything, the novel adds to this debate that the journal articles don't.
