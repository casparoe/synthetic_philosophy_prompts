I write historical fiction, and the novel I am finishing is set among the Company's civilians in the North-Western Provinces in the late 1850s. Its central figure is a young administrator who is sincerely, bookishly utilitarian, and I want readers to find him monstrous only after they have found him reasonable. To get him right I have agreed to record a long-form interview — imagined, for a series that will accompany the book — with John Stuart Mill at Avignon near the end of his life: a man who spent thirty-five years in the Examiner's office at India House, who never set foot in the subcontinent, who learned it partly by proof-reading his father's History of British India as a boy, and who drafted dispatches that governed it.

Draft ten interview questions for him that do not let him restate a position. I want questions that expose the machinery of his defence of tutelary rule, and I need each of them to come with the reply he is most likely to make, rendered in his own idiom as two or three sentences I could adapt into dialogue, together with the follow-up that reply invites — the question that stops working if he answers in the expected way.

At least one question each on the following:

Whether the exception he carves out for those he judged not yet of age to be consulted is a principled exception to the harm principle or merely that principle applied with different empirical beliefs about stagnation and the weight of custom — and, if the latter, what he owes to the possibility that those beliefs were false, and whether the correction would be a retrospective condemnation or only a change of policy.

Who certifies that a people has come of age, given that under his scheme the tutor sets the syllabus, administers the examination and marks the paper. I have a name for this in my notes — the examiner's monopoly — and I want the question to make him say what evidence would make him conclude that the schooling had failed and that Britain should go.

Whether a tutelary regime can be expected ever to declare its pupils adult, given that the officials who must make that judgement owe their vocation, their standing and their sense of usefulness to the tutelage continuing. Treat this as a structural conflict of interest inside the doctrine, not as corruption in particular men.

His own upbringing as the century's most deliberate pedagogical experiment, and what in it he would refuse to repeat on a nation.

Whether the transfer of Indian government to the Crown in 1858 made matters better or worse on his own principles, since it placed India under a ministry answerable to an electorate that does not include the governed — the very machinery of prevailing opinion whose tyranny he most feared at home.

Where India sits on the map he draws in A Few Words on Non-Intervention, between helping a people throw off a foreign yoke and taking sides in their internal struggles, and whether a people can cross out of his third category by its own efforts.

A concrete case: my protagonist faces an outbreak in his district that is at once anti-reform and anti-foreign, and must decide within a week whether to suppress it in order to protect roads, schools and courts. Ask Mill what the improvement test says to do before the week is out, and whether the officer's conviction that he is shortening the tutelage is the redeeming feature of his act or the most dangerous thing about it.

Finally, flag the two questions whose answers would work as a short monologue in a scene, and the one where he is most likely to deflect by calling the difficulty a question of fact rather than of principle — with a reformulation that closes that escape.
