I'm a senior in a philosophy of psychiatry seminar, and our current unit is psychiatric classification and Ian Hacking's looping effects — the way a classification of people can feed back on the people classified and change them, which then forces the classification itself to be revised. For the final paper we were handed an invented case (I'll reproduce it below; please treat every fact in it as stipulated and don't try to check it against anything real). In the discussion section last week my professor reached for a line I have now heard three times this term: "diagnoses aren't discovered, they're made." It seemed to be doing three different jobs, and I suspect it can only do them by sliding between readings. I want the readings separated before I commit to a thesis.

The three jobs it was made to do:

1. To close down a classmate's question "but is PMDS a real disorder or not?" — the answer was that the question is not well formed.
2. To conclude that the criteria are therefore a policy choice, to be settled on cost-benefit grounds by asking what the category does for patients, clinicians, insurers, and researchers.
3. To conclude that no psychiatric category could be a natural kind, so the search for validators is misguided from the start.

The seminar case, abbreviated. A working group is deciding whether to keep, split, or drop a proposed category, "prolonged motivational depletion syndrome" (PMDS).

- 2017–18: two neurology clinics publish a case series of about forty patients, mostly with a shared lesion pattern, who report intact wanting together with an inability to initiate action. There is no psychiatric label for this; the clinicians call it "the starting problem."
- 2023: PMDS is proposed: six months or more of reported failure to initiate volitional action with preserved hedonic response, plus two of five functional items, not better explained by a depressive episode.
- 2026: a bestselling memoir by someone from the original cohort.
- 2027–30: a large online community forms; members call themselves "PMDSers"; short videos circulate that teach the criteria as a self-check; referrals rise from dozens to tens of thousands; new patients describe their experience in the criteria's own vocabulary and report the symptoms in item order.
- 2031: field trials show the original functional items no longer discriminate — near-ceiling endorsement in both self-referred and comparison groups. The group narrows the criteria and adds a clinician-rated initiation-latency measure.
- Validators drift: family aggregation is 3.1 in the 2018 cohort and 1.2 in the 2029 cohort; a dopaminergic agent has a large effect in the 2018 cohort and a small one in 2029; an imaging correlate found in 2018 fails to replicate in 2029.
- Course: in the 2018 cohort it tracks the lesion; in the 2029 cohort symptoms frequently remit when people leave the online community.
- Institutions: a vocational-rehabilitation benefit now requires a PMDS code for eligibility. One patient organization lobbies to keep the category; a rival group of former patients campaigns against it, saying the label taught them to read ordinary fatigue as illness.
- The live dispute: some clinicians say the 2029 presentations are a different condition entirely; others say it is the same condition with a widened phenotype.

What I'd like from you:

1. A menu of seven to nine distinct precise readings of the slogan, ordered from the most platitudinous to the most radical. For each: what it asserts exactly; a verdict (trivial platitude / substantive and defensible / substantive but doubtful / false / self-undermining); and which of the three jobs it actually supports, if any. One clause naming the position each reading corresponds to is welcome, but the readings should be distinguished by content, not by labels.
2. Make sure the menu separates readings along these axes, since I think the ambiguity lives there: whether what is "made" is the term, the criteria, or the people and the kind; whether "made" means causally produced by the practice of classifying or conventionally selected from phenomena that were already there; whether the claim is constitutive (these instances could not have existed without the label) or merely causal and historical; and whether it is global (all clinical kinds) or local (only some, and if so which).
3. Say for each reading how it bears on Hacking-style looping and on his contrast between kinds that are indifferent to our classifications and kinds that are not: does looping entail the reading, is it merely compatible with it, or does it tell against it? I specifically want to know whether a looping effect, on its own, supports any antirealist conclusion at all, and why or why not.
4. Run each reading against the case at two time slices, the 2018 clinic cohort and the 2029 self-referred cohort, and say whether PMDS comes out discovered, made, both, or neither at each. Flag explicitly the readings whose verdict changes between the slices — my guess is that the interesting ones are time-indexed, and I want that made visible rather than averaged away.
5. Assess three inferences I keep hearing in seminar. (a) The criteria were written by a committee, so the kind is a committee artifact. (b) The label changes the people who have it, so the kind is not real at any given time, cannot be a natural kind, and cannot support stable science. (c) These individuals would not have had this experience without the label, so the category cannot be explanatory. For each: valid under which reading or readings, and what goes wrong where it is not.
6. Tell me whether any single reading supports all three of the professor's uses. If none does, say which use has to be given up, and say plainly whether the slogan's rhetorical work depends on moving from a platitudinous reading to a radical one without marking the move. If a reading is self-undermining, show the mechanism; my own suspicion is that the reading which best supports job (2) is the least stable, because looping also degrades the evidence you would use to estimate the benefits — the treatment effect shrinks and the validators drift, so there may be nothing fixed for the pragmatist to optimize against. Check that argument rather than agreeing with it.
7. Finally: which reading is the professor most likely to have meant, given that they used the line for all three purposes?
