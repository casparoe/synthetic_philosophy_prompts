I'm a structural engineer — mostly seismic assessment of ordinary buildings — with no training in philosophy or machine learning, and I've been reading about AI alignment on my own. The argument I keep running into is between people who want worst-case guarantees (safe under every model in some set, every input, an adversary who knows exactly what we did) and people who say that is unachievable or paralyzing, and that we should settle for average-case or high-probability safety (safe under our best guess about how future systems and their deployment actually go, with tail risk managed as it arises).

In my own field I have a well-worn vocabulary for problems of this shape: characteristic loads against characteristic resistances, partial safety factors, a target annual probability of failure, a design-basis event with a return period and a rarer maximum-considered event beyond it, fragility curves, ductility and redundancy so that failure is local and gradual rather than global and sudden, and the habit of treating the far tail of the hazard curve as uncertainty about our model rather than randomness in the world. Please translate the worst-case versus average-case dispute in alignment into that vocabulary as faithfully as you can: which of those tools has a real counterpart here, and what, in my terms, are the two camps actually disagreeing about? Then give me the residue report — what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression at all in the way I think about safety.

Three guesses of mine that I would rather you test than flatter: that a design-basis event cannot be defined in the first place, because there is no historical record from which to fit a return period; that the load/resistance split breaks down, because in my world the earthquake does not read the blueprints and here the load does; and that redundancy fails me, because I am used to separate members failing separately, while advanced AI systems look as though they would all fail together. What I cannot tell is whether that last point shows my framework is missing something, or shows that the average-case side is leaning on a probability distribution that was never really there. Please argue it in your own words — I have no library access, so don't send me to papers, reports, or named authors.
