I'm writing a paper for a graduate seminar on the moral status of artificial systems, and I've hit a disagreement with a friend that I can't locate precisely enough to resolve. We agree on the setup. Take for granted that human suffering matters, and that whatever it is about it that matters is the feature we are trying to locate in other systems; I don't want to relitigate that. We also agree on what I'll call the ascription problem: for essentially any physical system — a wall, a thermostat, a laptop — there are indefinitely many computations it can be described as implementing, including computations with states we would be tempted to call aversive, so the question of whether it implements the right computation cannot even be posed until something fixes which description is the intended one. Where we split is on what does that fixing, and I want you to compare our two answers rather than survey the field.

My friend's answer — call it the causal-organization criterion — is that the ascription problem is merely epistemic. There is, she thinks, a description-independent fact about which counterfactual-supporting causal organization a system physically realizes, a matter of which states interact with which other states, inputs, and outputs in which pattern. A process matters morally just in case the system's dynamics really instantiate the organization that, in us, is what pain is; our descriptions come and go, but the organization is either there or not. My answer — call it the self-tracking criterion — is that the ascription problem goes all the way down, so the only non-arbitrary tie-breaker has to be internal to the system: what matters is whether the system itself represents itself as undergoing a valenced process, where that representation is load-bearing for its own forward-looking self-regulation, shaping what it anticipates about itself, what it plans around, and what it will trade other goods to avoid.

The two criteria coincide for ordinary adult humans and agree about rocks, which is why I suspect the real disagreement is narrower than it sounds; but they diverge at the edges, and I'd like the comparison organized around those cases and around the arguments they turn on, not around a list of pros and cons. Consider, on one side, a system with a complete and behaviorally efficacious pain organization whose self-model says nothing at all about it — call this silent valence — and, on the other side, a system whose self-model richly and controllingly represents it as suffering, which avows it, plans around it, and acts on the avowal, while the underlying dynamics contain nothing structurally resembling ours and amount to something more like a well-organized table of scripts: the case that worries people who think we may end up manufacturing something that merely counterfeits a subject. Tell me which criterion handles each better and why, and then press on where the disagreement bottoms out. Does the self-tracking criterion actually escape the ascription problem, or does it just relocate it one level up, given that a system's self-model can itself be described as implementing many different computations? Conversely, can the causal-organization criterion say what singles out one causal organization as the morally relevant one without covertly helping itself to a description? I also half believe a deflationary argument I can't fully assess — that any system robust enough to realize the relevant organization across a wide range of contexts will be under engineering pressure to track that organization in a self-model, and that any system that tracks it will have to realize something isomorphic to it, so the two criteria coincide in every case we will in fact face — and I want to know whether you think that argument works, and whether, if it does, it dissolves the question or merely pushes it out to the boundary cases where it matters most. Finally, be concrete about what would change your mind: if you could run a single intervention on a candidate system — a lesion, a rewiring, an architectural change, a behavioral probe — which would it be, and what result would move you from one criterion to the other? And if you think I have drawn the contrast wrongly, and that some third thing is doing the work both criteria are reaching for, say what it is and why it does the job better.
