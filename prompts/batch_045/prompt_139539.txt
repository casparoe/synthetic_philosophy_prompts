Next month I'm defending the third chapter of my dissertation — on the problem of verisimilitude — in a joint session of my department's philosophy of science reading group and the formal epistemology workshop: forty-five minutes of talk, then twenty-five minutes of questions, and the questions are what I'm worried about. My thesis, in brief, is that the search for an absolute, language-independent ordering of theories by closeness to the truth is a pseudo-problem, and that the only well-defined notion in the neighborhood is what I call question-relative verisimilitude. The argument runs: Popper's motivation stands (a false theory can be closer to the truth than another false one, and that is what makes the history of science progress rather than mere change); the classic 1974 results of Tichý, Harris, and Miller sank Popper's own qualitative definition, and the subsequent language-dependence arguments showed that proposed orderings flip when a theory is redescribed in an equivalent vocabulary; the likeness approach developed by Niiniluoto and Oddie — closeness as distance between possible worlds — repairs the logical problem but only by choosing a metric and weighting respects of similarity, which reimports the arbitrariness one level down. So instead: fix a set Q of contrastive questions (equivalently, a partition of the space of possibilities — for a quantity like the number of planets, the questions Is it 0? Is it 1? Is it 2?, and so on), count a theory's verisimilitude as its expected share of Q answered correctly, weighted for importance, and note that any two descriptions inducing the same partition yield the same ordering, so the famous flip cases arise exactly where the redescription changes the partition. Flipping is then not a defect but a signal that closeness to truth is interest-relative: two research programs can both make progress, relative to different question sets, without contradiction.

The room matters more than the argument, so let me describe it. Two members are scientific realists of the likeness-approach stripe who think the logical problem is basically solved and that my view confuses closeness to truth with informativeness about whatever we happen to ask. Three are formal epistemologists in the accuracy-first, Bayesian tradition who will immediately suspect that I have rediscovered expected inaccuracy relative to a partition, or a proper scoring rule, and that my importance weights are just a prior in disguise. One is a historian of science with Kuhnian sympathies who will hear my talk as incommensurability dressed up in notation, and say so with some pleasure. One is a deflationist about truth who will ask what work truth is still doing once question-answering does the work. And one is a climate modeler who sits in on the reading group, has no stake in any of this, and will ask, politely and devastatingly, whether anything I've said should change how she compares two models on Monday morning.

What I'd like from you is a prep sheet, not a defense: the seven or eight objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it rather than in my vocabulary, each with a reply I could give in about ninety seconds, a note on what I should concede rather than fight, and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks — I think it's the trivialization worry, that if Q is chosen freely anyone can make any theory maximally truthlike by asking only questions it answers correctly (or its cousin, that choosing Q is itself an inquiry that aims at the truth, so I need an account of closeness to truth before I can pick my questions) — and tell me honestly whether my answer to it holds.
