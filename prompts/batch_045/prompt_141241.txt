I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on Hobbes on geometry next term — 16 students, one 80-minute slot, mostly philosophy majors but with two maths people who've done a proofs course and two who go quiet the moment a diagram appears. We've been through his account of what a science has to be: real demonstration runs from causes, the definitions you start a science with have to say how the thing defined gets made (a line is what a moving point traces out, and so on), and Euclid's definition of a point as that which has no part is no good — Hobbes's own version being roughly that a point is that whose magnitude we aren't reckoning in this demonstration. The Wallis fight and the circle-squaring claim are background colour.

Last time I ran it as a straight discussion and it died in ten minutes: everyone agreed proofs are fine, and we spent the rest of the hour on whether Hobbes was a crank. So I want them to run into it instead.

The thing I keep coming back to — I've started calling it generationism, tell me if that framing is a mistake — is the requirement that before a proof may name or quantify over an object, it has to hand over a production licence: a rule that actually brings the thing about (for geometry, a straightedge-and-compass construction, or at minimum a described motion). I want an activity where the rules and the scoring make the students start demanding licences themselves, without me ever saying the word.

What I have in mind, roughly:

- an early stretch where the licence demand feels good — bisect an angle, build an equilateral triangle — where insisting on a construction actually clarifies the proof rather than obstructing it
- a case that looks producible and isn't with the allowed toolkit, so they burn real minutes getting nearly there (trisecting an arbitrary angle, or squaring the circle if that plays better)
- a case where the object provably exists but no method is forthcoming; I was going to use the one about there being irrationals a and b with a to the b rational, but if another case lands harder with non-maths people, use it
- a case where the object comes only as the end of an infinite process — a curve given as the limit of a sequence of polygons, or an infinite sum — since that's where Hobbes's allergy to indivisibles bites

Can you give me: the actual rules, materials (paper, string, compasses, cards — I'd rather nothing digital), a timeline in minutes, the payoff scheme (points or something; no money and nothing that needs prep I can't do in a morning), and a final forced choice where a team either grants itself a waiver or gives up a result it wants to keep. I'd like that choice to end in a vote I can actually take, and I'd like you to predict how the room splits.

Two things I care about especially. First, the payoff has to create the dilemma rather than just reward compliance: refusing to reason about unlicenced objects should cost them something, and licencing everything should cost them something else. Second, I don't want anyone to hear constructivism, intuitionism, or non-constructive before the last ten minutes — it has to be named after they've already had the feeling.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific Hobbesian claims, including the one where his definition of a point stops them getting stuck. Plus an honest note on what will predictably go wrong — I'm guessing at least one student will announce that Hobbes was just an intuitionist ahead of Brouwer, and I need the two-line way to show that his demand is causal rather than algorithmic — and which part of the view the activity simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to state myself.

Last time half the room just wanted to allow everything. Build for that.
