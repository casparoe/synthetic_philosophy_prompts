I've been arguing with a friend in the comment section of one of the rationalist blogs about Hempel's raven paradox, and I have talked myself into an argument whose conclusion I am sure is false but whose broken joint I cannot locate. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

Let H be "all ravens are black" and H′ be "everything non-black is a non-raven". Measure the size of a confirmation, as usual, by the log of the likelihood ratio, in bits; call that the boost.

(1) E confirms H for me if and only if P(H|E) > P(H).
(2) Logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by exactly the same evidence, so H and H′ stand or fall together.
(3) An object that I observe to be both non-black and a non-raven is an instance of H′, so observing it confirms H′. On this view a white shoe is evidence about ornithology.
(4) The standard Bayesian reply I keep encountering concedes (3) and explains away the residual queasiness: the boost is real but astronomically small, because a randomly encountered object's turning out to be a non-black non-raven is something I was already almost certain of, and near-certain observations carry almost no information.
(5) Small is not zero, and boosts add in log-odds. If one white shoe is worth ε bits, then n white shoes are worth roughly nε bits. And since the objects need not be ravens, I can harvest them from an armchair: any object I have not yet examined will do.
(6) Non-ravens vastly outnumber ravens, so the supply of unexamined objects is not the binding constraint. Give me enough armchair time and I accumulate the confirmation that one black raven would have given me — call the exchange rate confirmation vouchers: one black raven equals some large number of white shoes.
(7) Worse, a single glance at one white shoe confirms "all ravens are black", "all emeralds are green", "all copper conducts electricity" and "all tigers are striped" all at once: the shoe is non-black and non-raven, non-green and non-emerald, non-conducting and non-copper, non-striped and non-tiger. One observation, arbitrarily many generalizations, all nudged upward.
(8) So the package in (1)–(2) says that universal generalizations of this form are settleable from an armchair, and that a single observation is evidence for an arbitrarily large pile of unrelated generalizations. That cannot be right.

I would rather keep (1) and (2) than (5), (6) or (7): I am committed to probabilism and to the equivalence condition, and I would sooner surrender Nicod's criterion or the additivity claim. But I suspect the break is somewhere I have not looked. Which step do you reject, why does it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me — in particular, can I keep (1) and (2) and still refuse to pay (8)?

One subsidiary thing I cannot adjudicate: a friend says that if I treat colours as exchangeable across kinds of object, then finding many white shoes should raise my estimate of how common non-blackness is in general, and so push me against H rather than toward it. Is that a real effect, or an artifact of how the model is set up?

Lead with the number of the step you reject, and keep the whole answer under 600 words.
