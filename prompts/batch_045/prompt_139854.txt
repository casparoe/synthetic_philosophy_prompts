I'm a third-year undergraduate in an upper-level seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and my term paper is on whether anekāntavāda — the Jain doctrine of many-sidedness, together with its companion theories of conditioned predication (syādvāda) and standpoints or partial standpoints (nayavāda) — is anything more than relativism with better manners. I don't want to answer that by restating doctrine. I'd rather start from my own verdicts on a set of cases, extract the principle they add up to, and only then ask whether it's a principle the tradition could recognize. So I'd like you to do reflective equilibrium on my behalf.

Here are the cases, and how I actually respond to them.

One: a man is both a father and a son, relative to different people. Both claims true — but I don't think this is a case of many-sidedness at all. There is just an unstated relatum, and once you state it the appearance of conflict evaporates. I'd be embarrassed to offer it as an illustration of anekāntavāda.

Two: the clay pot. From the standpoint of what it is made of, it is just clay; from the standpoint of the shape it currently has, it is a pot, and not merely clay. I want to say both are true, and I also want to say this one really is the doctrine in action, the way the first one isn't. I can't yet say what the difference is.

Three: light is a wave and light is a stream of particles, the first relative to one kind of apparatus, the second relative to another. Both true — but this feels to me like case one wearing a lab coat: an indexing to experimental arrangement. I'd be surprised to learn that a Jain logician would count this as a genuine standpoint. Yet if my principle can't distinguish two from three, I may have to give up saying that two is the real thing.

Four: two friends leave the same party; one says it was electric, the other that it was dreary and they wanted to leave after twenty minutes. Both fine. Nothing to adjudicate.

Five: two witnesses disagree about which driver went through the red light. Here 'both partly right' strikes me as a refusal to go and look at the traffic camera. Not fine.

Six: the man with the sign who says the earth is flat, and — more sharply — the person who says a well-documented massacre never happened. Not fine, and not merely not fine: here standpoint-talk feels like complicity. If any principle I end up with entails that his claim is true in some respect, I take that as a reductio of the principle, not as a discovery.

The pair I can't reconcile is four and five: same shape — two people, one event, conflicting reports — and opposite verdicts. The distinction I keep reaching for is that one concerns a response-dependent property and the other a plain fact, but I don't trust it, because I don't have a test for response-dependence that doesn't just restate my verdict.

Here is my tentative rule, which I don't think works. Call it the acknowledged-parameter rule: two conflicting claims both get a qualified yes only if (a) there is a specifiable parameter the claims are indexed to, (b) the parameter is one both speakers could in principle acknowledge as a legitimate way of carving up the subject matter — as opposed to merely being a fact about their psychology or their evidence — and (c) once you fix a value for the parameter, no residual disagreement is left. My worry about (a) is that everything can be indexed to something: the flat-earther's claim is true relative to the evidence available to him, the witnesses' claims are true relative to where each of them was standing, and in both cases my verdict was 'not fine.' My worry about (b) is that it looks like it just means 'the parameter I approve of,' and in any case the person in case six won't acknowledge my carved-up subject matter, which would make his claim unacknowledged by stipulation. My worry about (c) is that it may be too strong for the cases I most want to keep: in case two, does the disagreement really vanish when I fix the standpoint, or have I only changed the topic?

The case I go back and forth on is this. A clinician says a patient's panic attacks are a malfunction of the fear system. The patient says they are an intelligible response to a workplace in which she is being harassed, and that calling them a malfunction erases that. Half the time 'both are right from their own standpoint' strikes me as the deepest thing available here; the other half it strikes me as a way of changing the subject away from a question with an answer, namely whether her fear system is doing what it was shaped to do. I notice I also waver in the same way about two economists reading the same employment data and drawing opposite conclusions, where 'it depends on the sector and the time window' is both available and, to me, somehow evasive.

What I want from you: two or three candidate principles stated precisely enough that I could apply one to a new case without coming back to you; for each clause, which of my cases forces it; the verdict of your preferred principle on two cases I haven't listed, at least one of which should be a case where the verdict is one I would initially resist; and a plain statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would rather drop a verdict than keep a rule that works only by being vague. Finally, tell me what your principle says when turned on itself: is the claim that every unqualified metaphysical assertion is only partially true itself only partially true, or does the doctrine exempt itself — and if the exemption runs through the omniscient standpoint (kevala) that only the enlightened attain, why isn't that standpoint just a very well-informed one, which by my own clause (b) should be an illegitimate parameter? The TA says that exemption is exactly what keeps anekāntavāda from collapsing into relativism. I'd like to know whether my cases support her or not.
