I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in philosophy or history; I left school at eighteen and have been turning this over on my own, so it is quite possible that some of the distinctions I have built for myself are crooked. But I have been sitting in on a dispute in the town where I live for the better part of a year and I cannot referee it, and the two people involved have more or less asked me to.

The town, which I will call March Hollow, has told a story about itself for as long as anyone can remember. In the winter of 1913, during the strike at the works, three organisers being hunted by the company's deputies were hidden in the town, and when the deputies came through asking questions the town closed up and nobody said where the men were. Every year since, on the Saturday nearest the date, the town walks the old route with lanterns, and there is a plaque with four names on it: the works clerk, the minister, and two of the shopkeepers.

A young man here, Dev, spent last winter in the county records office and came back with something more awkward than a debunking. The hiding was real and the silence was real. But the initiative and nearly all of the risk belonged to the families along the creek, mainly recent immigrants in company houses; the four men on the plaque spent that week trying to persuade those families to give the organisers up, and one of them wrote the letter that told the deputies which doors to knock on. The organisers got out by the creek. Three weeks later the company evicted fourteen creek families and blacklisted the men, and the council, whose minutes survive, discussed it and did nothing. The Walk itself was started in 1951 by a grandson of the works clerk, partly to raise money for a new library.

Then the argument, which went roughly like this.

Marta, who runs the historical society: the Walk is not a claim about 1913 that could be true or false, it is a practice the town performs, and what it does is make the town the sort of place that hides people. It has done that. In 1982, when a family out on the creek road was being harassed, it was people who had walked the route as children who organised the rota that sat with them at night. Correct the story and you do not get a truer town, you get a town that has stopped practising, and truth-tracking and practice-sustaining simply can come apart. A town is not a seminar.

Dev: but the practice runs on a false picture of who did the hiding, and gratitude that lands on the wrong people is not a neutral error, it is a transfer. Every year the Walk passes the foundations of the houses the creek families were dragged out of, and thanks the descendants of the men who made the eviction possible. If a community's memory is in part a way of paying debts to the dead, then getting the names wrong is not a detail going wrong, it is the whole thing going wrong.

Marta: what you have produced is a counter-memory, built out of the same selective materials as the one you are attacking. What survived is the council minutes, the company's eviction ledger, the clerk's letter, the paper of people who had clerks and kept copies. The creek families left almost nothing. Your version is better documented, not less constructed, and if the standard is full itemised attribution then no community has ever met it and none will, so you are holding the Walk to a test that would dissolve every memory anybody has.

Dev: granted, both are constructed. That does not make them equally accountable. Mine has a procedure for being corrected: find me a letter and I change it. Yours is annual, public, and recited by children, which is exactly the shape a story takes when it is being made unrevisable. So the question is not which story is closer to the truth. It is which story has any way of being corrected later, and I think that is what should decide which of the two gives way, rather than the truth of the content at all.

Marta: corrigibility is a virtue in an archive, not in a promise. The Walk is not the town's belief about 1913, it is the town's standing commitment, and commitments are not supposed to be updated when the evidence changes, because a vow that you revise every time a new document surfaces was never a vow. You are asking us to treat a ritual as though it were a hypothesis.

Trying to sort this out for myself, I came up with four tests, and I genuinely do not know if any of them are sound.

The substitution test. If the four names on the plaque were quietly replaced with the creek families' names and nothing else changed, same route, same lanterns, same children reciting, would the town go on walking? If yes, the memory was only ever borrowing those men's authority and Dev's objection is cosmetic. If no, the Walk is essentially about those four men after all, and Marta's practice defence collapses, because the practice turns out not to survive the correction. Neither of them will say which way they think this comes out.

Credit versus example. Perhaps the Walk does two separable things: paying a debt backwards to particular dead people, and holding up an example forwards to the living. Accuracy of attribution looks constitutive for the first and merely incidental for the second, in which case the answer is not that memory or record gives way but that the credit should be reassigned while the example is kept. Though I cannot tell whether the two can be pulled apart inside a ritual performed by children walking past a plaque.

Revision by addition rather than retraction. Both of them assume the choice is between keeping the story and knocking it down. There may be a third option, amending by accretion: a second stop added to the route, names added to the plaque, the eviction walked as well as the hiding, without anyone ever formally declaring the old story false. I cannot tell whether that is the honest resolution or only a way of never giving anything up.

Standing. Possibly all of this is downstream of a question nobody has asked, namely who gets to decide: the people who walk now, the descendants of the creek families, some of whom still live two valleys over, or nobody at all, on the grounds that the dead cannot authorise a revision of what was done in their name. If the descendants hold some kind of veto that the current residents do not, the substantive argument between Marta and Dev may barely matter.

Please adjudicate the argument as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.

1. Your verdict on Marta's opening: the practice-not-claim move, and the 1982 instance she offers as evidence that the memory does forward-looking work.
2. Your verdict on Dev's reply: the transfer point, that gratitude directed at the wrong people is not a neutral mistake.
3. Your verdict on Marta's second move: that the counter-memory is equally constructed, that the archive is the paper of people who had clerks, and that the demand for full attribution would dissolve every collective memory.
4. Your verdict on the closing exchange, Dev's corrigibility against Marta's vow: who wins that exchange and why.
5. Which one or two of my four distinctions actually settle something here, which are beside the point or rest on a confusion, and then the bottom line: who is more right overall, and the one thing both of them are missing.

Take a definite position at every point and give your reason inside the sentence or two you have. I would much rather have a call I can argue with than a survey of what might be said on each side, and where you think I have built one of my distinctions badly, please say that plainly instead of working around it.
