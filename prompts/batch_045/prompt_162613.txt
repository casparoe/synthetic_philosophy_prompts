I'm giving a paper next month in a philosophy department seminar — 90 minute slot, so about 40 minutes of talk and then what will probably be an ugly Q&A — and what I want help with is the question period, not the paper. The audience is roughly twenty people, and the thing that matters about it is the mix: most of them are analytic moral philosophers, and of those a good number are consequentialists of the sort who think in expected-value and effective-altruism terms; two or three are virtue ethicists who are drawn to Buddhism precisely because the popular reading in Buddhist studies is that Buddhist ethics is virtue ethics with an Aristotelian flavour; there's one Kantian who comes to everything; and then, importantly, two people from religious studies who actually read Pali and Sanskrit and whose default reaction to any analytic paper on Buddhism is that it's anachronistic, rests on translation artifacts, and flattens together texts five centuries apart.

Here's the thesis. I argue that early Buddhist ethics is best read as a *split-level* theory: consequentialist in its account of what makes an act right — roughly, whether the act, through the karmic mechanism, conduces to the reduction of suffering across the causal stream it initiates — but non-consequentialist, and specifically aretaic, in its account of how an agent should deliberate, because the only part of that causal stream an agent can actually track or reliably predict is the trajectory of her own mind, i.e. whether acting this way feeds or starves the unwholesome roots (greed, aversion, confusion). So the deliberative question "what should I do?" gets cashed out as "what would doing this do to my mind?", while the theory of the right stays thoroughly consequentialist, and I claim the split is a feature rather than a fudge: it's the tradition's own answer to the epistemic demandingness objection, because it yields a tractable deliberative procedure without giving up a consequentialist criterion. I lean hard on the canonical identification of karma with intention, and I use the birth-story material where the bodhisattva gives away his own body or his children as the stress case.

What I want from you is a prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from *this* audience, in roughly the order they'll arrive and ranked by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would actually phrase it — the EV-talking consequentialist, the virtue theorist who thinks I'm smuggling Oxford into the suttas, the Pali reader, the Kantian — with a short (two-minute-ish) reply for each, what I should concede up front, what I should hold the line on, and where my reply is softest. Two things I can't fully answer myself and definately want covered: (a) whether the split-level move is just a re-description of subjective consequentialism with a restriction on the evidence available to the agent, in which case I've said nothing new to the consequentialists at all, and (b) whether, given that karma is individual — no transfer of merit, one mind-stream per being — my "reduction of suffering across the causal stream" quietly collapses into reduction of *my own* future suffering, which would make the whole view enlightened self-interest. Also tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive, and what the question I'm hoping nobody asks is: my own bet is the anattā one — if there's no persisting self, whose mental trajectory is being optimised — but say if you think the real landmine is somewhere else.
