Take an ordinary behavioural trait — educational attainment, or a cognitive test score, whatever — with h² ≈ 0.5 measured in a contemporary wealthy population. The question I actually care about:

Does that 0.5 carry any *real* information about how large a mean shift you could buy in that same population with a uniform intervention (everyone gets it, so nobody's rank changes)? Or is it, as the standard line has it, compatible with a mean shift of literally any size?

Here's where I got stuck. Suppose the trait is additive with no G×E. Then under the best environment everyone's value is G + e*, so the achievable mean shift is just e* − Ē, where Ē is the mean of the environments people in the population actually occupy. h² doesn't appear in that expression at all, so the standard line looks right. But the decomposition also hands you √Ve = √(1 − h²)·SD_T, and the environments currently realised in the population are a kind of sample of what environments are attainable for people like us. If the intervention has to be an environment drawn from roughly the range already realised — just better targeted and given to everybody — then the achievable shift is on the order of one to three environmental SDs, and h² does tell you something: it gives an order-of-magnitude ceiling. The textbook counterexamples (the single-gene metabolic one, iodine, the secular rise in test scores) all work by importing an environment from *outside* the realised range. So the crux looks like whether real interventions draw on the realised range or on environments nobody in the population currently occupies — plus how much of the non-genetic variance is a lever anyone could pull at all, as opposed to measurement error and developmental noise that no intervention touches.
