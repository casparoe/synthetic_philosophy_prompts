So I help out (badly) with one of those big volunteer projects where people formalize mathematical proofs in software — the kind where a checker grinds every step down to a handful of tiny rules, and the only thing you have to take on faith is a small core, a few hundred lines of code, that actually applies those rules. Everything above that has to be proved.

Last week someone new joined and asked why we trust the core. We said: it just implements the most basic rules of logic, like modus ponens. She said fine, then prove inside the system that modus ponens never takes you from true premises to a false conclusion, and let's make that proof a required part of every submission. We said you can't — any proof of that would have to use it. She said, then state it as a starting assumption: write down a line saying that if P, and if P then Q, then Q, and let proofs just cite that line. But as she immediately pointed out, citing the line doesn't get you anywhere either: to actually get Q out of P and the cited line you still have to *apply* the rule. And if you add a second rule saying you may apply any rule whose conditions are met, you need a third one to apply that one, and so on forever. Her conclusion is that we're all being dogmatic and just stop the regress wherever we get bored.

There's apparently an old Lewis Carroll dialogue where Achilles and a tortoise do exactly this, which makes me feel slightly better but settles nothing.

What I actually want to know is what our practice presupposes. The whole selling point of the project is "no step without justification," and yet the practice also seems to presuppose that some things — the rules themselves — get no justification at all, and couldn't have one. Is there a view of justification where that isn't just laziness? Does it presuppose that accepting a rule is a different *kind* of thing from accepting a proposition — closer to a skill or a disposition than to a belief? Or are we implicitly relying on self-evidence, or on the rules being somehow constitutive of what reasoning even is? I'd like to know which of these the practice strictly requires and which it merely gets along fine with, because I suspect people on the project are quietly holding three or four incompatible stories at once.

Two more things that confuse me.

First: is it even consistent to demand a justification for every step and then refuse to justify the rules? Or is the "every step" quietly scoped, and if so what's doing the scoping?

Second: we used to allow a rule that turned out to produce a contradiction — the old idea that any property you like picks out a set, before someone noticed it lets you build the set of all sets that aren't members of themselves. When that blew up, nobody said the rule had been *unjustified*. We said it was broken and swapped it for a more careful one, and we judged the replacement by whether it gave us the mathematics we wanted without contradictions. Doesn't that mean we already treat rules as things we choose and revise for broadly pragmatic reasons, rather than things we deduce? And if so, does that hand the tortoise a partial win — is the only justification available for the basic rules something abductive and a bit circular, like "it works, it hangs together, we can't do without it"? Or is that a different kind of justification from the one she was demanding, and does it count?

And concretely: if we caved and let everyone add a line saying "and we're allowed to use modus ponens" to every proof, what would actually break? Anything, or is her demand basically empty?
