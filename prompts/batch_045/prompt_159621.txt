I write and test problems for a puzzle competition, and a regular part of the job is the line that says "choose at random": a random chord of a circle, two random break points on a stick, three random points on a sphere. For continuous setups that line keeps turning out not to pin anything down. Draw a chord by picking two points on the circumference independently and uniformly and you get one answer to "is it longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle"; pick a random direction and then a random distance from the centre and you get another; pick a random point inside the circle and let that be the midpoint and you get a third. All three are what any reader would call uniform. I gather that much is a well-known cautionary example; it isn't my question.

What I keep hitting, and have no word for, is what happens after someone points this out. The move everyone makes, me included, is to say: fine, we don't know which procedure is right, so spread our credence evenly over the procedures we can think of and average their answers. That move seems to fail in its own way. The number you get depends on how many procedures you happened to list, and you can always come up with one more that is just as defensible as the others, so the average drifts and never settles. List the same procedure twice under two descriptions and it silently gets twice the weight. A co-editor and I once produced four different "averages" for the same chord question in one afternoon, each of them principled, mostly because we thought of the methods in different orders. Same story with the stick, where breaking it at two uniform points and breaking it once and then breaking the longer piece give different answers, and averaging the two tells me nothing I trust.

But it isn't always hopeless, and this is the part I most want pinned down. Sometimes the competing procedures look like one procedure seen from different angles — related by a rotation, or by a rescaling that plainly shouldn't matter to the question being asked — and then averaging over them feels legitimate, and even seems to produce an answer everyone accepts. Other times they look like genuinely different decisions about what to be indifferent between, and averaging just relocates the arbitrariness one level up. I cannot say what the difference is, and when I try I end up restating whichever case I am looking at.

So: is there an established concept for this second failure, the averaging-over-procedures move that doesn't converge, and is there a term for the distinction between the cases where such averaging is defensible and the cases where it isn't? I make this call four or five times a year and I have to defend it to a co-editor who is not a mathematician, so a name alone won't help me. I need to know whether this is really the first problem reappearing at a higher level or a different problem, what it borders on, and how I could recognise the favourable case before spending a weekend on it. If nothing established fits, say so plainly and give me a definition I could put in a house style guide, together with the cases where it would not apply.
