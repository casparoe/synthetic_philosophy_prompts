I'm a backend engineer by trade — distributed systems, mostly — with no formal philosophy background. I got into this through an evening reading group at a hackerspace, and after I gave an informal 20-minute version of this at a meetup I was invited to present a longer version to the philosophy of physics reading group at the local university: 25 minutes plus about 15 for questions. The thesis I want to defend is that the thermodynamic arrow is not a direction in the physics but a property of the interface between a computationally bounded, physically embedded system and the world. My argument has three steps. First, the usual point that a time-symmetric microdynamics cannot by itself yield increasing entropy, because for any entropy-increasing trajectory there is a time-reversed one, so you need both a special low-entropy boundary condition at one end and some coarse-graining; and if the boundary condition is genuinely at one end only, then entropy increases in both temporal directions away from it, and there is no global fact of the matter about which way is future. Second, my own addition: a coarse-graining is a lossy compression function over microstates, and the macro-variables any real observer can use are the ones that can actually be computed inside a finite memory, finite time, and finite energy budget, where erasing information has a real thermodynamic cost; so the coarse-graining is not freely chosen by a theorist but is forced on any predictor that has to run on hardware. Third, given such a compression, the direction in which it yields usable records — the direction in which local correlations carry information about states the predictor cannot see directly — is the direction of increasing entropy; so the arrow is the direction in which bounded prediction is possible at all, and it is indexical, in that an agent situated on the other side of the low-entropy boundary would call our past their future.

The room is the part I'm least able to anticipate. Expect roughly eight people: a senior philosopher of physics who is a convinced Boltzmannian and thinks the low-entropy boundary condition plus statistical-mechanical probability settles everything and that the rest of the literature is really psychology of temporal belief; a mid-career philosopher who works on causal and interventionist asymmetries and will want to know why my story isn't hers retold in computational vocabulary, or else why it isn't circular because I help myself to records, prediction, and information; a retired condensed-matter physicist who thinks this is all thermodynamics 101 and that philosophers manufacture problems by refusing to say what their terms mean; two graduate students who will press the fluctuation and Boltzmann-brain style worries about whether my boundary condition is stable under my own account; and one computer scientist who works on reversible computation and is likely to press hard on whether the thermodynamic cost of erasure is doing genuine work in my argument or is just decoration bolted on for local color.

What I'd like from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will arrive and ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would actually phrase it — the condensed-matter physicist's version of an objection should not sound like the graduate students'. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing at a whiteboard, an explicit note on what I should concede up front versus what I should hold, and a note on where my reply is soft. Please mark which objections are merely terminological and which have real force, tell me which of my three steps is the genuinely load-bearing one and therefore where I should concentrate my preparation, and flag the place where a physicist would say I have quietly changed the subject from what the arrow of time question was supposed to be. Finally, include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Assume I would rather concede something gracefully than bluff, and please don't suggest I add formalism I couldn't defend under follow-up.
