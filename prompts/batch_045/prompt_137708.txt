After forty years in experimental physics I have begun reading philosophy in earnest, and I would like help with a puzzle in Berkeley that I suspect I am mis-formulating.

The metaphysical thesis I think I follow: what we call a physical thing is a collection of ideas, and there is no unthinking material substratum underneath. The part I cannot reconcile with it is his economics. In the book-length series of numbered questions he published after becoming a bishop, he pressed for an Irish national bank issuing paper currency, and my reading is that he treats metal and paper alike as mere tokens whose real content is the industry of other people: it is industry, not the metal, that constitutes the wealth of a country.

Two things then seem to pull apart. First, if value is always a relation between a sign and what it can be exchanged for, and if signs are arbitrary, I cannot see what principled objection Berkeley has left to a purely nominal currency; yet he plainly thinks a currency can be issued to excess, which implies a real, non-conventional constraint on how much sign a given stock of industry will carry. Second, I have a conjecture I would like tested rather than indulged: perhaps the operative notion of sign is not the arbitrary, convention-based one but the one he uses in his account of vision, where the link between what is seen and what it signifies is learned through experience while resting on an order we did not institute, so that a currency becomes a sign of future industry whose efficacy depends on habits of expectation a legislature can exploit but not create.

Does that conjecture dissolve the first difficulty or merely relocate it? And, more generally, is there a reading on which his idealism does genuine work here — on which the fact that value resides in minds and their expectations rather than in stuff is what licenses the monetary scheme — or is the economics detachable from the metaphysics after all? I would be grateful for the structural shape of the answer rather than a survey of the secondary literature.
