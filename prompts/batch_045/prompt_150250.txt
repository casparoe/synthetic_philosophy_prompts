QUESTION 3 (25 marks)

A three-member appellate panel must decide whether the defendant is liable for breach of contract. The governing doctrine is: the defendant is liable (R) if and only if a valid contract was formed (P) and the defendant's conduct amounted to a breach of it (Q). The judges vote as follows: Judge 1 — P yes, Q no, R no; Judge 2 — P no, Q yes, R no; Judge 3 — P yes, Q yes, R yes.

(a) [6 marks] What does proposition-wise majority voting yield on the agenda {P, Q, R}? What do the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures yield? State the discursive dilemma in your own words.

(b) [8 marks] It is sometimes said that the discursive dilemma is "just Condorcet's paradox, or Arrow's theorem, in another guise." Assess that claim carefully. In your answer, say what the objects of aggregation are in preference aggregation and in judgment aggregation, what the relevant independence condition is in each setting, and whether the relation between the results is one of identity, of one being a corollary of the other (and if so, in which direction), or merely of formal analogy.

(c) [8 marks] A second panel faces a disjunctive doctrine: the claim is time-barred (T) if and only if the limitation period has expired (L), or the matter was settled in earlier proceedings (S), or the court lacks jurisdiction (J). The parties have conceded, and the panel accepts, that at least one of L, S, J obtains; the only live question is which. Each judge accepts exactly one ground and rejects the other two (Judge 1: L only; Judge 2: S only; Judge 3: J only), and each accordingly accepts T.
(i) What do the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures yield here?
(ii) Does this example show that the premise-based procedure is in general guaranteed to yield a consistent collective judgment set? Identify the assumption that is doing the work, and say what happens when it fails.
(iii) Comparing with part (a), does the direction of the divergence between the two procedures depend on the logical form of the doctrine?

(d) [3 marks] In one paragraph: given (a)–(c), what is the most defensible position on the choice between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation?

CANDIDATE ANSWER (verbatim, candidate #2201)

(a) Proposition-wise majority voting yields P (by 2–1), Q (by 2–1) and not-R (by 2–1). Since the doctrine makes R equivalent to P and Q, the collective set {P, Q, not-R} is inconsistent: the majority accepts everything that suffices for liability and yet rejects liability itself. Note that the panel has an odd number of members, so this is not a problem about ties. The premise-based procedure aggregates over the premises: majorities accept P and accept Q, and the doctrine then entails R, so the court finds liability. The conclusion-based procedure aggregates only over the conclusion: a majority rejects R, so the court finds no liability. The discursive dilemma is the fact that these two procedures, each of which looks like a faithful implementation of majority rule, deliver opposite verdicts on the same profile of individual judgments, while proposition-wise majority voting over the whole agenda delivers a set that is not even consistent.

(b) The discursive dilemma is, I think, best understood as Arrow's theorem in another vocabulary. Read a judge's accepting a proposition as preferring the outcome "true" to the outcome "false" for that proposition; a judge's judgment set then induces a preference ordering over the propositions on the agenda, and proposition-wise majority voting is majority rule applied to those orderings. The conditions line up neatly: universal domain requires that any profile of individually consistent and complete judgment sets be admissible; collective rationality is the analogue of the requirement that the social ordering be transitive and complete; and Arrow's independence of irrelevant alternatives is simply the requirement that the collective judgment on a given proposition depend only on the individual judgments on that proposition. On this reading, the impossibility result for judgment aggregation is a corollary of Arrow's theorem, and the discursive dilemma is the same phenomenon as Condorcet's paradox: in both cases majority rule cycles, and the collective outcome is one that no majority ever voted for.

(c) (i) Each judge accepts T, so the conclusion-based procedure yields T: the claim is time-barred, and unanimously so. The premise-based procedure aggregates over L, S and J: each ground is accepted by one judge and rejected by two, so the collective premise set is {not-L, not-S, not-J}, and the doctrine then yields not-T: the claim is not time-barred.
(ii) This example also shows why the premise-based procedure is the safe one. It simply conjoins the majority verdicts on the premises and lets the doctrine do the rest, so it can never produce an inconsistent collective judgment; inconsistency arises only when one aggregates over the conclusion as well, as proposition-wise majority voting does. That is the main argument in its favour.
(iii) Yes — the direction of the divergence depends on the logical form of the doctrine. With a conjunctive doctrine the premise-based route finds for the claimant and the conclusion-based route does not; with a disjunctive doctrine it is the other way around. So premise-based aggregation is not, as it is sometimes said to be, systematically the more demanding or the more claimant-friendly procedure.

(d) The defensible position is the premise-based one. It never generates inconsistency, it respects the reasons the judges actually give, and it lets the court issue an opinion that the parties can engage with; the conclusion-based procedure discards the reasons and leaves the outcome at the mercy of whoever decides what to put to the vote. A collegial court should therefore adopt the premise-based procedure as a standing rule.
