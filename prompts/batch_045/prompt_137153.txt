I'm on the staff of my school newspaper, and there's a rule in our style guide I've started to doubt. We're told to write chair instead of chairman, first-year instead of freshman, workforce instead of manpower, and singular they for any student whose gender we don't know; the stated reason is that male-marked words make men the default person and women a marked special case. Yet the same guide still lets us write man the ticket table, lets us say you guys to a mixed group, treats the he in old quotations as having meant everyone and therefore as not to be modernized when we quote it, and tells us to avoid phrases like female doctor on the ground that naming the gender marks the woman as the exception.

What does a policy like this have to assume about where a word's meaning lives — in its parts, in what speakers intend by it, in what the word has actually been used to do over time — for the substitutions to be worth making at all? Is the mix of changes and exemptions really one view, or does the guide need a different theory for each word: can manpower have gone generic while chairman has not, and can he include women in an old text but not in a new one? Which of these rules does any such theory actually force, and which does it merely permit, so that several theories would license the same page? Hofstadter's Person Paper satire, in which he rewrites an intolerant essay with neutral person-words swapped in for the gendered ones and the argument reads almost unchanged, seems to cut both ways — that the default lives in the reader's head rather than in the word, but also that swapping the words changes nothing else. Which way should it cut, and what would a style guide written by someone who thought meaning was fixed by speakers' intentions contain instead?
