I belong to a four-person reading group — two ex-physicists, one ML engineer, and me — that has spent the last few months working slowly through interventionist accounts of causation, after years of absorbing Pearl-flavoured causal inference secondhand from blogs and forum posts. Last week one of the physicists produced a case that none of us could settle, and I have found that every summary I try to write quietly begs the question in one direction or the other. So I would rather see the disagreement staged as an actual argument, with two people who each know the other side's machinery well enough to press on it.

The case, which I will call the Two-Way Chamber: a sealed chamber containing a handful of particles interacting only through perfectly elastic, deterministic, time-reversal-invariant dynamics — no friction, no dissipation, nothing radiating out, and nothing anywhere in the wider world that retains a record of the chamber's state at any time. Take two variables, A, the position of particle 1 at the earlier time, and B, the momentum of particle 2 at the later time. Every dependence you could want runs both ways: had A been different, B would have been different, and had B been different, A would have been different too, since the dynamics are reversible and the reverse counterfactual is perfectly well defined. Likewise, a hypothetical agent who could set A surgically could just as easily set B surgically, and in either direction the intervention would propagate to the other variable; there is no entropy gradient, no traces, and no asymmetry of overdetermination inside the chamber to break the tie. The asymmetry appears to come entirely from outside: creatures like us, with memories of the past and none of the future, can only ever reach in from one end. One further wrinkle, so that the two of them cannot win by ignoring it: the pathway from A to B runs through a deflector that would, if it fired, prevent B from occurring, and A's actual value is precisely what stops the deflector from firing — so the connection in the chamber is one of double prevention rather than direct production.

Please write a dialogue between Thea, who defends a Lewis-style counterfactual account (she is comfortable with structural-equation refinements of it, and with the idea that similarity between worlds does real work), and Ravi, who defends a Woodward-style interventionist account (he is comfortable with the manipulability and invariance machinery, and with the idea that do-calculus tracks something learnable from data plus experiments). Give them equal competence: neither should be a foil, and each should concede at least one thing that genuinely costs them something, rather than the two of them talking past each other. Somewhere in the exchange they should each say explicitly whether their disagreement is about singular causation in this one chamber or about the form of causal generalizations, and each should test their own account against the double-prevention structure in the chamber without being handed the right answer. I do not want them to collapse into a cheap agreement that causation is just in the map rather than the territory; if they converge on something perspectival, make them earn it by showing what each account would then still be committed to. Give me the dialogue as exactly five numbered exchanges, each exchange consisting of one substantive turn from each speaker labelled with their name, at most three sentences per turn, with no stage directions, no framing introduction, and no closing summary; and argue the case on its merits, since I do not need any references or suggested reading.
