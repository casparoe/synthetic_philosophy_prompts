I'm applying for my department's summer research grant: eight weeks of funding, with a deliverable of one paper of about 8,000 words to be presented at a fall workshop on metaphysics and the social sciences. I'd like you to draft the proposal itself: a single-spaced page, roughly 900 words. Please include no bibliography and no citations — I will supply those myself, and I would rather the space go to argument.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal. The debate over whether generations are real seems to me to have settled into an unsatisfying pattern. The skeptics run what I will call the variance argument: within-cohort dispersion swamps between-cohort differences; the birth-year boundaries are arbitrary and commercially motivated; measured differences are small and are confounded both with age and with the year of measurement; and the traits attributed to a generation are mostly traits that every generation has ascribed to its juniors. Defenders concede the crudity of popular generational schemes and point instead to shared formative exposure to large historical events. My suspicion is that both sides are arguing about the wrong unit. A generation could be at least three different things: an administrative partition of a birth-year continuum; a kind constituted by shared causal exposure to macro-events during some developmental window; or an interactive kind, one whose members partly become the way they are because the label is applied to them and because institutions have taken the label up. The variance argument looks devastating against the first of these, largely irrelevant to the third, and effective against the second only if that kind is required to deliver sharp boundaries and causal homogeneity, which it arguably need not.

Two further hunches, which I want you to use only if they survive your scrutiny, and to say so if they do not. First, the identification structure: since a person's age at a survey is just the year of measurement minus their birth year, differences attributed to cohort are not separable, from a single cross-sectional dataset, from maturational and period effects. I want the proposal to ask whether this is a remediable evidential problem or whether it reflects something metaphysical: that for formative-exposure kinds the causal pathways are entangled, so that the kind's causal powers are partly constituted by maturational and situational ones. Second, a notion I have been calling registration: a generational kind becomes causally projectible to the degree that institutions sort people by the label and allocate opportunities accordingly, in which case the differences cited as evidence for the kind are in part produced by the practice of invoking it. That yields, I think, a checkable consequence about the order in which differences and labels appear.

What I need from the proposal: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer in 8,000 words; why the existing positions leave that question open; the approach, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives (better statistics, conceptual analysis of the term, a study of ordinary usage, or a straight verdict on realism); the sections of the paper, and what I would actually do in each of the eight weeks; what would count as the project's succeeding, and honestly what would count as its failing, including the possibility that the threefold distinction is the entire contribution and the ontological verdict turns out thin; and where the scope is likely to blow up in eight weeks, with a recommendation about what to cut.

Finally, advise me on one framing decision: whether to include a short normative coda. Generational talk does real work in arguments about pensions, housing, and climate burdens, and if interactive structures are doing the causal work, then invoking generations in distributive arguments may manufacture the differences it cites. Is that a concluding section of this paper, or a separate one?

The committee is mixed — two metaphysicians and one sociologist — so the proposal must be legible to all three without hedging its claims.
