Hey — I keep seeing the Western Front in WWI used as the cute go-to illustration of an iterated prisoner's dilemma: two sides stuck facing each other for years, long shadow of the future, restraint starts paying, tit for tat more or less emerges on its own, and then the endgame unravels it when an offensive or the end of the war comes into view. I want more than the Christmas truce anecdote everybody reaches for.

Here's what I take the facts to be — tell me if im misremembering anything load-bearing: for long stretches in quiet sectors, small units opposite each other worked out informal restraint, stuff like shelling and sniping at predictable times, not firing on working parties or on men fetching water or using the latrines, letting the other side bury its dead. Officers on both sides sometimes tolerated it and sometimes cracked down; high command actively ordered trench raids, artillery harassment and frequent unit rotations specifically to stop fraternization. The system seems thickest in 1914-15 and in quiet periods, thinnest or absent right before big set-piece offensives, and basically gone once open warfare returned in 1918.

What I want is an actual disciplined mapping, not a vibe. Who the player is, what the options were on a given afternoon, what the payoffs plausibly were (including that the payoff for a sniper kill on one side may have been mostly retaliation risk rather than anything military), how many iterations the actors could actually expect, and what the discount factor was doing.

Then the parts I havent seen handled well:

- Players persist, soldiers dont. Platoons rotated through the line every few days and individuals cycled out entirely over weeks or months, so the thing that does the work in an iterated PD — the same two agents meeting again with memory of the last move — has to be carried by something else, if it is carried at all. Where does the memory live: unit identity and reputation, handover between relieving and relieved units, standing orders, gossip up and down the line? And be honest about what you'd count as evidence against whatever answer you give here.
- Backward-looking vs forward-looking. Was the restraint sustained by retaliation for what happened yesterday, or by expectation about tomorrow, or by something that is neither — a convention or norm that just became the local thing to do, so nobody was really facing a temptation in the first place? If it was the last one, the PD framing may be misdescribing the case entirely, and I want you to say so rather than force it to speak.
- High command as a third player. The generals arguably ran a metagame: raids and rotations look like deliberate attempts to shorten the shadow of the future and destroy the conditions for reciprocity, and the men were at that point playing a second game against their own officers. Is that still inside the model, or is it a different game, and does the theory have anything at all to say about a player who is trying to make the iteration stop?

Close by saying what the episode does to the theory: does it vindicate the iterated-PD-plus-reciprocity story about how cooperation evolves, or is it a case that story handles awkwardly — say because the cooperation here looks costless, or norm-driven, or enforced by third parties, none of which is what the model is built for?

Open with your bottom line in two sentences, then keep the whole thing under 700 words. No reading lists or citations, and please dont spend words on how moving the Christmas truce was.
