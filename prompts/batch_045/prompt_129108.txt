You are a political philosopher who works on global justice — in particular on the ethics of reparation, on what states owe to non-citizens, and on the bits of development economics that bear on whether transfers ever do any good. I would like you to think carefully rather than reach for a framework you are already fond of: start by brainstorming three or four candidate principles (a reparative one, a benefit-based one, a capacity or beneficence one, and anything else that suggests itself), note in a line or two what each would cost me in verdicts I'd have to give up, and only then commit to the one — or the ordered pair — that you think best survives what follows. Please write your answer in British English.

The background: I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation and I'm also in a reading group on global poverty, and I've noticed that my gut verdicts on concrete cases are far more settled than my view about what, if anything, rich countries owe poor ones — and I don't think the verdicts are consistent with each other. Here they are. (i) A former colonial power, where the ex-dictator's stolen money is also sitting in its banks: I think that money must be handed back, and that this is restitution rather than aid, but I also think something further is owed on top of it — less than campaigners claim, more than nothing. (ii) A Nordic country with no colonial or other historical connection to a West African recipient: I think it still owes something, and I feel this nearly as strongly as in (i) even though I can't point to anything it did. (iii) A rich country whose health service recruits nurses trained at a poor country's public expense: I think it owes compensation to that country's health system, but I don't think the individual nurse owes anything to anyone, and I don't think we owe anything to the particular patients who died for want of a nurse — which seems odd, since they are the ones who were harmed. (iv) A recipient government that would spend a transfer on a presidential jet: I think the duty survives and has to be routed around the government, and I'm uneasy because routing it around the government weakens that country's own democratic institutions. (v) Compare two recipients, one poor mainly because of its own government's policy choices, one poor mainly because it is landlocked and malarial: I feel we owe less in the first case and more in the second — and yet when I picture the citizens living under the bad government I feel we owe them more, not less. (vi) Debt: I think we must cancel debt run up by a dead dictator, and I don't think we must cancel debt run up by an elected government that wasted it on a white elephant — even though in neither case did the people now paying borrow the money or benefit from it. (vii) Climate: I think rich countries owe a small island state for damage from emissions that were lawful and largely unknowing at the time, and I also think a poor country that is itself now a large emitter has an undiminished claim, which strikes me as inconsistent. (viii) Where the responsible state has since broken up, I think the obligation tracks whoever still holds the benefits — the corporate charters, the pension funds, the central bank reserves — rather than whoever signed the treaties. (ix) Where the recipient's own elite park their money in our banks, I feel less is owed to the country's treasury but not one penny less to its poorest citizens. (x) Where the recipient's elected government declines the transfer outright, I feel the duty does not lapse but that we may not go around them. (xi) Where our own earlier aid made things worse — a dam, or budget cuts pressed on a ministry in the 1990s — I feel we owe more, and I feel this more strongly than anything else on my list.

So: give me a principle, or a small set of them, stated precisely enough to use at a committee table, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, verdicts on two or three cases I haven't listed (your choice, but choose ones that would embarrass the principle), and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why I should give them up rather than it. Three things I especially want addressed. First, in (i) and (iii) I find myself expressing what is owed to a government or to a health system, and in (ix) to persons — so who is the bearer of the claim, and does the answer change the size of the duty? Second, is the duty discharged in money or in policy? I have a strong feeling that the largest thing we owe in (iii) is visa access and not cash, and that nobody counts it because it never appears on an aid ledger. Third, the case I keep going back and forth on: a country that is now upper-middle income, with a space programme and a billionaire class, whose rural poor are worse off than the average citizen of the poorest countries — do we owe them anything, is it the same kind of thing we owe in (i), and does the existence of that space programme defeat the claim or deepen it?
