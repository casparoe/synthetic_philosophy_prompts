I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole on nuclear waste and now I can't stop thinking about it. As far as I can tell, the deal is this: in the US, the people running the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant were told they had to plan for keeping humans away from the site for something like ten thousand years, and a team going back to the early 1980s was set up to figure out how you warn someone that far in the future. The 1993 Sandia report they produced recommended messages at four levels of complexity — starting with something that just says, roughly, a human-made thing is here, then that it's dangerous, then the actual details, then full written records — and it sketched physical designs with names like Landscape of Thorns, Menacing Earthworks and Forbidding Blocks: giant spikes, black slabs, earth shaped like lightning bolts, whole landscapes made deliberately unpleasant so that nobody would want to farm or live there. The sample text they wrote to be translated is strange too: it says in effect that this is a message and part of a system of messages, that nothing valued is here, that the danger is an emanation of energy and is still present in your time as in ours. They even got the text translated into a handful of major languages plus Navajo. And in Finland, if I remember right, there's a deep repository planned on a timescale more like a hundred thousand years, and people there argue about whether marking it at all is a good idea, because a marker is also an advertisement that something interesting is buried here.

I'm not an academic, I've just been reading about this on my own, and what I keep coming back to is that this whole exercise is a forecasting problem wearing a hard hat. To decide what kind of marker to build you have to predict something about the next ten thousand years, and the only evidence you have is the last few thousand. So you reach for reference classes: how long do languages last, how long do writing systems stay readable, how long do religions or institutions or monuments keep carrying a meaning. But I can't tell what the practice is actually committed to versus what it just happens to fit with, and I can't tell if the parts are even consistent with each other. The one that bugs me: the same plans assume we'll keep records, keep guardians, keep repainting and re-carving things for some centuries of active institutional control, and at the same time assume that no institution of ours will survive long enough to matter. Those seem to pull in opposite directions. Also, the ten thousand years itself is weird — it's not obviously a forecast of anything, it looks more like the rough length of recorded history rounded off, and I have no idea if that's a reference class of one or a defensible choice.

Can you reconstruct the implicit theory behind this practice for me? I mean: what does a project like this presuppose about how you forecast from the past, about which reference class is the right one, and about whether the outside view or the inside view should be driving the design? What does it quietly rule out — does it, for instance, have to assume that distant future people are people we owe something to, and does it have to give up on the thought that whoever comes later will be richer and smarter and will simply handle it? Where is the practice underdetermined, meaning that several quite different views about the future would all license building basically the same spikes? And one more thing I want your verdict on, because it's my own idea and I suspect it's not as clever as it feels: instead of trying to push a *meaning* forward through ten thousand years, which seems to require predicting meaning systems, why not switch the reference class entirely and design for the class of things people dig up and puzzle over — make the site legible to a future archaeologist rather than readable to a future reader, on the theory that curiosity and digging are far more stable across history than any language or convention. Does that actually escape the forecasting problem or does it just smuggle in different commitments of its own, like that there will still be archaeologists and that a found object beats a understood warning?
