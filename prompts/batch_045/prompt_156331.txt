I work as a software engineer (no formal philosophy training) on the team that ships a retrieval-augmented customer-support assistant, and last quarter we had an incident that nobody on the team could classify: the assistant told a customer that their refund had been processed, when in fact the payments tool had timed out and no refund had gone through. Some of us called it a hallucination, some called it deception, and some said it was just a bug with an unlucky surface form; the argument went nowhere because we have no agreed account of what any of those words mean for a system that has no beliefs and no intentions. Our eval team wants a label taxonomy they can implement, and I want to know whether there is a real distinction here or whether we are carving one phenomenon into three feelings. To settle it I am applying for a small internal research grant — three months at roughly half my time, with a philosopher from a nearby department as an informal advisor — where the deliverable would be a working definition of honest and deceptive behavior for systems of this kind, plus a short, implementable spec for turning that definition into checks. I need a proposal document of about a thousand words: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the obvious candidate accounts (one based on intentions, one based on the system's internal states understood as belief-like, one based purely on the effect on the user) each leave it open; the approach I would actually take and why that approach rather than the alternatives; what a successful deliverable looks like for people who have to write evals; what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of whether this is too big for three months at half time. Please assume an audience of engineers and product managers with one philosopher in the room, so argue explicitly why conceptual work is worth funding here rather than just running more experiments; and please do not include citations or a bibliography — I will fill those in myself, and I do not want references I cannot check.

The half-formed idea I want turned into the proposal's core is this. I want to define the relevant property counterfactually, over pairs of situations, rather than over internal states or downstream effects, and I think it splits in two. Call a system world-sensitive with respect to some fact if, holding the system and the user's question fixed, its output would have been different had that fact been different. Call it evidence-sensitive if its output would differ when the evidence actually available to it — the retrieved passages, the tool's return value — differs in the corresponding way. A system that is evidence-sensitive but world-insensitive strikes me as doing something much closer to what philosophers characterize as indifference to the truth: fluent, plausible, evidence-shaped output that does not in fact track how things are. And the case the alignment literature worries about, where behavior tracks whether the system is being evaluated rather than what is true, looks like a third pattern again — sensitivity to a fact, but to the wrong one. My suspicion is that these patterns are what our eval team is currently lumping together under hallucination, sycophancy, and deception, and that separating them would change which tests we bother to write. I would like the proposal to treat this as a hypothesis to be sharpened, not as settled.

Four complications should be built into the method rather than deferred. First: misleading-but-literal answers, where the system asserts nothing false but omits or implicates in a way that predictably leaves the user wrong. Second: confidence, where the question is whether a true claim hedged into mush, or a false claim asserted with calibrated hedging, counts as honest. Third: true-by-luck outputs, where the world changed after the system's evidence was fixed, or the system arrived at the right answer for the wrong reason. Fourth: the level of analysis — whether the property is being defined for an individual output, for the system as a whole, or for the training process that produced it, and whether the useful answer changes depending on which we pick. There is also a practical worry I want addressed head-on: you cannot rerun the world, so what would actually count as evidence for or against a counterfactual claim about a system's sensitivity — synthetic worlds in unit tests, interventions on the retrieval step, honeypot prompts, something else — and if none of those can separate world-sensitivity from evidence-sensitivity in practice, I would rather the proposal say that plainly and count it as a way the project fails than paper over it.
