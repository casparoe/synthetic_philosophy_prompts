I write novels — mostly a slippery autofiction that keeps getting shelved as literary fiction, a category I've never once heard anyone define — and I run a very small, very low-stakes podcast where I get one guest on to argue about whichever idea my writing group has been circling all month. Next week's guest is a Wittgensteinian who holds that family resemblance isn't a theory of concepts at all but a reminder: the demand for a real definition is the thing that has to be dissolved, not satisfied. Give me eight or nine questions that don't let her just re-explain the remarks on games in the Investigations. I definately want one on whether family resemblance quietly collapses into a cluster or disjunctive theory the moment you ask which resemblances count — if any resemblance will do, isn't the claim trivial?; one on whether the novel, or genre generally, is a family-resemblance concept given that its borders are policed by prizes, workshops and bookstore shelving rather than by ordinary use; one on a case I made up: a village whose word we translate as game covers chess, lawsuits and flirting but not football, with total agreement on every particular case — do they share our concept, and how would we even tell; one on whether language-game talk can explain how anyone ever learns a word without smuggling back the rules it was meant to replace; and one on what, if anything, would count against the view. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, since her move is always to say that I'm still asking for a theory.
