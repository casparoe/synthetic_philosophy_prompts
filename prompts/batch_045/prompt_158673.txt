I keep going back and forth on money pump arguments, so here are my verdicts on a set of cases — I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Please answer in British English.

1. I prefer Lisbon to Crete, Crete to Oslo, and Oslo to Lisbon (sun, food, cost — a different reason each time). A travel agent charges me £1 to change my booking and I go round the loop twenty times, ending with Lisbon and £60 worse off. Something's wrong with me here. But I wouldn't pay more than about a tenner to fix it, and if I'd spotted the loop at the first swap and just said no, I think I'd have been fine.

2. Same loop, but the agent wipes my memory between swaps, so I can't plan to refuse the first one and I can't learn from it. I lose £100. I don't want to call this version of me irrational at all — it feels like bad luck plus a nasty dealer, even though my preferences are exactly the ones from case 1.

3. I sell my record collection for £20 because right now I'd rather have the cash, knowing that once I've done the course I'm starting I'll want it back and will pay £40 for it. I want to become that person. End state: same records, £20 down. Fine — not exploitation, a good trade.

4. Every night at 11pm I'd pay to change tomorrow's plan, every morning I'd pay to change it back. I know this about myself, I don't endorse either reversal, and the fix I want is a rule, not different tastes.

5. An agent shows me one holiday brochure at a time and I always want whatever's currently in front of me, so I pay £1 a go to switch and end up back where I started, £10 poorer. My preferences at any single moment are perfectly orderly — no cycle anywhere — and I'm still drained.

6. Three flatmates vote on the same three holidays and the majority cycles. Is the flat irrational, or is that just a fact about voting?

The principle I've been working with is: you're irrational iff there's a pump against you that you couldn't defuse by planning ahead. Case 2 breaks that from one side (can't defuse it, yet I won't call the person irrational) and case 5 from the other (drained, but there's no incoherent preference to point at). Can it be repaired — maybe as a claim about what policy you could adopt and endorse rather than about your preferences — and what would the repaired version say about someone who knows they're cyclic and adopts a blanket never-swap rule that costs them one genuinely good trade? And be honest about which of my verdicts I'd have to give up.
