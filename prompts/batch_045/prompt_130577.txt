I work as a backend engineer and I spend most of my time in a large legacy system that nobody on staff fully understands. Over a few years of that, I have talked myself into a fairly specific version of the Chesterton's-fence idea, and I now apply it outside work as well, to laws and institutions. I would like it stress-tested properly, because I suspect I have refined it into something that nothing could dislodge, and I would rather find that out from someone else.

The position, stated as precisely as I can:

The parable: a reformer finds a fence across a road, can see no point in it, and proposes to tear it down; the conservative reply is that if he cannot say what the fence is for, he is not entitled to remove it — he should go and find out first. I hold that this reply is not reverence for tradition and not the empirical claim that old arrangements are probably wise. It is a consequence of three structural facts about any arrangement that has survived inside a system nobody fully understands.

1. Latency. The harm caused by removing an arrangement can take a long time to show up, and the people who removed it are often not on the scene when it does.
2. Asymmetry. Restoration is typically much more expensive than preservation, and sometimes impossible: you can rebuild the fence, but not the hedge that grew around it.
3. Selection. Arrangements that genuinely did nothing tend to have been removed already, by earlier reformers with less scruple. So the fact that an arrangement is still here raises the probability that something is holding it up, even when nobody can say what.

The rule I actually use — call it the latency-weighted Chesterton rule — says: the strength of the presumption against removing an arrangement scales with (expected time-to-detection of the resulting harm) x (cost of restoring it) / (cost of finding out, right now, what it does). The burden is discharged by producing an account of the arrangement's present causal role, not a story about why it might once have made sense. Corollary: where removal is cheap, observable, and reversible — behind a feature flag, with monitoring and a rollback — the presumption drops to nearly nothing and the reformer's ignorance is no reason to wait. On my view, the classic fence argument has force entirely because most law and most legacy code is the opposite of that.

What I want from you:

What would count against this? I want defeaters of every kind you can think of — evidence, arguments, cases the rule cannot describe at all, and results showing that it collapses into a rival or is strictly worse than one — sorted from fatal down to merely awkward. In particular:

- Is my rule just expected-utility maximization with extra steps? If a rival says "estimate probability of harm, multiply by magnitude, compare against the benefit, act", what does the latency-weighted rule predict that this rival does not, and is there any observation on which the two disagree?
- Which defeaters would a defender of the plain, unweighted Chesterton presumption concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as changing the subject? I suspect my refinement and the original view come apart here, and I want to know where.
- Insulation. Moving from "do not remove what you cannot explain" to "the presumption scales with latency and reversibility" may have qualified the view into something unfalsifiable. Has it? And if it has, is that a defect in a principle of this kind, or is a burden-of-proof rule entitled to be insulated in the way a prior is?
- Please run the rule and its rivals against four cases and say where its answer looks wrong: (a) a 200 ms sleep in a payments pipeline that nobody remembers adding; (b) a tax exemption whose stated rationale was repealed a decade ago but which is still on the books; (c) academic tenure; (d) a constitutional provision that has never once been invoked.
- Two things would move me more than the rest. First, base rates: I have no idea how often a long-standing arrangement turns out to be genuinely vestigial, and my premise (3) looks hostage to that number. Second, whether premise (3) is simply false in politics, where arrangements can persist for decades because of who benefits from them rather than because they do any work.
- I also do not know whether distribution is a defeater or an implementation detail: the people who bear the cost of investigating are rarely the people who would bear the cost of the harm.
