I'm a second-year PhD student in computer science and I'm TA-ing our graduate complexity course this term; I'm also writing a short "what are we actually assuming?" note for our theory seminar's reading-group blog, and I keep running into a cluster of departmental habits that I can't tell are consistent with each other. First: everyone writes and talks about P, NP, and BQP without ever saying which machine model they have in mind, and when a student asks, the answer is that any reasonable model simulates any other with at most polynomial overhead, so the classes are the same — an invariance claim, not a definition. Second: the very same people use "efficient," "feasible," and "tractable" interchangeably with "polynomial time," and they are not confused about this — they will cheerfully write down n raised to the hundredth power, or a constant like ten to the sixtieth multiplying a quadratic, count it as efficient, and defend the move as a useful surrogate for what humans can actually finish. Third: when a visiting speaker last month mentioned supertask machines that perform infinitely many steps in a finite time and other proposed ways of deciding the halting problem, the room treated it as not worth discussing, and the reply afterwards was that computable just means Turing-computable, so there is nothing to investigate. Fourth: the same department is advertising a quantum computing colloquium whose blurb says quantum machines refute the extended Church–Turing thesis — that is, they reject polynomial-overhead simulability by a classical model — and nobody there is treated as a crank for saying so. That last pair is what bothers me: the invariance claim in the first habit and the claim being rejected in the fourth look like the same claim, and yet one is taught as bedrock and the other is celebrated as overthrown. And the third habit looks like it treats the base thesis as a stipulation, while the first treats it as a hard-won convergence result about independent formalisms.

So here is what I want: reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this practice. What must someone be assuming about the status of the Church–Turing thesis — as definition, as empirical or physical claim, as conceptual analysis, as something established by a Kreisel-style squeezing argument from informal premises about mechanical procedures, as something else — for all four habits to be reasonable? Then separate, carefully, what each habit strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with: does the habit of dropping the model name really need the full thesis, or only a much weaker simulation result; does identifying feasible with polynomial time presuppose anything about computability at all, or is it a wholly separate claim about resources, practice, and human lifetimes that merely piggybacks on the invariance result; and does the dismissal of hypercomputation require a different reading of the thesis than the model-independence of P does? Give me a verdict on whether the package is coherent — especially whether the definition-versus-discovery switch between the third and first habits is a real tension or only an apparent one — and say where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, in the sense that several incompatible readings of the thesis would license exactly the same behavior. I'm not asking for citations, history, or a reading list; I can find those. Work from the concepts. To make it concrete, my housemate Priya, who did a philosophy minor, insists that computable functions semantically like a natural-kind term: its reference is fixed by what the actual physical world allows, so the thesis is a contingent identity rather than a definition, and the right response to a claimed hypercomputer would be to run it and check, not to consult a definition. Tell me whether the practice strictly needs that reading, what would have to change in how papers are written and refereed if we took it seriously — would complexity claims have to be indexed to a physical theory, would physics belong in the assumptions section, what would count as evidence — and what would have to change if instead we adopted the definitional reading and treated a working hypercomputer as a reason to revise a convention rather than as a discovery or an impossibility.

Please organize the answer as: the reconstructed commitments, numbered; a strict-presupposition versus merely-convenient table or list for the four habits; the coherence verdict with the tensions named; the underdetermination section; and then, because I actually have to stand up at this seminar, the single sharpest question you think I should put to the instructor, the answer they are most likely to give, and the follow-up that makes that answer harder to rest in. One paragraph of scenario-free prose per numbered item is plenty; I want the argumentative structure, not a survey.
