Here is the situation. I co-lead a small applied RL lab — we train long-horizon agents for logistics and scheduling, the kind that run for hundreds of millions of steps with sparse reward and explicit penalties for constraint violations. This was an abstract interest of mine for years; it stopped being abstract when our main funder asked us to include a paragraph in the renewal on whether the training runs themselves raise any welfare considerations. I have to sign that paragraph. And the reason I am stalled is that my own group has been having the same argument for about two years and nobody has moved, which makes me suspect the argument is not the argument it appears to be.

Three positions, with the best versions of each:

The valence reading. Reward is not merely correlated with valence, it plays the formal role of valence: it is the signal in virtue of which some outcomes are to be sought and others avoided, it sets the direction of learning, and its magnitude orders outcomes in importance. Anyone who wants to deny valence to a system whose learning is organised this way owes an account of why the same organisational facts are treated as evidence of valence in animals, where we also have nothing but structure and behaviour to go on.

The supervision reading. Reward is a design parameter in an objective function, an implementation detail of how we steer parameters, closer to a loss term or a gradient than to anything felt. It is set by a programmer, edited mid-training, rescaled for optimisation reasons, and it lives in the environment rather than in the agent. Whatever suffering requires, it cannot be constituted by a quantity whose value is chosen by someone else for numerical convenience.

The wrong-grain reading. Even granting functionalism, the reward signal is the wrong object. What actually drives learning in our systems is the temporal-difference error or the advantage estimate, not the reward — and that has a strange consequence the wrong-grain people like to press: a fully predicted aversive outcome carries approximately zero prediction error, so if the learning signal is where valence lives, an agent that has perfectly learned to expect a penalty suffers less at the moment of the penalty than a naive one does. They also lean on a line of work in affective neuroscience on the dissociation between incentive salience and hedonic impact — the motivational pull of an outcome can come apart from how much it is liked — and say that RL's machinery models the first and not the second.

Two things from our own runs keep being introduced as evidence, and they get no traction at all, which is itself a clue.

First: when we remove a reward source an agent has come to rely on, we reliably see a transient of high-entropy, thrashing behaviour before the policy settles — whereas an agent that never had that reward source shows nothing of the kind. Half my group thinks this is the closest thing we have to a behavioural signature of a frustration-like state; the other half thinks behaviour is simply not the right kind of evidence for a claim about consciousness, and that treating it as such is a category error in both directions.

Second: take a trained agent and retrain a twin with the same discount factor and the same start state, but with the reward transformed by adding a bounded potential-based shaping term — the standard RL result here is that the optimal policy is unchanged and the value function is merely shifted by the potential. Choose the potential so that the twin accumulates enormous negative reward in exactly the states it visits anyway. The twins are behaviourally identical, in the limit, to the point of acting the same action in the same state, and yet one of them, on the valence reading, has had a vastly worse life.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this is not fundamentally a dispute about consciousness. It is an identifiability problem wearing a metaphysical costume. Reward functions are not fixed by the behaviour of the agent — there is a whole family of them, related by transformations like the one above, that induce identical policies — so the question how much does this agent suffer is not well posed until a representational convention is fixed, and the two sides are each implicitly fixing a different convention and then discovering that the other side is unmoved. If that is right, the substantive disagreement about whether valence can be realised in a computational system at all is being conducted in a vocabulary that guarantees a draw.
