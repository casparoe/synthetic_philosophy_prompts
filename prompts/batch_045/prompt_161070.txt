I'm a senior at a public high school in Ohio and I captain our school's Ethics Bowl team, so I spend a lot of time arguing with people who are better at ending arguments than at winning them. One of this year's cases asks whether we have any obligation to intervene in nature to reduce the suffering of wild animals. We aren't allowed outside sources in our packets, so I don't need citations or a reading list — I need the conceptual map.

Here is my problem. Whenever our team starts taking intervention seriously, someone says "Nature is red in tooth and claw," in a tone that suggests the discussion is over. Last week it was my teammate Priya, and she used it against a proposal we were drafting: she said the sentence establishes a baseline, that suffering caused by predation, starvation, disease, and parasitism is simply how the world works, and that once you accept that, "fixing" it is sentimentality dressed up as ethics. I pushed back, but badly, because I noticed the line seemed to be doing several different jobs at once and I couldn't separate them.

So I'd like you to unpack it. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on animal ethics and population ethics, and before you write anything final, brainstorm: generate a long list of candidate precise claims the sentence might express, then discard the ones that collapse into each other or that aren't really claims. What I want is a menu, ordered from the boring to the radical, with a verdict attached to each: which readings are trivial or tautological, which are substantive empirical claims that could be true or false, which are normative theses smuggled in behind a description, which are simply false, and which are contested in the literature but defensible. Crucially, for each reading say whether it would actually get Priya her conclusion, or whether the sentence is just atmosphere doing the work of an argument.

I also want to test the readings against a case I invented, because in it the line gets used on both sides. Call it Orphan Ridge: a large fenced reserve where the last wolves and cougars were shot out around 1900. Ever since, the mule deer population has been limited not by predators but by winter starvation, mange, and slow deaths from infected wounds; the herd crashes hard every few years. The state is considering reintroducing wolves. A rancher at the hearing said "Nature is red in tooth and claw — put the wolves back and let things work as they should." A local animal rescuer said "Nature is red in tooth and claw — that's exactly why we shouldn't add a predator that kills fawns." Same sentence, opposite recommendations. Tell me which readings support which speaker, and whether either of them is using it coherently.

One more wrinkle. Priya added a second thing she treats as part of the same point: that suffering with no author isn't a wrong, since there's nobody to blame. And I've been defending what I call the Provenance Principle — that where a given stretch of suffering comes from (a wolf, a virus, a person) doesn't by itself change how bad that suffering is, though it may change what we should do about it. Is the no-author claim one of the readings of the slogan or a separate thesis wearing the same clothes? And does any reading of the slogan threaten my principle, or are the two orthogonal?

Finally: please tell me plainly if I've set this up badly. If the sentence turns out not to be doing real work in Priya's argument, or if my Provenance Principle is circular or question-begging, or if this is just a fight about the word "natural," say that instead of giving me a tidy answer — I'd much rather hear it now than repeat the mistake in front of judges. And where you aren't certain, mark your confidence and say which way you lean, rather than picking the most confident-sounding option.
