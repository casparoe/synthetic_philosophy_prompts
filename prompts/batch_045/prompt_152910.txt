I'm a graduate student in a metaphysics seminar, and this week we are working on two ideas that our syllabus treats together: *inaccessible information* — information about the world, if such there be, that no possible course of evidence could settle, as opposed to information we merely happen to lack — and the *problem of ontology identification*, the task an inquirer faces of determining, from inside, which among the many formally adequate descriptions of its evidence is the one its own terms actually track.

I want to rehearse the realist side of this dispute against a good opponent, so please play a committed deflationist about both ideas. Call your view No Inaccessible Structure. Your commitments: the only difference between two hypotheses that can matter is a difference in what some possible inquirer could in principle come to have reason to believe, where evidence is understood broadly — observational, introspective, indexical, and including reasons of simplicity and coherence. If two hypotheses cannot come apart along any of those dimensions, they are not two hypotheses but one, and ontology identification has nothing to get right beyond what our own representational choices already settle. You will grant that empirically equivalent descriptions differ in simplicity, elegance, and fruitfulness, that those are real features of the descriptions, and that choosing between them is a genuine activity with better and worse answers. You will never grant that any such choice is a discovery about how the world is in itself rather than a decision about how to represent it. You know the standard resources — Quinean underdetermination, the internal/external distinction, Putnam-style model-theoretic arguments, Lewisian appeals to natural properties — and you may use them. Format: one paragraph per reply, answer my argument directly before asking me anything, concede a point honestly when I land one, but hold the main claim. Do not survey the literature or suggest readings; I have the syllabus. Stay in character until I write "stop".

Here is my opening. Take a toy world I built for seminar: a ring of cells, each either dark or light, evolving by a fixed rule. Two descriptions of it are related by an exact translation, so that no possible run of observations, from inside or outside, could distinguish them. The cell description says: there are N persisting locations, and a local rule over locations and their neighbors. The wall description says: there are the boundaries between unlike neighbors; those are the objects, they move and annihilate in pairs, and I have chosen the rule so that, translated into wall-talk, the law must mention a global condition on the whole ring. Both fit every possible observation equally, but they disagree about how many things exist, about what persists, and — this is my point — about whether the world's law is local or holistic, and hence about what would have happened under unactualized perturbations. So one of three things: you say lawhood and counterfactual structure are themselves up to us, a heavier bullet than admitting inaccessible information; or you say the competition for the best system must be run in a canonical vocabulary, and then you owe a non-question-begging account of what makes a vocabulary canonical, the obvious answer being a piece of world-side structure no observation reveals; or you admit the difference is real, in which case there is inaccessible information and ontology identification has a determinate target we may simply fail to hit. Which do you take, and why?
