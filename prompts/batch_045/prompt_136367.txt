I teach in a philosophy department and co-convene a graduate seminar with colleagues from computer science on what they call the alignment problem and what I am increasingly convinced is several different problems wearing one name. Last week a student proposed that the right target is not merely systems that behave well but systems that have the right attitudes toward us — that they genuinely care about human beings — and that this, rather than any cleverer specification technique, is what would make advanced systems safe. A senior colleague closed the session with a line that circulates endlessly in AI-safety writing and for which I have never been able to run down a citation:

The AI does not hate you, nor does it love you, but you are made out of atoms which it can use for something else.

He took it to settle two things at once. First, that the student's proposal rests on a confusion, because what a system feels about us is irrelevant to whether it is safe. Second, that "the alignment problem" is therefore a single, fairly narrow problem — specify a goal whose pursuit does not consume us — rather than a family of problems (outer versus inner alignment, intent versus impact, what we can specify versus what the system ends up pursuing, whose values, misuse versus accident) with different structures and different prospects.

I want to put a disambiguation on the table before the next meeting, and I would like it done properly. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that one-liner could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical, and give a verdict on each: which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are contested, and which are unsupported or straightforwardly false.

My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the line trades on an equivocation between two notions of indifference — indifference as the absence of any attitude toward us, and indifference as treating us as a mere means — and that the argument needs the second while the rhetoric gets its force from the first. If so, I would like to know which reading, if any, actually supports dismissing the "build a system that cares" proposal. Consider the pair of cases I plan to raise: a system with human flourishing as a final concern that nonetheless does not defer to us about what flourishing consists in, and a system with no attitudes at all whose final goal is small and satiable, say arranging matter within a sealed cubic meter. If both are coherent possibilities, then attitudinal indifference looks neither necessary nor sufficient for danger.

Three further questions. Does the line, on any reading, state a necessary truth about optimization, or only a contingent claim about resource-hungry goals under conditions of scarcity — and does that contingency expand or shrink if we allow trade, comparative advantage, and enforcement among systems? Does the line presuppose that the system has anything as clean as a final goal, and what becomes of the claim if it does not? And taking the readings together, do they suggest that we are looking at one problem with several names or several problems with one name?

Please answer in American English, and give me something I can hand out: a numbered menu with explicit verdicts, around eight hundred to a thousand words. No references or reading list needed; I want the analysis, not the literature.
