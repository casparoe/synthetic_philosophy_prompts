Retired field biologist here — forty years of small-mammal mark-recapture, a lot of time spent arguing with taxonomists, and now I'm reading a bit of philosophy of psychiatry on the side and getting annoyed with it in a way I can't quite pin down.

The thing I keep running into is Hacking's looping effects. As I understand the claim: you classify people under some psychiatric kind, the people so classified become aware of the classification, they change — how they experience themselves, what they do, what they report — and that feeds back so the kind itself isn't stable the way a natural kind is supposed to be. The writers I'm reading treat this as though it marks off human kinds as metaphysically special: not discovered but in some sense made, always sliding around under you.

My reaction is that this is a problem my field solved decades ago and it isn't metaphysics. A diagnostic category looks to me exactly like a taxon, and a diagnostic manual looks exactly like a dichotomous identification key — the sort of thing I used to run shrews and beetles through. The patient is the specimen, the criteria are the couplets, the clinician is the person with the hand lens, prevalence is a population estimate, and looping effects are just trap response: once a shrew has been caught and ear-tagged it is more likely to be caught again, and if you are naive about that you will overestimate survival and get your abundance wrong. We stopped using the plain Lincoln-Petersen style estimates and started modelling detection probability as a separate parameter so we could estimate abundance despite the reactivity. Nothing about the shrew became metaphysically slippery because of this. So: looping is a well-understood measurement nuisance with a known fix, and the philosophers are dramatising a calibration problem.

I've also got a second case that I think covers the more interesting half of it. Birders. Rare-bird alerts and phone apps changed where people go and what they report, which changed the checklists, which changed the published range maps, which changed what counts as an expected record, which changed where people go. The birds didn't change at all; the data-generating process did, and ecologists now spend a lot of effort correcting for observer effort and observer distribution. That looks to me like the "second-order" loop where the classifiers and their classifications move too, and it's still just a sampling problem.

So I want the analogy audited properly, because I suspect I'm being too smug.

Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the specimen, the key, the mark, the trap response, the population estimate, the sampling effort, the type specimen, the splitting-and-lumping fights, the renaming (as I understand it Asperger's got folded into a broader autism category, and the multiple-personality thing got renamed, which in my world is just synonymy and taxonomic revision).

Then the places it breaks. Some I can see coming: the specimens can read the key, they can want the label or want to avoid it, the label comes with money and legal standing and identity and other people in the same category, and — this is the one I keep coming back to — the specimens end up on the committee that revises the key. In my world nothing wants to be caught except by accident of bait, and no shrew has ever lobbied the taxonomic commission. Tell me which of those disanalogies actually carry weight for the conclusion and which are just noise.

And the part I really can't see my way through: in mark-recapture there is a latent abundance sitting behind the trap response, and the whole point of modelling detection probability is to estimate it despite the reactivity. Is there anything in the psychiatric case that could play that role? If there isn't, then the reactivity isn't a bias on an estimate, it's more like there being no stable quantity to estimate, and my analogy quietly assumes the thing that's in dispute. But if there is, I want to know what it is and whether anyone has tried to build the equivalent of a detection model for it.

Last thing: my guess is that categories differ a lot in how reactive they are, and that the interesting question isn't "human kinds vs natural kinds" but something like a reactivity spectrum with structural causes — how visible the label is, whether it's sought or imposed, whether it's stigmatising, whether there's an institution attached. Is that a real distinction or am I inventing it to save my analogy? And if my analogy is too broken to repair, tell me what a better one would be. Please don't send me to the literature — I'm not going to chase references, and my old library doesn't carry the journals anyway.
