hey — I'm in this seminar on rational choice and machine learning (half econ, half CS, everybody talking past each other) and we did revealed preference one week and inverse reinforcement learning the next, and I keep thinking theyre secretly the same move dressed up in different notation, but every time I try to say why in the pset I end up handwaving. Can you compare them properly for me?

Just so we're on the same page about what I mean. Revealed preference: you watch someone pick from a series of different menus or budget sets, you write down consistency conditions (the WARP / GARP style axioms), and if the data passes them there's a classical result saying you're entitled to posit a stable preference ordering — a utility function — that they were maximizing. IRL: you assume the demonstrator was acting roughly optimally for some reward function in an MDP whose dynamics you already know, and you search for a reward that makes their observed behaviour look near-optimal, so that you can carry that reward over and predict what they'd do in a new environment.

Here's the three-part case I keep banging my head against, featuring a hapless office worker called Sam.

1. Menu flips. Sam takes porridge over toast on days when only those two are out, but takes toast over porridge on days when eggs are also on the counter. Revealed preference calls that a flat inconsistency and says there's no stable ordering over these options. IRL says the state isn't just the menu — eggs changes what happens downstream — and once that's in the dynamics there's a perfectly good reward that makes both choices optimal. Who is being honest here, and who is buying consistency by re-describing the state until the data behaves?

2. The heavy door. Sam reliably takes the long corridor to the kitchen though the short one is on the map; I later find out the short door needs a shove and my dynamics model never represented that. IRL will cheerfully hand me a reward on which Sam dislikes the short corridor. Revealed preference never gets to be wrong in this particular way, because it has no dynamics model to be wrong about. Is that a point in its favour, or does it just show it's silent about everything interesting?

3. Trembling hands. Sam is a near-optimal planner who fumbles about ten percent of the time. Hard-optimality IRL reads the fumbles as preferences; the soft / Boltzmann versions can absorb them, but then the reward is only pinned down up to whatever, and there's the degeneracy worry that a reward which is zero everywhere makes every policy optimal, plus results showing you can add a potential-based term to a reward without changing which policy is optimal in the discounted infinite-horizon case. So what, exactly, got recovered? And is the thing IRL spits out even a preference in the economist's sense — something whose maximization is supposed to be what the agent is *aiming at* — or is it just a parameter that retrodicts motion?

Please be commited rather than even-handed: for each of the three, tell me who has the better warrant for saying "Sam really prefers X to Y", and who has produced the more useful object, and say why. Give the strongest thing the losing camp can say back, and flag where you're speculating. I don't need citations or a reading list, I need the argument.
