I'm drafting a paper on automation and the meaning of work — working title, "Who Works for Whom? Automation and the Direction of Subordination" — and I would be grateful for critical comments on the draft argument below. The draft is deliberately compressed; I've numbered the sections so you can refer to them.

**§1.** Two worries about automation tend to be run together: the displacement worry (that there will not be enough work, or enough income) and the meaning worry (that the work which remains will not be worth doing). The second is usually treated as a luxury concern that dissolves once the first is solved: secure the income floor, and the complaint that work has become hollow looks like nostalgia for toil. I want to deny this. The meaning worry is independent of the displacement worry. It survives full employment at decent wages, and it survives the arrival of genuinely agreeable leisure, because what automation threatens is not access to income or to free time but the availability of a certain practical role: being the one who determines, executes, and answers for some part of the world going one way rather than another.

**§2.** Start by splitting the phenomenon. *Substitutive* automation does the task (the self-checkout, the translation engine, the weld). *Prescriptive* automation tells you what to do next (pick-paths, routing, triage queues, dispatch). *Evaluative* automation scores the output (rating systems, QA scoring, model-based assessment of a worker's calls). These are usually discussed as one thing, but I think they attack three different conditions on work being meaningful: substitutive automation removes the exercise of skill; prescriptive automation removes authorship of the plan; evaluative automation removes what I'll call first-person authority over quality — the standing to be the first and competent judge of whether one's own work was done well. My claim is that the third is the deepest and the least discussed. Not because being scored is unpleasant, but because of what scoring does to self-assessment: the metric becomes the arbiter of something the worker previously took herself to be able to tell directly, and over time it does not merely measure her judgment but replaces the self-trust that made the judgment possible. (That last clause is an empirical claim, and I flag it as one.)

**§3.** When the three go together, what is left to the human worker is the residual class: the cases the system flags, escalates, or cannot classify. Two things follow. Formally, the residual task is constitutively the task where reasons run out — these are by construction the cases for which no rule was available — so the worker is left with maximal discretion and minimal support. Socially, it erodes what I call a community of judgment: skilled perception is learned and calibrated among peers who exercise the same discrimination on shared cases, but if the routine cases have been siphoned off, so has the shared training set, and the remaining workers become isolated exception-handlers with no common cases to reason from. This is why automation can make work easier and emptier at once — fewer decisions to make, but no structure of excellence and no peers to be excellent alongside.

**§4.** There is also, I want to say, an incoherence here, and not merely a loss. Accountability does not delegate the way a task does: you cannot hold the system responsible, and an organization is at best a defective address for blame and at worst a way of diffusing it until it settles nowhere. So accountability flows to whichever human is left standing, who now answers for outcomes she did not determine. Call that position a responsibility sink. The resulting practice is structurally defective in a specific way: we address demands for justification to someone who, by the hypothesis of the case, was not in a position to determine what happened. This is not alienation in anything like Marx's sense — estrangement from the product, the activity, one's species-being, and others — and it is not simply a low degree of autonomy. It is a mismatch between the presuppositions of holding someone responsible (control, or at least the capacity to have acted otherwise for reasons) and the actual causal and epistemic situation of the worker. That mismatch, I argue, is the genuine moral remainder of automation, the part that no redistribution of income touches.

**§5.** The obvious objection: none of this matters, because toil was never the source of meaning. Being liberated from required labor is the point of the whole exercise, and meaning is to be sought outside work — in art, friendship, politics, contemplation. My reply, which is the part of the paper I trust least, I call the capability-erosion reply. The capacities in question — framing ends, holding oneself to standards, being answerable — are not innate dispositions that survive indefinite disuse; they are trained by being exercised in a domain where failure carries costs. Remove the site of exercise without supplying another, and the capacities thin out; the leisure that was supposed to be meaningful then has nothing to be meaningful with. I want to concede immediately that this shows at most that the capacity is not costlessly portable. It does not show that work is the only school for it, or the best one.

**§6.** But not all automation destroys meaning, and any account that implies otherwise is dead on arrival: the power saw and the spreadsheet are the oldest story in the world. So I need a test. Here it is. Ask, of the actual practice, whether the machine is subordinate to the worker's plan or the worker is subordinate to the machine's outputs. The carpenter's saw is subordinate: she sets the end, decides the cut, and can tell whether the cut was good. The warehouse picker's scanner is the other way round: the system sets the sequence and the pace, and its output is not advisory but binding. So: automation degrades meaning just when it inverts the direction of subordination — when the worker's reasons come to sit downstream of the system's outputs rather than the other way round. My worry about this test, which I'll state rather than resolve, is that it may be nothing more than autonomy under a new name. What I would want to say against that is that it is narrower and more diagnostic: deference is compatible with meaning, since the junior surgeon can follow the senior surgeon's plan and learn from it, whereas inversion is not; and the test locates the question in a relation between two agents rather than in how much choice a worker happens to have.

**What I would like from you.**

Please comment on the argument rather than on the prose, and please argue conceptually: I am not asking for readings, citations, or bibliographies, and I would rather you not recommend any. Specifically:

1. Is the threefold typology in §2 exhaustive, and do the three conditions really come apart? Can you describe a case in which one is removed while the others are intact, and does my classification of it seem forced?
2. Apply the direction-of-subordination test in §6 to three cases and tell me whether it returns the verdict you would independently want, and whether it returns it for the reason I give: (a) a radiologist who reads alongside a model that flags likely lesions, on cases where she has no independent way to check the model's accuracy; (b) a long-haul driver whose route, rest stops, and speed envelope are set by a dispatch system, but who still physically drives; (c) a composer who generates raw material with a model and then selects, edits, and arranges it.
3. §4 rests on the idea that the mismatch is *incoherent* rather than merely regrettable. But our responsibility practices already hold people answerable for outcomes they did not control — strict liability, vicarious liability, commanders' responsibility for subordinates. If that is right, is the mismatch unremarkable after all, and if not, what distinguishes it?
4. Does the capability-erosion reply in §5 beg the question against the objection it is answering? Say precisely where, if so.
5. Name the single objection you think most threatens the paper as a whole, and say which section is weakest and why. Also flag any place where I have smuggled in an empirical assumption and then argued as if it were conceptual.
6. Finally, an interest test: state the paper's thesis in one sentence as you understand it. If you cannot, tell me where it becomes evasive. And tell me whether the thesis is genuinely distinct from the familiar complaint that automation is alienating — if you think it collapses into something more familiar, say what it reduces to, conceptually, without pointing me to a text.
