I am trying to reconstruct the implicit philosophy of a clinical and legal practice, and I would rather you argue from the protocol as I state it than from the exact content of any particular hospital guideline, professional statement, or statute. Treat the protocol as stipulated.

The setting is patients who survive severe brain injury and emerge from coma into a state in which they open their eyes, cycle through sleep and waking, and show reflex responses, but produce no reproducible response to command. Clinicians attach a label to this state — in many services, *unresponsive wakefulness syndrome* — and institutional consequences follow from the label: the patient is charted as lacking awareness; decisions about life-sustaining treatment are made by surrogates, guided by whatever the patient said or wrote before the injury; and nothing the patient currently produces counts as an expression of will. The protocol I want examined has, roughly, these features:

1. A finding of awareness requires a reproducible, voluntary response to command, observed behaviourally and confirmed by independent examiners on separate occasions. Single responses do not count; unreliable ones do not count.
2. In a minority of these patients, asking them to perform a motor imagery task (for instance, to imagine a particular physical activity) produces sustained, task-specific activation in the relevant cortical area, and in some of those cases the signal can be trained into a yes/no channel. Under the protocol, such decoded neural signals count only as a reason to search harder for behaviour. They cannot on their own ground a finding of awareness, and they cannot be used to elicit the patient's current wishes for the purposes of a treatment decision.
3. Behavioural and physiological signs that would normally be read as pain — grimacing, tearing, tachycardia — are treated as sufficient reason to administer analgesia during procedures, but not as evidence of awareness.
4. When a behavioural channel is eventually found — one reliable eye movement, say — the diagnosis is revised retroactively, and the team then speaks of the patient as having been conscious during the period in which no channel existed.

Two features of this puzzle me. Clause 3 uses behaviour as evidence of a subjective state in one context while clause 1 refuses to treat any non-behavioural evidence as evidence of subjective states at all. Clause 4 seems to concede that the patient's awareness was there before the behaviour appeared, which suggests behaviour is merely evidential for the practice and not constitutive of what it ascribes — yet clause 2 then refuses to count a different kind of evidence whose reliability has, on the face of it, been demonstrated by the very cases in clause 4.

So I want the implicit theory reconstructed:

- What view of mind, or of evidence for mind, does the protocol only make sense under? Is it a form of behaviourism on which the ascription of consciousness is fixed by what can be elicited from the patient, or is it a more modest view on which behaviour is simply the only evidence institutionally competent and reliable enough to act on? Say which rival views the protocol quietly rules out — for instance, any view on which consciousness is a state of the organism detectable in principle by direct neural measurement — and say whether it rules them out by its logic or only by its rules of procedure.
- Distinguish what the protocol strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. In particular, I have heard it suggested that the protocol is not behaviourist at all but rests on a view about addressability: that to treat someone as a subject is to be able to put a question to them and be answered, and that a decoded signal does not put the patient into that relation. Take a position on whether that reconstruction explains the clauses better than the behaviourist one does, or whether it is only a rationalization of them.
- Tell me where the protocol is philosophically underdetermined, so that several quite different theories of consciousness would license exactly these four clauses, and tell me which of the stipulated clauses is doing the most philosophical work — that is, which one I could change with the largest effect on what the practice presupposes.
- Locate this against the Turing test, since the protocol looks like an operational test for mind with the channel fixed in advance. There is an asymmetry I want addressed: in the original test we use behaviour to attribute mind to a candidate whose underlying nature we are deliberately ignoring, whereas here we already know the candidate is a human organism of the relevant kind. Does the defensibility of a behavioural criterion depend on that difference? And does the moral asymmetry of the setting — a false negative here means treating a conscious subject as if she were not there while decisions about her death are being made, a false positive means the opposite error — bear on which view of mind we should adopt, or is it a separate question from what the practice presupposes?
- Finally, say what would concretely have to change in the protocol if the implicit view were replaced by a straightforward total-evidence view, in which neural decoding is admitted on equal terms with behaviour. I am interested in the practical shape of the change — what *reproducible* would have to mean once the evidence is not behavioural, what becomes of surrogate decision-making and of advance directives, who would bear the risk of error — and in whether the protocol could survive that replacement unchanged in most cases. As a further wrinkle, suppose the much-publicized reports of lucid, structured experience during resuscitation from cardiac arrest, at a time when the patient is behaviourally unresponsive by every available measure, are roughly right: does that supposition force a change in the implicit theory, or can the protocol absorb it?

Please organize the answer around what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits with, where its parts pull in different directions, and what would have to change under the rival view.
