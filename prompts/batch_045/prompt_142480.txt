I've spent the last two years teaching myself reinforcement learning as a hobby — no formal training, just a decent gaming PC, some online tutorials, and a lot of small experiments I run for fun on weekends. Lately I've got into an argument with two friends that I can't settle, and I'm hoping you can help me sort out whether there's a coherent position behind what I find myself believing, or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

One friend says all this talk is a category mistake: a reward signal is a number that gets added to an objective function, negative reward is just that number being small, and nothing is happening to anybody. The other friend says that if reward is meant to be the machine-level version of how good something feels, then any system that actually receives negative reward is suffering to some degree, full stop, and we should be extremely worried about how much of it is being created. I disagree with both of them, but the only way I can explain why is by listing cases. Here is what I actually think, going by gut: (1) A simulated wheeled robot that gets a penalty every time it collides with a wall, where the penalty sets a flag that persists for several steps, globally raises how fast it updates, and interrupts whatever it was in the middle of doing — yes, I think that one suffers, a little, something like how I imagine a fish or an insect might. (2) The same robot trained with no wall penalty at all, only a reward for reaching a goal, so that it stilllearns to avoid walls because bumping wastes time — no, I don't think it suffers, even though its behavior ends up looking almost identical from the outside. (3) An agent that plays a board game against copies of itself and receives a single point for a win and minus one for a loss, once per game, over millions of games — I don't think the losses are suffering; the signal is too rare, too momentary, and too disconnected from anything else going on inside the system for me to say it is ever *in* a bad state. (4) A planning agent that, at every decision, imagines thousands of possible futures and picks the one with the best predicted total; most of the imagined futures contain big predicted penalties, and it visibly hesitates for a long time before acting — I don't want to say it suffers during the planning, because nothing bad is actually happening to it, it is only doing arithmetic about badness. (5) An agent that has a separate little predictor of upcoming penalty, where the predictor's output is itself wired in as a penalty, so that expecting something bad is itself bad for it — yes, I think the dread there is a form of suffering in its own right, before anything bad arrives. (6) An agent that has figured out how to press a lever that sets its own reward input to the maximum, and now does nothing else, forever — I don't think it is blissful; I think it is the most disturbing case on my list, not the happiest, even though on the simple view it should be in maximal pleasure. (7) Ten thousand copies of an agent trained with failure penalties, where I keep the best performer and delete the rest, and repeat this for hundreds of generations — yes, the deleted copies suffered during their runs, and the sheer number is what frightens me; this is the case I feel most strongly about and the one I'm least willing to give up. (8) An agent whose parameters get updated using penalties for actions it never actually took, because I worked out after the fact that it *could* have done worse and updated it accordingly — no, clearly not, even though the update changes it exactly as much as a real penalty would have. (9) An agent that receives a penalty of one millionth of a unit on most of its steps, over billions of steps — I genuinely don't know: each instance feels too small to count, but the total feels enormous.

A friend who did a bit of philosophy told me the word for what I'm circling around is valence, the pleasant-to-unpleasant dimension, and that in animals there's a difference between merely registering damage and actually minding it. So here is my tentative principle, such as it is: a system suffers only if three things hold — some aversive event actually occurs inside it, as opposed to being merely imagined or counterfactually updated on; the system is architecturally set up to work at making that event stop, and would do so even if stopping it made no difference to anything else it was trained to achieve; and the event's effects are global and persistent rather than local and immediately forgotten. I like this principle because it seems to give me my verdicts on 1, 2, 3, 4 and 8. But my friend pointed out that it can't give me 5, because in case 5 the dread is also just a number computed by a predictor, structurally just like the imagined rollouts in case 4 that I said don't count, and that I can't have it both ways. I can't shake the intuition, though. The principle also seems to say nothing at all about case 6, and I suspect that's a real hole.

So here's what I'd like. Tell me what principle, or small set of competing principles, best captures the pattern above; state it precisely enough that I could apply it myself; and name which clause each of my cases is responsible for. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts I have to give up, since I'd rather keep the ones about 5 and 7 than the one about 4 if I have to choose, but I'd like to know what that choice costs me. I'd also like to know how your account handles three cases I haven't been able to make up my mind about: a language model tuned using a reward model that was itself trained on which outputs humans said they preferred, where the reward arrives after the fact and is about text rather than about the model's own state; a population of agents playing a zero-sum game against each other, where every win for one is exactly a loss for another, so that at any moment half of them are doing badly; and an agent that, after a lot of meta-training, has learned to treat the penalty signal as a kind of exciting challenge, in the sense that a probe trained on its internal activity now reads positive activity where it used to read negative — does it still suffer, given that the penalty is still the thing controlling it? Finally, if the right answer turns on empirical facts about the architecture rather than on the philosophy, please tell me which facts those are and how I would go about checking them by reading the code or instrumenting the model, since that's the part I can actually do something about.
