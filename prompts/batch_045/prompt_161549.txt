My friend and I got into a three-hour argument at a barbeque last night — there was shrimp on the grill, which is what set it off — and I'm still chewing on it this morning. I'm in a bioethics-ish seminar this semester and it's made me try to actually write down my verdicts instead of just having vibes, so here they are, going with my gut. A dog: obviously yes. A pig: yes, and I feel worse about pork than about shrimp, which I can't fully justify. A salmon: yes probably, but much less than the pig. An octopus: yes, strongly, maybe stronger than the pig. A honeybee: yes, but barely, like a very thin version. A housefly: no. A tick: definately no. A chicken: yes. A lobster: honestly not sure, which is strange because I'd guess it has way more neurons than the bee. A crab: also not sure, though I feel worse about boiling one than I did ten years ago. A newborn baby: yes. A fetal pig at about 80 days: no, or so weakly that it basically doesn't count — even though right next to the baby verdict that sounds speciesist and I know it. A brain organoid grown in a dish: no, but I don't feel confident at all. A slime mold solving a maze: no. A person who, after a certain kind of brain injury, still says they feel pain but says it doesn't bother them in the slightest: obviously still fully conscious, whatever else is going on with them. A mouse brain simulated in detail on a server: I have no idea, and I hate that I have no idea.

The actual fight was about what the criterion is supposed to be. My friend says it's about being a unified subject — a single thing with a point of view that integrates information and acts as one agent — and that whether things feel good or bad to it is a separate question that shouldn't be what we're counting. I kept saying no, what I care about is whether anything matters to the creature, and I tried to give that teeth: call it the cost test. Roughly, a creature has experiences if it will pay a real cost — effort, risk, energy, delay, worse food — to get into a better state or out of a worse one, and the tradeoff is flexible: change the payoffs and it re-tunes, rather than it being one hardwired reflex sequence. So the animal that tends an injured limb for hours and takes worse food to sit somewhere that numbs it passes; the one that just yanks away and then goes right back to exactly what it was doing fails. He said that's going to count a thermostat, or a slime mold, or a reinforcement-learning agent with a reward function — which is kind of his point and kind of a bug to me. He also brought up some experiment with hermit crabs and electric shocks that I don't remember well enough to argue from.

So: is there one principle that captures most of my verdicts, and if not, which of them do I have to give up? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I can run it on cases I haven't thought about: a whole ant colony considered as one creature; a cat with most of its forebrain gone that still swipes at things; a person in a minimally conscious state who tracks a moving object with their eyes; a plant that slowly turns toward the light over days; a future AI system with long-term memory and its own goals but no body; a human fetus at 20 weeks. And if the best version of the principle tells me something about one of my verdicts that I really don't want to hear, say that plainly instead of pretending my gut was consistent all along.
