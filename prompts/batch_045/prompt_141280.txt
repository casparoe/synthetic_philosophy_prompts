I teach a final-year seminar on consequentialism, and I would like to close it not with another paper but with a screening of *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, treated as an examination case rather than a treat. So I need a reading of the film through the lens of infinite ethics that is faithful in both directions: it should say what the film dramatizes about the problem that the arguments alone do not, and it should be honest about where the film fudges, because I will be putting it in front of students who have just learned enough to be misled.

Three things I want handled carefully.

First, settle the ontology before deploying the machinery. Is the multiverse of the film genuinely infinite, or merely enormous and finite? I take this to matter a great deal and to be genuinely undecided by the text: verse-jumping traverses a branching structure that the film presents as unlimited, yet every traversal is a step to a particular place. If the infinity is real, then the film's premise — that every possible variant of a life is lived somewhere — is exactly the setup in which an agent cannot improve the total, because every qualitative state is already instantiated; the only remaining normative act is selection, choosing which slice you occupy, rather than production. If it is finite, the film is not about infinity at all but about a very large aggregation problem, and its central question is a version of the fanaticism worry: do the joys of a trillion branches of Evelyn outweigh the misery concentrated in the ones the everything bagel stands for? Please say which reading you think the film supports, and what changes if we switch.

Second, I want the two failure modes kept apart, because I think the film is often read as illustrating only the first. Call the standard one saturation: the total value is already infinite, so nothing finite that I do can make it better or worse. Call the second contrast collapse, and here I mean something the bagel seems to picture: an outcome-set containing every possible experience at once is not one whose value is too large to improve, but one where there is no longer any ordering, because nothing stands out against anything. The bagel is a value sink — you can put anything into it and nothing changes. My question is which of these Jobu's nihilism actually is: the familiar thought that nothing I do makes a difference, or the stranger thought that there is no longer a scale on which a difference could register? And a related puzzle: her search for a version of Evelyn who could understand her is, in an infinite multiverse, trivially satisfiable, and yet it takes her a whole film of travelling. Is that the film cheating, or is it the film registering that an infinity is lived branch by branch rather than surveyed as a whole?

Third, the resolution. Waymond's insistence on kindness, and Evelyn's choosing to stay in the least successful version of her life, look to me like either a piece of localism — when the total is unevaluable, the only intact normative notion is the character of the agent's response here — or a simple change of subject, in which an aggregative problem is quietly replaced by a virtue-ethical or existential one. Tell me which, and whether the film earns the move. I suspect it does not fully: it buys consolation while leaving the suffering branches unvisited, and the googly-eyes sequence solves the problem by changing the mood rather than the metaphysics. If you think that suspicion is unfair, argue against it. Along the way I would like you to notice that the film gives the camera to one Evelyn although, on its own premise, she is not special in any aggregative sense; that seems to me an implicit answer to the question of what the unit of ethical concern is, and I want to know whether it is a solution to the problem of which lives count or a refusal of that problem.

End by saying what a philosopher of infinite ethics might learn from the film that the third-personal literature does not say.
