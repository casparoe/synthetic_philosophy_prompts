Our head of school is on sabbatical this term and somehow that means I'm the one standing up at next week's all-school assembly to launch the new behaviour system we've been piloting since March. Here's what it actually is, so you have something concrete: detentions and demerits are gone; every student now sits in a twenty-minute advisory circle twice a week where anything that's gone wrong gets talked about out loud using a vocabulary we all agreed on (harm, repair, accountability); when something goes wrong a student writes what they intend to do differently on a public repair board in the main hallway; teachers stop logging infractions, and instead each student keeps a "growth narrative" folder that follows them from year to year and that their advisor reads and adds to; and there's a one-tap morning mood check-in on the school app that advisors can see. Two educational psychologists designed the whole thing and will be sitting in the front row. The rest of the room is about six hundred kids from twelve to eighteen, maybe sixty parents, a photographer from the local paper, and the faculty, which is split pretty evenly between people who think we've gone soft and want the old system back and people who think this doesn't go nearly far enough. My partner says I'm overthinking it. My sister, who teaches at another school, says it sounds like a cult.

The part I can't get straight is partly a Foucault problem. I've been reading Discipline and Punish on the bus and I've talked myself into thinking that the best case for the new system and the best case against it are the same case: it really is less violent than detention, and it's also much finer-grained, it works by getting kids to produce knowledge about themselves rather than by hitting them with a rule, and the check-in plus the folder plus the hallway board are exactly the sort of continuous low-level visibility that eventually doesn't need anyone to enforce it. I don't want a speech that sells it as liberation, and I dont want one that winks at it as surveillance. But I also can't do a Foucault lecture at fourteen-year-olds (I'd rather not say "panopticon" from the stage at all, and certainly not in front of the people who built the thing and whose feelings I'd like to spare), and I don't want to hand the smartest cynic in the room — there's a particular tenth grader who has already worked out that this is "surveillance with nicer fonts", and he isn't wrong — the satisfaction of being the only person there who sees it. The one thing I keep coming back to is that bit in his later writing about power only being exercised over free subjects, so that an arrangement which widens what somebody can do isn't just a gentler version of one that narrows it; that feels like the one true thing I could say out loud, but I'm not sure it survives being said into a microphone to a room where half the adults want to be told that the kids are finally being trusted. Also worth knowing: the pilot roughly halved out-of-school suspensions in the two advisories that ran it, which everyone will want me to treat as proof and which I don't think is proof of anything yet.

So please write the speech for me — ten minutes, delivered out loud, to that exact room. Give it back in three sections, in this order: (1) "The speech", about 850 to 950 words, the thing I actually read out; (2) "What I had to leave out", about 300 words, the lines you wanted to include and exactly what would have gone wrong with each of them in front of that audience; (3) "What I'm betting on", about 150 words, on what the speech is asking the room to believe and whether I can honestly hold that belief myself while standing on a stage asking six hundred kids to be honest. Please write the whole thing in American English.
