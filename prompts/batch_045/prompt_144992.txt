Hi — I need one more piece for a small newsletter I send to some ML researcher friends, and I want it to be a parody of the standard AI-lab research blog post: the "we're excited to announce" kind, with a results table and a pull quote from the Chief Scientist.

The setup: a fictional lab (call it Helix Methods or whatever) announces that their new system has, quote, "basically solved" analogy-making in the letter-string microdomain. You know the kind of item: you show a system "abc → abd" and ask what "ijk" becomes. "ijl" is what everyone says (replace the last letter with the next letter along), but "ijd" is also defensible (replace the letter c with the letter d), and once you see that the two answers come from two different descriptions of what happened in the first pair, you see why the item is underdetermined — nothing in three letters tells you whether "next letter along" or "the letter d" is what carried over. That gap between descriptions is the entire subject, and I want the joke to live there.

What I'm after:

- About 700–900 words, deadpan the whole way through. It should sound like it was written by a comms person who understands maybe 60% of the research and isn't worried about the other 40%.
- Work at least two concrete items all the way out, giving both reasonable answers and letting the reader feel why the choice between them isn't in the data. Every example has to be self-explanatory for a reader who's never heard of this microdomain.
- A results table over three families of items. One should be a family the lab invented to make the benchmark "harder": my idea is items built on a short custom alphabet the lab made up — say seven letters in a made-up order, with a couple of letters that repeat so you get groups like "kkk" — so the system can't lean on the alphabet it memorized in pretraining and actually has to find the relation inside the item. Have it do great on the ordinary families and quietly much worse on that one, with the writeup spinning the number rather than dropping it.
- The lab's actual method for handling genuine ambiguity: a data-filtering rule. Human validators, an agreement threshold, and items where people disagree about which description is the right one get dropped as "annotation noise." Then a straight-faced claim that performance on the filtered set measures analogical understanding. Nobody should say the word gerrymander.
- At least one pull quote from the Chief Scientist that a specialist in analogy research would wince at and a non-specialist would skim right past.
- A kicker at the end where the lab quietly redefines the thing it set out to measure as whatever its benchmark happens to measure. Bonus if there's a footnote that accidentally admits the whole game.

Constraints: no real companies, no real researchers named, no sci-fi, and plz don't explain the joke anywhere or tack on a winking final paragraph — the reader should be able to tell what's being made fun of, and someone who actually works on this stuff should find it fair rather than a strawman. Whatever the system is described as doing should be a plausible real mechanism (learned similarity, search over mappings, whatever), not magic. And don't cite anything or drop references at the bottom.
