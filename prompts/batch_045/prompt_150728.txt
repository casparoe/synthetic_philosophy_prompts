Next week I'm running the last session of a graduate reading group on decision theory, and one of the regulars, whom I'll call R., has become the group's unofficial oracle: she has a verdict on every case within about ten seconds and, more annoyingly, a verdict that survives an hour of the rest of us pushing on it. Her track record so far: in Newcomb's problem with opaque boxes and a predictor that has been right on 99 of the last 100 runs she has watched, she takes one box; in the transparent version, where both boxes are open and she can see whether the million is there, she still leaves the thousand. In Parfit's hitchhiker, already in town with the driver paid, she pays. In a counterfactual mugging where Omega tells her the coin landed the bad way, that it fixed its prediction of her behaviour in this very situation before the toss, that the prediction cannot now be changed or rewarded, and that she is being asked for a hundred dollars on the strength of it, she pays. In the prisoner's dilemma against an exact psychological twin with no communication she cooperates, and she still cooperates when the twin is noisy, meaning the twin runs her algorithm but an independent randomiser overrides its choice ten per cent of the time, on the grounds that the correlation is high enough to be worth it. In the smoking lesion she smokes. In a counterfactual mugging in which Omega's prediction was produced by a coin flip rather than by any modelling of her, she refuses. And in Newcomb's problem with a predictor that is right only sixty per cent of the time she still takes one box.

What I want to close on is a case I've been turning over, and I'd like your prediction of R.'s answer before I spring it on her. Two agents occupy regions that can never be in causal contact. There is no predictor, no simulation, no Omega, and nothing observes either of them. Each is offered the same thing at the same time: send any quantity between zero and ten units of your own welfare to the other, where whatever is sent is tripled on arrival as a matter of the physics of the two regions rather than by any benefactor. You weigh the other agent's welfare exactly as heavily as your own. The first wrinkle is that the other agent is not a copy and not a simulation: its decision procedure merely resembles yours, having been shaped by a broadly similar but not identical history, so that across decision problems of this kind the two of you return the same choice about eighty per cent of the time, and nothing in the setup lets that correlation be traced to a shared computation you could both be said to be running. The second wrinkle is that this agent is known to weigh its own welfare units twice as heavily as it weighs yours. I also want a variant: instead of one such agent there are a million, each correlated with you at roughly one per cent, none of them algorithmically similar to you at all, the correlation coming entirely from a shared environment rather than shared code, each offering the same tripling.

So: how much does she send in the first case, does the answer change if the other agent's weights were the same as hers, and what does she do in the million-agent variant? I'm less interested in the number than in the rule she seems to be running, in the other rules that fit her nine verdicts at least as well, and in what each of them predicts here, together with your reasons for preferring one and the further case that would best separate the survivors. Two things to watch: every verdict I've listed was on a binary choice, so it isn't yet clear whether her rule even has a scale in it or only a threshold, and I suspect the differing utility weights are doing different work in different theories, since for some of them the interaction is a joint decision by something like one spread-out agent while for others it is a bargain that has to be on terms the other side would accept. If her past answers already settle the case, say so and give me the closest variant that would unsettle it.
