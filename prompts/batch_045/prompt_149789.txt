I'm writing a short paper on supertasks for a graduate metaphysics seminar, and I keep coming back to Thomson's lamp as the case where the debate seems most frozen. Since Thomson's 1954 *Analysis* paper and Benacerraf's 1962 reply in the *Journal of Philosophy*, the lines have been these: Thomson argues that the story generates a contradiction — the lamp cannot be on at the limit time, because it was never turned on without immediately being turned off, and it cannot be off, because it was turned on first and never turned off without immediately being turned on, yet it must be one or the other — and concludes that no such task can be performed; he also insists that the mathematician's willingness to assign a sum to the associated divergent series is no help, since we attach no sense to the lamp's being half-on. Benacerraf replies that the sequence of switchings simply fails to determine the state at the limit instant, that many completions of the story are possible, and that no contradiction follows. Every writer I read picks one of these and the other side remains unmoved, which makes me suspect the argument isn't landing on a single question.

My hypothesis is that "is the lamp supertask possible?" bundles three or four questions under one label. First, whether infinitely many discrete acts can be performed in a finite interval at all — where I suspect the old demand that completion require a last step is partly an equivocation on what it is to finish something. Second, whether a system's state at a limit instant has to be determined by its earlier states when the sequence of states doesn't converge. Third, whether a story's failing to specify an outcome makes that story impossible or merely incomplete. Fourth, what "impossible" is meant to mean here: logical incoherence, or simply not performable by any mechanism we could build. To probe this I've invented three variants, which I'd rather you treat as diagnostic instruments than as things to look up. (i) The *independent-trigger lamp*: instead of one agent flipping the switch again and again, suppose an infinity of independently set timers, one per flick, so that no flick depends causally on its predecessor, and stipulate away any mechanical or relativistic strain. My intuition is that this silences the worries about causal structure and physical feasibility while changing nothing about the on/off question — which would mean those worries were never doing the work. (ii) The *readout lamp*: couple the lamp to a device that, one second after the limit time, records and prints whether it is on or off. Now "the story doesn't say" can't be the whole answer, since the world either settles the recorder's reading or contains a genuine indeterminacy in the lamp after the task. Does this help Thomson, or can the other side simply say that there are many admissible completions, some with each reading? (iii) The *dense lamp*: instead of flicks at times converging on a limit, have the lamp flicked at every dyadic-rational instant in the interval — a dense set, so that after the first moment there is no open stretch of time throughout which the lamp has any settled state. Here the pathology isn't located at a limit instant at all; it is everywhere. If the diagnosis is the same as in the standard case, then the "missing limit" framing was incidental and Thomson's lamp may be an unrepresentative test of supertasks generally; if it is different, the two cases are distinct phenomena that have been argued about as one.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict: which of these disagreements is actually load-bearing, which part — if any — is merely verbal, and are the two sides answering different questions under a shared heading? Is there a hidden question, for instance a general principle about when a system's state at a time is fixed by its states at earlier times, whose answer would settle it? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually commits itself to, and tell me what would count as progress given it: a formal result about limits and determination, a cleaner distinction between performability and describability, an argument that the lamp is a misleading example, or the conclusion that the question is ill-posed and that no progress should be expected. I'd also like an honest account of why this particular dispute has proved so stable — whether because both sides are rationally permitted to hold their ground, or because each side's key move looks question-begging to the other.
