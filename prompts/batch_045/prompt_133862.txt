I serve on the five-member art selection panel for a new county library, and I need a short decision memo to circulate to the other four members before our meeting in nine days.

A word about me: I have no academic background whatsoever — I was an insurance claims adjuster for thirty-one years — but I've been reading philosophy on my own this winter and got stuck on Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste," which I found in a used anthology bought at a library sale (so I can't give you page numbers). As I take him: beauty is not a quality in objects themselves but a sentiment in the mind that perceives them, and yet he still wants to say that some judgments are better than others, that a good judge is marked by practice, by comparison with many works, by freedom from prejudice, and by a fineness of perception he calls delicacy, and that such judges, however distant from one another in nation and century, largely agree.

The case is this. A bequest of roughly $240,000 must be spent, in its entirety, on one work for the library's atrium; it cannot go to anything else, and both finalists are within budget, so cost is not a factor. The donor's direction is that we acquire "the work of greatest artistic merit," and that we not put the choice to a public vote. We are down to two: an untitled wall-mounted piece of welded steel and salvaged barn wood by a local artist in her sixties who has shown regionally for decades, and a figurative bronze of a girl reading by a sculptor represented by galleries in three cities. The panel is split three to two. The three of us with no art-world training prefer the steel piece; the two who have it — a retired college art teacher and the owner of a small commercial gallery — prefer the bronze, and argue that training and wide comparison are exactly how merit gets recognized, that our preference is local fashion plus hometown feeling, and that "merit" in the donor's sense cannot be settled by counting hands. One of the three lay members is a neighbour of the local artist and has disclosed it; the rest of us don't believe it has affected her judgment, but the question of whether she should vote has been raised.

I've floated a procedure, and it has not gone down well. My proposal: postpone the vote for eight weeks (the executor expects a decision within three months, so we have room) and in the meantime do three things. First, each of us views the two finalists, unlabeled, in the atrium itself, for half an hour each, on two occasions at least three weeks apart, privately ranking them after each. Second, on both occasions, shuffled among the finalists and without our knowing which is which, we each also rank four sculptures the county already owns — all bought thirty to fifty years ago, none of them ever proposed for disposal, none of them ever discussed by this panel. Third, we compare rankings: a member's ranking of the finalists counts fully only if it was the same on both occasions, and we discount, without disqualifying, the rankings of any member whose ordering of the four older works departs sharply from the panel's consensus ordering of them. My reasoning is that we cannot identify the good judges by asking who has the best taste, since that assumes what we are trying to find out; but we can test how well a person's eye discriminates in a case where nothing is at stake for them and nothing fashionable is in play, and use that to decide how much weight their judgment deserves in the case where we disagree. The gallery owner's answer — the one I want treated honestly rather than knocked down — is that this is a rigged instrument: agreement about what the county happens to own shows only that five people share the habits of one time and place, which on Hume's own account is no evidence of merit at all, and that my procedure would entrench an untrained majority by quietly halving the votes of the two members who dissent from it.

Please draft the memo, addressed to the panel with To/From/Re lines. It should open with a single sentence giving the bottom line — adopt my procedure as proposed, adopt it in modified form (say how), or drop it and decide some other way — followed by a concrete schedule with intervals and a date for the final vote, then the reasoning, then the strongest objection to whatever you recommend, stated as well as you can state it. It should also say what we do if the weighted rankings still split, and give one paragraph on whether the member who is the artist's neighbour should vote. I should add that I currently prefer the steel piece and would like to be shown if I am wrong, and that I would like the memo to be usable without anyone having read Hume. Please don't cite or quote secondary literature; argue the matter directly, and keep the whole thing to 500 words.
