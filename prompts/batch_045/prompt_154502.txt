I teach a graduate seminar on collective decision-making, and for two years I have opened it with the argument below, which I originally wrote as a provocation. I now find that I cannot locate its fault, and since I assign it to students I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

(1) Condorcet's jury theorem, in its simplest form: suppose a question admits exactly one correct answer, fixed independently of what anyone believes about it; suppose n voters (n odd) vote independently, each with the same probability p > 1/2 of voting for that answer. Then the probability that a simple majority votes for it exceeds p, never falls as n increases, and tends to 1 as n grows without bound.

(2) Now take any binary collective decision that is answerable to outcomes — whether a central bank should raise its policy rate at its next meeting, whether a jurisdiction should license a repository for spent nuclear fuel. Let the correct answer be whichever option in fact produces the better outcome. The case then has the shape the theorem describes: there is one correct answer, and its correctness is not constituted by what the voters think.

(3) Individual voters are not experts, but they are better than chance: across a run of comparable questions, each citizen comes down on the better side more often than not. This is deliberately weak — p = 0.51 will do — and it is all the competence the theorem asks for.

(4) Ballots are secret, each voter marks one alone, and there is no deliberation, no whip, no caucus. So treat the votes as independent.

(5) Hence, for an electorate in the tens of millions, even at p = 0.51, the probability that the majority is correct is for practical purposes 1.

(6) Call this the swamping thesis: for any binary question with an outcome-correct answer, the best available procedure is the largest electorate one can assemble, and expertise, legislative deliberation, judicial review and delegation to agencies can only degrade the result — by shrinking n, or by correlating errors that would otherwise cancel. On this view the size of a decision body is fixed by the error rate one is willing to tolerate and by nothing else: a three-judge appellate panel is a crowd of three, and a randomly selected panel of 3,001 would be strictly better.

(7) Corollary: near-ties are always pathological. If a 51%-competent electorate of millions is effectively infallible, then an observed 50/50 split must be explained either by p ≤ 1/2 on that question or by correlated error. There is no third option of a hard question about which informed people reasonably disagree.

(8) I cannot accept (6) or (7). No one who has watched a monetary policy committee work thinks a mass plebiscite is the better instrument; and in (7) the excluded option is exactly the right one for the cases I care about. Something earlier must give.

I would like to keep (1) as mathematics and (2) as a description of what these decisions are answerable to, if I can. My main suspects are (3) and (4), and I cannot tell whether they are one problem or two. On (3): the theorem needs each voter's probability of being correct on this question, conditional on which answer is in fact correct; my premise offers a long-run frequency across a class of questions. Is that a real gap? It is aggravated by a selection effect I only recently noticed — the questions that get put to a vote are the contested ones, so a track record compiled over a mixed diet of easy and hard questions may say nothing about competence on the questions that ever reach a ballot. On (4): I have been told that what the theorem requires is independence conditional on the true state, whereas what my premise argues for is closer to unconditional independence, and that voters sharing a media environment and a set of cognitive defaults can satisfy the second while failing the first badly. Is that right, and is it the same failure as the one in (3)?

There is a further worry about (2) that I resist: on questions like the policy rate, the better outcome is not one state of the world but a distribution across creditors and debtors, so there may be no single answer for the majority to track, and the theorem's truth-tracking frame would not apply. But that line is dangerously convenient, since it would also explain the stable near-ties in (7) — and if (2) fails, I am left with a theorem that has no application to policy at all.

Finally, a colleague of mine says (5) is where the argument breaks: the limit in (1) is stated for a fixed p bounded away from 1/2, whereas the marginal voter added as the electorate grows is plausibly less informed, and if the advantage over chance decays like c/√n then majority accuracy converges not to 1 but to Φ(2c). I cannot follow her derivation well enough to check it, and I do not know whether it matters.

Which step do you reject? If several fail, which one carries the argument — the one whose repair leaves a chain that no longer delivers (6) or (7)? And what does each repair cost me: does any of them leave a usable institutional moral, and if the culprit is correlated error rather than size, does that argue for bodies that diversify information instead of bodies that simply get larger?
