I've been reading about population, food and natural resources on my own for the past year or so — I'm a retired engineer with no training in economics or demography, and I got into it after arguing with a nephew about whether the world can feed itself — and I ended up joining a small online reading group of six people that has now spent roughly a year going in circles about Malthus. Nobody has moved anybody by even a little, and I want to understand what kind of deadlock this is before I decide whether to keep showing up.

Nobody in the group disagrees about the data. Population has multiplied many times over since Malthus published his essay in 1798; food production per head has risen rather than fallen; fertility has fallen rather than risen as countries have got richer; the terrible famines of the last century are, as far as anyone can tell, explained by war, policy and distribution rather than by the world as a whole being unable to grow enough; and long-run real prices of most raw materials have drifted downward. The Malthusian in our group accepts all of this and says he was right but early: the reprieve, he says, was bought by burning a one-off inheritance of fossil carbon, by a single historical jump to high-yield agriculture, and by quietly spending down stocks and sinks that can't be spent down forever — aquifers, topsoil, phosphate rock, the atmosphere's capacity to absorb carbon dioxide — so that the constraint hasn't disappeared, it has moved from the source side to the sink side and the bill arrives later. The cornucopian in the group replies that a prediction which can always be deferred isn't a prediction, and adds a second point that I find harder to answer: maybe the causal arrow runs the other way, and population pressure is precisely what forces people to invent, irrigate and intensify, as a Danish economist argued in a 1965 book — in which case Malthus identified a real mechanism but got the dominant term wrong, which sounds less like earliness than like a different model wearing the same name.

Two things I tried to contribute have themselves stalled, which is what made me want outside help. First, I noticed there was a famous wager in 1980 between a biologist and an economist over whether a basket of five metals would get dearer or cheaper by 1990; the biologist chose the metals, the decade was fixed in advance, and he lost and paid up — and yet our group split three ways over whether this was evidence at all, one side saying market prices are a terrible proxy for scarcity, the other saying the proxy is exactly what the theory implies, and a third pointing out that over other ten- or thirty-year windows the same bet comes out the other way. A clean, dated, pre-registered test didn't settle anything. So I proposed what I called a dormancy rule: before anyone is allowed to say not yet about a long-run prediction, they have to state in advance the deadline or the trigger at which the prediction lapses, together with what would count as evidence that it was simply wrong; otherwise right but early is a mood rather than a claim. The Malthusian said this unfairly demands a close-ended forecast from an open-ended system. I also raised what I think of as the self-defeating prophecy problem: if alarm about limits is part of what produced high-yield agriculture and smaller families, then the prediction failed partly because it was believed, and I can't tell whether that vindicates the framework or is just a way of claiming credit either way.

My own hypothesis is that this is not really an empirical dispute at all, but two things in combination: a disagreement about how much deferral a theory is permitted before it stops being falsifiable, plus one genuinely hidden empirical question about whether our escapes were one-time windfalls or a repeatable process. What I'd like from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict — is the argument verbal or substantive, are the two sides answering different questions under one label (is the claim about a fixed ceiling, or about what happens whenever a ceiling is hit?), is there a real formal or empirical question doing the work, and is my dormancy rule a fair test or does it beg the question against open systems? Then tell me honestly what would count as progress here: a pre-registered horizon, a distinction between a prediction that was delayed and one that was displaced onto a different variable, a reframing in terms of how responsive carrying capacity is to population pressure, or nothing at all, because the question as posed is ill-posed. Please argue from what each side actually claims rather than summarizing the history of the debate, and please don't send me off to read things — I want your reasoning, not a reading list.
