Over the past year I've been going through a set of puzzles with a friend of mine — I work in logistics and have no philosophy training, but I got pulled into this after my sister went through IVF — and I'd like your help predicting where my friend will land on a case I'm putting to her next week. She is a retired midwife who now sits on the ethics committee at our local hospital; she has no formal philosophy either, but she is the most stubbornly consistent person I know on these questions, and she has given me firm verdicts on five things so far. First: a woman who could avoid a lifelong but mild impairment in her child by waiting a month before conceiving, and can't be bothered to wait; my friend says she does nothing wrong, because if she had waited it would have been a different child, and this child's life is well worth living. Second: the same woman, eight weeks pregnant, declining a safe and simple treatment that would prevent the very same impairment in the child she is already carrying — here my friend says she does do something wrong, and pinpoints the difference as being that this time it is the same child either way. Third: a couple doing IVF who choose, among several viable embryos, one that does not carry a serious late-onset disease; my friend says this is not merely permitted but the choice they ought to make, yet when I pressed her she would not call implanting the affected embryo wrong — she got as far as saying it would be very hard to defend, and no further. Fourth: a couple who continue a pregnancy knowing the child will have a short life of unremitting pain; clearly wrong, she says. Fifth, this one my question rather than a case: I asked whether we should always try to bring into existence the best-off child we can, or produce as much welfare as possible, and she said no, that on that principle we would all be obliged to have as many happy children as we could, which she thinks is absurd. (I have since learned that people call this general shape of problem the non-identity problem, but please don't lean on that literature in your answer — I want to reason it out from her verdicts.) What I keep noticing is that she has never once used the word wrong about a choice that determines which person comes to exist. She comes closest in the embryo case. I cannot tell whether that is a principled line she is drawing or just an unwillingness to say the hard thing, and the new case is designed to find out.

Here it is. A couple have exactly two viable embryos and will transfer one. One will become a daughter who, because of a condition she will have from birth, will never live independently, will need supported care for the whole of her life, will have very limited language and a shortened life expectancy, and will be vulnerable in ways most adults are not; but she will have affectionate relationships, real enjoyments, and no unremitting pain. My friend has already given me her own assessment of this kind of life in a different connection, having known a family in something like this situation: she said she would call the life worth living and often happy, but that it was not a life she would wish on anyone. The other embryo will become a different daughter with an ordinary, healthy life. The couple want to transfer the first one, and their reason matters to me: they cared for a younger brother with the same condition for years, they think a life like that can go well, and they do not treat the condition as a tragedy to be avoided at all costs — they are not indifferent to the child's welfare and they are not choosing her for any ulterior purpose. So: do you think she will finally say that this choice is wrong, or will she say it is permissible while calling it unwise, as she did with the late-onset disease? What are the different rules someone could be running that would fit all five of her earlier verdicts equally well, and what does each of them say here? Give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and be explicit about how much of the case is doing the work — whether it is the severity of the resulting life, the fact that a single child will exist either way, or the couple's reasons.

Finally, and this is the part I care about most: if the rules that agree on this case diverge elsewhere, what further case should I put to her that would separate them and that she could not answer both ways at once? I have a vague sense that the useful variations involve changing how bad the resulting life is while keeping the parents' reasons the same, and changing the parents' reasons while keeping the life the same, but I would like you to say which variation is diagnostic of which disagreement, and what her answer to it would tell me. Please write your reply as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a careful email to someone who has thought about this a lot without ever studying it.
