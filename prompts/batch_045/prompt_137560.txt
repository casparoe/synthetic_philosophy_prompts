I work in operations for a company that manages support services — cleaning, laundry, waste handling — across about a hundred and twenty hospital sites. I have no formal training in any of this. I got interested in measurement problems after reading about specification gaming in machine learning, and I have been chewing on it on my own for a couple of years.

Here is the concrete decision in front of me. For years we have scored our cleaning contractors against a fixed, published checklist, inspected monthly, with part of the fee tied to the score. Everyone agrees the scores have drifted to near ceiling and no longer mean much. A colleague and I have to recommend whether to replace this with what I call rotating inspection: the checklist for any given visit is drawn, unannounced, from a bank of several hundred individual checks drawn from the same underlying standard, so that no one can prepare for the specific list — only for the standard. It would be rolled out at forty sites for two years with the other eighty continuing as they are, and an independent team, one the contractors do not know about, would take bacterial cultures from the same categories of surface throughout. That culture result is what we say we actually care about.

My credence that the cultures come back meaningfully better at the rotating sites, and not merely that the contractors have got better at being measured, is about 0.75. My colleague, who is not a fool, puts it at about 0.2. She says I have not fixed anything, only moved the target up one level: the contractors will now optimize against the bank rather than the list, and the cheapest route to that is not to clean better in general but to be better at looking clean whenever someone might be looking — optimizing the inspection *process* rather than the inspection *content*. My reply is that a ratio decides this: what it costs to look clean on any of several hundred possible checks, versus what it costs to actually be clean. If the first is much cheaper, she wins; if the two costs are close, then pressure on the proxy has to push on the thing itself. I call it the cheap-fake ratio, and I think the live sub-questions are roughly these: how close those two costs really are in this setting; which of the failure modes people distinguish — the regressional one, the extremal one, the causal one, the adversarial one — dominates once the list is unpredictable; whether there is even a determinate quantity, how clean this surface is, that all these proxies track imperfectly, or whether once the checklist is unpredictable "clean" just collapses into whatever the culture shows, in which case the word gaming stops applying; and whether two years across forty sites is long enough for any real difference to show up at all.

Give me your own number for the proposition as I have defined it, but above all decompose it. Name the sub-questions you think it turns on, not just mine. Give a rough probability and a one-line justification for each. Say how you are combining them — multiplying them out, some kind of noisy-or, something looser — and why that is the right combination rule here. Then tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and what piece of evidence would move that term most. Finally: given that my colleague and I land at 0.75 and 0.2, tell me which sub-question we most likely disagree about, and if you think the disagreement is really about how to combine the terms rather than about any term, say that plainly. Please do not give me the standard statement of Goodhart's law back at me, and do not tell me that it depends — I want the number and its seams.
