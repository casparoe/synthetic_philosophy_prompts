I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice and artificial agents, and I am drafting the final exam. The last third of the course was on embedded agency — the cluster of problems that arise for an agent that is both smaller than, and physically located inside, the world it has to reason about — and I need one essay question on it that cannot be answered by running through the Newcomb / Parfit's hitchhiker dialectic the students have already met three times. I have invented a case for this purpose, described below; please build the question around it rather than around the standard cases.

The case:

A system I will call Ariel is implemented as one thousand parallel copies — call them shards — of a single policy network. Each shard receives a different, partially overlapping slice of the system's sensory stream and outputs a recommended action. A hardwired aggregation rule, majority vote with ties broken in favour of the lowest-indexed shard, turns the thousand recommendations into the single output that Ariel's effectors execute. Ariel has read access to its own source code, to the aggregation rule, and to the current outputs of all shards, but no ability to modify the aggregation rule. Everything the shards and the effectors do is part of the physical world that later shard inputs depend on.

Ariel is then placed in a variant of Parfit's hitchhiker. A driver, who on a faster machine has run a complete simulation of the whole Ariel system including the aggregation rule, and whose prediction is therefore reliable, will rescue Ariel from the desert if and only if he predicts that once in town Ariel's output will be pay. The simulation is run and the driver's decision is made before Ariel reaches town; by the time Ariel is in town the prediction is fixed and known to the shards. Paying costs the equivalent of one thousand dollars; being rescued is worth vastly more than that. The shards know all of this.

What I want the question to force:

First, that students settle what Ariel's action even is. Is there one agent here or a thousand and one? Does the aggregation rule belong to Ariel or to Ariel's environment? Is the driver's simulation a second Ariel, a part of Ariel, or neither? They should notice that nothing in the physics settles these questions, and say what turns on the answer.

Second, that they keep apart two families of obstacle that students routinely run together: the problems that come from being smaller than the world (no complete world-model, no guaranteed convergence on the truth, no model that can contain a full copy of a world that contains the modeller) and the problems that come from being inside the world (no privileged input and output channels, so that the distinction between acting and observing, or between intervening on a variable and conditioning on it, is not read off nature but imposed by a description). I want the question to make the second family vivid by stipulating away the first: the shards may be assumed to have unbounded computation and a complete physical theory, and the boundary problem survives that stipulation.

Third, that they identify what each familiar decision theory needs that the case does not obviously supply. Causal decision theory needs a privileged notion of intervention on the agent's own output. Evidential decision theory needs a determinate partition of possibilities into the acts available to the agent. A functional or logical-counterfactual approach needs a criterion for when two physical happenings implement the same computation, since here the driver's simulation, the thousand shards, and the aggregate are all candidates. The strongest answers will then say whether these needs can be met by a choice of description, and if so what could make one choice of description better than another — that is the move I most want to separate an A from a B.

Fourth, that they confront the grain-of-truth issue rather than name it: for a system that is a strict part of a world containing a copy of it, there may be no determinate fact, prior to the event, about what it will output, and no complete model of itself it could occupy; so the instruction to predict your own action and respond to it may not be well defined. They should have to say whether that undermines the verdict that Ariel should pay, or merely relocates it — from the level of a decision in town to the level of a policy or a design.

Fifth, and this is the crux, that they handle the design/decision distinction explicitly. That Ariel should pay looks irrational from the deliberative standpoint of the system in town and obvious from the standpoint of someone deciding which system to build. Students should say what rational is doing in each claim, and then whether the second standpoint is still available when the designer is itself embedded — say, when the modification is made by a successor version of Ariel reasoning inside the same world. That is where I expect even good answers to come apart.

Please deliver: the essay question itself, self-contained, since it is the only thing students will see, so it must state the case and the cost structure in its own words; a closed-book ninety-minute framing; a model answer of roughly eight hundred words; and a marking scheme out of twenty-five built around five or six named must-hit moves with the marks each is worth, an explicit statement of what an A answer contains that a B answer does not, and a one-line note per point on the typical way candidates get it wrong. Add a short list of three or four things that earn no credit on their own — I have in mind it depends which decision theory you accept, a rehearsal of one-boxing versus two-boxing, there is no fact of the matter deployed as a way of stopping rather than as a claim to be defended, and appeals to free will. The mark scheme must not depend on any student having read a particular paper: no citation should be required, and none should earn credit. If it helps to separate the top answers, you may add one short variant sub-question — for instance, what changes if the driver predicts the recommendation of shard one rather than the aggregate output — but only if it earns its place in the scheme.
