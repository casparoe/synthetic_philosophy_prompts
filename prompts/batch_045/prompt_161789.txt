I am designing a set of consultation materials for a fictional regulatory body, the Simulation Ethics Board, which is deciding whether a person who has spent their entire life inside a full-immersion haptic virtual environment can acquire knowledge of an external world by means of G. E. Moore's proof: that is, by holding up one's hands and reasoning that, since hands exist, an external world exists.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers advising the Board: a Moorean, who holds that the proof works even when the hands in question are virtual hands rendered by the simulation, and a skeptic, who holds that a simulated hand is not a material hand and that the proof therefore either fails or begs the question against the simulation hypothesis. Set the dialogue in a waiting room outside the Board's hearing chamber, where the two of them are passing the time by arguing the case before they go in. The skeptic should press the worry that a brain in a vat, or an inhabitant of a simulation, could go through the motions of the proof without ever establishing contact with mind-independent material reality, while the Moorean should reply that the proof's premises are more certain than the skeptical hypothesis and that the virtual hands are still objects external to the perceiver's mind, so the argument still establishes an external world of some kind, even if it is a simulated one. Both characters should respond to each other's actual points rather than talk past each other, and neither should be a caricature; give the skeptic the strongest available objection about the simulation hypothesis and give the Moorean a serious reply about the structure of Moore's proof and the demand for a non-question-begging argument. Please write the dialogue as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a long email or letter in which the writer recounts the exchange to a colleague who could not attend; do not include any bibliography, citations, or references to specific scholarly works.
