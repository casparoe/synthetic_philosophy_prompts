I'm in my fourth year of a PhD in political theory, and my dissertation asks whether the concept of recognition can still do critical work — whether it can underwrite the claim that some arrangement is a form of misrecognition rather than merely an arrangement we happen to dislike. My third chapter is a reconstruction of the lordship-and-bondage section of Hegel's Phenomenology, and I've gotten stuck in a way I think is productive but which I can't convert into a chapter on my own.

Here is where I am, stated as neutrally as I can. Two self-consciousnesses each want their own certainty of themselves reflected back by another who is himself free; the attempt to secure this by staking life generates an asymmetry in which one consciousness is reduced to a dependent, thing-like role; and that dependence then unravels from the inside, because the dependent one's relation to the object of work turns out to be the real engine of self-transformation, whereas the independent one's relation to another's work is merely consumptive. Two mechanisms are usually credited with driving that reversal: the fear-of-death moment, in which the subject's given self-certainty is dissolved; and the form-giving activity of work, in which the worker re-encounters itself in something that outlasts the act of working. My half-formed idea is that these two mechanisms are not doing the same kind of work, and that the second has a hidden precondition that Hegel's examples quietly supply: everything presented as labor there terminates in a durable, independent, re-identifiable object. Much of the labor that recognition theory is now asked to talk about does not terminate in that — care work, service and affective work, platform-mediated and data-generating work, work whose product is a state of a person or a profile rather than a thing. Either the reversal needs durable-object production, in which case that literature is running machinery that doesn't fit its own cases, or it doesn't, in which case the object is expendable and something else is doing the work.

To settle this I want to run what I'm calling a substitution test, and I need help turning it into a fundable plan. First substitution: replace the fear-of-death episode with a structurally analogous but non-lethal episode of unrecoverable loss — one the subject cannot subsequently narrate back as its own achievement — and ask whether the reversal still goes through; if it does, the mortality premise is inessential and the real input is something like loss that resists re-appropriation. Second substitution: replace artifact production with a non-durable analogue — labor that is structured, repeatable and resistant, but whose product is dissipated in the act or absorbed into another person, leaving no standing object to be re-encountered. If the reversal survives both substitutions, the machinery is formal and can be stated in a few lines; if it survives one and not the other, we learn which mechanism Hegel is actually leaning on, and I suspect the answer will be awkward both for readers who want recognition without mortality and for readers who want the chapter to be fundamentally about work.

What I need is a two-page proposal for a one-semester, single-chapter project of about eight thousand words, containing: the question stated sharply enough to have a determinate answer, including what would count as the substitution test succeeding or failing and how I would tell; why the two broad families of existing reading leave that question open; the method, staged month by month across a semester, distinguishing what is close textual work from what is conceptual reconstruction, since my committee will ask; the range of possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them; an honest account of what would count as failure and where the project is most likely to outgrow a semester; and a closing paragraph on why anyone outside Hegel scholarship should care, pitched at people who work on care, service and platform labor. Please don't include a bibliography or citations — I'll supply the secondary literature myself. Where you can, refer to interpretive positions by description rather than by author, and don't rest any step of the argument on a particular commentator's reading being correct. And please assume only the bare outline I gave above rather than confident recall of the chapter's details: I argued this through with a friend who works on care ethics and she kept making me restate which step I was actually challenging, so I'd rather the proposal be explicit about that than efficient.
