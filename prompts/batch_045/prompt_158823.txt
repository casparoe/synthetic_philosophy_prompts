I run a fortnightly lunchtime discussion group at the company where I work: twelve machine-learning engineers, no philosophy background, thirty minutes, one whiteboard. It has lately stopped being academic. Our team is about to ship a model that triages patient messages, and in the review meeting one engineer said the model obviously doesn't understand anything, it has merely been fine-tuned until its outputs look like understanding; another replied that the same argument would work on any of us, and the room stalled exactly there. So I need to teach the fine-tuning argument — roughly, that for any behavioral profile, and in particular for any behavioral test we might devise, there is a system shaped by fine-tuning alone that matches it, and that behavior therefore cannot settle whether a system understands — without the session collapsing into either "so it's just a parrot" or "so the question is empty."

Please act as a philosopher of mind who has taught this material to engineers many times, and plan the session rather than give me the explanation itself. Before you draft anything, spend three or four lines noting the dependency constraints you think force the order: what has to be in place before the argument can even be stated, and what its conclusion does and does not depend on.

Then give me, in no more than 700 words:

1. A numbered sequence of five to seven stages with minute marks, each stage naming the single move it makes and why it belongs at that point. The order matters to me more than the content.

2. One carrying example, in a short paragraph, with one sentence on why it is the right example and one on what it must not be: it must not be a case where the system is transparently reciting memorized text, which makes the argument feel cheap, and it should not be a case where the behavior is trivially out of distribution. I would rather struggle with a hard example than win with an easy one.

3. A misconception map: the four or five ways this specific audience will misread the argument — that it shows LLMs don't understand; that it shows the question is meaningless; that it applies to us too and so refutes itself; that it can be escaped by identifying understanding with having a world model that generalizes — and, for each, the stage at which it should be defused rather than answered.

4. In one sentence each, what I should openly refuse to cover.

One thing I want you to settle explicitly, because I think it changes the shape of the whole session: whether the argument is better taught as a causal point — the fine-tuning objective fully explains the behavior, leaving nothing for understanding to do — or as an evidential point — behavioral evidence is screened off because many different training histories would have produced the same behavior. Choose one, justify the choice in a sentence, and let it show up in the sequence. The session should leave the room able to state the argument's scope precisely; it should not leave them with a verdict about our model.
