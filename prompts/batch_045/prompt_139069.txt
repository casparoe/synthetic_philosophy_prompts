I work on the methodology team at a small forecasting platform. We publish probability forecasts for a few hundred recurring questions, and with them a written methodology document that says how our numbers move when new data arrives. The rule we actually use is a shrinkage rule: when a batch of data comes in we compute what our model says the posterior is, then pull the published forecast a fixed fraction of the way back toward our prior, at a rate we state in advance, because our track record suggests the model treats new data as more informative than it really is. Last week a colleague — a strict Bayesian, who thinks the shrinkage is a sin against conditionalization — said the problem is not that we are modest about our model but that we are *tradeable against*: that being exploitable in this way just is what diachronic irrationality amounts to. I said the only cost of publishing a deterministic update rule is that someone could run a book against us, which is a cost you can decide to pay. I would like to settle this with a concrete case instead of slogans, so I am asking you to build one for me.

What I want is a fully worked diachronic Dutch book run against an agent whose updating rule is public, deterministic in publicly observable inputs, and is not conditionalization, but is also not obviously a bad rule — shrinkage toward the prior to guard against over-read signals is the candidate I have in mind, though if you think a better-motivated non-Bayesian rule makes a cleaner case, use it and say why. Constraints: (i) an ordinary institutional setting with ordinary events — my platform, a clinic, an insurer, a logistics desk; no casinos and no seminars about betting; (ii) the agent's credences at every individual time must satisfy the probability axioms, so that any defect is purely diachronic and no synchronic incoherence is smuggled in; (iii) at most four bets, finite stakes, ordinary money, and no appeal to long runs, laws of large numbers, or merely expected losses — the loss has to be sure, verifiable by adding up the branches; (iv) the bookie has no information about the outcome that the agent lacks, the only asymmetry being that the bookie has read the methodology document; (v) no trickery and no cognitive handicaps — the case must not depend on the agent being confused, forgetful, bad at arithmetic, or unaware of what is on offer, and if the agent's only escape is to refuse, at the later time, a bet priced at their own new fair price, say so and tell me what that escape costs them.

Then the analysis, please. Lay out the book: each bet, its price, which branch it is live in, and the arithmetic showing the loss is guaranteed; if your case only guarantees a loss conditional on the signal coming out one way and breaks even otherwise, say that explicitly and tell me whether the argument I want to make needs the stronger, all-branches version. Run the identical strategy against an otherwise identical agent who updates by conditionalizing on the same signal and show that it makes nothing, identifying the exact line of the arithmetic that goes to zero for them — that comparison is what I will use to explain what the case is really about. Then give both verdicts: the strict Bayesian's (the plan is irrational, in this specific sense) and the methodology team's (the rule beats conditionalization on expected accuracy under the data-generating process we actually face — specify the misspecification and do the scoring-rule comparison with numbers, not hand-waving, including the obvious reply that we should simply fix our likelihoods rather than shrink, and whether that reply dissolves the case). Tell me which verdict you think survives and why. Finally, be honest about the load-bearing feature: what in the case is doing the work, what remains of the book if that feature is removed, and the strongest reply available to whichever side you come down against, including the reply that the whole construction works only because a published forecast has been conflated with a credence.
