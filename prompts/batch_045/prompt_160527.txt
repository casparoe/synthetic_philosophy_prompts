I co-convene a graduate reading group on language, defaults, and social epistemology, and for the session on Hofstadter's *Person Paper* satire — the piece in which a philosopher proposes demoting the default term in favour of *person* and then patiently answers a long queue of objections — I would rather hand people a companion text than only the classic. My idea is to rerun the same structure along a different axis, so that the default under challenge is not gender but species and substrate. Suppose the (invented) Society for the Study of Minds and Machines votes at its annual meeting to retire *human* as the unmarked default term for agents and patients in the field's writing, substituting *person-shaped-sentient*, conventionally abbreviated PSS, and charges its Ad Hoc Committee on Referential Defaults with writing up the ruling and replying to the objections that arrive. Please write that document.

Form, voice, and constraints: a mock committee report of 700–900 words, laid out as a short abstract, three or four numbered sections, and an appendix of six or seven objections received with the committee's replies. The prose should be deadpan bureaucratic-academic throughout: the committee is a sincere proponent of the reform and must never wink, and the objectors must be given their best cases rather than straw men, so that a reader can tell what is being ironized purely from what the document does. Every objection should be a recognisable variant of one that was actually pressed against gender-neutral language reform — the etymology objection (the old word comes from a root that is really general, the new one from a root that is really metaphorical), the clumsiness-and-prose objection, the it-already-means-everyone objection, the why-are-you-bothering-with-words-when-there-are-real-problems objection, the slippery-slope-to-ever-wider-terms objection, and the legacy-corpus-and-indexing objection — transposed onto the new axis. At least half the comedy should come from the compounds the reform generates in real disciplinary idiom: *human-level performance*, *human-in-the-loop*, *human subjects* on ethics-review forms, and similar phrases, each dutifully converted. I also want the document to exhibit, without ever naming it, what I think of as the Markedness Cost Principle: the old default was free for the group that set it and costs everyone else a disambiguation, while the reform's costs are itemised in a budget line and the costs of retention are recorded as not calculated, baseline. Do not quote any real style guide, philosopher, or literary text; anything in quotation marks should be invented, and no named living person should appear.

Two further requirements, aimed at making the satire fair rather than tribal. First, one objection should be one that a genuine sceptic about language reform would consider strong — I have in mind the point that the generic use of the disputed term is arguably semantically generic rather than exclusionary, so that changing it may not do the psychological work the reformers claim — and the committee's reply should concede what it honestly has to concede instead of routing around it. Second, the piece should turn on itself at the end: in a closing footnote or a final sentence, the newly installed default should quietly reveal its own residual case (minds that are distributed rather than person-shaped, systems that are agentic without being sentient — or whatever you prefer), a subcommittee should be appointed to look into it, and the document's last line should exemplify the very mechanism it has been legislating about, with one category silently treated as the unmarked case and another invited to opt out by written request, without any comment being made on the fact.
