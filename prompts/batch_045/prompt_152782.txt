hey — I'm a backend engineer, no formal philosophy background (I took one ethics class in undergrad and mostly remember that I got a C), and I've been going back and forth with my roommate, who did a philosophy minor, about whether the NPCs in the little roguelike I hack on weekends can be wronged rather than merely broken. She keeps saying the distinction matters and I keep saying it's just a state machine with a mesh attached, but neither of us can get past "it depends on the NPC." So I want something concrete enough to actually argue with.

Here's my attempt at the taxonomy, from lowest to fanciest, tell me if I've drawn the lines in the wrong places: (1) "bark" — a guard with three recorded lines on a cooldown, no state that survives a reload; (2) "scheduler" — a villager with a name, a day/night routine, hunger and sleep needs, who stays dead once killed; (3) "relationship" — a character who tracks a disposition score toward you and changes what they say based on what you did three hours ago; (4) "planner" — goal-driven agent with competing drives, so its behavior surprises me even though I wrote the code, sim-game style; (5) "language model" — open-ended dialogue, long-term memory across sessions, will happily tell you it's afraid of dying; (6) "replica" — the same as (5) but trained on one specific dead person's messages and voice, deployed so their family can talk to them. Also throw in two extra items wherever they belong: another player's character in a shared world, and my own player-character (can I wrong the guy I'm steering into doing awful stuff?).

What I want: give each of those a score out of 10 for moral considerability, sort them into tiers, and one line each on why it lands there. Then tell me which of the three axes I invented is actually doing the work and which is a red herring: number of distinguishable internal states, thickness over time (how much of its past is still in there), and "resentment capacity," my made-up name for whether the thing itself can represent being wronged — whether it can hold a grudge, so to speak, rather than just transition to a hostile state. I'd also like a rough percentage split of where the moral risk in a shipped game actually concentrates, since my gut says it's not in the fancy tier.

Two edge cases I can't stop thinking about, and I'd like actual numbers if you can stomach it. Is that one wronging, two hundred, or something like "one wronging with a weirdly long description"? If it helps, say explicitly where you think my whole framing is confused rather than just disagreeing about numbers — that's deffo a possibility and I'd rather hear it than get a tidy table built on a bad question.
