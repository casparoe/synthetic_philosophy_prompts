Hi — I'm a PhD student in philosophy (I work on rational choice and coordination, so this is adjacent to my stuff but not actually my literature) and I need someone to referee an argument I ended up stuck in the middle of.

Setup: after the decision-theory reading group on Tuesday, Mira (a postdoc here) and Tobias (visiting from another department) went at it over whether acausal trade survives being made realistic. They were arguing about a modified twin prisoner's dilemma. Payoffs in arbitrary units: if both cooperate, +2 each; if both defect, 0 each; if you cooperate and they defect, you get -1 and they get +3; and the mirror image of that. The twist is that the counterpart isn't running bit-identical code — imagine it was produced by taking your source and mutating a small fraction of it, or by training the same architecture on a different data shard — so the correlation is imperfect: conditional on your cooperating there's a 0.8 chance the counterpart cooperates, conditional on your defecting there's a 0.2 chance. And stipulate that the two of you never causally interact, so this is meant as a stand-in for the real acausal-trade situation.

(1) Mira opened with the arithmetic. What does the work in the classic case, she said, is correlation, not identity, and identity is just the limiting case. Expected value of cooperating is 0.8 times 2 plus 0.2 times minus 1, so 1.4; of defecting, 0.2 times 3, so 0.6. Cooperate. She added that the recommendation is continuous in the correlation — she said the cutoff is two thirds — and that a theory with a verdict only at correlation 1 and at correlation 0.5 is two anecdotes, not a decision theory.

(2) Tobias said that at correlations below 1 this is evidential decision theory in a fake moustache. In the classic case your output doesn't merely predict the counterpart's, it fixes it, because you are the same algorithm; that's what functional decision theory was supposed to exploit. At 0.8 nothing is fixed, you're just treating your own act as evidence, which is exactly the move that both the causal decision theorists and the FDT people rejected, and it's the move that gives the intuitively wrong answer in the lesion-style cases where a common cause generates the correlation and the act is useless as a lever. So, he said, you owe an account of why this correlation is the good kind, and "shared algorithm" is doing the same job that "shared lesion" does in the bad case.

(3) Mira said those cases aren't parallel: in the lesion case there's a screening-off node — condition on the lesion and the correlation disappears — whereas here there's nothing available to condition on that breaks the link, because the link runs through the decision procedure itself, which is the thing being chosen with. The correlation is constitutive, not confounded. And she pressed the continuity point harder: no two real implementations of "me" will ever be bit-identical across different hardware, seeds and inputs, so if exact procedural identity is required, acausal trade never applies to anything actual.

(4) Tobias replied that "never applies to anything" might just be the right answer, and then raised what he called the real problem: there's no privileged cutoff for how similar is similar, and worse, the number is reference-class-relative. Condition on "runs a mutant of my source" and you get 0.8; condition on "runs a mutant of my source that was selected for stability" and you get 0.55; condition on "runs some agent in this simulation" and you get 0.5. Procedural identity was supposed to be a yes-or-no matter, and that's what made acausal trade feel non-arbitrary; once the relation is graded, you've imported the reference-class problem and nobody has a solution to it. He also threw in that you can't treat constitutive dependence as ordinary statistical evidence, since conditioning on your own action isn't the same as intervening on it.

(5) Mira said the reference-class complaint is generic — Bayes nets need a partition into variables too, so the causalists have the same repair job at home — and that the 0.8 needn't come from a hand-picked reference class at all: if the mutation process is specified (each of N lines perturbed independently with probability epsilon, say), you can compute the agreement rate from the mechanism. And on intervention versus observation, she said that's the whole point: the dependence FDT cares about is subjunctive — what this algorithm would output, if it output C — and attenuation just makes that subjunctive link probabilistic rather than deterministic, which is a generalisation and not a category mistake. Tobias conceded the arithmetic and conceded that a fully specified mutation process does fix the number.

(6) Then Tobias went for the jugular and said the setup is unstable anyway: if both agents know the numbers and both apply this rule, both cooperate, and the observed agreement rate is 1 — which contradicts the stipulated 0.8. The 0.8 is supposed to be a fact about the world that grounds the decision, but it can't be a fixed point of the policy it recommends.

(7) Mira said that only bites if the numbers are supposed to be derived from assuming the counterpart reasons like you, and hers aren't. They're mechanical — the mutation process sometimes deletes or flips the bit that does the cooperating, some mutants end up causal-decision-theory-ish and defect — and they're conditional on her actual information, not on a hypothesis of shared rationality. And she added that if you insist on conditioning on common knowledge of rationality instead, you get probabilities of 1 and 0, which clears the threshold by more, so she cooperates either way.

(8) Tobias's parting shot was that this just relocates the entire weight of the argument onto an empirical estimate of algorithmic agreement rates that no one can actually produce for real agents, and that "cooperate when the other guy is enough like you" is generosity with a formula stapled to it.
