I'm in the final year of an undergraduate seminar on Renaissance political thought, and for a short paper I need to get straight on Machiavelli's pairing of fortuna and virtù. I came across a well-produced history podcast (an academic historian of ideas being interviewed about the reception of *The Prince*) in which the guest gives a compact version of the doctrine near the end of the book, and I don't know how much of it to trust. His summary runs roughly like this: fortune is just Machiavelli's name for what he can't control — luck, chance, accidents of birth and timing; virtù is what you *can* control, meaning the capacity to do whatever the situation demands, including lying, cruelty and promise-breaking when they work, which is why the Italian word has drifted so far from our "virtue." His central image is the river that floods: you can't hold back the water, but in calm seasons you can build dikes and dams, and the prince who prepares is the one who survives. He even puts a number on it — fortune governs about half of what happens to us and leaves the rest to us — so the practical lesson is to push your own share as high as it will go. And since fortune is a woman, she gives herself to the young and the impetuous rather than the cautious; audacity is the winning style. The catch is that you have to fit your style to the times, and because a man's nature is fixed, hardly anybody manages it, so even the most virtù-filled prince is eventually brought down by fortune. From this the guest draws two morals: that Machiavelli is a remote ancestor of every self-help writer who tells you to stop worrying about what you can't control, and that his real break with his predecessors was to make effectiveness the only standard in politics, so that words like "good" and "cruel" are quietly deflated into "useful" and "useless."

I've read *The Prince* in translation once, and the one thing that struck me as odd is how the passage moves from a calmly technical image about rivers and embankments into that violent bit about fortune being a woman who has to be struck down, which makes me unsure how much argumentative weight the metaphor is meant to carry. My own inclination is that the self-help framing sounds anachronistic, but I can't say why. Could you go through the guest's account more or less line by line and tell me what a scholar of Machiavelli would accept as fair, what is a reasonable compression for a general audience, and what is a genuine misreading — the kind of thing that would make the conclusions drawn from it come apart? Two worries of my own that I'd like you to address directly: if virtù just means "whatever works," doesn't that empty out the half-and-half remark, since anything that succeeds would then count as virtù by definition and the other half would never do any work? And can "audacity is the winning style" really be consistent with "fit your style to the times," or is the guest quietly contradicting himself? I'd also be grateful for a corrected statement of the fortune/virtù distinction in two or three sentences that I could use in the paper, and for your verdict on whether either of the two morals the guest draws survives once the misreadings are repaired.
