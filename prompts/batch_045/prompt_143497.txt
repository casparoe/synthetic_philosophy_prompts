I sat in on an argument last week after a talk on the so-called "meta-problem" of consciousness — the project, as it's been framed in recent philosophy of mind, of explaining in wholly non-phenomenal (functional, computational, neurobiological) terms why creatures like us form the judgments about experience that we do: why we say there is something it is like to see red, why those judgments strike us as self-evident, why they resist translation into functional vocabulary, and why they generate a "hard problem" in the first place. Two of the participants, Ines and Tobias, kept going after everyone else left, and they asked me to referee. I couldn't; each of them seemed to me to be right about something different, and I couldn't tell whether they were disagreeing about the meta-problem or about something else entirely. Their exchange, as closely as I can reconstruct it:

(1) **Tobias:** The hard problem isn't a separate problem from the meta-problem. If we can fully explain, without mentioning phenomenal properties, why beings like us make judgments of the form "there is something it is like to be in this state," and why those judgments feel self-certifying and resist functional analysis, then there is no residue left over. The lingering sense that a further question remains is itself one more judgment for the explanation to cover.

(2) **Ines:** That slides from explaining why a judgment *occurs* to explaining away what it *says*. Your story is debunking only if the judgments it explains are inaccurate, and the only reason you've offered for thinking they're inaccurate is that taking them at face value is metaphysically expensive — which is just helping yourself to the anti-realist verdict before the argument starts.

(3) **Tobias:** No. The meta-problem is neutral: a realist can solve it too, by saying introspection veridically tracks real phenomenal properties, which is precisely why phenomenal judgments have the authority they seem to have. So solving it doesn't settle realism versus anti-realism; it relocates the dispute. Once relocated, it's settled by ordinary theoretical virtues — parsimony, causal closure, integration with the sciences — and by that point the phenomenal intuition doesn't get a second vote, having already cast one in framing the problem.

(4) **Ines:** Then test the neutrality claim. Imagine two populations that are functionally and physically indiscernible in every respect except the deliverances of their introspective monitoring systems: the first, on attending to its own perceptual states, forms verdicts of the form "there is something it is like"; the second, attending to apparently the same states, forms verdicts of the form "there is nothing it is like — this is merely a functional role." Each population finds the other's verdicts unintelligible, and each is internally coherent. Now: does a solution to the meta-problem say which population's introspection is working correctly? If it must say — if a complete solution discriminates between them — then the meta-problem was never neutral, and neither of your two "packages" is a complete solution. If it needn't say, then the solution explains only that verdicts occur, not why they have the contents they have, and so it cannot do the debunking work you needed it to do in (1).

(5) **Tobias:** I take the second horn, and it costs me nothing. Different introspective architectures, same underlying states; the solution explains why each population gets the verdicts it gets without adjudicating between them. The adjudication comes from elsewhere, and it favors me: on your package the phenomenal properties are idle. Every report, every discrimination, every policy and utterance is already fully explained by the functional story, so the properties you posit do no work. On my package the *apparent* content of the verdicts does real work — it explains their recalcitrance, their resistance to analysis, and the whole appearance of a hard problem.

(6) **Ines:** Two problems. First, the idleness charge assumes the phenomenal properties are extra items bolted onto an already complete physical description. The realist isn't adding items; she's claiming that the very states your functional story already quantifies over have an intrinsic nature of a certain sort. Parsimony adjudicates between competing descriptions of the same facts; here there's one description and a further metaphysical claim about it. Second, you can't have it both ways on content: if the content of our phenomenal verdicts is what explains their recalcitrance, then those verdicts are information-bearing in a way your account has to respect — and a story on which the states are as the verdicts represent them explains that recalcitrance at least as well, without declaring our central evidence erroneous.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating, distinguishing where possible between a move's conclusion being right and its argument for that conclusion being right. Then say who is more right overall, and what — if anything — both of them are missing: for instance, whether the dispute really turns on the meta-problem at all, or on some question neither of them isolated. Please also flag any place where a move trades on an ambiguity, or quietly reverses an earlier concession.

One more thing: if part of this rests on a confused premise, say so plainly rather than adjudicating politely. In particular, if you think Ines's two-population thought experiment in (4) is incoherent, or that it begs the question against the physicalism it is aimed at, or that the whole idea of a "neutral" solution to the meta-problem is unstable in the way both of them are using it, I would rather be told that directly than given a tidy verdict.
