I write novels — literary-ish, three out — and there's a word my workshop throws around that I want to get to the bottom of: "manipulative." You all know the accusation. Kill the dog, kill the kid, put a swelling string section under the deathbed scene, and you're charged with manufacturing a feeling instead of earning it. "Earned" is the compliment on the other side. But I cant figure out what the norm actually is, and it looks inconsistent to me in a few places.

One: critics praise books for being frightening or nail-biting, and nobody thinks it's a defect that the fear was produced by pure craft — pacing, withheld information, a short chapter ending on a cliff. But make them cry by equally deliberate craft and half of them says cheap. Two: "show, don't tell" says give the reader the material and let the feeling arrive on its own, and telling the reader how to feel is a beginner move — yet the devices that get called manipulative are usually the ones that work hardest, not the ones that tell. Three, and this is the bit actually bugging me: in the book I'm finishing there's a stretch in second person where the reader knows, from about page nine, that the brother is already dead and the narrator doesn't know yet. Readers tell me they cry through the whole stretch. But nothing sad has happened yet in the story, and nobody in the book is sad. So what am I supposedly earning?

A philosopher friend said this connects to something called the "wrong kind of reasons" problem — something about a demon offering you money to admire somebody, which is a reason to get yourself to admire them but not a reason having anything to do with whether theyre admirable. Sounds plausible. No idea what to do with it.

So: what does this earned-vs-manipulative business presuppose about emotion, and about fiction? Does it presuppose that feelings about made-up people can be warranted or unwarranted, correct or incorrect, and not just caused? Does it presuppose that fiction can hand you reasons at all, given that nobody in it is real? Is the horror-vs-weepie asymmetry consistent with whatever theory the workshop is running, or is it just genre snobbery? What does the practice strictly presuppose versus what merely fits it well — could a couple of different theories of emotion both license it, and where exactly is it underdetermined? If we dropped the idea that responses to fiction can be warranted and replaced it with "there are only effects, caused by technique," what would my critique group have to change about how it talks, and what would be left for it to complain about? And on my second-person section: what would the feeling even be about, if no character has it?
