Hey — I've somehow ended up running the "how we actually pick causes" session at my giving group's weekend meetup for the second year in a row, and I want to do better than last time. Context: about 25 people, mostly engineers and data scientists from the same company, we pool donations and also volunteer hours, 45 minutes with maybe 15 for argument at the end. Last year derailed in two specific ways — one guy spent twenty minutes on whether you're allowed to count flow-through effects (people earning more, paying more tax, etc.) and nobody could tell if we'd resolved it, and someone else made a spreadsheet where everybody scored causes 1–10 on importance, tractability and neglectedness and then multiplied the three numbers together as if the product meant something. I get why that's wrong but I couldn't articulate it fast enough in the room, and I definately don't want to just ban the spreadsheet.

The things I personally keep tripping over, which I want the session to fix rather than paper over: I can never cleanly seperate "this problem is solvable at all" from "the next dollar or the next hour here does a lot", and I keep catching myself sliding between "how big is this problem" and "how much better is the best thing here than what we'd do instead" — I've started calling the second one the decision gap, which is my own made-up term, so tell me if that's a bad framing or if there's a cleaner way to hold the two apart. There's also a thing I noticed last year that nobody had a name for: a field can look neglected by total dollars while the particular slot we'd step into is actually contested, because everyone with our skillset would land in exactly the same role, and then our entry adds less than the headline numbers suggest. I don't know if that's a fourth factor, a correction to neglectedness, or just a reason to stop trusting the framework.

So: don't explain importance/tractability/neglectedness to me, plan how I should explain it. What has to come before what, and why — what genuinely depends on what being in place first, given this audience will want to start multiplying immediately — and which one of those three ideas has to be introduced first to stop the multiplication instinct from doing damage. One concrete example to carry the whole session, with a verdict on the two I was considering (a municipal lead-pipe replacement program in a mid-size US city, versus building capacity for a state-level ballot measure campaign): say what a carrying example has to have and what it has to lack, and I'd rather it not be a disease or a vaccine, and not AI safety, because half the room works on ML and we will lose forty minutes. Then the misconceptions in the order you'd expect them from this crowd and exactly where in the sequence to head each off — including the ones I listed, plus whichever one I haven't thought of that you think is the real killer. One question at the end that tells me they got it rather than just got quiet, and one thing I should openly refuse to explain in 45 minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing.
