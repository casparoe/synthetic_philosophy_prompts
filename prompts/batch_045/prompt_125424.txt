Three weeks from today I am defending the central chapter of my dissertation, in a ninety-minute session that is open to the wider lab, so I should expect questions both from my committee and from senior research engineers in the room. What I need is preparation for the question period, not a defense of the view: assume the chapter is written and give me the prep sheet I would want in front of me.

The chapter's thesis is a claim about what it means for two trained systems to implement the same mechanism, and a claim about what anomaly detection over systems should therefore measure. I argue that mechanism identity should be treated as a graded and interest-relative relation fixed by counterfactual transport: two systems implement the same mechanism for a phenomenon, to a degree, just insofar as interventions validated on one system transport to the other across a specified family of distribution shifts that was held out when the interventions were designed. I call this counterfactual-transport equivalence. The operational claim is that monitoring a model family — asking whether the mechanism behind a safety-relevant behavior in an audited reference checkpoint is still "the same" mechanism in a fine-tuned, distilled, quantized, or continued-pretraining successor — should target failures of transport rather than either behavioral agreement on a probe suite or similarity of internal representations. I argue these three come apart in both directions: two checkpoints can agree on every behavioral probe and score high on the usual representational similarity measures while cross-model activation grafting fails in one direction, and two checkpoints can share almost no representational geometry because they solved the task in different layers and bases while every intervention anyone designs transports cleanly. The protocol I propose is a pre-registered bank of roughly thirty interventions (activation patches, ablations, narrow fine-tunes, prompt-level perturbations), including positive and negative controls — interventions known to destroy the behavior in the reference model and known to leave it intact — scored by the fraction that transport within tolerance, with transport required in both directions. I flag two unresolved problems in the chapter: whether the relation should be symmetric at all (a smaller model's mechanism may contain a larger model's without the converse), and the fact that transport presupposes a unit-to-unit alignment between the two systems.

The committee, whose commitments matter more to me than their names do:

My advisor works in mechanistic interpretability — circuits, sparse autoencoders, activation patching. She is sympathetic, but she thinks cross-model grafting failure is mostly an artifact of basis and permutation freedom rather than evidence about mechanisms, and she will worry that a protocol requiring interventions on every new checkpoint is too expensive to run in a real deployment pipeline.

The external examiner is a philosopher of science in the new-mechanist tradition — mechanisms as organized entities and activities that produce a phenomenon. She is likely to say that mechanisms are individuated relative to a phenomenon description and to explanatory interests, that on her account identity is not the kind of thing that admits of degrees, and that a graded criterion is a similarity metric wearing metaphysical clothing. I expect a multiple-realizability version of this too.

The third member works on distribution shift, out-of-distribution detection, and deployment monitoring. His likely line is that my protocol is a richly specified two-sample test over a summary of intervention responses, that deleting the word "mechanism" costs me nothing, and that the held-out shift family encodes a prior about deployment that I have chosen rather than derived.

The fourth is a computational cognitive scientist who does cross-system comparison between models and brains. She will press the cross-substrate case, and she is the person most likely to notice that the alignment step does the real work: given enough units and enough freedom in choosing the mapping, structure preservation becomes close to vacuous, in the way that objection is standardly raised against purely structural claims in philosophy of science.

What I want from you:

Eight to ten objections, ordered by how likely they are combined with how much damage they would do, each attributed to the member or kind of person who would raise it and phrased in the vocabulary that person actually uses rather than in mine. For each: whether it has real force against the chapter or only against a careless version of it, what I should concede in the course of answering, a reply I could actually deliver in about two minutes out loud, and a note on where that reply is weakest. Then, separately: what I should concede myself in the first five minutes, before anyone asks anything, so that the obvious objections lose their sting; the single reply the chapter cannot survive without; the objection I am privately hoping nobody raises, together with what I should say if they do; and an assessment of which objections I can answer by retreating from the metaphysical claim about mechanism identity to the operational claim about what to monitor, without thereby gutting the chapter.

Please do not pad the sheet with generic skepticism about interpretability, and do not spend time on literature I should read or on how to revise the chapter. I want the questions and what to say back to them.
