I set the following question on a final exam in an upper-level course on philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I would like an independent read on one answer before I finalise grades.

The question was:

Fodor's language of thought hypothesis comes in two grades of strength. Call the modest version the claim that thinking consists in operations over mental representations with combinatorial syntactic and semantic structure: complex representations have constituents, the arrangement of constituents mirrors the structure of the thought, and the content of the whole is a function of the contents of the parts. Call the ambitious version the addition of the full apparatus of a logical language — variables, quantifiers, and rules of inference defined over syntactic form — so that thinking is inference over sentences in the head.

(a) Distinguish the two versions, and say which Fodor is committed to.
(b) Set out the argument from the productivity and systematicity of thought, and say what it is evidence for: the modest version, the ambitious version, or both.
(c) Set out Fodor's argument that a system of representation cannot itself be acquired by learning, and explain what this is supposed to establish about Mentalese.
(d) State the strongest objection to the hypothesis and say how a defender should reply.

One student answered as follows:

Fodor's target is the view that thinking might be a matter of manipulating mental images or associations; he argues that the only workable account treats thought as computation over symbol structures. The hypothesis is not the claim that we think in English: Mentalese is a distinct internal system, and Fodor holds that it is innate rather than learned. That is the modest thesis, and the ambitious thesis adds that this system has the expressive apparatus of a logical language.

(b) The positive argument is from productivity and systematicity. There is no finite bound on the thoughts a thinker can entertain, and the abilities thinkers have come in packages: anyone who can think that dogs chase cats can also think that cats chase dogs, and anyone who can think that Mary loves John can think that someone loves John. The best explanation is that thoughts have constituents that are recombined by rules and that content is inherited from the parts. Because the systematicity data concern the recombination of the very same constituents in new configurations, the argument supports the ambitious version: only a format with variable binding and quantificational structure can take a constituent from one position and reuse it in another, so the two versions really stand or fall together here.

(c) The second argument concerns acquisition. Learning proceeds by forming and testing hypotheses, and framing a hypothesis already requires a representational system at least as rich as what is to be learned; one cannot acquire a representational system from scratch by learning it, on pain of regress. So Mentalese, or at least its basic stock of constituents, must be innately specified — which is what explains the speed and uniformity of first-language acquisition. It also explains why Fodor denies that prelinguistic infants and nonhuman animals have thoughts of the same kind at all: they lack the combinatorial vehicle that thought requires.

(d) The strongest objection is from connectionism. Fodor's reply is decisive: distributed representations have no constituents to recombine, so networks cannot in principle exhibit the systematic generalization that thought displays, and the connectionist programme survives only as a story about how a classical architecture might be realised in neural hardware.
