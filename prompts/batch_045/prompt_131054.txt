I'm the person who ends up owning the merge path in our config/policy service, so I'm fluent in the usual stuff — Raft, quorums of three out of five, per-record last-writer-wins, CRDT joins, read repair, invariant checks that run after a merge, idempotence/commutativity/convergence, all of that. Philosophy I know basically nothing about, though a colleague mentioned the "discursive dilemma" when I complained about this at lunch: we keep a small policy state replicated across five regions, every record a boolean, with an invariant roughly like allow_deploy → (tests_green ∧ security_signoff). Each region holds a perfectly consistent state, we merge per record by plain majority, and last month the merged state came out with allow_deploy true, tests_green true, security_signoff false. Nobody was shocked for long, and we patched it with what we call minimal-flip reconcilliation: take per-record majority, then run a repair pass that flips the fewest records needed to get back inside the invariant, ties broken by a fixed priority order over records. My colleague says the philosophers have a name for this whole shape of problem and a pile of impossibility results hanging off it.

So here's the ask. Restate the discursive dilemma and the judgment-aggregation problem around it as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — replicas, local state, quorums, per-record merge, integrity constraints as invariants, repair passes, monotonicity, determinism, recomputing a derived value at every node versus shipping the derived value around as its own record — and then give me the residue report. Which bits translate cleanly? I take it conclusion-based aggregation is "only replicate the output bit, don't care about the inputs," premise-based is "replicate the inputs and recompute the derived bit locally," and the impossibility result says something like "no merge rule that is symmetric across nodes, symmetric across records and their values, and local/per-record can be guaranteed to land inside the invariant." Is that the right shape, and is the result basically the same animal as an FLP- or CAP-flavoured tradeoff, or a genuinely different kind of thing?

Then the distortion and the residue. On distortion: I'm suspicious of "the group believes that p." In my system the cluster is designed to behave like one machine, so the collective state is just the spec and there's nothing to argue about, whereas a committee was never designed and the collective attitude is the thing under dispute. Relatedly, our merge is trying to track an external ground truth, so I'd grade premise-based against conclusion-based by error rate against that truth, but the philosophers seem to care about coherence whether or not it tracks anything. On residue: what has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? My guesses are (a) the requirement that each node's local state already be consistent and complete, when our replicas are routinely stale and we just tolerate it and repair later, (b) the fact that the agenda and the integrity constraint get fixed by somebody with power over the group rather than by a schema designer who also wrote the consumers, and (c) whatever is meant by the group counting as an agent rather than just as a service.

Be honest in both directions. If from inside my framework the result comes close to "per-record majority merge doesn't preserve cross-record invariants, obviously, so run the invariant check after the merge," say that plainly, and then tell me whether the philosophers are actually getting more than that — for instance whether the force of the result depends on constraints that only look mandatory once what you're aggregating is people and not machines, or on the fact that a voting body can't just add a repair pass that overrides what the members said. And if I'm the one missing something, I'd rather hear exactly what it is than be flattered. No references or reading list please, just the translation and what breaks in it.
