I'm in my third year of a PhD (metaphysics, mostly causation stuff) and I was at a work-in-progress session last week where two senior people in my department — call them M and R, I'd rather not name them — got into a pretty long argument after the talk, and I've been turning it over since. I've tried to reconstruct it faithfully below though I'm definately smoothing it a bit; I kept switching sides while writing it up, so please don't read my reconstruction as taking one. The case they were using throughout: a patient on oxygen in a small clinic dies overnight because the nurse on duty never checked the tank, which had run empty. (1) M opened by denying that the nurse's not checking is a cause of the death at all. Causation, he said, is a physical relation: it needs a mechanism, a continuous process, something with causal powers doing the producing, and an absence has no location, no duration as an event, and no powers — it can't push anything. The real causal story is entirely positive: the tank emptied, oxygen delivery fell, brain cells died. The nurse's not checking merely fails to interrupt that sequence, and our willingness to call it a cause is blame in disguise. (2) R replied that the difference-making notion is the one actually doing work everywhere we care about — law, epidemiology, accident reports — and that production accounts can't even handle ordinary special-science causation (smoking causes lung cancer isn't a mechanism claim at the level of an individual patient). She also pointed out that the counterfactual picks out the *right* omission: the nurse checked at 2am and the tank was fine, she didn't check at 3am, and only the 3am non-check is the difference-maker, which no mechanism story will single out for you. (3) M conceded all of that and denied the inference: omissions are difference-makers, yes, but difference-making is *explanation*, not causation. Citing an omission explains why the normal preventive process didn't run, the way citing a law or a reason explains; the causal relation itself holds only among positive occurrences. And legal talk of "cause," he added, is a stand-in for responsibility, so it's no evidence about causation. (4) R said that severance costs too much: the default reason citing causes is such a good explanatory strategy is that causes make their effects happen, and if explanation floats free of that you owe a replacement story. She also argued absences are manipulable in experiments exactly like presences — you randomize whether a reminder is sent and you get a treatment effect for the no-letter arm — so any interventionist criterion already treats them as causes. (5) M's last move was that the manipulation point begs the question, since you bring about an "absence" only by doing something else, so what you really manipulated was a positive event; and that letting absences in makes causation hopelessly promiscuous (the Queen of England's failure to check the tank, and infinitely many other non-occurrences, become causes of the death) while breaking down in preemption cases — if the backup nurse on the ward would also have skipped the check, then neither omission looks like a difference-maker, yet we want to say both are, and if you patch that by letting each count as sufficient-in-the-circumstances you've quietly abandoned the difference-making test you started with.

What I'd like from you is a move-by-move adjudication: for each of the five moves, say whether it's clearly right, mostly right, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and why in a sentence or two. Then tell me who's more right overall, whether the dispute is partly verbal (i.e., are they using "cause" to mean different things), and what — if anything — both of them are missing that I should read up on conceptually rather than bibliographically.

Three things I also want you to apply whatever verdict you reach to, because I think they're where the argument actually bites and neither of them addressed them. (a) A timing variant: suppose the tank in fact emptied at 2:30 and, even if the nurse had checked at 3, the patient would have died by 3:10 anyway; the non-check only changed *when* he died. Is the omission a cause of the death, or only of its time, and does it matter whether you think of the death as an event with temporal parts? (b) A higher-order variant: the ward manager never installed a low-pressure alarm that would have woken the nurse, so her omission stops something that would have stopped something that would have stopped the death — an omission that prevents a prevention of a prevention. Does causal distance of that kind dilute anything, or is it just more of the same? (c) A worry about the test itself: the relevant counterfactual for the nurse seems to be drawn against what she was *supposed* to do, whereas in a gardener-and-withered-plant case it's drawn against a statistical regularity. If omission causation needs a normative baseline, is that embarrassing for a metaphysical account of causation but fine for an account of responsibility — and does that vindicate M's blaming remark in move (1) after all?
