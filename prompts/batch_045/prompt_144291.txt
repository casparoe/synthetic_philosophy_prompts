I am building a decision tree for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of markets, and I want it to function as a genuine classification instrument rather than as a list of positions with labels attached.

Run every path from the same starting commitment: the no-trade result is sound — traders who share a prior over a commonly known state space, and whose willingness to trade at the posted price is sufficiently common knowledge, cannot all be trading because each expects to gain at the others' expense. Each branch node below that starting point should be either a single assumption one can drop or a single diagnostic question that a student could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to.

The distinctions I care about, and that I want to see represented as branches, are these: whether to drop the common prior itself; whether to drop the assumption that agents can and do compute and condition on their posteriors, as opposed to reasoning coarsely, heuristically, or under informational and computational constraint; whether to drop the assumption that trade is driven by belief at all, as opposed to liquidity needs, hedging, inventory management, or a taste for gambling; whether to drop the assumption of one commonly known state space, so that what traders disagree about is the coarseness of the model or which data-generating process is the right one; whether to drop unified attitudes to risk and ambiguity alongside the prior; and whether to retain belief-driven disagreement but explain the observed volume of trade through short-sales constraints, resale option value, or limits on arbitrage under delegated capital.

Give me roughly eight to ten leaves, each carrying the name of a position on why actual markets trade as much as they do, or "unnamed" where the combination has no established name. For each leaf, add one line on what the position implies about the epistemic authority of prices — concretely, whether learning that a trade cleared at price p is itself a reason for a bystander to move her credence toward what p implies — and one line on that position's most awkward commitment.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate for someone who answers honestly, and flag any combination of answers the tree permits that nobody seems to occupy, with a one-line note on whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
