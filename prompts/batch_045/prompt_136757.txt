I keep a running document where I write down, as carefully as I can, where I've actually landed on contested questions, and before I inflict the entry on the gender-equality paradox on my reading group I'd like someone to audit it. I've been arguing about this one on the blogs for something like fifteen years, and I can pass the ideological Turing test for both camps on it, which is precisely why I can't see which parts of my own package are load-bearing and which are decoration. I don't need citations and I don't want a reading list: I've read the literature, and the literature is where the disagreement lives. What I want is an audit of the joint tenability of the six commitments below.

(1) The pattern is real. In the richer and more gender-egalitarian countries, fields of study and occupations are more gender-skewed, not less, and I don't think this is mostly an artifact of how the equality indices get constructed — it shows up on several independent proxies, and it shows up within countries when you look at what people choose once money stops being the binding constraint. I'd add the meta-commitment that the 'it's all measurement error' reply strikes me as just as ideologically driven as the 'it's all testosterone' reply, and I've tried not to pick the reading that flatters my politics.

(2) Part of the explanation is that prosperity and a safety net remove the scarcity filter, so what's left doing the sorting is intrinsic interest — the people-versus-things orientation, roughly — and I take the sex difference in that orientation to be substantially innate: heritable in the ordinary behavioural-genetics sense, visible very early in children's activity preferences, and stable across cultures. So the paradox is, at least partly, what intrinsic preference looks like when you stop forcing people's hands. Case verdict: I am not scandalised by the Norwegian nursing and engineering numbers. I don't think Norway has anything to fix here.

(3) But I also have a model I like, and I think it explains the same pattern with no biological loading at all. Call the two channels the interest channel and the insurance channel. A field's female share is set by (i) how well it matches the population distribution of intrinsic interests and (ii) how much security and status it buys relative to a woman's realistic alternatives. In poor countries the two channels point the same way for women: engineering and medicine are among the very few routes to an independent, respectable, non-precarious adult life, so women who are not especially interested enter them anyway. In rich countries the channels come apart — a woman who wants security and status can get it from law, medicine, psychology or management — so only the interest channel is left sorting, and the share falls. Note what this model predicts: the effect should track development and the local prestige gradient across fields, not gender-egalitarian policy as such, and it predicts that raising engineering's relative pay in a rich country shouldn't move the female share much even though it obviously would in a poor one.

(4) I also think sex discrimination in the STEM pipeline is real and substantial in my own country, which is rich but not at the frontier of egalitarianism: discouragement early on, hostile classroom and workplace climate, bias in hiring and in who gets encouraged. The 'it's all free choice' reading strikes me as complacent. Case verdict: my university's computer science department has a genuine climate problem and should be spending real money on it.

(5) Justice is about individuals, not about distributions. Nobody is owed a group share; what matters is that individuals aren't blocked, misled, or coerced. Relatedly, I'm against the style of intervention that treats the interests girls already have as a defect to be corrected — no 'let's make girls like robots' programmes.

(6) I nevertheless think the underrepresentation of women in engineering in rich democracies is a genuine social problem, and that universities, funders and professional bodies should be spending serious money trying to reduce it.

A friend who read the draft said (3) is a motivated-reasoning shelter: that I have built a model which lets me accept the paradox without being the kind of person who believes in innate differences, and that (2) and (3) cannot both be doing the work I want them to do. I genuinely don't know whether that's a real objection or just a rude way of calling me a coward.

So: which of these pull against each other, and how badly? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally serious; for each, say exactly what has to give and what the cheapest revision would cost me. Tell me which apparent conflicts dissolve on inspection and what the dissolution consists in — especially whether (4) really is compatible with (2)'s deference to the terminal choice, or whether I'm only getting away with it by looking at two different points in the pipeline. Tell me if any of (1) through (6) is doing no work in the set, and if the set needs some further commitment I haven't stated. And then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'm not asking to be told what to think about sex differences. I'm asking which of these six can't stay as they are.
