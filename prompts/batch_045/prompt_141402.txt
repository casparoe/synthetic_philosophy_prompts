I teach one fifty-minute section next week introducing the Frege–Geach problem to second-years who can recite the non-cognitivist package — moral judgments are not beliefs, they are desire-like states that assertion expresses — but who have met none of the responses. Give me a plan for the hour, not an explanation of the problem.

My half-formed idea is about sequencing. Presentations I have seen all open with the moral modus ponens, and I now think that is a mistake: the conditional is exactly the embedding where the standard reply, states directed at combinations of states, gets its grip most easily, so students conclude they have answered the problem before they have felt it. I want to open with negated and disjoined moral sentences instead — Murder is not wrong, and Either lying is wrong or promise-breaking is — where there is no tidy combination of attitudes for that reply to take hold of, and only then move to the conditional and to inference. Tell me whether that ordering survives scrutiny, what it costs (I worry it lets the problem look like a puzzle about disapproval rather than about validity), and whether I should also drop the usual warm-up of paraphrasing each moral clause into an attitude and watching the argument stop looking valid, which has always struck me as making the whole thing feel like a parlor trick.

Then: the one example that should carry the hour and what it must have and lack; the misconceptions in the order they will form — I expect the crude boo–hurrah caricature, the idea that expressivists can simply hand over a truth-conditional semantics, and the conflation of this problem with the problem of moral disagreement — each with the point in the sequence where it should be defused; what I need to say about validity before any semantics is on the board; what to decline to get into at this level; and one question for the last five minutes that would show me a student has the problem rather than the slogan.
