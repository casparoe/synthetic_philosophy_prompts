I have just come out of a long and, towards the end, rather heated Sunday afternoon argument with a friend about whether any of the classical Indian epistemology I have been reading on the side does work that an ordinary evidence committee is not already doing, and I want to settle it by trying to use it. Please answer as a professional philosopher who knows the pramāṇa literature properly and who also has some feel for how real committees behave: be blunt where my framing is muddled, and correct me where I have the doctrine wrong. Before you set out anything procedural, spend a few hundred words thinking freely about where this translation is most likely to break, and let that shape what you build.

The setting. I chair the Evidence and Assurance Panel of the national screening advisory body. We meet monthly, we see about forty proposals a year, and each gets roughly half a day. The panel is mixed: two clinicians, an epidemiologist, a laboratory director, a health economist and two lay members who cannot be handed anything with Sanskrit in it. For every proposal we must return exactly one verdict — ADOPT, PILOT, SUSPEND or DECLINE — and we must minute, in a paragraph the Board can read without us present, which of our reasons were evidential and which were judgements about acceptable harm. Recommendations go to the Programme Board and then to the Department, they are public, and they are occasionally challenged in public. Anything I hand the panel has to be runnable in the time available, has to fit on one side of A4 at twelve point, and must not require arithmetic the lay members cannot follow.

The reason I dragged Indian epistemology into the argument is that our proposals pretty much sort into four kinds of support, and — as it happens — those four look like the four knowledge sources the Nyāya school is standardly said to accept: our own monitoring and audit data (perception); epidemiological and statistical evidence, usually observational (inference); national and international guideline documents and the expert witnesses who defend them (testimony); and, very often, an argument from what happened in another health system (comparison or analogy). My friend, who is an epidemiologist and a sceptic about anything that smells like theory, says the mapping is decorative: that we are just talking about data, studies, authorities and precedents, that what actually matters is effect sizes and intervals, and that nothing in this literature could change a single verdict we have ever issued. I think she is wrong but I could not say precisely where.

So: write me the procedure, not another exposition. Concretely, I need the following.

First, the inference test. I have been trying out an operational version of the three marks of a good reason: is the marker present in the case at hand; is it present in cases where the outcome does occur; and is it absent where the outcome does not. Give me that as a check the panel can actually run, tell me what counts as a sufficient answer to each of the three, and give me plain-English versions of the two failure modes I find most useful — the straying or irregular reason, where the marker also turns up without the outcome, and the reason that is defeated outright by a better source. Say what the panel should write down when it finds each.

Second, and this is where the argument got stuck. On one reading, a cognition stands as knowledge until something specific defeats it; on another, it needs positive warrant before it counts. My friend says we either trust and then look for defeaters, or we demand warrant up front, and that the first is laziness in a nice costume. My memory of the texts is that the Nyāya position is subtler than either — that validity is not self-certifying for them, and yet they still run something like innocent-until-reasonably-challenged. Settle this for the panel: which regime should we run, should we switch regime by decision class (irreversible versus reversible harms, population-scale versus contained), where exactly does the switch sit, who authorises it, and what does a challenge have to consist in to count as one rather than as a vague worry?

Third, testimony and chains. Our evidence is constantly second- or third-hand: a partner organisation's audit, cited in a guideline, cited by an expert. My own invention is a transmission register with a depth rule — the further a claim has travelled from whoever actually saw the thing, the more it has to be shored up. Tell me whether that is the right shape, and press on the case I cannot settle: a national guideline is not a person's word but a collective artefact with its own procedures, so the usual account of a reliable speaker seems to fit it badly. Does a chain inherit the strength of its first link, or can an institution add strength of its own?

Fourth, conflict between sources. Give me the ordering rule for when two sources disagree, and the meta-rule I take to be the interesting one — that a standing conflict between two sources is itself evidence that one of them carries a defect, and that the job is to locate the defect rather than to average the two.

Fifth, reasoning from absence. A great deal of what we do rests on nothing being there: no signal in the safety register, no harms reported, no excess mortality in the audit. I gather the later classical authors argued about whether knowledge of an absence needs a source of its own or is a case of something else. Give me the test the panel should apply — I would guess something like a detectability condition, that the thing would in fact have turned up if it were there — and tell me how demanding that condition has to be.

Sixth, hypothetical reasoning. Sometimes the strongest thing in the room is a counterfactual: if this did not work, we could not be seeing what we are seeing. My understanding is that such reasoning is standardly treated as an auxiliary rather than a fifth source, able to clear doubt away but not to establish anything positive, and I want that built into the procedure as an explicit rule about what the panel may and may not certify. If the tradition is divided on whether it yields knowledge, say where the weight of the position lies and what turns on it for us.

Throughout, mark the points where judgement cannot be eliminated, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to the Board because the question is one of values rather than evidence.

Then run it on three real-shaped proposals.

One: a blood-based biomarker panel proposed as a triage test before colonoscopy for patients with bowel symptoms. The main study is a large case–control study with an impressive odds ratio, but the comparison group was drawn from healthy blood donors rather than from symptomatic patients who turned out not to have the disease. There is also a specialist society's endorsement and a favourable line in an international guideline. The clinicians on the panel want ADOPT. My suspicion is that the procedure says SUSPEND, and I would like to see whether it says that for the reason I think.

Two: lowering the starting age for bowel screening from fifty to forty-five, supported by a neighbouring health system's ten-year experience, against our own two-year pilot which shows no mortality difference but was powered only to detect a very large effect. Which source defeats which here, and what changes if the defect turns out to be in our own monitoring data rather than in the analogy?

Three, and this is the one where I expect to dislike the answer: extending screening to a much rarer condition where a randomised trial cannot be had, because prevalence is too low and the comparison arm would be denied an established service. The only real support is that the stage distribution in our pilot is inexplicable if the test does not work. Everyone on the panel is convinced, I have already minuted that I intend to recommend ADOPT, and I suspect a properly built procedure tells me I may not. Show me what it should return, and what the minute to the Board should say instead.

Finally, be honest about what this cannot do. The account of inference I am borrowing was built for conclusive ascertainment, whereas everything we handle is probabilistic, and I want to know whether the translation survives that gap and what it costs us if it does. And settle the argument I started with: tell me which parts of the four-source taxonomy are doing real work in the procedure, and which are, as my friend claimed, decoration on top of what we were already doing.
