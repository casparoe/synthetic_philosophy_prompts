I teach an undergraduate seminar on the ethics and measurement of well-being, and I want to close the term with Ursula K. Le Guin's 1973 parable of Omelas: the city of permanent festival whose prosperity and cheerfulness depend on a single child kept in filth and darkness behind a locked door, shown to each citizen once they are old enough to understand, accepted by almost all of them after an initial shock, and refused by a few who simply walk away into the desert and never come back. I want you to read the story through our seminar's central question: whether the national happiness rankings measure happiness at all, and whether topping them is something a country should try to do.

Start by placing Omelas in the rankings. The headline measure is a life-evaluation question on a Cantril ladder from zero to ten, and the current leaders sit somewhere around 7.7, so give me a number for Omelas and justify it. I want you to work through what actually drives the figure: the citizens' happiness is informed rather than naive, and their acquiescence is settled rather than anguished; the child is doubly outside the sample frame, since national polling is of resident adults and she is neither; the ones who walk away have left the population altogether, so the average is computed over those who stayed. Then consider two variants. Suppose the headline number were replaced by experienced affect, by how the hours of a day actually feel, or by a well-being-adjusted-life-year sum of the sort the report now recommends to policymakers: would Omelas's position change, and in which direction? And note that the report's only floor concept, the hypothetical nation it calls Dystopia, is assembled from the lowest national averages on six explanatory variables, so it floors countries and not people — press that as hard as it will go.

Then do three more things. Invent one modification of the index that you think a defender of the rankings could not reasonably refuse, such as discounting a country by the share of its people who leave or who would leave if they could, or disqualifying any country with a resident below some absolute floor, and tell me whether Omelas passes your test and what it would cost a real government to game it. Say whether a country should try to top the table, distinguishing aiming at the headline number from aiming at the six variables that explain it from aiming at well-being-adjusted life years, and say whether a government genuinely at the top would have any reason to open the basement door. Finally, tell me where your own reading breaks down, since the story is a parable, its narrator refuses to describe where the walkers-away are going, and those who leave exit rather than reform, which may make a measurement-based objection to their city beside the point; and tell me what the story adds to the methodological debate that the methodology papers cannot say.

Give me your bottom line in the first paragraph — roughly where Omelas lands and what the parable does or does not do to the authority of the rankings — then develop the rest in continuous prose. No headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables: write it the way you would write a long letter to a colleague, about nine hundred to twelve hundred words. Where you are guessing or making a judgment call, say so plainly rather than hedging, and end by naming the single claim you would press my students hardest on.
