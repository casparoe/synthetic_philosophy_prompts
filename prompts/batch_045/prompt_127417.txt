Write a parody of an official university document: the minutes, with two or three appendices, of an ad hoc Committee on Unperceived Space at a small college whose trustees have become convinced of Berkeley's master argument for idealism. Aim for 600 to 700 words in a deadpan register of academic administration — numbered resolutions, budget lines, a staffing table, the peculiar passive-voiced courtesy of committee minutes.

Have the committee reason honestly from the argument rather than from a caricature of it. The step they rely on is narrow: one cannot so much as frame the thought of a thing existing unperceived, because in attempting to do so one has an idea of that thing before one's mind, and an idea can exist only in a mind that is perceiving it. They should draw only the institutional consequence, not the metaphysical one — nothing about tables being unreal — namely that a space nobody is currently sensing is not a cost the college is incurring. Build the policies on that: unoccupied rooms struck from the space inventory, heating and insurance prorated to intervals of actual occupancy, a moratorium on new construction on the grounds that a building no one has yet visited cannot be shown to be needed.

Then push it further in two directions the real position invites. First, the committee hires a Perpetual Perceiver to keep the campus in being overnight, and a footnote records the objection that an uninterrupted divine perception already does this work more cheaply — record it without letting the joke collapse. Second, someone notices that the Perceiver is herself only a collection of ideas, and so requires perceiving, and a subcommittee is struck on the resulting regress. The final appendix should leave the reader with the worry that the argument's conceivability step proves too much — that it seems equally to forbid thinking about an absent colleague or about a meeting next Tuesday — and with the sense that a distinction between having an idea of a thing and perceiving that thing has been quietly collapsed, while no character ever names either worry. No winking, no tree-falls-in-the-forest gags: the comedy should come entirely from institutional literalism applied to a real feature of the argument.
