Hi there. I teach a graduate seminar on practical rationality and self-knowledge, and I'm rewriting the week on transformative experience because I've never been happy with how the discussion lands. The dialectic as I set it up: for a decision like whether to have a first child — or, in Paul's more colorful version, whether to become a vampire — the standard expected-utility story seems to break down, because the whole point is that you can't know from the inside what the outcome is like, and other people's reports, however detailed and however well-confirmed, don't seem to give you the thing you'd need in order to have your *own* preference about it. The move Paul makes is to say that the choice isn't really utility-maximizing at all: it's a matter of whether you want to find out, an exploratory or epistemic choice rather than a maximizing one. My students always push back with some version of "come on, people make these choices constantly and mostly do fine," and I want an essay that takes that pushback at its absolute strongest and then actually delivers a verdict: is there a genuine, distinctive normative problem here for the individual decider, or is it an epistemic predicament that perfectly ordinary resources already handle well enough?

The bit I keep chewing on, and which I want the essay to engage with rather than skim past, is a variant I've been calling the Escalator. Take the same kind of choice — say, a one-shot irreversible procedure that permanently gives you a new sensory modality nobody can describe to you in advance — except that now it's available in eight graded doses, each one cheap and fully reversible, where each dose hands you a partial, time-limited revelation of what the next dose up would be like and then wears off. So you can climb rung by rung, and after each rung you're slightly a different person with slightly different tastes, deciding whether to go one rung further. Does the availability of that ladder dissolve the original problem, or does it just reproduce it in miniature at every step? And notice it seems to add a second problem of its own: what authority does the self at rung three have over the self at rung six, and is climbing best understood as self-creation, as self-experimentation, or as something closer to using your earlier selves as mere instruments on behalf of a person who doesn't exist yet? There's a nearby move that doesn't need the ladder at all — you can just hand the decision to someone who's already had the experience, or to your own future self via a binding pre-commitment — but that looks like it relocates the puzzle one step over rather than solving it, since you'd be letting preferences that were *shaped by* the transformation settle whether to undergo it.

So: an essay that weighs the case for thinking there's a real failure of rational choice here against the case for thinking it dissolves under scrutiny, and that ends by picking a side and defending it. Two things I'd especially like pinned down. First, where the burden of argument actually lies — is it on whoever claims expected-utility reasoning gives out, or on whoever claims it soldiers on? Second, on the Escalator specifically: if you think the problem survives the ladder, give me the right normative description of the agent's situation standing at rung three; if you think the ladder dissolves it, say exactly which feature of the ladder does the dissolving work, and why that feature isn't also quietly present in the ordinary one-shot case. Please don't hedge your way to a both-sides-have-a-point conclusion at the end — I can get that from my students, and I've read enough of it lately to last me a while.
