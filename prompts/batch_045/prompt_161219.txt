I am a novelist, and I have rashly agreed to run a ninety-minute session at a writers' conference this summer on whether beauty is objective, for a room of roughly twenty novelists and short-story writers who have no philosophy and no appetite for any. Do not explain Hume's essay on the standard of taste to me; tell me how to lead the room into it. The obstacle I most want help with is that these writers are thoroughgoing relativists about taste in principle and unshakable critics in practice — they will say de gustibus with one breath and rank the shortlist with the next. Give me the order the ideas must go in and why: what has to be in place before the notion of a true judge can be introduced without sounding either like snobbery or like taking a poll, and where I must separate a sentiment from a claim about a sentiment, so that the essay's reconciliation — beauty no quality in things themselves, and yet some verdicts right and others wrong — does not land as a contradiction smoothed over by fine writing. Give me one case to carry the session and the constraints it must meet: a written work, one whose admirers can locate a specific and removable defect in those unmoved by it, and one where what fails is the pleasure rather than the opinion — plus what the case must avoid, since I can feel the hour draining away into sales figures and into whether anything lasts a hundred years. Three misconceptions I expect and want placed at particular points in the sequence rather than answered up front: that a standard must be a rule applicable by someone with no taste, that the joint verdict of qualified judges is only the majority verdict of a smaller room, and that if the verdict is right the rest of us owe the pleasure. Name a fourth I have not thought of; mine is the prejudice of knowing who wrote the thing. End with one thing I can ask for in writing that would show me the point has landed rather than that they have merely been told experts exist, and with an explicit statement of what I should refuse to go into at this level, and why each would sink the ninety minutes. Answer in American English, in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — as you would write a long email to a friend doing you a favor.
