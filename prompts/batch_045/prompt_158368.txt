I am a graduate instructor putting together a one-page paper handout for the session of our democratic theory seminar on the boundary problem: not who gets to vote on what under an already-settled constitution, but who is entitled to make up the demos for a given decision in the first place. Last week we lost the last twenty minutes to an argument about whether someone who sleeps in one jurisdiction, works in another, and pays taxes in both has a stake in the first one's transit levy, and it became clear that half the room was using being affected by a decision and being subject to the authority that makes it interchangeably even though they come apart in exactly that sort of case. So what I want is not another argument for one answer, but a handout that lays out the space of answers and lets a student locate herself in it.

Concretely: a decision tree in plain text, with a single start node, nine to twelve branch nodes carrying explicit routing (if yes, go to node 7), and seven to nine leaves. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first settle what the load-bearing questions actually are and which combinations of answers they permit, then place the familiar positions in the space you have constructed, and where a combination of answers is unoccupied keep it as an empty leaf with a one-line note on whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. A starting commitment along the lines of the composition of the demos having to be justifiable to those it excludes is fine, but replace it if you think something else routes better. The distinctions I care about for this seminar are: how affectedness itself is counted (any causal difference at all, versus a setback to important interests, versus an impact on someone's ability to carry out a life plan, versus difference-making relative to the alternatives actually on the ballot); whether the correct criterion is level-relative or whether every issue has its own demos, and whether a demos has to be capable of acting as a corporate agent over time in a way that would rule out per-issue floating membership; and whether the criterion is meant as necessary and sufficient or merely as a pro tanto consideration that can be outweighed by feasibility, stability, or an existing community's claim to self-determination. I also want the tree to have a branch for the view that the boundary question has no principled answer at all and that the demos is constituted by an exercise of power it then ratifies, with a flag if you think that is really a refusal of the question rather than an answer to it.

The cases I want doing the diagnostic work, phrased so that a politically engaged first-year could answer each node without knowing what any position is called and without being asked whether she accepts some named principle: the cross-border commuter above; a long-term non-citizen resident who has lived, worked, and paid taxes in the polity for two decades; a citizen who has emigrated permanently and retains no property in or intention to return to the polity; farmers in a neighbouring country whose water supply depends on an upstream dam the polity is voting on; future generations who will bear the costs of a nuclear waste repository; a tourist subject to local law for three days; a resident who rejects the polity's authority altogether; children affected by a school funding decision; incarcerated people disenfranchised by statute; and a foreign corporation or a foreign state as an affected party. Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or unnamed plus a one-line description if nobody defends it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please also flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and tell me why. It has to work printed, so no drawn diagrams: explicit if/then routing and a compact table of the leaves at the end.
