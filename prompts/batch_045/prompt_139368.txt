Two years ago my family moved from Singapore to the Netherlands for my husband's work, and I have since joined the parents' council at our children's school, which votes next month on scrapping the negotiated sanctions and the loose dress code in favour of a fixed detention schedule and a strict uniform. Both camps have started invoking the tight-versus-loose cultures literature, so I read a well-reviewed popularization of it by a cultural psychologist, and before I repeat any of it in a meeting I want to know what survives. My notes on the book's argument run roughly as follows: tight cultures have strong social norms and little tolerance of deviance, loose ones the reverse; Gelfand's team measured this in some thirty countries by asking people how much latitude there should be in everyday situations and how far rules ought to be enforced even when nobody is watching, and the tight end came out as East Asia and the Middle East, the loose end as the United States and Western Europe. Tightness, the book says, is really just collectivism plus uncertainty avoidance under another name, and it is caused by ecological threat, since societies with more disease, denser populations and scarcer resources historically needed firm rules and still do. Tight societies get less crime and more order; loose ones get creativity and openness, which is a luxury affordable only in good times, so any institution under real pressure should tighten. Hence one governor's proposal that we run a tightness survey on the parents and set school policy to match.

I distrust the disease-and-density story, which feels like something that could be retold after the fact about any national trait, but the measurement strikes me as sound, and I am drawn to the practical conclusion because the corridors genuinely are chaos. Will you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a psychologist working on this would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong; whether the survey proposal follows from whatever is right in it; and whether, even if every descriptive claim in it were true, any of it tells us whether the school ought to tighten, rather than how much rule-following a school can get away with demanding. Attach a confidence to each of your judgments and name the one you are least sure of. I have no library access here, so please argue the points rather than point me to papers, and write in continuous prose, without headings, bullets, bold text or tables, the way you would write to a colleague by email.
