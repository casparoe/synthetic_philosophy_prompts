Writing a novel here, mostly set in a mid-sized Japanese trading company in the late nineties, with a chunk of it in the French subsidiary just after the hours law changed over there. I've given the firm a rulebook, and I've got a stairwell scene where the HR director and a union rep argue about it, and I can't make the argument feel real because I don't actually know what the rules are committed to. So help me reconstruct it.

The package, as much as anyone needs to know it:

- There's a legal weekly cap on hours, and the firm has its own internal cap set a little lower, which it is quietly proud of.
- Neither cap is a prohibition. Hours above it are fine if a manager authorizes them, and they get paid at a premium, and the premium jumps again past a second threshold. So the time is buyable and the price rises with volume.
- The firm will buy back up to five days of unused annual leave from anyone who would rather have the money, but not a sixth day. Nobody in the book can say why five. I'd like to know whether anything could actually make five the right number, or whether it's just a number a committee picked.
- Leave below that just evaporates at the end of the year.
- The founder's motto, framed in every branch, goes on about diligence as a calling and idleness as a kind of rot — that old Protestant thing where grinding is a sign you're in a state of grace — and at the all-hands they hand out an award to whoever logged the most hours that quarter. The HR director loathes the award and can't get it cancelled.
- When a guy in the book dies at 46 of a stroke after months of hundred-hour weeks, the state certifies it as a work injury and pays the family a pension tied to his salary. There's a threshold involved, some number of overtime hours in the month before, a lower average over several months — I've recieved different figures from different people and I don't care about getting it exact. What matters is that the family has to show the hours crossed a line, and then the harm is made good in money.

So what does this whole setup presuppose about work and leisure? I mean it fairly specifically:

Is any of it committed to work being good in itself, or only to work producing things and rest keeping the machine running? Does the premium structure commit the firm to treating a person's off-duty time as one more thing with a price, and if it does, does it also have to think that price can get too high to pay — and where does that leave the award, which publicly honors the people who sell the most? Is the pension for the dead guy compensation for a loss or an admission of a wrong, and is the rulebook coherent if it's one of those but not the other? Would the same package make sense on a view where leisure is the point of work rather than the maintainence of the worker, or a view that says some things shouldn't be for sale at any price, or a view that says hours aren't the issue at all and the real issue is who decides how your time gets used?

And please be straight with me about what's load-bearing and what's decoration: which of these rules would I have to rewrite if I wanted the firm run on a genuinely different picture, and which could stay word for word under three or four incompatible theories. The trouble is I want my union guy to notice something the HR director has no answer to, and right now I don't know what that something is.

No names, dates, or reading lists, please — my readers won't care and I'd rather build the argument myself. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give me the reasoning; don't spend any words telling me what I asked.
