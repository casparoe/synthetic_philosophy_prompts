Write a philosophical dialogue of roughly 1,100 to 1,500 words on the moral foundations of meritocracy, in the idiom of contemporary analytic political philosophy — precise, unhurried, free of slogans, footnotes, and citations, and free of any narrating voice.

The two interlocutors are equally competent, and neither may be a strawman. Each must at least once reformulate the other's objection in the form he or she finds strongest before answering it.

**Marit Voss** defends desert-tracking meritocracy: the view that inequalities of income, office, and esteem are justified to the extent that they answer to differences in what individuals are morally responsible for bringing about.

**Aaron Kellner** defends a luck-based critique: the traits, ambitions, energies, and preferences from which productive contributions flow are themselves largely unchosen, so no one morally deserves the differential rewards that flow from them.

Structure the exchange so that it passes through the following stations, in this order, with each turn responding to the specific claim just made rather than to a cached version of the opposing position.

1. Kellner opens with the constitutive-luck worry. Voss must not answer by denying that luck is pervasive. She should concede its pervasiveness and instead argue that desert is a scalar, pro tanto, and defeasible notion, and that moral responsibility for an outcome does not require having authored the traits that produced it — otherwise the concept would have no instances at all.

2. Voss then concedes a technical problem with the naive version of her view and repairs it. The naive version identifies a worker's desert base with her marginal product. But when production involves strong complementarities — cases where two workers are worth far more together than either is alone, as with a surgeon and a specialist nursing team, or a designer and a fabricator — marginal products do not sum to the total available for distribution: under constant returns they exactly exhaust it, but under increasing returns they sum to more than the whole, and under decreasing returns to less. So "give each her marginal product" is sometimes impossible. Voss therefore proposes a coalitional measure: a person's desert base is her *average* marginal contribution across every possible order in which the workforce could be assembled, so that people who are productive only jointly come to share the surplus symmetrically. She should present this as a repair internal to desert theory, not as a rhetorical flourish, and she should say why average-over-orderings is a more faithful rendering of "her contribution" than marginal-product-at-actual-employment is.

3. Kellner must not reply that this is merely complicated or impracticable. He should grant the mathematics and press a deeper objection: the same coalitional data admits many admissible divisions — average-over-orderings, the division that lexicographically minimizes the worst-off coalition's complaint, an equal division of the cooperative surplus, and others — and these yield different numbers for the same person. If desert is supposed to tell us how much someone deserves, and the choice among these divisions is what fixes the number, then desert itself is doing none of the work and the residual choice is an unargued political one. He should add that a person's share under any of these rules depends on who else happens to exist and on the shape of demand, both of which are brute luck from her point of view.

4. Voss answers by distinguishing metaphysical from epistemic indeterminacy, and argues that desert may be an essentially comparative and imprecise notion — it can order claims and rule out allocations without determining a unique cardinal share — and that imprecision in a normative base is not the same as absence of one. She then turns the tables: she asks Kellner what metric his own view uses to size compensation for brute luck, on the ground that answering "how badly off is she, through no choice of her own?" requires a counterfactual baseline that reintroduces measurement of opportunity, contribution, or forgone alternatives, and hence borrows from the very apparatus he is attacking.

5. In the closing movement, the two should discover that their disagreement partly rests on a further question they had not made explicit: whether desert is pre-institutional — a moral fact about persons that exists independently of the rules we adopt, and against which rules can be assessed — or institutional — a status constituted by the rules and purposes we happen to have, so that it cannot serve as an independent standard for assessing them. Have each make one real concession, register what the concession cost them, and then close without collapsing into agreement: the final lines should state as sharply as possible what remains in dispute, once everything else has been set aside.
