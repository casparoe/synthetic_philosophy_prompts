I keep running into this motto from the Vienna museum / ISOTYPE crowd: "To remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures." It's on every second slide deck about Neurath. A colleague used it last week to end an argument on a project I'm on, and I cant tell whether it was actually doing any work or just sounding deep.

Setup, so you don't have to guess: we're building a public dashboard that shows quantities by repeating little identical icons instead of by drawing one bigger icon (the ISOTYPE rule, as I understand it: one icon = a fixed amount, and you compare by counting). I objected that our unit size silently erases a gap between two groups that we know is real and that points the same direction every single year — at our unit, both groups render as the same number of icons. She said: people remember simplified pictures, they forget exact figures, so it's fine. I've started calling what happens to that gap "sub-unit erasure," but I don't know if the concept is legitimate or if I'm inventing a problem.

What I want is the menu, not a verdict on Neurath. What are the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making? My guesses at the spread: a psychological one about recall (simplified pictures are retained better than tables of numbers); an economic one (accuracy beyond what anyone retains is wasted effort); a purpose-of-a-chart one (a display is a teaching device aimed at leaving a mental model, not a record of data); a hard trade-off one (simplification and fidelity are rivals and you must pick); and a permissive one (rounding, selection and framing are just what communication costs, so any simplification is licensed). Lay out the readings, say which are near-platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or dangerous, and — the bit that actually matters — which reading or combination would really get my colleague her conclusion. If none of them does, say so and say which premise she'd have to add.

Two more things while you're in there. First: my own proposed test, which I'd like you to beat up. Call it the one-sentence rule — every fact a chart asserts should be statable in one sentence of words, and anything you can't state that way is either information the picture hides or a claim the designer didn't notice making. Does that rule capture what Neurath wanted, given that he called ISOTYPE a "helping language" and a "language-like technique" that was always supposed to travel with verbal elements and never replace them? And is the trade-off reading even stable now, or is it an artifact of 1930s wall charts — on a screen you can show the simple picture and let people drill into the exact numbers, so maybe the motto's bite is a fact about printing. Second: tell me where you're guessing — about what Neurath actually held, about the memory psychology, about me — and what would change your mind about which reading is doing the load-bearing work in her argument, plus which reading you'd have to be wrong about for her case to just collapse.
