I've been stuck on this for about a week and my reading group is no help, so I'm bringing it here. Context, so we're using the same numbers: in Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma an eccentric trillionaire gathers 20 people and offers a billion dollars to the sender if and only if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon, no communication allowed, and the superrational answer is supposed to be that each of them sends with probability 1/20. In the Luring Lottery from the same 1983 column (the one where the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, paid to whoever submitted a randomly drawn entry, and you were allowed to write any number you liked on your single postcard as your number of entries) the superrational prescription was roughly: roll a die with about as many sides as the number of people you expect to respond, and mail in a 1 only if you roll a 1.

Here's the argument I built, and I hate the conclusion:

(1) The actual content of superrationality is: in a symmetric situation I should choose the strategy whose adoption by every rational player maximizes each player's expected payoff, because my deliberation and theirs are not independent events — in choosing for myself I am in effect choosing for all of us. That non-independence is the whole thing; it's what separates superrationality from just guessing what the others will do and maximizing against the guess.

(2) What makes my choice and theirs non-independent is that we run the same reasoning on the same situation: same inputs, same argument, same conclusion. So the dependence superrationality asserts is as strong as dependence gets. Whatever strategy I settle on, they settle on too, and we all end up doing the same thing.

(3) Now take a randomized prescription — send with probability 1/20, or roll a twenty-sided die and send on a 1. If (2) is right, the rolls aren't independent either. Either we all roll a 1 or none of us does. Under (2) every randomized prescription collapses into the corresponding all-or-nothing one.

(4) In Platonia, all-or-nothing pays nothing. Twenty telegrams is nothing, zero telegrams is nothing. So under (2) the expected value of any superrational prescription in Platonia is zero no matter what probability you pick, and superrationality cannot recommend anything — certainly not 1/20.

(5) Nor can it sneak out through a deterministic rule like "I send, because someone has to": if everyone follows that, it's twenty telegrams again. Every symmetric deterministic strategy in Platonia pays zero. So there is no superrational recommendation to be had.

(6) The only escape is to say the dice come out differently for different people: we all adopt the same policy, but the tosses are independent. Yet that is just the causal decision theorist's picture returning through the back door. My action doesn't influence theirs, theirs are random draws from a shared policy, and I maximize my own expected payoff against a fixed distribution over what they do. That's ordinary expected-utility maximization plus the empirical guess that other people are like me. That's a belief about the world, not a decision theory.

(7) And once you are maximizing against the others' fixed behavior, the Luring Lottery shows where it terminates. Your best response to a total of T entries from everyone else is to submit T entries yourself; if everyone best-responds, the number of entries runs away without bound and the prize becomes worthless. Which is more or less what happened with the actual magazine contest.

(8) So superrationality is either silent exactly where it was supposed to shine (given (2)), or it is plain expected-utility maximization that reproduces the tragedy of the commons in the one case where it was supposed to beat it (without (2)). Either way it earns nothing over causal decision theory, and Hofstadter's apparatus is a slogan with one worked example.

I'm convinced something in the middle is smuggling, but I can't name it. One of my friends keeps muttering about correlated equilibrium: if we're all perfectly correlated, she says, we could also correlate *negatively* — agree that player 7 sends on heads and everyone else stays home on heads — and then perfect correlation wins a billion with certainty, which would make (4) just false. I think that's cheating, since the setup forbids communication and a shared coin isn't obviously available, but I can't say exactly why it's cheating. My other suspicion is (6), because "the dice are independent" and "my choice tells me nothing about theirs" feel like different claims, but I can't pry them apart.

If I have to choose I'd much rather keep (1) and (2) than keep (8) — I'd give up nearly anything before I give up the thought that superrationality is a real thing — but I want to know what I'm paying, and I want the step named precisely, not gestured at.
