ok so — context dump, sorry its long. I'm one of seven people on my city's Public Space Standards Panel (we're about 90,000 people, one of those mid-size places that used to be a manufacturing town and now mostly isn't). The council gave us a job that sounded simple and isn't: we've got roughly a hundred and forty rules about what people may do in public space — when bins go out and come back, leash zones, amplified sound in parks, busking, yard sales, fence heights, leaf burning, shovelling your bit of sidewalk, camping in the riverbottom — and the city actively enforces maybe six of them. Council wants a recommendation: keep the list and enforce it properly, keep it and keep doing what we're doing, or cut it down to something short and actually mean it.

Two of us have been going at it for three meetings and I think they're both right, which is why I'm outsourcing this.

Marcia (retired city clerk, thirty years in licensing) says enforcement is a budget, not a mood. Every rule you actively enforce spends some of a finite stock of residents' willingness to go along with things — she calls it compliance capital — so twenty rules enforced well buy more actual order than a hundred and forty enforced at random. And her sharper claim: a rule you keep but don't enforce is worse than no rule at all, because it teaches people that rules are theatre, and it piles up what she calls rule-debt, which comes due the first time you really need people to do something costly. On her view the bins rule should go.

Dev (runs a tenants' union, grew up here, thinks Marcia is a scold) says rules do work even when nobody enforces them: they say what the neighbourhood is for, they give someone a non-personal way to ask the guy with the speaker to turn it down — you're not being rude, you're citing — and they're what lets the city act on the rare occasion it has to. Repeal the bins rule and you don't remove the norm, you remove the city's vocabulary for it, and the only people left asking anyone to move their bins are the ones who can't afford to move themselves. He also thinks looseness isn't free: visible disorder reads as nobody's in charge, and the people who pay for that are the ones who can't leave.

Three cases we're actually stuck on:

1. Bins out after 6pm the evening before collection, in again by 9pm the day of. Compliance is maybe 40%, enforcement is complaint-driven, and the complaints come almost entirely from two neighbourhoods — Riverton, mostly older homeowners who've been here forever, and Southbank, which has absorbed most of our recent arrivals, a lot of them from places where a rule like this is a suggestion and where you sort noise out with your neighbour rather than calling City Hall. That enforcement pattern is exactly what makes me nervous, but I also think Marcia's "just repeal it" is too quick.

2. Off-leash dogs in the two designated quiet zones. This one has real safety weight, but the signage only exists in Riverton and officers have been writing tickets almost entirely against younger people and recent arrivals in Southbank.

3. A brand-new proposal, very popular with the people who show up to meetings: ban audio played out loud — phone speakers, portable speakers, all of it — anywhere outdoors, not just in parks. So we'd be adding a tight rule at exactly the moment we're being told to loosen up.

What I want from you: a memo I can circulate before the 14th, addressed to the panel, that gives a recommendation on each of the three and — the more useful bit — a rule of thumb we can run over the other ~137 without calling you again. Concrete, with a timeline; assume we could pilot something in one or two districts for a year if its worth it. Someone floated running different rules in different districts — tighter in Riverton, looser in Southbank — and my worry is that a patchwork is its own kind of unfairness to anyone who crosses the river for work, but I'll hear it if you want it. (We are not running an experiment: no money, no ethics board, and I don't want a memo that pretends otherwise.) I have to read this aloud at a neighbourhood meeting where half the room thinks the city has gone soft and half thinks it's turned into a snitch state, so the strongest version of whichever side you reject has to be in there properly, not flattened.

Two constraints: don't tell me to enforce everything consistently — we can't pay for that and you know it. And don't tell me to repeal everything we can't enforce, because I'm fairly sure the bins thing is doing something in Riverton even on the days nobody's watching.

On the mechanics, please: you're a professional philosopher who's spent years thinking about norms, rules and how institutions get people to go along with things — or pretend hard. And actually think before you write. I'd rather you spend the effort up front working out which of Marcia's and Dev's frameworks survives pressure than hand me something even-handed and useless; if you think one of them is basically right, say so plainly. You presumably know the psychology on "tight" and "loose" societies — strong norms and low tolerance for deviance at one end, weaker norms and more tolerance at the other — so feel free to use it, but argue from it rather than gesturing at it, and don't go looking anything up.

Last thing: this gets read on phones. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose, like an email. Lead with the bottom line in your first couple of sentences, and give me one closing line I can quote at the meeting.
