I'm in a political philosophy seminar this term and we did Burke and Chesterton's fence last week — the thing about not tearing down a fence until you've found out why whoever built it put it there. I keep noticing that my own university actually *runs* on something like this and I can't tell whether its committed to anything, or whether its just laziness with a nice slogan.

Concretely. My department has a rule on the books that majors have to take a Research Methods module. Nobody currently on staff remembers why. The person who proposed it retired years ago and the minutes only say proposed and carried. The curriculum committee has an explicit rule though: no requirement gets removed until someone produces a documented statement of what problem it was introduced to solve. If nobody can find one, the requirement stays.

Same university, different policy: every committee, centre and degree programme gets reviewed on a fixed cycle and gets shut down unless somebody makes an affirmative case that it should continue. Budget lines work the same way, and every few years there's a zero-based exercise where a unit has to rebuild its whole budget from scratch on paper and justify every item as if nothing were inherited.

And a third thing: when they *do* delete a requirement, students already enrolled are usually allowed to finish under the old rules, even in the cases where it got dropped for being pointlessly annoying rather than for being harmful.

So: what does the first practice actually presuppose? I want a few seperate things pulled apart that I can't pull apart myself.

One, it asks for the *historical* reason something was made, not for evidence that it's doing anything useful now. What's the implicit bet — that the original rationale is good evidence for the current function? Because those two can come apart completely. A rule introduced to solve problem A can, ten years later, be load-bearing for reason B that nobody ever wrote down and nobody knows about: it props up some other requirement, or it's the thing that stops a bottleneck somewhere else, or people have quietly built their planning around it. I've started calling this the orphaned-function problem but I don't know what the literature would call it. If the practice is really worried about B, it seems to be asking the wrong question, and if it's really only worried about A, it's assuming something about how rules survive that might just be false.

Two, is the force of it epistemic or not? Either it's all about ignorance — there are reasons you don't know, so don't — in which case it should dissolve the moment the document turns up; or it's the Burkean version where the relevant reasons aren't fully articulable even in principle, in which case finding the old memo doesn't discharge anything. Our committee seems not to have decided, since the rule says go find the document and then... I guess you delete it?

Three, does it need irreversibility? Nobody ever invokes the fence about things that are trivially easy to put back. Is the cost asymmetry doing more work than the ignorance point, and is the slogan misleading about which is which?

Four, and this is the bit that actually bothers me, the sunset policy flips the burden of proof entirely — justify yourself or die — and it's applied to the same kind of object by the same institution. One defaults to keep, one defaults to delete. Is that a flat inconsistency, or can a single principle cover both? Maybe it's about the cost of being wrong in each direction, or about whether the thing is a constraint on people versus a spending commitment, or about how cheaply you can reverse a mistake. And notice the fence rule never gets applied to absences: nobody says before you leave this unfenced, find out why no one ever fenced it, even when the absence is itself the residue of an old decision that other things now lean on. What does that asymmetry presuppose about the status quo being the natural baseline?

And grandfathering feels like a fourth principle bolted on the side, since it isn't about anyone's ignorance at all, it's about reliance. Is that independent, or is it what the fence principle becomes when you aim it at a person rather than a rule?

Things I'd want in an answer: an actual reconstruction of the implicit theory behind the fence rule; which views it strictly presupposes versus which it merely fits nicely with (does it *have* to reject the audit picture where every rule earns its place on present merits, or is it just a cheap heuristic for approximating an audit when information is expensive?); where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. where epistemic humility, precaution under uncertainty, deference to tradition as such, reliance-based fairness and interaction effects between rules would all tell you to do the same thing, so the practice itself can't discriminate between them; and what a consistent version would look like — fence everywhere, sunset everywhere, or the two sorted by some feature I should be attending to. Then the practical bit: if we replaced the fence rule with its opposite, every requirement presumed gone unless argued for on current merits with no credit for history, what would we concretely lose and gain?

Sorry, this got long.
