I sit on a ridiculous number of committees — parish council, a housing co-op, a small charity board — and there's a thing that keeps happening in meetings and in emails and I've never had a word for it. Two instances, then the general shape:

At the co-op, a chair said "right, so we're all agreed the maintenance sub-group takes this forward" at the end of a discussion in which nobody had agreed anything. Two people looked unhappy. Nobody said anything. And from that moment the sub-group *was* taking it forward, and at the next meeting the minutes recorded the agreement as having been reached in the previous meeting, and the chair referred back to it — "as we agreed last time" — completely sincerely, as though the agreement had been there first and he'd just reported it.

Second: an email went round at the charity saying "following the governance review, the trustee rota is changing to X". The "review" had been one phone call between two people who happened to be free on a Tuesday. But once the email existed, there had been a governance review, in the only sense anyone ever checked. Nobody was lied to exactly — everyone who read it could have worked out what had happened, and most of them did. It just didn't seem to matter that they had.

And here's why I think this is a real phenomenon and not just people being slippery: it sometimes doesn't work. Same chair, same room, but someone says "hold on, we didn't agree that," and the whole thing collapses — it becomes instantly obvious that there was no agreement and never had been, and the chair looks like an idiot rather than like someone who got away with something. So there's a difference between the cases where the loop closes and the cases where it doesn't, and I can't put my finger on it. My cynical guess is that it's about whether anyone in the room has an interest in letting it pass, but that feels too crude, and it doesn't explain why the *same* trick works on me when I'm the one doing it and I know exactly what I'm doing.

I did a bit of Austin at uni years ago, the "doing things with words" stuff, though I remember almost nothing. From what I recall he has categories for utterances going wrong — a misfire? — when the circumstances aren't right, like a marriage not happening because the person officiating had no licence. But that feels like the opposite of my cases. In mine the thing *does* happen, and it happens precisely because the sentence gets away with it. It's not a failed performance, it's a performance that kind of issues its own licence, retroactively, and everyone including the speaker then treats the licence as having been there all along. I also thought about self-fulfilling prophecies — a run on the bank and so on — but that's a prediction making its own content true, and these aren't predictions. They're phrased as reports of what's already the case, and it's the report being accepted that makes it the case. Also the run on the bank works whether or not anyone intends it to; mine only work when someone is aiming at exactly that.
