i co-write a dramatized history podcast — real people, real events, invented dialogue — and we ship every two weeks, so these calls come up constantly and there's never more than a day or two for one, sometimes it's a rewrite at midnight in the edit. legal reads our scripts but they only care about living people and music clearance; once somebody's dead nobody's watching at all. it's got personal recently: my great-aunt ran a home for unmarried mothers in Cork in the 1950s, she left basically nothing behind — no diary, no letters, two signatures on documents — and I'm the one pushing to build a season around her while two cousins think it's a betrayal. so I need something I can read out loud to them, not just my gut.

what I want is a decision procedure, not an essay. the question in the room is always some version of "can we put this in her mouth" or "can we show this happening", and I want ordered questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands off to a person because the question is values rather than facts. ground it in whatever philosophy actually knows about what we owe people who are dead and can't be harmed in the ordinary sense — but no bibliographies, and don't build it on the law, the law is silent here.

it has to handle scenes where the record is documented, silent, contested, or known to be otherwise, and it has to handle the four devices we definately lean on: compressed chronology, composite minor characters, "borrowed lines" (giving her a real line somebody else actually said, because it's better), and "silent scenes" (she's in the room, the record says nothing, we write what we think happened). we already tag every scene in the script with a certainty level 1–4; tell me whether that tag is doing any real work or just making us feel better.

distinctions I don't want it to collapse: wronging her versus misrepresenting her; what's owed to her versus to my cousins versus to listeners who'll take the season as history; getting her actions wrong versus getting her character wrong; "could have happened" versus "probably happened" versus "what she'd want said". and I want it to be able to say: don't dramatize this one at all.

it has to be runnable by a writers' assistant in under an hour, with no theory jargon in the checklist itself (a glossary is fine), and it should say which answers need my sign-off and which need the family's.

then run it on three: the finale where we give her a speech assembled out of someone else's testimony; the scene of her signing a document we know she signed but not why, with invented voiceover of her thinking; and the cold open of our first season, allready aired and publicly defended by me, that I'm now worried was indefensible. I'd rather be told that last one's wrong than right. and tell me where the procedure will misfire.
