So here's the situation. I'm a science journalist — I cover psych and medicine methods stuff, meta-science, the replication beat — and I'm finally writing the column I've been circling for a year: why "don't worry, we meta-analysed it and the effect holds up" is much weaker reassurance than readers (and editors, and grant panels) take it to be. I got into an argument about this with a friend who does meta-research, and she said something I can't shake: in almost every piece I write, the p-value is the villain and the Bayesian is the grown-up in the room. So for this one I want the reverse of my usual direction. I want a case where it's the *Bayesian* machinery that gets fooled and the *frequentist* error-rate habit that saves you.

What I need is a case built to order: a published literature on some intervention (a hospital, a school district, a workplace — nothing with coins or urns, please), small studies of the size a real lab can actually recruit, where a Bayesian analysis and a frequentist analysis of the same set of papers disagree, and the Bayesian comes out confidently positive while the frequentist says "this is exactly what a null effect plus a file drawer looks like" — and the frequentist is the one who's right. Contraints, because I can already see how this could be rigged. No fraud, no within-study p-hacking: every study in the literature was run and analysed honestly, and the only thing wrong is that the nulls and the wrong-signed results never made it into print. Both analyses have to be ones a careful, non-strawman analyst would really run — the Bayesian gets a standard random-effects/hierarchical model with weakly informative priors (no point mass at zero, no sceptical-prior trick), and the frequentist gets one of the publication-bias corrections people actually use, p-curve or p-uniform or a selection model, your call, but don't just name it, show me the reasoning. And no links or reading lists: you can't browse, and I'd have to chase them all down anyway.

Beyond the scenario itself (a paragraph, two at most), here's the rest of what I want. Give me the numbers you'd actually expect to see in that literature — roughly how big the published effect sizes are, what the hierarchical model's pooled estimate and interval come out as, and what the selection-corrected analysis does with them — and be honest about whether numbers like that could really arise the way you say, with the arithmetic shown loosely enough that I can follow it. Then: which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to each analysis if you take that feature away (e.g. if the unpublished studies were all recoverable from a registry). Then deal with the objection I know will show up in the comments: that this isn't a Bayesian failure at all, just a Bayesian who forgot to model the selection. Is there anything in the Bayesian set-up that *forces* you to think about the file drawer, or is modelling it purely optional? Would a Bayesian who models it land where the frequentist does? And the bit I care about most, at the end: tell me what would change your mind about the verdict, and where the argument is most likely to break — I'd rather print a case with it's soft spots showing than a tidy one. If you think the reverse direction genuinely can't be built without cheating somewhere, say that instead and tell me where exactly it breaks; I'd much rather kill the column than run a rigged demo.
