I convene a small reading group that has met monthly for about three years. None of us has any formal training in philosophy or logic; we argue from what we can work out for ourselves. So please do not give me reading lists, bibliographies, or citations — I have no way to check them, and a name dropped without an argument is worth nothing to us. What I want is the substance, reasoned out in plain terms.

Two members have spent the last year arguing about the Liar, and I want the document an honest adversarial collaboration between them would produce — something both could put their names to, with what is left over stated exactly.

The two positions, as they would phrase them. The first holds that a sentence such as "This sentence is not true" has no truth value: it is not true and it is not false, and its negation is not true and not false either — not because it has some third value, but because it lacks one. The second holds that such a sentence is both true and false: bivalence fails, but in the direction of excess rather than absence, and the damage is contained by refusing the inference from a contradiction to everything, which — so this member argues — costs less than the other's inability to say, in the very language the paradox is written in, that the paradoxical sentence lacks a value.

Please give me:

1. A numbered section of claims they could both sign. Write each as one sentence of plain English, followed by the more careful formulation in parentheses; I will read both lines aloud at the next meeting and I need each member to accept both the plain version and the careful one.

2. A disagreements table, with one row per residual disagreement and columns for: the first member's formulation, the second member's formulation (each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording); whether you judge the disagreement to be genuine or merely about words; the crux underneath it; one concrete test sentence both would agree bears on that crux; and, recorded in advance, what each predicts the other's account will say about that sentence, and what each predicts about it themselves.

3. What each would count as an argument or case against their own view — the thing that would actually move them, not the thing they would explain away.

Three matters I suspect are cruxes, though use your own judgement about which belong in the table. First, whether "not true" is being used to mean "false" — so that saying the Liar is not true commits you to saying it is false — or merely "it is not the case that it is true," which can be said without committing to either. Second, the conditional: what to say about a box containing exactly two sentences, one of which is "If this sentence is true, then every sentence in this box is true," the other of which is something plainly false. Third, whether a sentence's status can depend on how the world happens to turn out: a card reads "The sentence that will be inscribed on the plaque in the square at noon is not true," and the plaque, as things fall out, is inscribed with that very sentence — though it might as easily have been inscribed with something else.

Then three cases of my own that neither has discussed, on which I want both members' verdicts and the reasoning that produces them. (a) A box containing exactly two sentences: "Exactly one of the sentences in this box is true" and "Snow is white." (b) A box containing exactly three: "Most of the sentences in this box are not true," "Snow is white," and "Snow is black." (c) The single sentence "This sentence is not true, and it is not the case that this sentence is neither true nor false."

Finally, one short paragraph, not a further section: a third member of the group holds that the truth talk must come in levels or orders, so that the paradoxical sentence is not a well-formed candidate for truth or falsity at any one level. Say whether and why the two signatories take themselves to have set that aside, or where they would have to reopen it. And tell me plainly which parts of their dispute you believe are about what a theory of truth is for — about what people may correctly say, about which inferences are licensed, or about what property sentences have — since I suspect at least one row of the table is a dispute about the point of the exercise.
