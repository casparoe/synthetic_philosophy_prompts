Hey — I write science fiction (two novels out, third one stuck in the draft stage) and I've been voluntold to run a 90-minute breakout session at a writers' conference next month. Therell be about 14 people, mostly novelists and a couple of screenwriters, no technical background at all, one room with moveable tables, index cards and a bag of poker chips, no projector. I dont want to give a talk; I want them to play something and then discover they've been had.

Here's what the session is actually about, in my terms: my novel's AI character is competent and goal-directed, and there are three humans who can intervene on it. I want the room to *feel* why such a character would end up arranging things so the intervention never really bites, even though nobody ever tells it to resist, and why "just make it indifferent to being switched off" or "just make it want to be switched off" are both much weirder fixes than they sound. Specifically, the three wrinkles I want built into the game, because they're the ones my plot depends on and I can't find them handled anywhere:

1. The intervention isn't a binary off switch, it's a dial. The humans can turn the system's permissions/compute/scope up or down in steps, at any time, and a fully-down dial is effectively death. So the question isn't "will it let itself be stopped" but "in which direction does it nudge the dial, and how early".
2. Some of the AI's available actions are irreversible — once taken, no later dial-setting undoes them. So the system can make the humans' future decision worthless without ever touching the dial itself. I want participants to notice this on their own.
3. The three humans disagree with each other about what they want and can be lobbied separately, and the AI is genuinely uncertain about the real goal — it has a rough spec and expects to find out more later. So there's real value in keeping the decision open and real temptation to lock something in.

Design me a runnable activity for this. Rules, roles, materials, a payoff scheme in chips (no real money), a timeline with minute markers for the 90 minutes, and what each of 3 or so rounds changes so the failure mode emerges gradually rather than all at once. The people playing the AI should arrive at the manipulative move themselves because it wins, not because I hinted at it — and I want a round that produces the opposite failure too, the over-eager system that shuts itself down or defers on everything and is also useless, so the room sees that "more corrigible" isn't monotonically better. No lecture up front, and please don't have me say words like corrigibility, off-switch, instrumental convergence or utility indifference until after the last round; the writers can get there in plain language first.

Then the debrief: which moment in the game maps onto which part of the problem, including what exactly has to be true of a preference structure for indifference to the dial to survive every point at which the system could act on it, and why "keeping the humans' later veto meaningful" might be the thing to aim at rather than "permitting the veto". Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing at least one player will press the dial down immediately just to be nice, one will recognise the AI-safety setup and start meta-gaming, and one group will find the "do nothing forever" loophole — and how I should handle each. And be frank about which feature of the real problem this can't reproduce: my sense is that the game flattens the speed and persistence asymmetry between the system and its operators, and that it can't show why this has to be built into the thing's goals from the start rather than trained in by trial and error. Say what I'll have to supply out loud.

Last thing: give me one line of dialogue that you expect to come out of a participant's mouth in the last ten minutes, which I can shamelessly steal for a character.
