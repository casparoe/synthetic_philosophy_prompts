I am presenting a paper three weeks from now to a development economics workshop, and I would like a question-period preparation sheet rather than a defense of the thesis. Please do not pad the answer with references or reading suggestions; I want the arguments themselves.

The setting. The paper is about the standing dispute over why some countries are many times richer than others — the three-way contest between institutions, geography, and culture — and more specifically about the fact that nearly every influential empirical strategy in that literature leans on one historical episode or one instrument (a colonial-era mortality gradient, a disease-ecology or crop-suitability gradient, an arbitrarily drawn border, a mining or plantation concession, a pre-modern state's boundary) and then reports a single number as if it were a parameter of a general law.

My thesis has a destructive and a constructive half.

Destructive: the three camps are not, in the first instance, disagreeing about evidence. They are disagreeing about which counterfactual contrast is the interesting one, and each camp's design is purpose-built to isolate a contrast the other two regard as uninteresting or as a confound. Their numbers are therefore not rival estimates of one quantity; they are estimates of different quantities, and more data will never make them compete.

Constructive: the field should stop trying to estimate the effect of *institutions* (or of geography, or of culture) and instead estimate distributions of effects across *matched episodes*. A matched episode is a pair of places that looked similar, on outcomes and observables, before a documented change in the rules governing them; that then diverged in rules while remaining adjacent and comparable in other respects; and that can be followed for several decades afterwards. My proposal is to assemble a few hundred such pairs and report not one treatment effect but the shape of the estimated effects across pairs — the median, the interquartile range, the share of pairs whose effect has the wrong sign. My empirical claim is not that the median effect is small. It is that the spread is wide enough that the median carries almost no information for anyone who has to choose a reform in one particular place, and that the spread is not noise to be averaged away, because it is structured: it tracks whether the rule change altered anyone's actual payoffs, whether it was imposed or bargained, and whether there was an organized group that lost by it.

The policy conclusion is the part people will fight about: that the average effect should carry close to zero weight in a reform decision, and that the reformer's real question — will this work *here*? — is better served by a structured judgment about which part of the historical distribution this place resembles than by any published average.

The audience. About thirty people. Present will be: two senior economic historians whose careers were built on the instrumental-variables designs my destructive half criticizes, fastidious about identification and allergic to *heterogeneity* used as an excuse for weak designs; a quantitative geographer who works on disease ecology and agricultural suitability, thinks the institutionalist literature is one enormous omitted-variable problem, will be delighted by my destructive half and hostile to my constructive half; a cultural economist who studies the intergenerational transmission of trust and norms and thinks the institutionalists have the arrow of causation backwards, because what they call institutions is largely culture that has already crystallized; a philosopher of social science who holds that causal claims resting on single historical episodes are close to unfalsifiable and will press me on what, exactly, I take myself to have established; a room of younger development economists who run field experiments on local governance, are impatient with history altogether, and want to know why any of this should change what they do; and the head of a bilateral aid agency's research unit, who is in the room because the agency funds the workshop and who wants one usable sentence about whether to keep financing rule-of-law and public-finance reform.

What I would like from you. A prep sheet of the eight or so objections I will actually get, in roughly the order they will arrive, each attributed to the kind of person who will raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it — not in my vocabulary, and not the objections I would get from my own adviser. For each: how much damage it does if I handle it badly, the reply I should have ready and can deliver in about two minutes, and one honest sentence about where that reply is soft. Then: which two objections have enough real force that I should concede them from the podium rather than wait to be asked; the single claim I should refuse to give up even under sustained pressure; the weakest joint in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope the room misses; and, finally, the one question I am privately hoping nobody asks.
