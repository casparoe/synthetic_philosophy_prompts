Context: I teach a seminar on epistemic injustice, and last week we spent the whole session on whether a philosopher who isn't a member of a marginalized group can write knowledgeably about the injustices that group faces. I'd tried to seperate three worries — that the outsider's account is more likely to get the facts wrong, that it may not even be about the same thing the insiders are talking about, and that even when it's right it won't be heard. One of my best students closed the session with 'nothing about us without us,' and the room kind of exhaled like it had been settled: no inquiry about a group without members of that group in it.

I don't think it's settled, and I think the slogan is quietly doing about four different jobs at once. So I need a disambiguation I can paste into the seminar Slack: lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, and for each say whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, or actually false.

Three axes I want covered. First, what 'about us' means: is it about topic, about who is affected by the conclusions, about who bears the risk of being described wrongly, or about whose concepts are being used? Second, what 'without us' requires — that we're asked, that our answers are actually weighted, that we help set the questions in the first place, or that we can stop the project. Third, what the point of the requirement is supposed to be: better epistemic output, legitimacy or something like consent, standing/respect, or fairness in who gets to do this work at all.

Two hobbyhorses of mine to fold in. (1) I've started distinguishing *answering-inclusion* — the group gets to give input — from what I've been calling *framing-inclusion*, where the group gets to help decide what is being asked before anyone collects anything, and I suspect the slogan slides between them constantly; say what each reading would actually require of a researcher. (2) I also want the 'credibility overshoot' worry addressed: if you correct for a systematic credibility deficit toward a group, you can end up manufacturing a credibility excess for members of that group about claims in nearby but different domains, and the slogan seems to say nothing about how to avoid that.

Then the payoff: tell me which reading, if any, would actually support the thing my student wanted — that the outsider's account is in some way epistemically defective — and whether the most the slogan really supports is a procedural point about how the inquiry is run. And flag what happens to the slogan when the group disagrees with itself: some members want the research done and others don't. Who is 'us' then?
