I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and for the past year I've been volunteering a few nights a week on a small piece of software for a grantmaking team that funds long-term public-health and infrastructure work. The tool takes a policy we might fund, simulates its downstream effects over roughly the next century, and writes an entry into a "harm register" whenever some person ends up worse off than they would have been had we funded nothing. The register is meant as a guardrail: entries get reviewed by a human before a grant is approved. Recently I noticed a pattern that I initially logged as a bug. For near-term interventions the register fills up as expected, but for anything large enough to shift the timing of conceptions it comes back empty: a vaccination rollout that changes when people have children, a cash-transfer program that changes migration and marriage patterns, anything that perturbs who meets whom and when. Under the do-nothing branch, numerically different people come to exist, so my matching code finds no one to compare, and it reports zero harms. I've been treating this as an engineering problem with two candidate fixes, and I'd like to know which one is right before I spend a sprint on it. The first is resolution: with a fine-grained enough demographic and genetic model, we could in principle say which specific individuals would exist on each branch and compare them one-to-one, so the emptiness of the register is really just a data-horizon limitation, and presumably shrinks as the forecast improves. The second is the identity criterion: we currently match people across branches by genotype, and I've been wondering whether matching by social role, or by some best-match or closest-continuer rule, would recover the comparisons we're losing. A friend who took a couple of philosophy courses said this is a known thing, that Parfit is the person everybody cites for it, and that the standard move is to read it as a reductio of the idea that wrongness must consist in making someone worse off rather than as a discovery about harm. I don't think I fully follow that, and I also don't see what it leaves me with: if the people who come to exist have lives that are worth living and there is nobody who is worse off, what is the objection to the policy supposed to be, short of signing up for summing total welfare, which as I understand it carries that implication about enormous populations of lives barely worth living? Is there a defensible position in between, one that lets a grant reviewer say that a policy which reshuffles who exists makes things morally worse without committing the team to full aggregation? And concretely, for the register: should I keep the harm condition as is and accept that long-horizon grants just generate no entries, or is there a principled way to encode a comparison against the best feasible alternative policy?

Please correct the framing wherever it is confused before answering the question I actually asked, since I suspect I may be asking the wrong one, and please translate out of the jargon: I think in terms of types, invariants, and what a function returns, so I'd find it useful if you flagged the places where I'm using a word in a way a specialist would reject. I'd also like you to be explicit about provenance in the epistemic sense: separate what is settled among people who genuinely work on this from what is live controversy, tell me roughly how the split goes where it is contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus or smooth over a disagreement by picking a side silently. Where the honest answer is that there is no agreed resolution, say that rather than choosing for me, and mark which of your own claims you are confident in and which you are offering as the best available guess. In particular, tell me whether the emptiness of my register is a substantive moral finding, an artifact of a stipulation I happened to hardcode, or something in between, and whether any amount of additional computing power or forecast resolution would change that.
