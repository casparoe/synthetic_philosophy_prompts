I am writing an essay of about four thousand words on H. G. Wells's *The Island of Doctor Moreau*, and I want to organise it around Descartes on animal minds — in particular the argument in Part Five of the *Discourse on Method* that no machine, however exactly it reproduced the shape and movements of a human body, could pass either of two tests: putting signs together as we do in order to declare its thoughts to others, and acting through knowledge rather than merely through the disposition of its organs, since reason is a universal instrument that serves in unforeseen circumstances whereas the arrangement of organs has to be fitted to each particular action.

My half-formed claim is this. Moreau's Beast Folk are, almost accidentally, the very case Descartes constructs: creatures given a human silhouette and human organs, trained to walk upright, to recite prohibitions against going on all-fours, and to chant that they are men. On the first Cartesian test they look like partial passers — the Law is vocalised, and the Sayer of the Law says words — but the recitation is not responsive to circumstance in the way Descartes demands, which is why the Law collapses the moment the creatures are outside Moreau's compound. And on the second test they fail by construction: everything about them is held in place by surgery, by the knife and by trained dread of the House of Pain, that is, by the disposition of organs rather than by knowledge. If Descartes is right, Moreau's project cannot succeed even in principle, no matter how far the surgery improves, and the reader's horror is at an experiment whose failure was settled a priori. I also want to use the detail that Wells's only formal scientific training was in biology, under T. H. Huxley, who published a well-known 1874 essay attacking the animal-automaton hypothesis, and to ask whether that makes the novel a participant in an anti-Cartesian consensus rather than an attack on Descartes himself.

What I need is that reading tested rather than confirmed. Three things in particular. First, whether it needs the popular version of Descartes, on which he denies that animals feel anything at all, or whether it works with the weaker reading on which he denies them thought and reason while allowing a kind of bodily sensation — the House of Pain seems to presuppose that pain is Moreau's most reliable instrument, and I want to know whether that makes Moreau himself inconsistent, or whether it only shows that the strong version is a later caricature I should not build on. Second, where the Cartesian frame distorts the book: the novel is at least as much about degeneration and evolution, and about Victorian vivisection, as about the metaphysics of animal minds, and I would rather be told where the frame is anachronistic than have it applied everywhere. Third, what to do with Prendick's ending, in which he cannot look at ordinary people in London without seeing the beast latent in their faces and fearing its return; that looks like the Cartesian hierarchy inverted — the machine is discovered inside the human rather than the animal — and I am unsure whether that inversion is a real philosophical claim the novel makes or only Prendick's trauma.

Please give me a reading that is fair to both the novel and the philosophy: the scenes you would build the argument around, the points at which the text resists the reading and an honest critic has to say so, and an account of what the novel adds to the debate about animal minds that the arguments alone do not supply. Don't give me quotations or citations; I will add the passages and references myself.
