In the reading group I'm in — mostly people from an effective-altruism and rationalist-adjacent background — the term Moloch gets used for what I'd independently describe as a race to the bottom: credential inflation, escalating ad load, faster and slacker model releases, jurisdictions undercutting each other's labour and tax standards. The move people then make is to say that such a race is just a many-player Prisoner's Dilemma, and to draw three conclusions from that: that the correct diagnosis is collective-action failure, that the correct remedy is coordination plus a binding enforcer, and that proposals aimed at changing preferences, at changing the selection environment, or at exiting the competition altogether are treating symptoms rather than the disease.

I want that inference audited rather than accepted or rejected wholesale. My suspicion is that the mapping is loose in ways that matter, and that the conclusions are being carried partly by the picture rather than by the structure.

Two distinctions I have started using may or may not be doing real work, and I would rather be told they are relabellings than have them indulged:

First, matrix-taking versus matrix-shifting defection. In a Prisoner's Dilemma, defecting moves you around inside a payoff matrix that the moves themselves do not alter; the cooperate-cooperate cell stays where it was, available as a target for coordination. In the cases I care about, escalation seems to change what the payoffs are. Once one set of firms has trained its product on maximally engrossing content, the attention that used to be obtainable for a given amount of effort is no longer obtainable at that price, and once one cohort has added a credential, the signalling value of the previous credential is gone. So mutual restraint after some defection has occurred does not denote the same outcome as mutual restraint beforehand, and it is not obvious that there is a stable cell to coordinate on at all.

Second, choice-maintained versus replacement-maintained traps. In the first kind, the bad outcome persists because each incumbent has a dominant strategy to escalate, so removing the temptation removes the problem. In the second, it persists because any incumbent who restrains itself loses market share, loses the arms race, or is acquired and replaced by one that does not restrain itself — in which case unanimous willingness to stop among everyone currently at the table changes nothing, because the relevant competitive pressure arrives through entry and succession rather than through anyone's choice.

Please do the following.

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly, as a table if that is clearest: players, available strategies, the payoff ordering, the role of iteration and the shadow of the future, communication, enforcement. Then say what plays each of these roles in a race to the bottom. Use at least two concrete cases and pick them so that they differ from each other in a relevant way — I would suggest one drawn from firms competing for revenue or attention and one drawn from individuals competing for positions, since I suspect the mapping fits those differently.

2. Say where the mapping fails, and sort the failures into two piles: those that are matters of degree which the framework can absorb (many players, imperfect information, noisy or uncertain payoffs, repeated interaction), and those that are structural, where the race is not an n-player Prisoner's Dilemma under any parameterisation but a different object with a different solution set.

3. Assess my two distinctions specifically. Is matrix-shifting genuinely outside the fixed-payoff structure, or can it be absorbed by treating the game as one with a state variable that the players' moves update? Is the choice-maintained versus replacement-maintained split a difference in which game is being played, or merely a difference in how the same equilibrium is sustained? If it is the latter, say so plainly.

4. Grade each of the three conclusions. Does the diagnosis of collective-action failure survive? Does the therefore-we-need-a-Leviathan remedy survive, and does it survive differently for the two kinds of trap I described? Does the dismissal of preference-change, selection-environment, and exit strategies survive, or was that dismissal never supported by the analogy in the first place? I would like each conclusion placed somewhere on a scale from follows from the analogy, through consistent with the analogy but not supported by it, to supported only by a feature the analogy quietly imports into the target.

5. Finally, if the Prisoner's Dilemma is the wrong picture, what is the least misleading one? Candidates I have heard in the group are an evolutionary arms race or Red Queen treadmill, a cascade in positional goods, a Dutch auction against oneself, Gresham's law, and the invisible hand running in reverse. For each, say what it gets right about the phenomenon and what it drags in from its own source domain that the target does not have. If one of them is only marginally better than the Prisoner's Dilemma, or if the honest answer is that no single picture covers both of my cases, I would rather hear that than a tidy recommendation.
