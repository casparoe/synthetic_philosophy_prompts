I'm trying to write the background section of a paper on de re modality and I keep tripping over the fact that everyone in this argument seems to be working from a different shopping list of what a theory has to deliver. So before I read any more of it I want the spec, not another survey.

What the theory is for: an account of de re modal claims about ordinary concrete objects — this table could have been made of something other than wood, I could have missed that flight, Humphrey could have won, there could have been one more person than there are — good enough to sit underneath arguments that move from modal premises to conclusions about essence, about coincidence and composition, and about supervenience. So it has to actually deliver truth conditions, or at least a decent story about what makes those claims true, and not merely a translation scheme that rewrites modal English into first-order talk about a plurality of worlds.

Here's the pile of things I've seen people demand, and I genuinely can't tell which are real requirements and which are just loyalty to a camp. Sort it for me: that an object can literally exist at more than one world versus that the theory never commits you to one thing being wholly present at two worlds; that de re claims be about the very object rather than about some surrogate; that the whole thing work without concrete other worlds; that it respect the necessity of identity and of distinctness; that it have something to say about objects that exist at only one world and have counterparts nowhere; that it not automatically make every property of such a lonely object necessary; that it make origins essential while leaving accidental intrinsics contingent; that similarity do the work without dragging in symmetry or transitivity; that modal truth not swing around with whatever is salient in the conversation; that it take a principled line on the Barcan formulas and contingent existence; that it use one and the same machinery for a thing's persistence through time and for its goings-on at other worlds; that it either allow merely possible objects into the domain or at least not blur could have existed into does exist.

And here are some cases I invented because the standard ones all seem rigged in somebody's favour. What I want to know is what a theory must be able to say about each, not what your favourite theory does say.

1. The two heirs. Some object divides into A and B, perfectly symmetric, both of them equally similar to the original and to each other. A similarity-based relation either picks arbitrarily, says both are me (so one object has two successors), or goes indeterminate. Should a theory be required to give a determinate answer here, or is branching an acceptable output?

2. The swap. Two worlds, w and v. In w, X has the causal biography and modal role that Y has in v, and Y has X's; everything intrinsic about them is traded. Which is which? Is there a fact of the matter? And is a theory that has no answer any worse off than one that answers by stipulation?

3. The merge. A and B, distinct origins, fuse into C. Somewhere else a single thing splits into two that each trace back to the fused material. Whatever your theory says here has to stay consistent with what it said about case 1, and I suspect that constraint is doing more work than anyone admits.

4. The lonely object. Exists at exactly one world, zero counterparts anywhere else. Does it come out having all its properties necessarily, and is that a cost or a consequence the theory should just own? If it's a cost, what structure does the theory need in order to avoid it?

5. Near tie. A world with two candidates, one slightly better along respect R1 and the other slightly better along R2, no weighting given. How much of a story about the respects of comparison does a theory owe us before it can be assessed at all?
