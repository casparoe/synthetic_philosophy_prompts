Please answer as a philosopher of mind who works closely with clinicians on disorders of consciousness, and think the problem through carefully before you write: I'd find it genuinely useful if you began by loosely brainstorming — without self-censoring — the different jobs the word "consciousness" might be doing in the practice I'm about to describe, and only then narrowed down to the question I actually want answered.

I sit on a hospital ethics committee that is being asked to ratify a revised protocol for analgesia, prognostication, and end-of-life decisions in patients with severe brain injury. The current practice turns almost entirely on demonstrated capacity to respond: a patient who opens her eyes and cycles through sleep and wakefulness but shows no reproducible response to command at the bedside is recorded as lacking awareness, and that classification quietly drives a great deal — whether we pre-medicate for painful bedside procedures, how we frame prognosis to families, whether a withdrawal-of-treatment conversation is had at all. Yet when such a patient is later found to show command-following on task-based fMRI or EEG — the phenomenon now called cognitive motor dissociation, reported in a widely publicised 2024 multicentre study in roughly a quarter of patients who showed no behavioural response to commands — she is retroactively reclassified: we say she was conscious all along, we treat the earlier period as one in which she may have been suffering, and we say the behavioural assessment merely failed to detect her. So the pivot in practice is not behaviour as such but something behaviour is taken to be evidence of.

What puzzles me is that the same committee is untroubled by several adjacent cases that seem to pull the other way. It regards patients with locked-in syndrome as fully conscious even when the only remaining output is a vertical eye movement, and it would continue to do so if even that were lost and a brain-computer interface were doing the reporting. It authorises analgesia for preverbal infants and for animals, where nothing report-like is available at all, on the basis of neural homology and behaviour. It treats dreaming as conscious experience despite there being no reporter online during REM sleep. And it is fairly relaxed about intraoperative awareness, where the whole point is that experience can occur with no behavioural signature. Meanwhile the committee has split into two factions over the revision. One proposes what I'll call the broadcast rule: any patient showing sustained, stimulus-triggered, widely distributed frontoparietal recurrent activity of the kind global workspace theory identifies with consciousness is to be classified as conscious irrespective of behaviour, and any patient showing only early sensory evoked responses is to be recorded as not conscious rather than as unknown. The other proposes the opposite: record unresponsiveness as unknown, never as absence, and treat unknown as a reason to give analgesia, on the grounds that phenomenal experience unaccompanied by global availability is something we can never rule out from the bedside.

So here is what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory of consciousness that the existing practice presupposes, and say precisely which parts of it the practice strictly requires, which it merely fits comfortably with, and where it is underdetermined so that several theories would license it equally well. Tell me whether the different strands I listed are mutually consistent — in particular, whether whatever distinguishes the locked-in patient from the unresponsive one can be stated without collapsing the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, and whether the infant-and-animal reasoning, if it is sound, does not generalise to the adult patient with a preserved cortex. Then tell me what each of the two proposed revisions would commit us to: what has to be true for the broadcast rule to be anything more than a relabelling of the current test, and whether the unknown-default rule is really a claim about consciousness or only about risk tolerance and the asymmetry of errors. A colleague insists none of this is a disagreement about consciousness at all — that we are simply using responsiveness as the best available fallible test, and the practice is thus compatible with any theory. I'd like you to take that deflation seriously and tell me how much of it is right, and where the confidence of the practice (withholding analgesia, framing prognosis as we do) outruns what a purely evidentiary reading licenses. Finally, please be explicit about your own epistemic status: separate what is reasonably settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested — including how well the global-broadcast signature actually tracks report, how to read global workspace theory itself, and how strong the empirical basis for the covert-response findings is — flag any place where you are relying on a contested empirical or interpretive claim rather than a consensus, and note which of your conclusions you would expect a reasonable neuroethicist to reject.
