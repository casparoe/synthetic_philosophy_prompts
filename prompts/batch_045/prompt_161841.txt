My supervisor has agreed to let me open the second chapter of my thesis — a chapter on what, if anything, makes a probability claim objectively rather than merely subjectively true — with one worked example, but only if it is an example she is willing to call a case of objective probability. For the past few weeks, in our supervision meetings, she has been ruling on a string of cases, always on sentences of the form "the probability that ___ is p," and always sorting them into objective (true or false in virtue of how the world is, whatever anyone believes) or not objective (a report of someone's degree of confidence). Her verdicts so far:

1. This fair six-sided die comes up 3 on its next roll = 1/6 → objective. She added that the symmetry of the die is what makes it so, and that it would still be objective if the die were rolled once and then melted down.
2. This particular atom of an isotope with a sixteen-hundred-year half-life decays within the next sixteen hundred years = 1/2 → objective, even though it is a single atom that either will or will not decay.
3. The coin already lying under my hand came up heads = 1/2 → not objective. The coin has landed; nothing in the world is still 1/2 about it.
4. The defendant in this burglary trial is guilty = 0.9 → not objective.
5. A randomly drawn adult resident of Leeds has hypertension = 0.17 → objective. She said the sentence is true or false depending on the city's actual composition, and that 0.17 is an estimate of something real rather than a report of how confident she feels — though she also muttered something about proportions not really being probabilities, which I didn't press her on at the time.
6. The ten-billionth digit of π is 7 = 0.1 → not objective; she said 0.1 merely registers how little we know.
7. This ticket wins Saturday's lottery = 1 in 45 million → objective.

The case I actually want to lead with is a coin-flipping machine: a rigid, calibrated device that launches a real coin from a fixed initial orientation with a launch velocity drawn from a narrow band, with no quantum noise anywhere in the setup and no dependence on any earlier launch. A calibration run of ten thousand launches at one fixed setting came up heads forty-seven percent of the time, and the sequence of outcomes passes the standard statistical tests for randomness. The machine is then used for a single further launch at that setting, and the sentence is "the probability that the next launch at this setting comes up heads is 0.47." Whether she will call that objective is, as far as I can tell, genuinely unsettled by her seven verdicts: she has never ruled on anything whose probabilities are merely empirical long-run frequencies over a stipulated distribution of initial conditions rather than symmetries or quantum half-lives, and someone in my study group pointed out that if classical mechanics is true then the die in case 1 is just as deterministic as this machine, which makes me less sure than I was that her die verdict settles anything.

So here is what I'd find useful, in advance of Thursday: a prediction of her verdict on the machine case; the candidate rules that fit all seven of her earlier verdicts, stated sharply enough that they disagree somewhere; what each of those rules predicts about the machine; which one you'd bet on and why; an honest reading of the hypertension case — in particular whether it is really a verdict about objective probability at all, or whether it is ambiguous between "objective chance" and "objective quantity we happen to be uncertain about"; and, since I get to ask her exactly one follow-up question, which single further case would best separate the surviving rules. The candidates I have in mind are the same machine run once and then dismantled, and the same machine whose heads-frequency shifts from 0.47 to 0.83 when the distribution of launch velocities is changed — but if there is a sharper discriminating case than either, say so and say why. I'd rather have that than a general survey of the interpretations of probability; I need the prediction, the rule, and the case that decides between them.
