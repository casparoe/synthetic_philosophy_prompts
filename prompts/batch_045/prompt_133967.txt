I'm putting together a one-page flowchart for a philosophy of biology reading group. A few synthetic-biology grad students come too, so I'd like the cases to be ones you'd actually meet at the bench rather than philosophy-of-biology teleporters. The question it should sort people on: in what sense, if any, does a stretch of DNA carry information or count as a code?

What I want is five or six mostly yes/no branch questions, each one a concrete case, and eight to ten leaves. Every branch should be answerable by someone who doesn't already know which view it leads to. Each leaf gets a label (the usual name if there is one, "unnamed" if not) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Cases I'd like used, in whatever order works best, though swap any out if you have a better one:

- an organism whose whole genome has been recoded: every codon swapped for a different one, the tRNAs rewritten to match, the same proteins come out, the animal looks normal. Does this genome say the same thing as the original?
- same, except the permutation was done by a machine shuffling at random and the translation machinery was then evolved to fit. Now does it say anything?
- a sequence thats never transcribed but is kept by selection because of its physical properties, bendy stretches or spacing. Information, or just scaffolding?
- one stretch read in two different frames, or spliced two ways, giving two different products. Codes are supposed to be unambiguous, so is this just context-sensitivity like a shift key, or is the analogy coming apart?
- a lethal frameshift. Does the mutated sequence say something false, or just do something different?
- methylation marks, or prion conformations. If those are codes too, is that a reductio of the whole way of talking or just an extension of it?

I also want one branch on where the content is supposed to come from in the first place: what the cell will do with the sequence, the selection history that put it there, or what the sequence reliably indicates about the organism. I suspect people in the room split there before they get to any of the cases.

Two things don't be polite about. If two of my cases are secretly the same question, or the tree can't really seperate two of the leaves, say so instead of inventing a distinction. And if you think "is DNA a code?" is mostly a verbal dispute that no set of cases settles, lead with that rather than handing me a tidy tree. Where you're genuinely unsure which leaf my answers would put me in, mark it instead of guessing.
