Here is where I am. Our math club had a talk on the doomsday argument last semester, I got pulled into the self-sampling versus self-indication debate, and after arguing about it for months with a friend I've ended up holding a version of SIA that I patched myself and haven't seen stated anywhere. Briefly: SSA tells you to treat yourself as a random draw from the observers in your reference class, while SIA tells you that your own existence is evidence for hypotheses containing more observers — which is what generates the presumptuous philosopher, who declines to run the crucial experiment because the big-universe theory already swamps the small-universe one on sheer headcount. My patch is that SIA should not count observers, only observers who are in a position to raise the anthropic question at all — call them deliberators. Hypotheses get weighted by the number of beings who actually reason about their own indexical situation, not by population size. I like it because it blunts the presumptuous philosopher (a trillion creatures that never think about cosmology shouldn't let me skip the experiment) and takes some of the sting out of the doomsday argument, while keeping what I find attractive in SIA, namely its ability to explain why I find myself in a crowded world rather than a lonely one. My friend's objection is that I've just reinvented SSA with extra steps and bought immunity from the standard objections by giving up everything that made SIA distinctive.

Two cases I made up to test this, which I'd like you to take seriously rather than swap for cleaner ones. First, the Late Registrar: theory A yields ten deliberators now and no one thereafter; theory B yields ten deliberators now and a trillion more a million years hence. My restriction gives B no boost whatsoever, so SIA does no work here — is that a collapse into SSA, or a legitimate consequence? Second, the Sleep Clinic: you are woken either once, or a thousand times with your memory erased each morning, but on only one of those mornings are you told the rules and asked for your credence; my restriction counts just that awakening, which seems to hand me the halfer answer in a case where I'd rather not be committed to one. Given all that, I want a map of my exposure: what would count against the deliberator restriction, ranked from fatal to merely awkward — formal collapse into a rival view, order-dependence between when deliberation happens and what it delivers, gerrymandering of the reference class, borderline cases where deliberative capacity is vague or continuous (a chimpanzee, a dreamer, a future machine, a person one minute before they first hear of anthropic reasoning), the general suspiciousness of a patch whose only function is to block objections, or some consequence about ordinary evidence that I can't swallow? Which of these would a defender of my own view have to concede as genuine defeaters, and which could I reasonably dismiss as smuggling in an assumption I already reject? Does the restriction predict anything that neither plain SSA nor plain SIA predicts — any case where it sticks its neck out and could actually lose?
