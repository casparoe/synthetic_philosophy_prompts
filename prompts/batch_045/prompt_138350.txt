Hey — possibly a weird one, but let's see. I'm part of a small volunteer group drafting a charter for an imaginary three-level federation (communes / cantons / federation) that we're using as a teaching case in a civic-ed thing, and I want to nail subsidiarity down before we build the rest of the document around it.

My working understanding is pretty simple: subsidiarity means decisions get made at the lowest level that can competently handle them, and only move up when the lower level obviously can't cope. So our draft pushes basically everything downward by default — we even put in a compulsory devolution rule where any federal competence sunsets after ten years unless the centre re-justifies it, plus a rights floor the federation can enforce against a commune. The EU treaty's "can't be sufficiently achieved by the member states" language looks to me like the same idea with more syllables, so I figure a properly consistent charter would leave the centre with almost nothing: army, currency, rivers.

Two places where I'm stuck. First, capacity: half our communes are tiny and would clearly botch water treatment or procurement, so a couple of us want a "capacity-first" clause making the federation fund and train a commune before anything is actually devolved to it. One person says that's the opposite of subsidiarity; another says it's the entire point. And a friend who reads a lot of Catholic social teaching says I've got the principle backwards — that "subsidium" means *help*, that the original target was the big centralised state swallowing up smaller associations, and that the higher level can actually be required to step in. Which honestly sounds like having it both ways to me.

Second: does the principle say anything at all about who pays? We keep sliding into that question. If a canton is made to run a service, does subsidiarity imply it gets the tax base too, or is that a seperate principle altogether?

So: is "lowest level that can handle it" even the right test, or is there a better one for deciding when something genuinely has to move up? And what's the standard way people handle the case where the lower level *could* handle a decision but its decision wrecks something for the level above — an upstream commune damning a river, a commune zoning out a rail line, that sort of thing. Mostly I want to know whether our whole setup is resting on a misreading.
