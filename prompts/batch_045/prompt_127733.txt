My cousin and I got into a dumb argument after we watched the old black-and-white Henry Fonda jury movie (12 Angry Men? the one where they're all sweating in a single room and Fonda is the guy who won't say guilty). He says the movie is actually on the side of the majority: eleven guys sat through the same trial and all voted guilty, so the kid probably did do it, and the movie just got lucky picking the one weirdo to root for. I said no, the whole point is that the eleven were wrong. Then somebody in the comments mentioned some French guy named Condorcet and a "jury theorem" that says if each voter is better than 50-50 at getting it right, and they each decide on their own without copying anybody, then the majority gets more reliable the more people you pile in, and with enough people it's basically never wrong. Which honestly sounds like it proves my cousin right, and it's been bugging me for a week.

The thing I keep coming back to is that those eleven guys aren't independent votes at all. They all heard the same witnesses, and half of them look like they're voting guilty mainly because the loud angry guy is voting guilty, or because they want to get out and see the ball game. So I invented something I've started calling the stubbornness multiplier: the idea that the holdout is worth way more than his one vote, not because he's smarter than the rest (he might well be dumber — half of what he does is just say he isn't sure) but because refusing to go along breaks whatever was making everybody else's vote the same as his neighbor's, and once that's broken the group's actual accuracy goes up even though nobody in the room got any smarter. Is that a real thing anywhere near this theorem, or am I just making stuff up to win an argument?

Can you read the movie through all this for me? I'd love: (1) whether that opening 11-to-1 vote is genuine evidence of guilt under the theorem, or whether the film gives you a reason to throw it out; (2) what happens to the theorem's cheerful conclusion if you swap plain majority rule for the rule the real jury is under, where all twelve have to agree or there's no verdict at all — my guess is that under that rule adding people makes a correct result less likely instead of more, and that the movie is partly about that; (3) two or three specific scenes to point at (the secret ballot where a second hand goes up, the switchblade that turns out not to be one of a kind, the old man downstairs and the noise of the train, the walk-the-timings experiment on the apartment diagram, the woman across the street and the marks glasses left on her nose, the angry dad at the end ripping up the photo of his kid) and tell me which one is the moment the votes stop being copied off each other and start being real; and (4) the bit that bugs me most — if Fonda's guy had been the one who was wrong, if the kid really did stab his father, would the theorem still say the process was a good one, or can it only ever grade the process over the long run and never the verdict in front of you? And if the movie ends up saying something the theorem has no way to say, tell me what that is. Plain language please and go easy on the math — I've never taken a philosophy or a stats class in my life.
