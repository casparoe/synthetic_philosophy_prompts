For a feature I am writing on population ethics for a magazine that covers science and ideas, I need a thought experiment built to order. It has to do two jobs at once: survive being read back to me by a philosopher who defends the person-affecting restriction on betterness, and stay legible to a general reader who has never heard of the Repugnant Conclusion. I have tried to write this case myself and it keeps collapsing into the standard mere-addition setup, where the whole disagreement is generated by one option containing extra happy people, so the constraints below are meant to block that escape route.

The thesis of the piece is that the Repugnant Conclusion is normally presented as a quarrel about how many people there should be, but that the fracture it exposes is really about something else: whether a theory is allowed to enter, on the credit side, the well-being of people who would not exist under the alternative policy, when the cost of securing that well-being falls on identifiable people who will exist either way. So here is what I need: a single-paragraph scenario, realistic enough to run in a newspaper (a hospital, a public health agency, a fertility clinic, a Medicaid program), in which an institution chooses between two policies, A and B, and in which: (i) exactly the same number of people ever live under each policy, and the mechanism that settles which individuals those future people are is something a real institution genuinely decides rather than something stipulated; (ii) every life in both options is clearly a life worth living, and nobody is pushed below the neutral point, since a case that works by creating misery is a different case; (iii) the policies cost the same and differ in no other respect a reader could seize on; (iv) total utilitarianism prefers B; (v) average utilitarianism prefers B as well, so the familiar total-versus-average fight is switched off; (vi) the person-affecting restriction together with the asymmetry — the fact that a person's life would be worth living gives us no reason to create them, while the fact that it would not be worth living does give us a reason not to — ranks A above B, and gets there by argument rather than by stipulation. I want to see the actual step where it says that the losers under B are people and the gainers under B are not.

Give me the scenario, then the welfare levels you are assigning, preferably in a unit a reader can picture (years of healthy life, or points on a scale you define and defend) rather than in abstract hedons, and then do the arithmetic for each principle so the verdicts fall out instead of being announced. After that, four things. Which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to all three verdicts if I remove it by stipulating that the very same future individuals exist under both policies with only their well-being differing. How a defender of the person-affecting view would attack the case, and in particular whether she reaches for the same-number quality claim or instead denies that the policy really does fix who comes to exist. Whether my attempt to neutralize the total-versus-average dispute by fixing the number of people is simply question-begging, because I suspect that at a fixed population size the two orderings coincide automatically; if that is right, say so, and tell me what a small, still realistic relaxation of the fixed-number constraint would do to the verdicts. And finally, a health economist I interviewed insisted that critical-level utilitarianism with a moderately positive critical level would rescue the totalist verdict here; check that claim, and tell me whether it matters if the critical level is applied to everyone or only to those whose existence is at stake. If any two of my specifications turn out not to be jointly satisfiable, tell me which pair conflicts and why, and give me the nearest case that satisfies the rest — and flag it if making the numbers work requires a ratio of future gain to present loss so extreme that it strains a reader's credulity.
