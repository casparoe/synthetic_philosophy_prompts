Hey — I'm a science journalist, and I've been working on a long piece about the cluster of problems that gets labeled "embedded agency": the thought that a real agent is inside the world it acts on, made of the same physics as everything else, smaller than that world, able to model itself only using the same resources it uses to model anything else, and so on. I've now sat through three workshops and maybe forty interviews, and I keep watching the identical exchange happen. One camp says the standard pictures — the Bayesian agent whose prior ranges over a space of environments that includes the true one, the RL picture with a clean split between policy and environment, the decision theorist's agent that gets to treat its own action as a free variable — all smuggle in something like a Cartesian divider between agent and environment, and that this is exactly why none of our formal tools say anything reliable about very capable systems. The other camp says it's a grab bag dressed up as a unified problem: partial observability, bounded computation, self-reference, learning when your model class doesn't contain the truth — each of those is real, each is already studied under some other name, and the phrase itself is doing mostly rhetorical work. Everyone is smart, everyone has heard the other side out repeatedly, and in roughly a year of this I have definately not seen a single person move or concede a point. Whenever I ask what would settle it, I get either "a decision theory that doesn't fall apart when the agent reasons about its own output" or "systems that work, which we're already building" — which makes me think they aren't even agreeing on what a solution would look like. My editor keeps asking whether this is a real debate or a lot of smoke, and I don't have a good answer.

The half-baked taxonomy in my notes is that there are three complaints hiding under the one label. First, a self-reference complaint: an agent trying to predict a system it is itself part of runs into something structurally similar to a liar paradox or a halting problem, and this is supposed to be a deep obstruction rather than an engineering annoyance. Second, a calibration complaint: the usual convergence and regret results quietly assume the truth sits inside your hypothesis space, and once the agent is smaller than its environment that assumption is just false, so the guarantees people wanted were never on offer. Third, a boundary complaint: there's no fact of the matter about where the agent stops and the environment begins, it's a choice of description, and a lot of the arguments seem to turn on people quietly picking different levels of description and then talking past one another. My hunch — I'd like it stress-tested — is that the first two are genuine technical problems with existing homes in logic and learning theory, and that nearly all the heat, the feeling that something foundational is missing, is coming from the third, which may not be a problem at all so much as metaphysical discomfort wearing a technical costume.

So what kind of stalemate is this? Is it verbal, a fight about words like "agent", "boundary" and "foundations"? Is it two groups answering different questions under one heading — one wanting a formalism with guarantees, one wanting empirical adequacy? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work that nobody has stated cleanly? Is my three-bin split useful, or does it misdescribe what either side is actually committed to? And given whatever you think the diagnosis is, what would count as progress — a theorem, a worked-out formalism, a demonstration, a change of question — or is this a case where no resolution should be expected, and if so, why? I've read plenty of the blog-post-level material, so please don't just survey the positions at me; I want the diagnosis with reasons, and an honest verdict on whether this beat is still going to be worth covering in two years.
