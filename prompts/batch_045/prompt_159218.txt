I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar on theories of well-being — fourteen students, mostly philosophy majors, plus two psychology majors who keep dragging in empirical work on mood and reward. Last night, after a departmental colloquium, I had a long and entirely circular argument with a friend who works in affective neuroscience. Her claim: that there is one thing all pleasant experiences share is a folk illusion, and what we call pleasure is just a grab-bag of states that happen to move us in similar directions. My reply, repeated too many times: if you give up on a shared feature you lose whatever made pleasure the thing worth having in the first place, and you are left with a list instead of an explanation. We got nowhere, and I would rather my students run into the same wall than hear me describe it.

So I want a structured activity for that session in which they assemble the problem themselves. The three words they must not hear until the last ten minutes are hedonism, valence, and heterogeneity; before that, everything should be in their own vocabulary. The shape I have in mind: first they each generate the experiences they would unhesitatingly call pleasant, and the ones they would call pleasant only under protest, and the group tries to write a single definition that covers all and only the first set; then the definition breaks in a specific, discoverable way, and they find themselves pushed toward either a felt-quality account (pleasure as a distinctive way an experience feels) or an attitudinal account (pleasure as liking or wanting the experience while you are having it). I also want them to feel the pull of a third option I have been toying with and cannot make airtight: that pleasant names a role rather than a quality — the state such that, when you are in it, you want to go on being in it, and its presence settles whether things are going well for you at that moment. Two invented materials I would like built into the activity as evidence the students have to rule on rather than as illustrations from me: a fictional ethnographic vignette about a small community whose classificatory term covers relief, absorption in a hard task, being proven right, and the cold alertness after a long fast, but not the taste of ripe fruit; and a fictional neural marker that tracks self-reported pleasantness cleanly across sensory cases but splits off sharply for absorbed, effortful ones. There should be something at stake in the room — perhaps they are advising a clinician, or programming something benevolent — so that the choice between the accounts is a decision with consequences rather than a poll.

Please give me the activity as exactly five numbered beats — the setup (with materials and timing folded in), the first round, the turn where the invented cases land, the forced choice, and the debrief — each beat one or two sentences, with no preamble and no closing summary. The debrief should map specific moments in the room onto the three accounts, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
