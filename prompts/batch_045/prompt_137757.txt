I work in psychometrics, on large-scale educational assessment — I build, link, and equate latent scales for a living. My working vocabulary is item response models; item characteristic curves; person and item parameters expressed on a single logit scale; the fact that a latent scale is identified only up to a positive affine transformation and that something has to be fixed to pin it down (a reference population's mean and variance, a threshold parameter, an anchor item's difficulty); linking designs, including common-item nonequivalent-groups equating; measurement invariance, differential item functioning, and the graded business of establishing that an instrument means the same thing in two populations. I am fluent in all of that and I would like you to meet me there rather than in the vocabulary of moral philosophy.

The thing I need translated is the philosophical dispute about interpersonal comparisons of utility — whether a claim like this policy takes more from her than it gives to him has any determinate content, and if so what it rests on. Please restate the main positions one by one in my terms. Here is how I have been provisionally formalizing them, and I would like you to work with my formalization, correcting it where it distorts:

For each person i, suppose a utility function u_i, and suppose the only thing that is well defined is the family a_i + b_i · u_i with b_i > 0, where the person-specific origin a_i and unit b_i are unidentified. On this framing: the skeptical or ordinalist position says no restriction is available across persons, so only each person's own ordering is meaningful; unit comparability says the b_i can be set equal across persons while the a_i remain free, so differences in gain and loss are comparable but levels are not; level comparability says the a_i can be set equal too, so levels are comparable; full comparability adds a common zero. The so-called zero-one rule — setting each person's worst outcome to 0 and best to 1 — is a particular normalization. And there is the proposal that one gets interpersonal comparisons by asking a single judge to imagine herself in another person's position and register an extended preference over being that person in that situation, which I am inclined to read as a linking design: one respondent, two populations, and an imaginative simulation playing the role of the common item.

Give me, for each position, what translates cleanly into my vocabulary, what has to be distorted to fit, and what cannot be said at all. Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want:

Where the zero-one rule and the reference-population standardization differ. In my work the choice of origin and unit is a convention with no consequences: every substantive conclusion is invariant under the transformation, so nobody argues about it. In the utility case the choice of normalization appears to change which policy ranks first. Is that because the invariance class is different, because the social ranking is not invariant under person-specific affine transformations in the way my scale-based conclusions are, or because I am misdescribing the situation and the utility problem was never a problem of scale indeterminacy at all but a problem of choosing a numeraire or a standard of comparison across things that are not on one scale? Say which, and say what follows for how much of the philosophical dispute is really a measurement dispute.

Whether extended-sympathy comparison is usefully thought of as common-item equating: what is the anchor, is the linking symmetric and transitive in the way my linking functions are required to be, and is the standard objection — that my imaginative simulation of being her is contaminated by my own psychology, my own risk attitudes, my own adaptation levels — best expressed as differential item functioning, as a failure of identification, or as something measurement theory has no name for?

Whether there is a measurement model for utility at all. In my field a latent trait is inferred from indicators, with error, and comparability is an empirical hypothesis you test. In much of the philosophical literature utility is stipulated by the agent's preferences rather than measured, which means there are no indicators, no error, and nothing to test — so measurement theory would apply only vacuously. But when people use multi-item well-being or health-state instruments, there are indicators, and the philosopher's skepticism begins to sound exactly like a claim of non-invariance across respondents. Is that a genuine convergence or a category mistake?

And the reverse direction: what does the philosophical debate contain that my vocabulary has no room for — the fact that utility is supposed to be defined by preference rather than indicated by it, the fact that the aggregation step is normative rather than statistical, the idea that a normalization can be unfair rather than merely arbitrary — and, equally, what does my field have that the philosophical literature seems never to take up: graded and partial comparability, testable invariance, and an actual technology for linking scales that were never designed to be linked?

Finally, the decision this is in service of, so that the translation has somewhere to land: a committee I sit on is arguing about whether a single self-report item answered on a 0 to 10 scale may be averaged and summed across individuals and then used as a policy outcome, with the sums compared between groups. On your own translation, say whether that question has an answer of the sort my field already knows how to give, whether it has an answer at all, or whether the practice has to be justified by something outside measurement theory — and be explicit about which parts of your answer are consequences of the framework and which are your own view.
