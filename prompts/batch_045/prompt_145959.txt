Q2. Thomas Szasz argued that mental illness is a myth.

(a) Reconstruct his argument as charitably as you can. Your answer should say what he takes a genuine disease to be, what he thinks psychiatric diagnoses in fact do, and what he means by calling mental illness a myth — in particular, explain why myth here does not mean a lie, and does not mean a term with no meaning. [10]

(b) A critic replies: psychiatry is just neurology that hasn't located the lesion yet; once we identify the neural abnormality behind something like schizophrenia, the diagnosis will name a real disease and Szasz will be refuted. Assess this reply. Keep three questions separate: (i) what present-day psychiatric concepts in fact pick out; (ii) what future neuroscience might discover; (iii) what would follow about the justification of current psychiatric practices. [8]

(c) Andrea is 27, diagnosed with schizophrenia after a psychotic episode two years ago. She is stable on medication, hates the side effects, wants to come off it, and the two times she has come off it she has ended up sleeping rough. Her family have asked her psychiatrist to have her committed. Using the two-track distinction from the lecture — that whether a condition is a disease is a different question from whether an intervention is justified by the consent of the person it is done to — say what Szasz's position implies here, and whether his conclusion depends on the diagnosis being a myth. [7]

And here is the answer, more or less verbatim:

(a) Szasz's target is the claim that mental illnesses are diseases in the same sense that pneumonia is. On his account a genuine disease is a demonstrable abnormality of the body — a lesion — and in the case of the mind it would have to be a lesion of the brain, the sort of thing you could in principle find on the dissecting table. Nothing like that has been found for schizophrenia or depression, so the phrase mental illness does not name anything real. By calling it a myth he means that it is literally meaningless: sentences like "she has schizophrenia" have no cognitive content, they cannot be true or false, in the way that talk of demonic possession has no truth value. That is the sense in which the concept is empty rather than merely false. What psychiatric diagnoses really do is dress up moral disapproval as medicine — schizophrenia is just a verdict that someone is behaving in a way the rest of us disapprove of — and calling it an illness lets the state deprive people of liberty while appearing to be doing them good. Szasz, along with the rest of the antipsychiatry movement, held that diagnosis is therefore a form of social control, and his own version of the point is that these categories are looping kinds: classify people as schizophrenic and they start to behave like it, so the classification creates the very kind it pretends to discover. He did insist that he was not denying that people suffer; they have what he called problems in living. But that suffering, on his view, is not an illness, and so it is none of medicine's business.

(b) The critic's reply seems to me to succeed, and it is the strongest objection available. If the lesion is eventually found, as it probably will be, then the diagnosis will after all name a real disease, and Szasz will have been wrong — which shows that his claim was an empirical and falsifiable claim all along, not a conceptual one. This is the respect in which he has dated badly: brain imaging already shows measurable differences between people diagnosed with schizophrenia and everyone else, and that is precisely the kind of evidence he said would never turn up. Notice that this also settles question (iii). If the abnormality is really there, then treating Andrea against her will is justified, because we would know that her refusal is a symptom of the disease rather than a choice she is making. Once a lesion explains the behaviour there is nothing left to respect in the refusal.

(c) What Szasz's position implies is that Andrea should not be given psychiatric treatment at all. If mental illness is a myth then there is no disease to treat, so prescribing antipsychotics is treating a fiction, and psychiatrists who do it are practising medicine on something that isn't there; the honest conclusion of his argument is that psychiatry should be abolished as a medical specialty. It is true that Szasz went on practising psychotherapy with consenting adults, and some readers take that to show he wasn't against treatment as such. But that is just a failure to live up to his own conclusions — people are allowed to be inconsistent — and it doesn't alter what the argument implies. As for the two-track distinction, his argument sits entirely on the first track: it is an argument that the condition is not a disease. Nothing he says is on the second track, and nothing could be, since the question of consent only gets a grip once you have granted that there is something here to consent to. So the distinction doesn't really add anything. The myth thesis does all the work, and if it stands, Andrea should simply be left alone.
