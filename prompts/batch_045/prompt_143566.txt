I run a volunteer evening series on information design in the back room of a public library — about sixteen people per session, mostly self-taught graphic designers, two data journalists, a couple of retirees, and three or four for whom English is not a first language — with one 90-minute slot, a whiteboard, a cheap printer, and essentially no budget. I have no academic background in any of this; I came to it on my own after reading a short essay about Otto Neurath's picture language (the Vienna Method developed at his social and economic museum in Vienna, later renamed ISOTYPE), and I have been arguing ever since with a friend who designs infographics and insists that a well-made pictogram is simply transparent — anyone can read it, no learning required, and that is exactly why pictures beat words. What I read about Neurath suggests he thought something subtler than either of us: that a picture language is *not* self-explanatory and reading it has to be learned (what he called "visual education"), that getting from a table of figures to a chart involves a step his team called "transformation," in which somebody decides how much one small figure stands for and what gets rounded or dropped, and that he insisted on calling the finished thing something like a "language-like technique" or a "helping language" rather than a real language, because on its own it cannot say no, or if, or because. I want my group to collide with that rather than hear it from me.

My rough sketch, which I am not attached to, is a sequence of short rounds in pairs: one person (the encoder) gets a card with a factual claim drawn from a dataset, the other (the decoder) gets nothing, and the encoder may pass only pictures — no numerals, no letters, no words — with the decoder's reconstruction being scored against the original claim. Round by round the tasks escalate: first freehand drawing with no shared conventions; then a shared, standardized sheet of simple icons with the rule that greater quantity is shown by repeating same-sized figures rather than enlarging one; then numbers far too large to draw one-for-one, forcing each pair to invent a scale and a rounding rule; then a target sentence that contains a negation and a causal "because," which pictures alone seem unable to carry. Somewhere near the end I would like a confederate — a participant quietly given a different convention in advance, say for what an outlined or crossed-out figure means — to misread a chart that everyone else reads fluently. Please give me the actual design: the materials and prep, the exact rules for each round, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes including setup and teardown, the source data and the precise target claims to encode (I was going to use our library's branch lending figures for the last decade, but use whatever makes the failure modes sharpest), the scoring or payoff scheme, and the debrief.

Two hard constraints: no lecture up front, and nobody should hear the name Neurath, or the words ISOTYPE and Vienna Method, before the last five minutes — the point has to be visible in what happens at the tables rather than in what I say afterwards. The debrief should map particular moments onto particular claims, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover, and be frank about which part of the point the activity cannot make anyone feel and which I therefore have to state myself. I also want your judgment on two things: whether my instinct that a pictogram is never simply transparent is in fact too strong, and what to do with the participants who do not share a first language — whether the activity can honestly test Neurath's internationalist hope for pictures, or whether trying to would just fake it.
