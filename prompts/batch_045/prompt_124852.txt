I maintain a medium-sized open-source project — around 200 contributors, of whom about 20 have commit rights — and we are in an argument about our own governance document that I think is more than a normal project-politics argument. I'd be grateful for your help with it. I have no formal background in philosophy, so I'd rather have the argument spelled out than have a name attached to it, and I'd prefer you not point me at books or papers: I want the reasoning, not a reading list. Please answer in British English.

Background: since 2016 we have been governed by a written Charter. It was adopted informally — the founder, who then controlled the repository, posted it to the mailing list and said it was in force. Since then it has been treated as binding by everyone, and several hundred decisions, releases, expulsions and elections have been made under it. Four of its clauses matter here, paraphrased closely:

*Clause 12 (Amendments).* — *This Charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the Committers, provided fourteen days' notice of the proposed amendment was given.*

*Clause 13.* — *Notwithstanding Clause 12, Section 4 (Rights of Contributors) may be amended only by a three-quarters vote of the Committers together with a majority of all Contributors.*

*Clause 14.* — *This Charter is the supreme authority of the Project, and no act of the Project, including any amendment, is valid unless it is consistent with this Charter.*

*Clause 15.* — *The Steering Committee may, by majority vote, issue a binding interpretation of any provision of this Charter.*

There are three live disputes.

**The first.** A group of Committers wants to make the project harder to change: they propose to raise the general amendment threshold from two-thirds to 90%, and they propose to do it using Clause 12 itself — that is, a two-thirds vote is supposed to be sufficient to change the number "two-thirds" to "90%". A number of contributors say this is not merely unwise but somehow self-undermining: that a rule governing how rules are changed cannot be rewritten by the very procedure it lays down, that you would have to satisfy the new rule in order to install it, and that this is circular. Others reply that this objection proves far too much — on that view no founding document could ever have its own amendment procedure fixed, or repair an obvious drafting error in it, and the result would be the worst outcome of all. A third group points out something I find harder to dismiss: that even if the move works, it only works once, because 90% can later lower the threshold to 51%, and then 51% can do anything at all — so any protection written into the Charter is, in practice, only ever one or two votes away from being nothing. Someone has therefore proposed entrenching the entrenchment: amend Clause 12 to 90% and add, in the same amendment, a sentence saying that Clause 12 as so amended may not itself be amended except by 90%. Is that different in principle, or is it the same move one level up?

**The second.** A separate faction, which happens to hold three of the five Steering Committee seats, has noticed Clause 15. They propose to have the Committee issue a "binding interpretation" of Section 4 whose practical effect everyone agrees would be to change it — removing a protection that would require a three-quarters vote plus a contributor majority to remove by the ordinary route. They insist this is interpretation rather than amendment and so needs only a Committee majority. I cannot locate the difference. If the distinction between changing a rule and authoritatively saying what it means is real, where exactly does it lie, and what would make Clause 15 something other than a general licence to bypass Clause 13? If the distinction is doing no work here, I'd like that said plainly too.

**The third.** Someone has argued that the 2016 adoption was itself irregular: the Charter says it is the supreme authority and that no act is valid unless consistent with it, yet the Charter was brought into existence by one person's declaration, which under Clause 12 as it now stands would not count as a valid amendment or adoption. On this view the Charter has been void from the beginning, and so has everything done under it, including the protections in Section 4 that people are currently fighting about; we should dissolve and start again. The reply I find intuitive is that essentially every organisation is founded in some way that its own later rules would not permit, that nine years of everyone treating the document as binding must count for something, and that this objection is available to anyone who ever loses a vote. But I can't say why the first argument is wrong, only that I don't like where it leads.

What I actually want from you is a map of which of these moves are genuine problems — incoherent, circular, or resting on a confusion — and which are merely contested, together with the strongest version of each side. Concretely: (1) Can a rule that governs its own amendment be amended by the procedure it specifies, and does the answer change depending on whether the change makes amendment harder or easier? (2) Is there a real difference between changing a rule and binding interpretation of it, or is the difference only one of degree? (3) Does an irregular founding undermine a document's authority, or can sustained acceptance repair it — and if so, what exactly does the repairing? (4) Is the "two-step" argument a good reason to think entrenched clauses are decorative, or does it only show that a single threshold is the wrong shape for the job?

If several of these turn out to be different faces of one underlying problem, I'd like to be told that as well — and if any of my framings embed a mistake, please say which. Where you need a term of art, explain it in passing.
