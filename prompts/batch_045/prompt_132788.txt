**The question**

Much of the recent literature can be read as a dispute not about whether free will is compatible with determinism but about which variety of freedom is the one *worth wanting* — that is, which variety is such that its absence would genuinely disappoint someone who cared about free will, and not merely about being held responsible. Three candidate varieties:

(a) *Leeway.* At the moment of acting, the agent is able to do otherwise than she in fact does, holding fixed the actual past and the laws of nature.

(b) *Guidance.* The agent's action issues from a mechanism appropriately responsive to her reasons: she acts because of the considerations she takes to favour acting, and, had those considerations been relevantly different, she would have acted differently.

(c) *Ultimacy.* The agent is the ultimate source of her action: the sufficient conditions of its occurrence lie in the agent herself, and not ultimately in conditions she did not herself choose.

Consider the following case.

**Mira.** At forty, Mira requests a neurological procedure, and she has excellent reasons for requesting it: it will reorganise her decision-making so that thereafter she reliably acts on the balance of her reasons as she sees them. The procedure is carried out and succeeds. Some years later Mira finds a wallet in the street and returns it to its owner. She returns it because she judges that she has most reason to; she would have kept it had the balance of her reasons been different; and, because of the procedure, she could not in fact have done otherwise at that moment, holding fixed the actual past and the laws. Assume in addition that Mira's original request for the procedure was itself the product of prior causes she did not choose.

(i) Say, with one or two sentences of explanation each, which of (a), (b) and (c) Mira has with respect to her returning the wallet. Which of your three verdicts is the hardest to defend, and why? (10 marks)

(ii) Someone argues: Mira's case shows that the variety worth wanting is (b), since (b) is exactly what we care about when we ask whether someone is a fitting target of praise, blame, and the reactive attitudes — the resentment, gratitude and indignation that, on one influential account, partly constitute holding someone responsible. Give one good reason to accept this argument and one good reason to resist it, and explain how your reason for resisting bears on the claim that the dispute between compatibilists and incompatibilists is at least partly a dispute about which variety is worth wanting rather than a dispute about determinism. (10 marks)

**The candidate's answer**

Mira lacks (a). (a) requires that she be able to do otherwise holding fixed the actual past and the laws, and the stipulation is that the procedure guarantees the outcome; so there is no world with the same past and the same laws in which she keeps the wallet. She has (b): she acts because of the considerations she takes to favour returning it, and the relevant counterfactual — had her reasons been different, she would have acted differently — is true. She lacks (c): the sufficient conditions of her returning the wallet include the procedure and the team who performed it, and, further back, the causes of her request for it, none of which she chose. So she is not the ultimate source of the action.

The hardest verdict is (c), and not because it is difficult to apply to Mira but because it is unclear that (c) is coherent. If being an ultimate source requires being an uncaused cause, then nothing within a causal order could ever satisfy it, and (c) would be an impossible ideal rather than a variety it is even coherent to want. A defender of (c) is therefore in a difficult position: she must hold that determinism undermines an agent's responsiveness to reasons, since on determinism the agent's responses are themselves caused, and this threatens to collapse her position into the view that nothing one does makes any difference, which practically everyone rejects.

That said, the loss of (a) is not really the loss of anything Mira could have wanted. She was able to return the wallet *because* she judged that she had reason to, and she would have kept it had she judged otherwise; the sense in which she could not have done otherwise is only that she could not have done otherwise given the reasons she actually had, and an ability to act against one's own considered judgement is not a freedom anyone is worse off without. Her action was up to her in the only sense that bears on (a).

For part (ii): the reason to accept the argument is the familiar point, associated with the family of cases in which a counterfactual intervener stands ready to bring about the outcome should the agent show any sign of acting differently, that moral responsibility does not require alternative possibilities. What matters for praise and blame is not that the agent could have done otherwise but that the action genuinely issued from her own take on the reasons — from her, rather than from coercion, compulsion or a nervous tic. Mira's action does issue from her own evaluative judgement, so she is a fitting object of gratitude and of blame in a way that a coerced agent is not, and the fact that she could not have done otherwise does not undermine this.

The reason to resist is that the argument moves from what is sufficient for moral responsibility to what is worth wanting, and these can come apart. Even if (b) is all that responsibility requires, it can still be disappointing to discover that one's deepest commitments were manufactured rather than made — that one's character is something one was handed rather than something one authored. A person can grant that she is answerable for what she does and still reasonably regret the absence of (a) or of (c). So the compatibilist cannot settle the matter by pointing to our practices of praise and blame: those practices track what is necessary for responsibility, not what we are after when we care about freedom. That is why the real dispute is in part a dispute about which variety is worth wanting, and why it will not be settled by settling whether determinism is true.
