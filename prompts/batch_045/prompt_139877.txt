I am writing a literary novel set against a near-future effective-altruism summit, and in one chapter I use a fictional device to bring the English philosopher and utilitarian Jeremy Bentham into a conference room where the delegates are debating the moral urgency of insect sentience. The delegates present what they call the scale argument: there are vastly more insects than mammals, perhaps by many orders of magnitude, so even if the probability that any individual insect is conscious is low, the expected amount of insect suffering or enjoyment may still be larger than that of all vertebrates combined; on that basis they argue that insect welfare should be a dominant, perhaps the dominant, moral priority. My characters assume that Bentham will be an easy convert, because he notoriously held that the capacity to suffer, rather than the ability to reason or speak, is what marks the boundary of moral concern. But I want the scene to be more than a slogan, so I need you to help me work out what he would actually say.

To make the problem concrete, suppose the delegates also put to him a specific case from an agricultural-ant species that lives only a few weeks, is extremely numerous, and exhibits threat-avoidance behaviour that looks distressingly like pain but might be a hard-wired reflex; the scientists assign a low but non-trivial credence, say five per cent, to these ants being sentient. How, if at all, would Bentham's own hedonistic or felicific calculus—its attention to intensity, duration, certainty or uncertainty, and above all extent, the number of beings affected—handle a case where the expected number of sentient victims is enormous but the probability of sentience, and the richness or intensity of each insect's possible experience, is low? Would he conclude that the ants' expected suffering outweighs that of a far smaller number of mammals whose suffering is much more certain, or would some feature of his theory stop the sheer numbers from doing all the work? Please distinguish, as clearly as you can, between what is a fairly direct implication of Bentham's actual commitments and what is an extrapolation to a debate he never faced; give me your confidence in each major step (high, medium, low, or a rough percentage if you prefer) and tell me where a Bentham scholar would wince at anachronism.

I should add that, if the exercise is misconceived in some way—if, for example, the scale argument as I have formulated it already presupposes a modern expected-utility framework that Bentham's texts cannot bear, or if predicting a long-dead philosopher's reaction is really just costumed guesswork—then please say that plainly rather than manufacturing a confident speech. I would much rather have a careful reconstruction that admits its own limits than a vivid pastiche that sounds authentic but quietly imposes twenty-first-century assumptions on him.
