I'm applying for a one-year dissertation write-up fellowship and need a two-page proposal for the chapter I'd write during it. Here's the half-formed idea, which I can't get straight and keep rewriting in circles.

The standard fight about whether lives teach anything goes like this: one side says we latch onto admirable people and work out what virtue is by looking at them, the other says biographies of great men idealise, airbrush out the failures, and end up teaching deference to rank and public success rather than virtue. I think both sides are arguing about the wrong object. What Plutarch actually built in the Parallel Lives is a *pairing* machine — 23 pairs, each putting one Greek next to one Roman he took to be similar in character, and something like eighteen of them closing with a formal comparison where he weighs the two against each other. So the unit of instruction isn't the life, it's the contrast between two lives. And my guess is that what the contrast does is force the reader to sort what belongs to the man's character from what belongs to his office, his city, his luck. That sorting operation *is* the skill. On that picture hagiography breaks the genre structurally, not just distortingly: a scrubbed-clean life has nothing to be set against, so it can't be used to make the character-versus-circumstance discrimination at all. It isn't that the exemplar is set too high; it's that a purified life is a degenerate case of the form.

Second half, which I'm less sure about and which may really be a separate project: a life only has a readable shape once it's finished. The biographer and the reader look back at Alexander's early decisions knowing where they led, which Alexander never did. So maybe whatever the Lives impart is a *retrospective* skill — recognising the shape of a virtue in a completed life — and that doesn't automatically convert into anything you can use while you're in the middle of acting. Call it the biographer's privilege. Possibly that's precisely why the pairing is there: two lives with similar beginnings and divergent ends is the nearest you can get, on the page, to cancelling out hindsight. Possibly it's a fatal objection to the whole genre and Plutarch is quietly relying on it. I go back and foward on this and could be talked out of including it.
