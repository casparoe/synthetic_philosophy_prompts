I'm in the final year of a philosophy degree, and I'm writing the term paper for my seminar on virtue ethics and its empirical critics (target length 3,000 words). Before I commit to the full draft I'd like detailed comments on the argumentative core of it, which I've compressed below into about 900 words of notes. Please treat the notes as the skeleton of the paper rather than as a finished text: I care far more about whether the argument works than about how it reads, and I would much rather have hard criticism than encouragement. If a section is fine, say so in half a sentence and move on.

The paper's question: the situationist critique is usually presented as an existence claim about character traits, but the practical conclusions people draw from it (about praise and blame, moral education, self-trust, and the design of institutions) need a quantitative claim about how widespread trait-failure is. I want to argue that the critique's evidence base cannot support that quantitative claim, for three independent reasons, and that what survives is still genuinely revisionary for the virtue theorist.

1. Structure of the critique as I see it. (i) Empirical premise: in the canonical social-psychological paradigms, behaviour is far better predicted by apparently trivial features of the situation than by any measured individual-difference variable. (ii) Bridge premise: virtue ethics is committed to robust, cross-situationally stable traits that explain behaviour. (iii) Practical conclusion: the trait attributions that our ethical practices rely on are largely false, and those practices need to be rebuilt around situational design. I grant (ii) and I grant that (i) is true of the paradigms. I want to attack the inference from (i) to (iii).

2. The supplied-reason objection. In each of the canonical paradigms the experimenter supplies the reason to the subject. In the obedience study the victim's claim on the subject is delivered by an authority figure standing next to him, with the instruction repeated on demand. In the Good Samaritan study the need is made perceptually unavoidable: a slumped, coughing confederate in the subject's path. In the phone-booth study the dropped papers land at the subject's feet. Every one of these designs tests whether the subject acts on a reason that has already been made maximally salient to her. None of them tests whether the subject registers a reason that no one has pointed out, where the need is present in the environment but not cued: the colleague whose contribution is quietly written out of the report, the administrative cruelty that the form does not mention, the bystander whose discomfort never quite becomes a claim. But on the Aristotelian picture the capacity to see a situation under the right description, to notice that this is a case in which someone needs something from you, is not an optional extra bolted onto the virtues; it is the part that practical wisdom consists in. If so, the canonical designs are structurally silent about precisely the capacity that virtue ethics is most invested in, and the empirical premise is far less damaging than it looks.

The objection comes with a prediction, which I would like to keep because it makes the point falsifiable: strip out the supplied cue, and cross-situational consistency should rise. The dimension that would show up is something like receptivity to reasons that are available but not salient. If situationism is right, noticing should fragment by situation just as helping does; if I am right, people should differ stably in how much salience a reason needs before it moves them, and that difference should predict behaviour across contents that share no surface feature.

3. Individuation parity. The situationist explains the variance by appeal to situation-kinds: being in a hurry, having an authority present, having responsibility diffused, being in a good mood. But this taxonomy is post hoc. The labels are generated by inspecting the manipulations that happened to produce large effects, and they are not validated by showing that fresh instantiations of the kind — a different way of being hurried, a different kind of authority — reproduce the effect. So the situationist buys explanatory power with an unvalidated taxonomy of kinds and then charges the virtue theorist for hers. Either the demand for cross-situational robustness applies to both taxonomies or to neither. If it applies to both, the situationist owes us evidence that situations are robust kinds; if to neither, the objection to traits loses its force. Either way (i) does not get you to (iii).

4. The dramatic-instance problem. These paradigms became canonical because they produced surprising results. But the surprise partly consists in the failure of trait prediction, so a literature selected for surprisingness is selected partly for trait-failure — the observed cross-situational correlation is biased downward in something like the way effect sizes in a literature selected for significance are biased upward. That supports at most the claim that there exist situations in which trait prediction fails badly. It does not give you a base rate, and the practical conclusion needs a base rate.

5. What I want to concede. Even if the situationist overreaches on the existence claim, the critique establishes something important about fragility and cultivability. If a virtue is a capacity whose exercise depends on a narrow and easily disrupted range of situational supports, then exhortation is not a moral-educational method and the virtue theorist's burden shifts to the design of environments. And this concession presses back on §2: if the perceptual capacity is itself cue-dependent, then perhaps what the virtue ethicist should want is not stability of behaviour but stability of the capacity-plus-environment pair. That would make the virtues environmentally scaffolded states rather than properties of the agent alone — a real revision of the standard picture, and one I am not sure my paper can absorb without losing §2.
