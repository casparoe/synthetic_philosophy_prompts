Me and my friend spent like two hours last night arguing about the dichotomy of control and got nowhere, so I want a second opinion. (Also worth noting: that phrase itself isn't ancient, it's a modern self-help label, which I think matters.)

The fight was about what the Epictetus move at the start of the Enchiridion is actually doing. My friend says it isn't about causal control at all: the phrase marks what's attributable to you, what you can be answerable for, so the dichotomy is a claim about what can be good or bad for your character, not a tip about where to aim your effort. I said that can't be right, because the whole reason the modern Stoicism crowd finds it useful is that it tells you to stop burning energy on stuff your effort won't move.

Compare the two readings directly and tell me which one holds up, using these three pressure points:

- The bit where Epictetus lets you go after an external anyway, provided you do it with the reservation that it may not happen. On my reading that's obviously a concession about causal uncertainty. He says it's about not staking your character on the outcome.
- The modern version adds a third bucket, things partly up to you, and handles it by internalizing the goal: you aim to play well, not to win. Does that maneuver even survive on his reading? I think it smuggles the external back in, because "playing well" still gets graded by whether you win the point.
- His reading looks useless for the anxiety thing people actually use this for, since it says nothing about whether trying will pay off. He says that's a feature.

Last thing, he hated this when I floated it: treat externals as evidence rather than as goods. The outcome isn't a good, but it's information about whether your judgment was sound, so you attend to it without desiring it. Does that make sense on either reading, or is it just the modern trichotomy wearing a hat?
