I'm presenting a 25-minute works-in-progress paper next week and I want to walk in with the question period already mapped out. I'd like a prep sheet, not a defense of the view.

**My thesis.** Virtual objects — a sword in an MMO, a cathedral built block by block in Minecraft, a parcel of land in Second Life — are genuine artifacts, not fictions and not mere permission slips issued by a platform. But the wrong done when someone destroys one is not, in the first instance, a setback to the player's welfare. It is a distinct kind of harm that I call practical usurpation: the destroyer takes over the victim's practical authority over a project the victim was in the middle of. A long-term player project — build the cathedral, train the character, hold the guild treasury — is partly constituted by the objects it is executed in, so destroying the object does not merely cause distress; it makes the project fail, in roughly the way burning someone's half-finished novel would, except that the manuscript here is an artifact constituted partly by code and partly by a community's practices.

**Three load-bearing moves.** (1) *Identity*: a virtual object's identity conditions include its causal provenance — who built it, when, under which rules — and not merely its qualitative profile. That is my answer to the copy problem: you can duplicate the cathedral bit for bit on another server and still not have restored what was lost. (2) I distinguish restorability from replaceability. An object can be restored, same history and same practice-role recovered, or merely replaced, a qualitative duplicate with a new history, and only restoration answers the victim's complaint. I argue that the common move of saying the loss is trivial because the object is cheaply reproducible conflates the two. (3) I claim that server shutdown and griefing differ in exactly the way my account predicts: shutdown destroys the object without usurpating the project, because no agent takes over the victim's practical authority, whereas griefing usurpates. I take the fact that players describe the first as grief and the second as something closer to outrage to be evidence for the distinction, not merely a consequence of it.

**The audience.** A works-in-progress session in a philosophy department, roughly twenty people. Four constituencies matter:

- The chair is a metaphysician of artifacts and social ontology, sympathetic to creationist and institutional accounts, impatient with anything that sounds like it is multiplying entities. She will press on whether an artifact can really be partly constituted by code and partly by practice, on whether provenance-sensitive identity conditions can be stated without circularity, and on whether I have said anything the artifactualist did not already say more carefully.
- A property theorist who holds that virtual items are nothing over and above bundles of contractual permissions against the platform operator: the terms of service are the whole story, and the fact that operators may pull the plug at will shows players never had anything. He will want to know what work usurpation does that a licence-revocation analysis does not, and he will phrase everything in terms of standing, remedies, and who owes what to whom.
- Two HCI and game-studies researchers who work on presence, embodiment, and player experience. They like the phenomenology and distrust the metaphysics. They will ask what evidence I have that players make the grief/outrage distinction I lean on, whether the effect survives across game genres and across cultures of play, and whether I am illegitimately importing a clinical notion of grief into a design context.
- Several ethicists of a broadly welfarist cast who think welfare is the only currency of harm and will apply what I think of as the translation test: if the object is deleted and the player is handed a pill that erases every trace of distress and leaves the memory intact, is any harm left? If I say yes, they want to know what it is a harm *to*.

There is also a graduate student who plays EVE Online and who will, I am fairly confident, raise some version of the permitted-heist case: the game's rules explicitly allow a certain amount of theft and fraud between players, so is the great in-game bank heist a usurpation on my account, or is it simply the game working as designed?
