I'm applying for a nine-month, teaching-free research fellowship and need a two-page proposal for a project on satisficing and bounded rationality. Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into something fundable.

The standard dialectic feels stuck to me: on one side, satisficing with an aspiration level is presented as a genuinely different account of rational choice for agents who can't compute an optimum; on the other side comes the reply that this is just optimization in disguise, because once you put the costs of search and deliberation into the objective function, the satisficer turns out to be a maximizer of a net payoff, and the aspiration level is only a rough proxy for the point where the marginal cost of further search exceeds its marginal expected return. My sense is that both sides are arguing about a *single* decision made against a *fixed* menu, which is exactly the case where the optimization-with-search-costs reply is strongest, and that the interesting content of satisficing only shows up when you look at an agent choosing repeatedly over time. Two wrinkles I keep coming back to, which I can't find handled and which I want the proposal to make central. First, call it the leakage problem: satisficing in one domain of a life frees up attention and resources that must go somewhere, and if the agent optimizes with those freed resources, it looks like the logic of optimization leaks back into the original domain and the threshold gets bid up to an optimum — so satisficing appears to be unstable, either spreading to every domain or collapsing into optimizing everywhere; but that argument seems to presuppose that the freed resources have positive marginal value everywhere, which is just to deny from the start that any value is genuinely satiable, so I can't tell whether leakage is a real result or a restatement of the disagreement. Second, call it the calibration regress: the aspiration level itself has to be set by some procedure, and if setting it well is itself a hard deliberative problem then satisficing seems to need a second-order satisficing rule, and so on; the obvious escape is that aspiration levels are supposed to *adapt* to experience, but then I want to know what stops adaptive thresholds from drifting downward in a way that quietly erodes the agent's own standards over a lifetime, and whether "don't let that happen" can be stated as a constraint without bringing the optimizer back in through the back door.

What I need from the proposal: a question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer in nine months and isn't just a proposal to clarify vocabulary; an honest account of why the two standard pictures leave that question open rather than already answering it; a method section saying what I'd actually do month by month (I'm open to a small formal toy model of sequential choice with an endogenous threshold, but also to purely conceptual work, and I'd like the proposal to pick one and justify it against the alternative); a description of what a positive result and a negative result would each look like, including what would count as the project failing; and a frank scope section, since I suspect my real ambition here is three projects and I need help cutting it down. Two audience constraints: one reader of the application works in the Simonian tradition and will want satisficing treated as a serious normative idea, and one is a decision theorist who will ask what the *result* is and will not be moved by terminology, so the framing has to be legible to both.
