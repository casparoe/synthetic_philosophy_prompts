I've ended up holding a fairly hard line on generational cohorts, and I'd like it stress-tested rather than supported. Here is the position.

Nothing in the vicinity of "Boomers", "Millennials" or "Gen Z" is a real kind. Shared formative experience is real — people who came of age during a particular war, depression, epidemic or technological transition can genuinely differ in durable ways — but the packaged units that journalists, marketers and pollsters use do not track those experiences. They are conveniences: coarse bins laid over continuous variation, with boundaries set by convention and then retro-fitted with a story about why the boundary falls there. On this view a cohort label is like "the top decile of earners": a serviceable device for some purposes, but not a division the world comes in independently of our having drawn it, and not something that earns its keep in a causal explanation once you already have a person's age, the calendar period, and the specific events that person actually lived through.

Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I have three stipulations.

First, don't just hand me the age–period–cohort identification problem as if it settled the matter; I know it, I grant it, and I want defeaters beyond it — plus an honest verdict on whether that problem is itself decisive here or only awkward for the kind of claim I'm making.

Second, one of the devices I lean on is a retrodiction test I made up: hand independent researchers behavioural, attitudinal and consumption data with no access to anyone's self-reported generation label, and ask where the boundaries fall. If the recovered cutoffs cluster near the conventional ones across several unrelated domains, I'll treat that as evidence the bins are finding something; if they don't, or if they cluster instead around identifiable historical events at dates the convention doesn't respect, that's evidence for me. Say whether that test is well designed, what it would and wouldn't show, and what a defender of my position should concede would count against them if it came out the wrong way — I want what my own side would grant, not only what a critic would say. A second device I use: the labels should travel. If cohort kinds were real, roughly comparable units ought to show up in countries that have no Anglophone generational vocabulary of this sort, and where they do show up they shouldn't be cut at different years with different content.

Third, I'm aware the view can be insulated: failed generational claims get absorbed by re-dating the boundaries, inventing micro-cohorts at the seams, or retreating to "it's social construction anyway." Assess how exposed the position really is once you allow those moves, and whether insulation of that kind is a defect for a claim in social ontology in the way it would be for a claim about chemical kinds — or whether, given that the target is partly constituted by our own labelling practices, some of that insulation is legitimate and my test is simply the wrong shape.

Where you cite a real-world pattern, study or statistic, say how confident you are that it is as you describe; I'd rather you flag uncertainty than produce an illustrative finding. Alongside the ranking, keep separate what is settled among people who work on social kinds and cohort methodology from what is genuinely contested there, and mark clearly where you are giving me your own judgment rather than a view with wide assent. For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is a case where a cohort term does explanatory work that age, period and event variables jointly cannot — not another debunking of some headline claim about Millennials, which refutes the claim without touching the ontology.
