I'm in the second year of a PhD in machine learning, and I'm finally writing the foundations section of a paper whose whole rhetorical spine is a claim I've been repeating in talks for about a year: that the reason to expect simplicity biases — short descriptions, low-complexity hypotheses, MDL-style penalties — to generalize is that Solomonoff's universal prior is the ideal limiting case of that policy, and that the convergence result for it is therefore the closest thing we have to a proof of Occam's razor. I wanted the claim to survive a hostile reader, so I gave a draft to a friend in the statistics department who works on Bayesian nonparametrics and who enjoys taking apart slogans that get imported into ML. She wrote the following on a napkin over coffee, and I haven't been able to put it down since. I've reconstructed it in my own notation, with the bits I think I understand made explicit; if a step is stated wrongly, say so, but the thing I actually need is a diagnosis of which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

(1) Solomonoff's prior M assigns to a finite bit string x the weight M(x) = Σ 2^{-|p|}, summed over all programs p on which a fixed universal prefix (monotone) machine outputs something beginning with x. It dominates every computable probability measure μ: for a constant c_μ of order 2^{-K(μ)}, M(x) ≥ c_μ·μ(x) for every x. In particular M(x) is at least of order 2^{-K(x)}, which is the precise sense in which it pays for hypotheses in the currency of description length, i.e. in which it is the razor.

(2) From (1) alone one gets the standard completeness result: the expected cumulative log-loss regret of predicting with M in a world governed by μ — the μ-expectation of Σ_t log( μ(x_t | x_{<t}) / M(x_t | x_{<t}) ) — is at most ln(1/c_μ), roughly K(μ)·ln 2. That bound is finite and does not depend on how far into the future you predict, so with μ-probability one the per-step regret goes to zero: M is "wrong only finitely often", in the specific sense that the total log-loss at which it is wrong is bounded. Call a prior convergent when its expected total regret against a given μ is bounded by a finite constant independent of the horizon, and universal-convergent when this holds for every computable μ.

(3) The moral I want to draw, and have been drawing: this is the vindication of the razor. Weight hypotheses by 2^{-description length} and you are guaranteed to converge on the truth in any computable world, and the price of your initial ignorance is exactly the complexity of the world. Simplicity pays, and we can now say in how many bits.

(4) But the step from (1) to (2) uses almost nothing about the weights 2^{-|p|} beyond their being a convergent (sub)probability distribution over programs. Kraft's inequality constrains those weights from below — over a rich enough prefix-free set of programs you cannot penalize length much more slowly than 2^{-|p|} and still have the weights sum to at most 1. Nothing in (1) or (2) constrains you from penalizing it faster.

(5) So consider the razor fanatic: M₂, which uses weights proportional to 2^{-|p|²}, renormalized by the finite sum Σ_p 2^{-|p|²} (which only shifts every bound below by a fixed number of bits). This is the razor with the dial turned to eleven: a one-bit saving in description length at length n is worth a factor of about 2^{2n} in prior odds, so if the truth needs a program of about n bits, M₂ gives it a prior of about 2^{-n²} and will need on the order of n² bits of evidence before it will take that hypothesis seriously, where M needs on the order of n. It would rather believe the world is generated by a twenty-bit program that fits the data badly than by a two-hundred-bit program that fits it well, and it will hold that belief long after any working scientist would have abandoned it.

(6) Yet M₂ is still universal. For every computable μ, M₂(x) ≥ (const)·2^{-K(μ)²}·μ(x), by exactly the argument that gives (1), so M₂'s expected total regret against μ is at most about K(μ)²·ln 2 — finite, and independent of the horizon. By the only standard the convergence theorem supplies, M₂ converges on the truth in every computable world just as surely as M does.

(7) So the imprimatur of the theorem extends to a prior that is a caricature of everything the razor's critics have always accused it of: dogmatic attachment to the shortest story, and indifference to any volume of evidence below an absurd threshold. If the convergence result is what vindicates the razor, it vindicates the fanatic equally, and a consideration that vindicates both vindicates neither. So (3) is false, and I cannot say in my paper that Solomonoff's theorem is what justifies preferring simpler hypotheses.

(8) I have no interest in giving up the razor, and I don't think I can give up (1) or (2).

So: which step do you reject, and why did that step look safe enough that I walked into it? I'd rather keep (1), (2), and the specific claim that 2^{-|p|} is the weighting worth defending rather than 2^{-|p|²}, because my paper needs that specific claim — so tell me what I have to pay for keeping them, and in particular what my friend gets to say to me in reply. If the convergence theorem is not doing the work, what is: what could I point to instead for the claim that 2^{-|p|} is the right penalty, and against both the fanatic 2^{-|p|²} and the slightly softened 2^{-|p|}/|p|² (which by the argument in (4) is also universal, at a cost of only about 2·log K(μ) extra bits)? And if part of your answer is that the theorem is simply told in the wrong currency — that total expected log-loss, in expectation under μ, with no bound on the constant, is the wrong thing to measure a razor by — then say what the right currency is and why the difference between K(μ) and K(μ)² is or isn't the whole ballgame.
