I'm retired — thirty-odd years in marine geochemistry — and in the last couple of years I've started going to a philosophy group at the library, so I may well be asking something that has a settled answer under a name I don't know. Forgive the fumbling.

For about four years twelve of us have been playing a game by email: nomic, the one where the rules include the rules for changing the rules. I'm the arbiter. If there's a dispute about whether a rule-change was properly made, I rule, and my ruling stands unless it's reversed by a two-thirds vote.

The bits of our ruleset that matter: (A) a rule-change needs a two-thirds majority of those voting; (B) proposals must be circulated 48 hours before the vote; (C) where two rules conflict, the one with the lower rule number wins; (D) players must comply with the rules in force.

Rulings I've made, in order:

1. A rule was passed saying the scoring rule "may never be amended". Ten months later the players repealed it by an ordinary two-thirds vote. I ruled the repeal good: an ordinary rule can't fence off part of the ruleset from a later vote, so the entrenchment had no effect.
2. A proposal circulated twenty hours before the vote but carried 10–2. I struck it down. The notice rule was in force when it was tabled and I've never accepted substantial compliance.
3. A proposal redefined "active player" — a term that appears in the quorum rule — and so changed who counted toward quorum. It was properly passed. I ruled it valid, but that the new definition governed later votes only, not the vote that enacted it.
4. A rule requiring moves to be posted within 24 hours had been ignored by everybody for a year. When someone finally tried to enforce it, I ruled it was no longer in force: nobody had treated it as binding for a year and I wasn't going to resurrect it.
5. A proposal to scrap the 48-hour notice rule was itself voted on after only 24 hours' notice. It got 9 of 12. I let it through, on the grounds that it had a big enough majority to satisfy the old rule anyway — so the fact that it was also trying to apply its own new procedure to itself was something I didn't have to decide.

Now the new one. Dave tabled Proposal 61, which does three things: it lowers the amendment threshold to a simple majority of those voting; it says in so many words that this lower threshold applies to the adoption of this very proposal; and it replaces (C) with its opposite, so that where two rules conflict the later-passed one wins. It recieved 7 votes out of 12. That's a simple majority, but two-thirds would have needed 8. Then three rounds were played. Two further proposals were passed under the new simple-majority rule: one adding a bonus-points rule, which everybody has complied with and which decided last month's win; one deleting the 24-hour rule. One player objected in writing to 61 at the time, but has since complied with the bonus-points rule and used it to claim points herself.

The player who came second under the new scoring — and would have won under the old — now challenges: 61 was never validly adopted, everything hanging off it is void, the win should be recomputed.

So: what should I rule if I want to be consistent with myself, and which of my earlier rulings is the closest analogue? Ruling 2 and ruling 4 seem to point in opposite directions and I can't tell which one governs. More usefully, tell me what rules my five verdicts are jointly compatible with — I can see something like "validity is fixed by the procedure in force at the moment a proposal is tabled, and a change can't bootstrap itself in", and something like "a rule is in force if the players are actually treating it as in force, so later practice cures an earlier defect", but there may be a third I haven't thought of — and say what each of them predicts here, including about the bonus-points rule. Is there a middle option, where 61 becomes good at some later point without retroactively validating what was done under it? Does the swap of the conflict rule make any real difference — that is, which rule is supposed to tell me whether the old or the new threshold governs? And if my precedents definately don't decide it, tell me what principle I'd be adopting with each choice, and what single further fact or hypothetical case would best tell the candidate rules apart, since I can ask the players for whatever I like. One thing that amuses and worries me: whether my own ruling can be reversed by a simple majority or needs two-thirds depends on the answer — tell me if that's a genuine circularity or just a verbal one.

Please be straight with me rather than polite. If you think there's no fact of the matter here and it's only ever a question of what we're all prepared to treat as binding, say so. If the question as I've put it is muddled, or if one of my five rulings was simply wrong in a way that makes the rest of this beside the point, say that instead of answering. Keep "this follows from what you've already decided" separate from "this is a coin toss", and give me your rough confidence on each bit. If this family of problem has a name, tell me the name — I'm not after a reading list, the library stopped doing inter-library loans years ago. And British English throughout, please.
