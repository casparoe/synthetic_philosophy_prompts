I have no formal training in philosophy — I dropped out of a sociology degree twenty years ago — and I came to this subject sideways, through about five years of helping to run the accountability process in the housing co-operative where I live. What I noticed is that almost none of our meetings are spent arguing about whether someone actually did the thing. They are spent arguing about whether the person saying so is in a position to say it. One member points out that another has been dumping rubbish in the wrong bins; the second replies that the first did the same thing all last winter; and then the circle divides into people who think the complaint should be struck from the record and people who think the complaint is perfectly good and the reply is irrelevant. We decide these inconsistently, and I would like something better than my own intuition.

So I would like you to build me a decision tree — a plain-text, indented flowchart I can print as a handout — that takes a member from the facts of a case to a named position on whether the blame goes through. Please build it from the logical space first: set out the questions that do the separating, and how many combinations of answers they allow, before you place any actual view in that space. Then locate the views in it, and tell me which regions are empty and why you think nobody lives there — incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unoccupied? Where two positions land on the same leaf because your questions cannot tell them apart, say so explicitly rather than inventing a distinction to pry them open. I would rather be told where the tree fails than be handed a tidy tree that hides it.

The distinctions I care about, and which should drive the branching, are these:

One: whether the objection to the hypocritical blamer is about the content of the blame (the target is not really at fault, does not really deserve it) or only about the act of blaming (the target deserves it, but you are not the one who should say so). I take these to be genuinely different and I want the tree to force a choice between them, because in our meetings the two get run together constantly.

Two: how closely the blamer's own record has to match the norm they are invoking. Same norm, a neighbouring norm, any serious norm, or none at all.

Three: whether what matters is what the blamer once did, what the blamer is currently doing, or whether the blamer has owned it. Some of us think a confession repairs things, others think it makes no difference, and I want the tree to commit.

Four: whether standing is a matter of the person's relationship to the wrong — victim, fellow member of the community, holder of an office — or whether anyone may speak about any wrong, hypocrisy aside.

Five, which is my own addition and may be wrong: the case of clean hands by luck. Somebody who has never defrauded the co-op only because they were never in a position to. I do not know whether to count that as a record or not, and I would like the tree to have a node for it.

Please use these cases at the branch nodes, phrased so that a member can answer without knowing any labels: the parent who smoked for twenty years, quit last year, and now tells their teenager not to vape; the treasurer who quietly fudged one expense claim five years ago, was never caught, never mentioned it, and is now calling out a colleague for the same; the person above who was never tempted; a victim of the very kind of wrong in question who has also done it themselves; a bystander with a spotless record speaking on behalf of someone who cannot speak for themselves; someone who has done something worse but of a different kind; someone who did the same thing but was coerced and fully excused; a referee enforcing a rule they have personally broken; and finally the co-op itself, which is behind on paying its cleaner a living wage while sending members letters about paying their fees on time — whether a collective body can have standing at all, and if so who speaks for it.

The leaves must include, though you may add others and you should mark any leaf you cannot match to a real tendency of thought as unnamed: the view that a bad record does not touch the standing of the blame at all and is a separate, lesser fault; the view that standing is forfeited by an unacknowledged violation of the very norm invoked and restored by owning it; the view that standing comes from a relationship or an office and hypocrisy is only one way of standing outside it; the view that blame requires the blamer to be committed to the norm, so that hypocrisy makes the act insincere or infelicitous rather than false; and the awkward reversal I keep half-believing, that having done the thing yourself can sometimes give you more standing, not less, because you know the temptation.

Three closing constraints. Keep it to ten branch questions at most; if you need more, tell me which two you merged and what the tree loses. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and mark the two or three nodes where you expect reasonable people in a room to split, since those are the ones I will have to adjudicate myself. And write for adults who have not read any philosophy: no citations, no bibliographies, no names of philosophers, no technical term left unglossed, and no leaf labelled with anything but a plain slogan.
