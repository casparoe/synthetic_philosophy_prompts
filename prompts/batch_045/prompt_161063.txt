I am redesigning the first session of a third-year undergraduate seminar on truth — the students have a first course in logic and one in metaphysics, but no prior reading on truth specifically — and I want to replace my usual handout with an instrument they have to operate rather than read. What I have in mind is a dichotomous identification key in botanical style: numbered couplets, each offering two descriptions and ending in a cross-reference ("go to 4" or "go to 7"), such that a student working alone in about fifteen minutes arrives either at a named position or at an honest verdict that no one occupies that part of the space.

The organising idea I want to impose is my own, and I would rather it drove the structure than the usual list of —isms. I take a theory of truth to be hired for up to four jobs, and I want the opening couplets to sort students by which jobs they think a theory of truth must discharge:

(i) the generalisation job — making sense of blind and infinite generalisation and semantic ascent, as in "everything she said was true" where the sentences cannot be listed;
(ii) the semantic job — supplying truth-conditions that do the work of fixing what sentences mean;
(iii) the normative job — explaining why truth is a norm of belief, assertion and inquiry;
(iv) the success job — explaining why acting on true beliefs tends to go well.

So the question "is truth a substantive property?" should come only after the job profile is fixed; in my experience, students who start there merely report a pre-theoretical hunch and then rationalise it. If one of my four jobs turns out not to be a job that positions actually differ over — if every view can discharge it — say so and demote it rather than building a couplet on it.

Nodes below that should separate positions by what they say about cases and claims, never by the labels they like: no couplet may contain a technical term on the answering side, and no node may ask anything of the form "are you a deflationist?". Three cases must appear as, or inside, couplets: blind generalisation over an unenumerable set; a truth attribution to sentences in a language we cannot yet translate; and a truth claim in a domain where truth seems to do more than disquote — moral, aesthetic or comic discourse is what I have in mind, though use whatever does the work best.

I need between twelve and fourteen leaves, and these must be among them: substantive correspondence realism; coherentism; pragmatism; the epistemic view in its superassertibility form; minimalism; deflationism of the no-property variety; prosententialism; expressivism about truth; primitivism; alethic pluralism or functionalism about truth; and the eliminative or "there is no question here" option. Add further leaves only if the logical space demands them. Each leaf should carry its name, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and — this is the point of the exercise for me — a note on which of the four jobs it discharges easily, which it discharges only with strain, and which it cannot discharge at all.

Two further things. First, the space your couplets define will contain combinations that no named position occupies; I want at least four of these flagged explicitly as cells, each with a one-line diagnosis of why nobody is there — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Second, the trap I most want beaten is the students' assumption that deflationism amounts to saying truth does not matter; the key should separate the thin-property deflationary leaves from each other and from eliminativism, and if two leaves genuinely cannot be separated by any couplet you can write, flag the pair and say what further question would separate them, if any can.

Please set the key out as a numbered sequence of couplets with go-to cross-references, preceded by a short paragraph of instructions to the student and followed by a table of the leaves. Write the answer in British English, and do not include a reading list or any bibliographic references.
