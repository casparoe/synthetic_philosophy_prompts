Posting this after yet another interminable argument in the LessWrong-adjacent group chat I've been lurking in since ~2015 — the one where everyone is a Bayesian and also, mysteriously, everyone is a physicist. Whenever "is the wavefunction actually real?" comes up, the house view is basically: of course not, it's just a probability distribution. The move everyone reaches for is the coin one: before you look at a flipped coin you assign 50/50, nothing physical is 50/50, when you peek your credence "collapses" and that's just conditioning, nothing happened to the coin. So: superposition = ordinary uncertainty about an already-settled outcome, collapse = updating your map, the measurement problem is dissolved rather than solved, no need for Many Worlds or for a real dynamical collapse mechanism, and anyone saying the quantum state is a physical thing smeared over 3N-dimensional configuration space is committing the mind projection fallacy. I've used this myself a bunch and it still feels obviously right, which is exactly why I want it audited.

What I want is the correspondence table first — coin → system, heads/tails → what, 50/50 credence → what, peeking → measurement, the second person in the room who also peeks → ?, "how the coin was flipped" → preparation — and then the places the mapping breaks along with a blunt verdict on which of those breaks are actually load-bearing for the conclusion we're using it for. My candidate list, please clean up whatever I've garbled: (a) probabilities add and amplitudes add, and amplitudes cancel, so the wavefunction isn't a probability distribution over pre-existing values and interference in an ordinary two-slit setup is the everyday evidence; (b) for a coin, two agents' distributions can overlap — you assign 50/50, I peeked and assign 100/0, both are descriptions of the same coin — whereas there's a no-go result I half-remember (Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph, 2012?) showing that distinct pure states can't correspond to overlapping distributions over the underlying ontic states without getting the Born-rule probabilities wrong, which if right makes the quantum state maximally informative about whatever is real rather than a coarse-grained summary of it; (c) for a coin there's a joint distribution over everything you could ask about, but non-commuting observables in QM don't have one, and formalisms that write one down anyway assign negative "probabilities"; (d) Wigner's-friend-style setups where two agents both apply nothing but "update your beliefs" and end up assigning each other certainty about incompatible outcomes.

The bit I actually care about is the rhetorical payload: does "the wavefunction is just a credence" survive as a way of dissolving the measurement problem, or is the coin analogy smuggling in the very thing at issue — that there's a single settled outcome there before anybody looks, and that finding out is non-disturbing — and quietly doing all the work? And if the coin is the wrong source object, what's the least bad replacement: something like Spekkens' toy model, where an epistemic state is constrained by a knowledge-balance principle and you get interference, non-commutativity and teleportation out of pure ignorance, or is the honest answer that any analogy drawn from macroscopic uncertainty fails in the same spot? Keep it under 700 words including the table. No citations needed, I just want the argument.
