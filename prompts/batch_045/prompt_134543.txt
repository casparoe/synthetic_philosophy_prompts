I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and for the last two I've run a small in-person study group — six of us, all comfortable with statistical mechanics at roughly the level of a first graduate course, none of us a specialist in the foundations of thermal physics. Our session on Maxwell's demon went badly, and I think I know why. Each of us arrived with a one-line exorcism: the demon settles its account when it erases its records; the demon is simply a heat engine running on information as its working substance; no entropy really drops, only the demon's ignorance does; there is no single exorcism, each gadget fails for its own local reason. Each of those one-liners quietly packs in three or four logically independent commitments, so we were agreeing and disagreeing in a different order than we thought.

What I want from you is a sorting instrument, not an essay. Please build me a decision tree that takes a member of the group from "there is a box of gas at temperature T and a device that appears to cool it for free" to a named position, and build it from the logical space rather than from a catalogue of names: start with the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space, and mark the cells nobody occupies, with one line each on whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Ten to twelve internal nodes and a matching number of leaves — merge rather than pad if the branching runs away from you. Every node is a single question, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which leaf it leads to. A node is disqualified if only someone who knew the labels could answer it: "is entropy ontic or epistemic?" fails, whereas "in the one-molecule engine, once you have charged the account for inserting and removing the partition, is there any net gain left to explain?" is what I'm after.

The splits I want doing the work are: (i) where the bill is paid — in acquiring the information, in storing or resetting it, in consuming correlations the setup already contained, or nowhere, because the setup never offered a free lunch; (ii) whether the entropy in the ledger is a property of the gas or a property of the demon's information about the gas, i.e. whether the puzzle is solved or dissolved; (iii) whether the bound on erasing a bit is a theorem of statistical mechanics, an independent postulate about logically irreversible operations, a contingent fact about the environments we happen to have, or something reversible computation can route around; (iv) whether the device's limit is principled or merely practical — it runs until the tape, the memory, or the correlations give out; (v) whether there is one exorcism every demon must pay or a family of mechanisms that fail one at a time.

Every case below has to appear somewhere in the tree, either as a node or as the illustration of a leaf: the one-molecule engine with the partition's insertion and removal counted; a demon with a finite tape it never erases, appending records until the tape runs out; a demon handed a pre-randomized tape, so that writing a record looks locally free; a demon whose entire computation is logically reversible and which simply halts when its memory fills; a device coupled to two baths that sorts with a tape, with no measurement step and no erasure step anywhere in its cycle; a demon that exploits a pre-existing temperature or correlation asymmetry without measuring anything; a quantum version in which the device has coherence or entanglement available instead of a record; a purely mechanical ratchet and pawl whose pawl sits in the same bath as the gas; a demon in a finite universe that eventually runs out of room for records; and a lazy demon that opens its door on a fixed schedule, using no information at all.

For each leaf give a descriptive label of your own, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and — only where you are actually confident — the conventional name in parentheses; write "unnamed" otherwise. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and say whether some further question would separate them or whether they are one position in two costumes. Note that I came in sympathetic to the "entropy is missing information" reading that circulates in this corner of the internet; I would rather be sorted than confirmed, so if that reading collapses into another leaf somewhere, tell me where. No citations, page numbers, or reading list — we have no library access and I don't want guesses.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: the one structural claim the tree rests on, meaning which split does the real work and which apparently deep disagreement turns out to be verbal or to collapse on inspection. Then give the tree, then the notes, with no preamble and no restating of what I've asked for. Close with two sentences — how confident you are in the instrument as a whole, and the single node, leaf, or collapse you are least confident about.
