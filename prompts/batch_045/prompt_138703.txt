I am drafting the opening chapter of a book manuscript on the metaphysics of persons, and I have found that I cannot get anywhere until I fix what I am asking of a theory in this area. Every dispute I read seems to be a dispute about which datum a theory is permitted to ignore. So before I assess any candidate, I want the specification it will be assessed against: what a theory of the self has to be able to do in order to count as adequate.

Fix the purpose first, since adequacy is relative to it. The theory I need must do two jobs at once. First, it must underwrite diachronic moral responsibility: the practice of holding this person answerable now for what was done years ago, and of excusing them when the link has failed. Second, it must underwrite prudential concern: explain what it is about a future person that makes their having a headache a reason for me now to take an aspirin, and why that reason does not extend with equal force to everyone else's future headaches. A theory that does one job and is silent on the other is inadequate for my purposes, and I would like you to say whether those two jobs can in fact be served by a single account or whether I have illegitimately bundled them.

Here are the fixed points I take a theory to have to accommodate. Turn them into requirements, and give me the case or consideration behind each requirement rather than a bare list.

1. *The archivist*: someone with exhaustive episodic recall of their own past who organizes none of it into a story, feels no ownership of the recalled events, and lives entirely from moment to moment.
2. *The repudiator*: someone whose later self-narrative disowns everything earlier — a conversion, a political reversal, a slowly accumulated change of character — and who is genuinely the continuer of the earlier person's memories, habits and projects while repudiating them.
3. *The ghostwritten*: someone whose self-narrative is coherent, socially ratified, and largely authored by others — family stories, an institution, a culture's script — with no point at which they can be said to have originated it.
4. *The silent*: an infant, or an adult with no language or no narrative capacity at all.
5. *The interrupted*: a decade of dense amnesia, anterograde and retrograde, from which the person then emerges; the biological life is unbroken and the narrative is not.
6. *The shared*: one organism, two apparent narrative centers.
7. *The divergent*: two narrative centers that separate from a common origin gradually, with no moment of division and no fact of the matter about when there ceased to be one.
8. *Mine-ness*: the requirement must be stated without helping itself to *me*, *I*, or *my* — the theory has to earn the pronoun, not presuppose it.
9. *The appearing subject*: experience presents itself as belonging to a single, simple, persisting subject, and this presentation is itself something the theory owes an account of, whether by vindicating or by explaining it away.
10. *Being wrong about oneself*: someone who sincerely narrates themselves as cowardly and reliably acts bravely. There must be something for the narrative to get wrong.

Add whatever cases you think I have omitted that would generate requirements I have not listed.

I also want your view on which of the following are genuine desiderata and which are merely the prejudices of one camp: that the theory return a determinate yes-or-no for every case; that it withhold selves from no creature we intuitively grant one; that it be reductive, leaving no residue subject, homunculus, or owner of the narrative; that it be empirically respectable, given that no anatomical seat of the self has been isolated and that people confabulate motives and memories under prompting; that it vindicate rather than revise ordinary self-talk; and that it leave the self as something we can author, so that self-creation is possible rather than merely self-discovery.

Two things matter most to me. First, tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — if the list itself is a result, that is the result I want, and I want to know which requirement you would drop first and what dropping it costs. Second, tell me which single requirement excludes the largest number of otherwise attractive candidates.

Only then, briefly, and in a few lines each: score Dennett's metaphor of the self as a center of narrative gravity, the neo-Lockean psychological continuity view, animalism, the minimal or pre-reflective phenomenal self, and the self-as-model picture against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria — I know the contenders well enough to do the scoring myself, and I want the spec to be one that the narrative-gravity view could genuinely fail rather than one gerrymandered to protect or to sink it.

Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or negotiable, and say how confident you are in that marking; name the one you are least sure belongs on the list at all and why. If my framing is confused — if the two jobs I have fixed cannot be served by one theory, if the cases above smuggle in an answer, or if some requirement is not the kind of thing a theory can be asked to deliver — say so plainly instead of producing a polite list. And please argue from the cases themselves: I do not want references, a reading list, or a bibliography, and I do not want any point carried by an appeal to what some author said.
