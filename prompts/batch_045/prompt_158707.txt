I'm a philosophy PhD student working on population ethics and longtermism, and I want a piece of satire to circulate among the graduate students in my department's informal reading group on the far future. Please write a 600-700 word parody: an internal memo from the research committee of a large longtermist foundation, addressed to its program officers, announcing a reprioritization after the committee provisionally accepts that we are almost certainly living in an ancestor simulation.

The reasoning should be deadpan and technically straight-faced: the simulation's computing budget bounds how many people there will ever be, and a simulation with a research question gets terminated once that question is answered, so the expected value of safeguarding the far future drops by many orders of magnitude and the committee reallocates away from existential-risk reduction. The joke should be that the fanatical structure survives the cosmology change untouched: the memo should discover a new lever with the same tiny-probability/astronomical-payoff shape — keeping the simulation interesting enough that its operators do not shut it down, or improving our chances of being re-run on a larger cluster — and should pursue it using exactly the quantitative apparatus it announces it has retired. Include at least one piece of deadpan accounting (a probability with far too many significant figures, a "shut-down hazard rate," a footnote that raises the possibility that we are copied many times over and then quietly sets it aside), and end on a recommendation that is plainly fanatical but presented as the sober, moderate option.

Format it as a memo: To/From/Date/Re, then a few headed sections and optionally a small table. The target is the register of sober cost-effectiveness talk, not any person; give the foundation an invented name. It should land for someone who has read the fanaticism literature and still be readable to someone who knows the simulation argument only from a newspaper article.

Below the memo, in about 100 words, add a note on where the piece takes liberties: which premise it leans on hardest, and what would make the satire unfair — in particular, say what would follow if the default effect of simulation considerations is amplification rather than dampening, and whether anything in the memo depends on a contested reading of the argument.
