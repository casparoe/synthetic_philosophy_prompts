hey — I'm taking a philosophy of AI seminar this semester and we just spent two weeks on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence, and I came out of it feeling like everyone in the room was nodding along to the same slogans while meaning different things by them. My half-baked way of splitting it up: call O1 the design-space claim — for any level of general intelligence and any final goal, there is some possible agent with that level and that goal, i.e. intelligence places no constraint on final-goal content. O2 is the diachronic or reflective version: take an agent that already has a final goal, then let it get smarter, model itself better, reflect on why it wants what it wants, and maybe self-modify — is there pressure for the goal to drift, and does that pressure get stronger the smarter it gets? O3 is the distributional one, which is the one I actually care about for practical reasons: in the space of agents that real selection processes actually spit out (gradient descent on some objective, market competition, cultural/evolutionary filtering, whatever), is there a correlation between capability and goal-content? O1 could be true while O3 is false, if the processes that make something smart systematically also make it want particular things.

The second thing that bugged me in section: the standard argument for convergent instrumental subgoals (self-preservation, acquiring resources, resisting goal modification, and so on) seems to quietly assume the agent knows its own final goal. But plenty of the systems we'd actually build are uncertain about what they want — trained on messy human feedback, still being corrected, still sorting out their own objectives — and under goal uncertainty I think the argument changes shape: if you don't know what you'll turn out to want, hoarding resources isn't obviously convergent in the same way, but preserving *optionality* — keeping your future self able to pursue a wide range of goals — might well be. So maybe the real convergent drive isn't power-as-resources but power-as-optionality, and those come apart (an agent that could lock in one goal and cash out now versus one that keeps its options open). Call that the optionality reading of instrumental convergence.
