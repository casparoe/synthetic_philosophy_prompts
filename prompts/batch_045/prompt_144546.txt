**Slurs, for people who write regexes**

**1. The badness isn't in the string.** Our first pass was a pattern match over a word list. It failed in both directions: it fired on "the word ____ shows up forty times in this transcript," and it stayed silent for ____ used warmly inside the group it targets. Same bytes, opposite effects. So whatever the offensive thing is, it isn't a property of the character sequence. It's a property of an event: somebody using the string, at somebody, in some context.

**2. And it's not an inference the hearer makes, either.** This is the part I'm most confident about and least able to defend. Take a conditional: "If anyone on this team is a ____, it's Dave." Nothing has been asserted about Dave. The sentence still lands as an insult to Dave and to the group. Same with questions, with negation, and with "he's not a ____, he's a ____." The offense climbs out of whatever you embed it in instead of staying where a normal implication would stay. That reads to me like it isn't derived from what was said plus the context; it's attached to the act of saying it, like a side effect at the call site. Think of a deprecated function: calling it emits a warning no matter what you do with the return value, and wrapping the call in a conditional doesn't suppress the warning.

**3. My analogy has a hole I can't patch: masking.** "F____" or "the n-word" often *does* feel less bad, sometimes a lot less. If the offense lives in the act and not the letters, censoring the letters shouldn't change anything. Sometimes it doesn't — everyone knows what you meant — and sometimes it clearly does. I don't know what variable I'm missing. My best guess: the masking is itself an act, a visible withholding, and what people react to is *that*, not the letters.

**4. Where I think the writing I've bumped into is aimed at the wrong cases.** Most of it seems built around slurs that have a clean, agreed-on neutral counterpart: a slur for group G, a boring descriptive phrase for G, and the puzzle is what the slur adds on top. A lot of what hits our queue isn't shaped like that. Three buckets:

- **Type A:** a stable neutral counterpart exists. The textbook case.
- **Type B:** the neutral counterpart is contested and moving. The group doesn't agree on its own label, subgroups want different words, and last decade's polite term is this decade's slur. Here the offense is partly a fight over which description is even on the table.
- **Type C:** no neutral counterpart, because the slur doesn't pick out a group so much as gerrymander one — it builds or inflates the category it insults. There's no descriptive content to hand back, because there's no shared concept to hand back.

Hunch: a theory that nails Type A can be completely silent on B and C, and B and C are where the actual damage happens.

**5. The bit that matters for my job: derogation needs somebody to pin it on.** A string sitting in a dictionary, a corpus, or a linguistics example isn't derogating anyone. The identical string inside quoted dialogue, attributed to a character, is. So "who is speaking, and are they endorsing it" isn't metadata we bolt on afterward — it's an input to what the utterance even is. Which is why model output is such a strange case: there's a speaker-shaped thing (the assistant persona) and no endorsement in either direction.
