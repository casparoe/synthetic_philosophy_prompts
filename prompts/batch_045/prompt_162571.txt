I teach a graduate seminar on concepts and categorization, and for a case study I have been collecting the verdicts of a professional type designer on whether particular marks count as instances of the lowercase letter a. Her yeses so far: the two-storey a of a Venetian old-style face; the single-storey a of a geometric sans; the a of a blackletter textura that none of my students can identify unaided; a stencil a whose strokes are cut so that nothing in the glyph is fully enclosed; an a reduced to four pixels in a bitmap font, illegible even to her; and a Cyrillic a set in a Russian word, which she accepts on the grounds that she is judging the letterform rather than the character. Her noes: Greek lowercase alpha, the schwa, the at-sign, and a designer's prank in which the glyph mapped to the a key is drawn as a plain equilateral triangle.

The case I need help with comes from a rain-damaged playbill in a local archive: in the word CABARET the third letter survives only as a closed oval with a stub of a stem descending from its right side, roughly a fifth of the length of the stems in the intact letters around it. Set in the word, everyone reads it as an a; shown to my students in isolation, most call it an o.

Given her track record, what will she say about this mark? I am less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule she seems to be running: resemblance to a stored prototype above some threshold; an abstract relational structure of parts (bowl, stem, orientation, no extra appendages) with tolerances; the role the mark plays in a writing system or the reading it elicits in a competent reader; or descent from earlier a's, whether from the historical letter or merely from a prior token of that shape. Tell me which of these fit her earlier verdicts equally well but come apart on this one, what each predicts here, which verdict you would bet on and why, and which single further case would most cleanly separate the rules that survive.
