I'm not a physicist — I make maps for a living (GIS, mostly), so I spend a lot of my day thinking about how much of a coordinate grid is actually out there in the world and how much is just something we lay over the territory. Somehow that got me into Einstein's hole argument, and I think I have the shape of it, but I keep loosing the thread right at the point where it gets interesting. So: what is that argument actually an argument about?

The version I can reconstruct goes like this. You take a region of spacetime with no matter in it — the "hole" — and you reassign the points inside it, sliding them around by a smooth map that's the identity everywhere outside, dragging all the fields along with them. Because the equations are generally covariant, what you get is another perfectly good solution: one that agrees with the original everywhere outside the hole but disagrees inside it. And if the hole sits entirely to the future of some starting slice that the two solutions agree on completely, then it looks like the theory hands you two different futures for exactly the same past. The usual reply, as I understand it, is that these aren't two situations at all: the reassignment is just a relabeling, and both solutions are the same physical situation written down twice. (I also never know whether people mean the active or the passive version of the sliding, and whether that distinction is doing any work here.) Tell me where I've garbled it, because I'm sure I have.

Three things I specifically can't straighten out.

1. Everything I've read insists this is not just Leibniz's old "shift the whole world three feet east" argument wearing a costume, and I can't say clearly why not. The shift argument also seems to hand you two models that differ only by a relabeling — so why does one of them threaten determinism while the other only threatens our ability to tell where we are? Is the difference just that the hole version is local and doesn't need space to have any symmetries, or is something else doing the work? Relatedly: in the version I can reconstruct, the charge is only that the outside of the hole doesn't fix the inside, which sounds weaker than "the past doesn't fix the future." Are those the same complaint, or is one of them the real argument and the other a rhetorical upgrade?

2. I tried to kill the argument by bolting down furniture, and the results made no sense to me. Toy world: a featureless plane, one field (call it warmth, a number at every point), one law — the warmth has to vary smoothly, no jumps. Fine, the argument gets its grip there. Now add, as fixed non-dynamical structure that the law never mentions, a single marked point: a "home point." My first thought was that this pins things down, since any reassignment has to leave the home point where it is. But then I noticed you can swirl the points around inside the hole while leaving the home point fixed and everything outside untouched, and you get the problem right back. Whereas if I glue down graph paper — a fixed background geometry that the law is then written in terms of, so that the warmth has to satisfy, say, the flat Laplace equation with distances supplied by the paper — then sliding the field around changes how it sits relative to the paper, stops producing solutions, and the whole thing evaporates. So what's the actual principle? Is it how much fixed stuff there is, or whether the law is allowed to refer to it, or whether the stuff is "rigid" in the sense that nothing non-trivial can move while it stays put? What's the least you'd have to bolt down, and why is one marked point worth nothing while a whole background geometry is worth everything?

3. Is my toy just rigging the game? With "the field is smooth" as your only law, of course nothing determines the warmth inside the hole — but that's because the law is pathetically weak, not because of anything about relabeling. So what does the hole argument need from the laws? Does it need them to be strong and deterministic-looking (as Einstein's surely are, on the naive reading), and if so, how am I supposed to see that the argument isn't secretly just the observation that a law stated without reference to fixed background structure can't say which point is which?

If the upshot is that this was never really about determinism but about whether spacetime points have an identity of their own, say that plainly — and then explain what the determinism framing was doing in the story in the first place. Go ahead and use words like substantivalism, diffeomorphism and gauge, but tell me in plain terms what each one buys you inside the argument.
