I'm taking an upper-level seminar this term on comparative societal collapse — we've worked through the palace economies of the Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui — and I keep coming back to something our TA said in section: that "collapse" is less a description of what happened than a report on who stopped writing things down, and that in each case what actually ended was a particular institutional arrangement (palace redistribution, divine kingship, monument-building) rather than a population. My econ-major roommate thinks the whole subject is just Malthusian overshoot with extra steps. I want to write something that makes both of those positions look silly without ever arguing against them directly.

The piece: a deadpan press release of roughly 700–800 words, issued by a fictional intergovernmental continuity body — the Interim Secretariat for Continuity, or whatever better name you come up with — announcing that "collapse" has been retired as a diagnostic category and will no longer appear in official reporting, replaced by something like "unscheduled institutional restructuring" or "complexity right-sizing." Give it the real shape of an institutional announcement: a dateline, an opening paragraph of bloodless reassurance, a numbered or bulleted schedule of "reclassified episodes" covering the Aegean and Near Eastern palace systems around 1200 BCE, the southern Maya lowlands in the Terminal Classic, and Rapa Nui, plus at least one quoted spokesperson and a closing line about continuing confidence in the underlying population.

The constraint I care about most is that the humor comes from getting the archaeology right rather than from inventing absurdity. The jokes should be recognizably riding on features of the actual debates: that we call these moments collapses because what stopped was the stuff we use as evidence — literacy and archives, dated monumental inscriptions, long-distance trade in prestige goods — that the same moments can look like an improvement for the people who had been supporting those institutions, that the term is hopelessly vague about threshold and timescale (how much demographic loss counts, over what interval, whether the abandonment of a center counts as the abandonment of a region), and that what follows is often dispersed settlement, ironworking, and alphabetic scripts rather than an empty landscape. An archaeologist should recognize the critique and find it fair, and should be able to tell what is being ironized without the piece ever breaking character: no winking, no explicit statement that collapse narratives are elite-biased, no punchline keyed to a single adverb. And the Rapa Nui entry should not sneer at the islanders — the target is the discourse of collapse, not the people inside it.
