I am revising a paper on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and before I build the rest of the paper around a single line of argument I want that argument stated as strongly and as carefully as it can be stated. Please write the argumentative core of the paper: continuous prose, 900 to 1,100 words, with three or four short section headings. Write for a reader who already knows the basic paradox but has not thought hard about how to answer it, so you may spend a couple of sentences on orientation and no more. Hard constraints: no citations, no bibliography, no footnotes, and no appeals to what any named philosopher has argued or would say. Every claim must be carried by the reasoning itself. State each formal assumption explicitly at the point where you use it, and flag any step that depends on a modal principle stronger than the weakest system your argument needs.

Here is the set-up, in my own notation. Let K be a knowledge operator read as someone knows at some time that, and let the diamond be metaphysical possibility. The knowability principle is the schema p implies possibly-Kp, for every proposition p. Assume that factivity (Kp implies p) and distribution over conjunction (K of a conjunction implies the conjunction of the Ks) both hold, and hold of necessity, as constitutive truths about knowledge. Now substitute q and not-Kq for p. If q and not-Kq is true, the knowability principle gives possibly-K of (q and not-Kq); distribution gives possibly-(Kq and K-not-Kq); factivity applied to not-Kq gives possibly-(Kq and not-Kq); and factivity applied to q rules that out. So q and not-Kq is false, hence q implies Kq, and since q was arbitrary, every truth is known. Somewhere in that chain of five steps a friend of the knowability principle has to intervene.

The thesis I want you to defend is this: the paradox is not really a refutation of verificationism by reduction to absurdity, and it is not really solved by restricting the knowability principle either. It is better read as a dilemma that closes the two routes the verificationist cares about, and the argument should make the closing of each route explicit.

First horn: the restrictionist route. The most common response is to restrict the schema to some privileged class of propositions, for instance the non-modal, the non-epistemic, the observationally decidable, or the atomic and their negations. Argue that this route is dialectically unavailable to the verificationist rather than merely inelegant. The reason to give is not that restriction is ad hoc in general but that this particular restriction is self-defeating for this particular theorist: a verificationist account of what it is to grasp the meaning of a sentence is supposed to cover every sentence of the language, including sentences about what is known, and the construction only ever uses negation, conjunction, and the knowledge operator itself. So any class that is expressively adequate for the verificationist's own semantics is a class in which the construction can be run. Make this precise by saying what closure conditions on the restricted class suffice for the derivation to go through, and then say what a verificationist would have to show to establish that their favoured class lacks those conditions.

Second horn: the operator route. Grant that the only remaining place to intervene is factivity, and argue that factivity is not an optional extra that can be traded away. Suppose we replace K with a non-factive surrogate V, say there is conclusive evidence for, whose only truth-related constraint is the weakened principle that Vp implies possibly-p. Show that the derivation now stalls, and then show that the stall is a disaster rather than a rescue: with only weakened factivity, the knowability principle applied to a contingent p yields at best possibly-possibly-p, and so the principle that was supposed to articulate the constitutive claim that truth consists in verifiability now says no more than that true propositions are possible ones. That is the vacuity charge, and I want it stated as a claim about what the principle can express, not as a rhetorical flourish.

Then address the objection I find most serious, which I will call the anchoring reply. The thought is that the verificationist never needed a schema about propositions and their truth at the actual world in the first place. What they need is a claim about speakers and their capacities: for any sentence a speaker understands, there is some possible course of experience after which that speaker would be in a position to correctly decide that sentence, where correctly is anchored to the world of that experience rather than to the actual world. Argue that this reply either collapses back into the paradox or loses the constitutive claim. If the decision is evaluated at the base world, factivity is back in play at that world and the construction runs as before, but now relative to every world, which requires an omniscience-like collapse everywhere rather than only here. If the decision is evaluated at the anchor world, then for any contingent sentence the principle is satisfied trivially or not at all, because a sentence true here and false at the anchor cannot be known at the anchor. Say which of the two you think the anchoring theorist should prefer, and why that preference does not help them.

Close with two short sections. In the first, identify the weakest link in the argument you have just given and say what it would take for a verificationist to exploit it; I want the honest version, not a restatement of the thesis. In the second, check whether the paradox can be revived against the non-factive surrogate by some other substitution instance, for example one that embeds the negation of possibly-Vq rather than the negation of Vq itself. If it can be revived, say what that would show about the second horn. If it cannot, say why not, and say how much of the second horn survives that result.
