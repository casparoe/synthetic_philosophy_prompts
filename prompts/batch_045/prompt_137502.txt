I have no academic background in philosophy. I work in logistics, and for the past year or so I have been working through, on my own and mostly on my phone, the argument that says our moral beliefs are undermined by the fact that evolution explains them. I have also been arguing about it with a friend who is much better read than I am, and we have reached the point where we keep talking past each other because we are both fine with prose and neither of us can say how much any particular move actually buys. So I would like numbers rather than more discussion. Please answer in American English, and please do not point me toward books, papers, or authors; I am not asking for reading, and I could not chase it up anyway.

Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it, in my own words. Premise one: human beings hold a fairly distinctive cluster of moral convictions, and we have a decent empirical story about why we hold them, since pressures of ancestral social life rewarded cooperation, reciprocity, reputation management, and readiness to punish, and our moral convictions look very much like the psychological residue of that. Premise two: that story explains why we hold the moral beliefs we hold without ever mentioning whether they are true; the causal chain runs from fitness to belief rather than from moral fact to belief. Premise three: when the best explanation of why someone holds a belief does not advert to the belief being true, the belief is not justified, or at least the person loses whatever standing reason they had for taking it to be true. Conclusion: taken as a body, our moral convictions are unjustified, or we should treat them as probably false, or we should suspend judgment on them.

First, I want to know where the weight sits. Please divide one hundred points among the three parts of that argument, in whole numbers, according to how much of its total skeptical force each one carries for someone who is otherwise a fairly confident moral realist: the empirical story about our ancestry; the claim that the causal explanation is insensitive to the moral facts; and the epistemic bridge premise about what a causal explanation does to justification. One sentence of justification per number, please. Then tell me which single part, if it turned out to be false or much weaker than I have assumed, would do the most damage to the argument, and whether that damage would be total or only partial.

Second, and this is the main thing I want, please rate the replies I have collected. Score each one from zero to one hundred on this scale: how much of the skeptical sting it removes for someone who has already granted the first two premises in full. Then sort them into tiers, roughly: rescues moral belief entirely, blunts the argument substantially, relocates the problem rather than solving it, and buys nothing. My list, with my own labels:

1. The independence claim is simply false, because the things that made us fit were independently morally good, so the causal story and the truth story point in the same direction.
2. Where a belief came from has no bearing on whether it is justified, since justification is a matter of the reasons we have now, not of the belief's causal history.
3. Companions in guilt: the same template would debunk our beliefs about other minds, the external world, memory, or elementary logical inference, and since we already refuse those conclusions, either the template is faulty or it proves far too much.
4. The argument eats itself, because its conclusion is a normative claim about what we have reason to believe and it presupposes that we ought to follow arguments; if evaluative judgment generally is unreliable, then the argument's own load-bearing normative premises are unreliable too, so at best it undercuts itself.
5. Scope restriction: the argument only reaches the beliefs the evolutionary story actually explains, which are the ones about cooperation, reciprocity, and harm within a small band, and it leaves untouched beliefs about obligations to distant strangers, future generations, or animals, so the honest verdict is a partial debunking rather than a global one.
6. Conceptual fixed points: some moral claims look partly constitutive of having practical reasons at all, so that giving them up would be a matter of stepping outside agency rather than of discovering that something is false.
7. My own attempt, which I cannot tell apart from reply one and would like you to adjudicate: even granting that the process was not selected for tracking moral truth, it had to be exquisitely sensitive to fine-grained natural structure, such as who cooperated, who cheated, and how much harm was done, and if moral facts exist they plausibly supervene on exactly that sort of natural structure, so insensitivity at the level of evolutionary function need not imply insensitivity at the level of content.

Please put this in a table with a column for the score, a column for the tier, at most two sentences of reasoning each, and a flag wherever you think two of the replies are the same move in different clothing, telling me which collapses into which.
