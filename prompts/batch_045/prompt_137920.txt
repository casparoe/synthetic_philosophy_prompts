So, context: my wife and I went through IVF a couple of years back, we've got a three-year-old from it, and there are still two embryos in a freezer in Colorado. The clinic sends us a form every year about what we want done with them and we keep not sending it back, partly because I got stuck on what I think is a dumb question and now I can't let it go: if they'd transferred the other embryo first, would the kid we have just not exist, or would he have been the same kid, born to us anyway? I've been running cases in my head for weeks and my gut verdicts don't add up. I want to know what principle I'm implicitly using, whether it survives, and which of my verdicts I'd have to throw out.

My verdicts ("my kid" = the actual kid we have):

1. A different one of my sperm, same egg: not my kid. That's a different kid — my kid's brother.
2. Same sperm, different egg of hers: not my kid either.
3. Different sperm, different egg, same two of us: definately not.
4. We'd conceived a month later, so a different cycle and a different egg: not my kid. Sibling.
5. Same conception, but she'd been on a different medication for the pregnancy: obviously still my kid.
6. Born three weeks early, or three weeks late: obviously still my kid. And if the transfer had occured twelve years later instead: still my kid, just born in a different year. So a decade of slop in birth timing is fine, but conception landing in a different cycle is fatal to identity. That pair already bothers me.
7. One single base pair in the sperm's DNA chemically corrected before fertilization: still my kid, easy yes.
8. A different sperm of mine that happens to carry an identical DNA sequence (yes, I know, astronomically unlikely, just suppose it): my gut says not my kid. But I can't say why, and it makes me suspicious of whatever I'm doing in 7.
9. My hypothetical identical twin brother's sperm instead of mine, same egg: not my kid, that's my nephew. Even though the genetic contribution is basically the same. So the genome can't be what's doing the work.
10. Egg nucleus from my wife but the rest of the cell — cytoplasm, mitochondria — from a donor: still my kid, I'm fairly confident. Flip it, nucleus from an unrelated donor and cytoplasm from my wife: not my kid.
11. The embryo splits into identical twins at the blastocyst stage: I want to say neither twin is the embryo that was in the dish — or that the question is broken. The thing that was there isn't there anymore, there are two new things.
12. That embryo fuses with another embryo at the 8-cell stage and one baby develops from the combined cells: my gut says that's still my kid, just with some of the other one mixed in. Which drives me nuts, because in 11 I said one origin can't yield the same person, and here two origins apparently can.

The principle I've been playing with, in the only terms I have: a person is identified by pointer, not by value. What makes my kid my kid is that he's the individual who developed from that particular zygote — that one object, with that history — and the gametes only matter because they're what brought that object into existence. Later changes to the object don't matter, which is why 5, 6 and 7 come out yes; swapping what brought it into existence does, which is why 1 through 4, 9, and the second half of 10 come out no.

Where I think it breaks: 11 and 12. Splitting looks like the original object going away and two new ones starting, so nobody is the original. Fusing looks like two objects going away and one new one starting, so by the same logic the result should be nobody — but my gut says it's my kid. I can't have both.

What I'd like:

- Test my principle against all twelve cases and either repair it or replace it. If there are two or three candidates that each save a different subset of my verdicts, give me all of them.
- State whichever principle you land on precisely enough that it actually delivers verdicts, clause by clause, with the case that forces each clause. If a clause is ad hoc — bolted on just to rescue one of my verdicts — say so.
- Run it on cases I haven't thought of. At minimum: (a) the embryo splits, one half is transferred now and the other is frozen and transferred twenty years later, so there are two kids twenty years apart from the same original zygote; (b) starting right after fertilization, one base pair of the embryo's genome is rewritten per hour, and after a few months the genome is exactly my brother's — at what point does it stop being my kid, or does it never; (c) an embryo is made from gametes generated in a lab out of skin cells taken from my kid himself.
- Tell me straight which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate, and which one you'd overturn if you were me. If the honest answer is that some of these cases have no determinate answer at all, say that too, and say what that does to the principle.

I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so don't assume I know any of the jargon, and please skip the reading list and the famous-philosopher-attributions — I just want the argument and the principle.
