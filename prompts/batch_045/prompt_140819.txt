I sit on the committee of a small animal-welfare charity in Bristol, and for the past two years we have been going round in circles over whether to extend a campaign that currently covers farmed fish and chickens to farmed shrimp and, further down the line, to the insects used in animal feed. The argument started, of all places, over whether a lobster is distressed by being put into boiling water, and it has since spread to prawns, black soldier fly larvae and octopus. What puzzles me is that the factual part of the argument seems to be finished. Nobody on the committee disputes the biology we have managed to work through together: crustaceans and insects have nociceptors, they groom or protect an injured body part, they learn to avoid places where they were harmed, some of them appear to trade a painful stimulus off against a reward they want, and their responses to injury are often modulated by the same kinds of chemical that modulate ours. Nobody denies any of that. And yet one half of the committee thinks this settles the matter, or very nearly, and the other half thinks it tells us nothing at all about what any of these animals actually experiences. Two years, the same eight people, no movement whatsoever.

I have a suspicion about why, and I would like it tested rather than confirmed. My suspicion is that the word 'conscious' is being made to carry two different questions at once — one about whether an animal is the sort of thing that has experiences at all, and one about how much experience, and of what kind — and that the argument keeps stalling because the first question invites a yes-or-no answer, which is what a campaign needs in order to be decidable, while the underlying phenomenon, if these animals have it at all, plausibly comes in degrees and along several dimensions at once, with no threshold that nature has drawn for us. On that reading the two sides are not disagreeing about evidence at all; they are disagreeing about whether to force a graded question into a binary answer, and about who should suffer if the grading is wrong. But I can see at least three other diagnoses and I cannot tell them apart: that this is really a clash of evidence standards, with one side applying something like a precautionary rule and the other wanting something closer to ordinary scientific proof; that it is a verbal or conceptual disagreement in which 'conscious', 'sentient', 'feels pain' and 'has an interest' are quietly being defined differently by different people; or that there is a genuine hidden empirical question doing all the work — roughly, which neural arrangements, if any, are sufficient for experience — that nobody can currently answer because we have no agreed test for consciousness even in the one case where we are certain of it, namely each other.

So what I want is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, not a verdict on shrimp. Starting from what each side in arguments like this actually says — the marker-based case built on nociception, analgesia, trade-off behaviour and flexible learning on one side, and on the other the replies that nociception can come without suffering, that the behaviour can in principle be produced without any experience at all, that insects and crustaceans have vastly fewer neurons and nothing resembling a cortex, that the midbrain may or may not be enough — tell me which of these disagreements this is, or whether several of them are stacked, and which one is load-bearing. Tell me whether my degrees-not-a-threshold hypothesis is correct, and whether, if it is, it actually dissolves anything or simply relocates the fight to the question of where to set the line. If there is a hidden empirical question, state as precisely as you can what it is, and say whether anything we could realistically do — a behavioural test, a neural marker first validated in humans, an experiment that forced rival theories of what consciousness is to make competing predictions — would settle it, or whether it is the sort of question we should expect to remain open for a very long time. And please finish with an honest account of what would count as progress for a group in our position, given your diagnosis: a better question to ask instead, a distinction we should adopt, a burden-of-proof argument that can actually be won, or a plain admission that our decision will in the end have to rest on something other than evidence. I would rather you argued from the positions themselves than surveyed the literature; I have no access to a university library, I am reading about this in my own time, and I am not asking for anything to read.
