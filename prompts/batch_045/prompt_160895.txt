I teach philosophy, though my own work is in philosophy of mind, and for some years now I have been noticing a configuration in moral life that I cannot name. I assume moral epistemologists have a term for it; if they do not, I would like one coined properly. What I want is not a label but a carving, and I say that because in my experience these requests get answered with a single phrase and a one-line gloss, which is of no use to me whatsoever.

Two cases, both of which I have watched from close by.

The first. A first-generation graduate student of mine grew up in a farming town where jokes at the expense of the seasonal workers were simply part of the air; no one in her family had ever registered them as anything at all. A friend from a different background told her, flatly, that telling jokes like that is degrading. At that moment she had no route to the thing herself: not the history, not anything antecedent she could derive it from. She took it on his word. Two years on, she sees it — and sees it in cases nobody handed her, and, which is the part I cannot stop thinking about, she traces the seeing back to the taking: having accepted it as true, she began to notice what she had previously skimmed past, and the noticing rebuilt what she was able to judge. The borrowed verdict behaved like a lens she then learned to look through.

The second. A senior physician of my acquaintance makes a principle of never accepting a moral verdict he cannot re-derive from commitments he already holds. When a nurse told him that his manner on rounds was humiliating to patients, he declined to take that on trust and set out to reach it from his own principles. He never arrived, and the result is that he still does it — and, not incidentally, the report itself got filed as grievance rather than as information, which is a further thing that seems to be going on here and for which I half-remember a name concerning people being discounted in their capacity as knowers.

The structure the two share is this: taking a moral verdict on another person's say-so, not *instead* of coming to see the thing for yourself, but as the mechanism by which you later do. I have the impression that the literature on taking moral views on trust treats that as a substitute for something — as second-best, as leaving you to act without the thing you would want to have — and I have only encountered that discussion second-hand, so correct me if it is not so. But on my cases the standard question looks like it is being asked at a single time slice, and what interests me is the trajectory: in one, the borrowing produces the very thing it is supposed to displace; in the other, the refusal to borrow is what forecloses it.

So, please:

1. Does this configuration have an established name? If it does, give it and draw the boundary between it and the ordinary business of taking a moral view on someone's word — a definition of the latter is not what I need, I need the line between the two. If it does not, coin a term and define it, and tell me which neighbouring notion it would most easily be mistaken for.

2. Boundary cases, which I will use to test the definition: how is this distinguished from (a) accepting a verdict and then, as a merely causal matter, landing in circumstances where you would have noticed anyway; (b) being handed a new moral concept or vocabulary rather than a verdict; (c) indoctrination, which has very much the same shape and is precisely what makes me reluctant to praise my first case; (d) accepting a verdict for pragmatic or relationship-preserving reasons while withholding judgment; (e) being given the non-moral facts and doing the moral inference yourself?

3. Whether the same trajectory exists outside morality — the critic whose verdict about a novel you accept sight unseen and later come to appreciate, the senior engineer who tells you a design is ugly before you have any eye for it — and if it does, whether that shows the moral case was never special in the way I take the literature to assume, or whether there is a disanalogy that keeps the specialness intact.

And one question I genuinely cannot settle: if borrowing is a route to first-hand grasp rather than a replacement for it, does that make the original borrowing permissible, or merely excusable, or does it make the grasp at the end somehow less the borrower's own?

In prose, in no more than 700 words — a compact list for the boundary cases is fine. No references and no bibliography: I am writing somewhere I cannot chase them, and the names of views in plain prose are quite enough.
