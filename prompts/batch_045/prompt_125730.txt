I am drafting a paper on the conditional analysis of dispositions, and I have reached the point where I need to know what my own case verdicts actually commit me to. Below are the cases with the answers I am inclined to give. What I want from you is the principle that fits them — or, if no single principle fits, the short list of best candidates with an honest statement of which of my verdicts each one forces me to abandon.

I use fragility as my worked example, but I care about the general form: whatever account emerges should apply to any disposition ascription, not only to glassware. I do not need the history of the debate, and I do not want references or a reading list. I already know the standard vocabulary (finks, masks, antidotes, and the cases where the manifestation occurs without the disposition), and I am not interested in who coined which term. I am interested in the pattern in my verdicts and in the price of capturing it.

The principle I start from is the familiar conditional one, stated as carefully as I can:

(CA) A thing x has a disposition at time t to give response R to stimulus S if and only if, were S to befall x at t, and were x to retain throughout the interval up to its response whatever intrinsic properties it has at t, x would give R.

Here are the cases.

1. The guarded glass. An ordinary fragile wine glass, wired to a device that detects the approach of a hammer a millisecond before impact and, in that interval, alters the glass's own molecular structure so that it survives the blow. The glass has never been struck. My verdict: not fragile.

2. The packed glass. The same glass, unwired, packed in sawdust inside a crate, so that a blow of the same force is absorbed and the glass survives. My verdict: fragile.

3. The loaded mimic. A glass made of a tough polymer that would not break when struck, but fitted with a charge that detonates on impact and shatters it. It shatters every time it is struck. My verdict: not fragile.

4. The reassembling glass. An ordinary fragile glass which, a microsecond after any fracture, has its shards drawn back into their original arrangement by a surrounding field. Anyone striking it sees an unbroken glass a moment later. My verdict: fragile.

5. The shear-thickening glass. A glass whose material stiffens in direct response to shear, with no measurable lag: it is never struck without hardening, and the hardening is a change in its intrinsic state, but the change is triggered by the strike itself rather than by anything that precedes the strike. My verdict: I go back and forth, and this is the case I would most like help with.

6. The antidote glass. A glass that is in fact tough, but wired so that any blow first renders it fragile and then breaks it. It breaks when struck. My verdict: not fragile — but I hold this much less confidently than I hold 1, and I cannot tell whether the difference in confidence is principled or merely squeamishness.

7. Two cases involving a person, included to test whether the account generalizes beyond the physical. (a) An official who is scrupulously honest, but whose public answers pass through a device that inverts their truth value, so that whenever she is asked anything she ends up asserting a falsehood. My verdict: honest. (b) The same official, unwired, but with a standing arrangement whereby, the moment a question is put to her that would be costly to answer truthfully, she takes a drug that makes her lie. My verdict: not honest, or at any rate I want to withdraw the ascription — and I notice that (b) has exactly the structure of case 5, which makes me suspect that 5 and 7b should receive the same answer whatever it is.

What I would like back, in this order. First, a principle stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause named as you go, so that I can see what I am buying and what it costs me. Second, its verdicts on cases I have not listed: a glass that is fragile now but is guaranteed to anneal itself into toughness within the hour; a guarded glass whose stiffening device is timed to activate at the moment of impact rather than a millisecond before it; a guarded glass whose device is triggered by the mere intention to strike; and a person who would answer truthfully under any questioning but who is never asked. Third, a direct answer to three questions: does the account need a causal clause to handle 3 and 4, of the sort requiring the response to issue from the thing's own constitution, and if so does adding that clause render (CA) circular or quietly convert it into an account in terms of categorical bases; can any single account deliver both my verdict on 1 and my verdict on 2, given that the interfering mechanism is extrinsic to the glass in both; and if it cannot, what grounds the asymmetry other than my willingness to call one a mask and the other a fink. Fourth, and most important, which of my verdicts you think I should drop, and why.

If the price of saving all seven verdicts is a principle with several clauses that do no work outside the cases that prompted them, say so plainly and give me instead the more elegant principle that costs me a verdict, together with your recommendation as to which verdict is least costly to lose.
