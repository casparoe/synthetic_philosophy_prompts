Two colleagues at my work got into it over lunch about something small and ended up somewhere big, and I stupidly offered to referee. Here's the exchange as close as I can reconstruct it.

The trigger: our office introduced a rule that anything left in the shared fridge overnight gets thrown out. On friday someone's clearly labelled stuff got binned, including a box of insulin. The person who did it (not either of them) said that if she starts deciding what's important enough, the rule is finished.

Anke (grew up outside Munich, has been here about twelve years) said she was basically right. A rule like that exists so nobody has to weigh, at 10pm, whose stuff matters more — and the moment it becomes a judgement call it turns into a question of who you like and who'll complain loudest. She added that the rule does something positive, not just defensive: it's a promise that everyone gets treated the same, and you can't keep that promise and also carve out exceptions whenever you feel sorry for someone.

Dave (American, from Minnesota) said that's precisely the corruption: the rule lets the enforcer stop being a moral agent, and "I was following the rule" is a way of not noticing what you're actually doing to a person. You don't get to outsource your judgement to whoever wrote the fridge policy.

Anke said conscience is exactly the faculty that tells you that *you* deserve the exception. People have defended genuinely awful things with a perfectly sincere conscience — she brought up slaveholders appealing to theirs before God — so sincerity isn't worth much.

Dave said that cuts both ways: the worst things of the last century were largely done by diligent people following rules, so if we're comparing columns of horrors you can fill one out from either side and the comparison decides nothing.

Anke said it does decide one thing. A rule is public, so when it produces something bad you can see it, argue about it and change it; a conscience is private, and the only person who can really audit it is the person who has it. That's why, she said, the rule-follower's failures are corrigible and the conscience-follower's aren't.

Dave's reply: you can't follow a rule without interpreting it, so "I was only following the rule" is never literally true. You're always already exercising judgement — you're just not admitting it, which is worse than admitting it.

Anke: yes, but interpreting a rule isn't the same as deciding whether it applies at all, and the discipline of asking "would I enforce this against someone I don't like?" is itself a rule-shaped thing, not a conscience-shaped thing. Then she said the cultural bit: Americans treat rules as suggestions they're entitled to overrule with their own feelings, whereas the rule is what makes a shared space possible in the first place.

Dave said fine, then we agree you need both, and the real disagreement is about who owes whom a reason — he thinks the person enforcing has to show the rule fits *this* case, she thinks the breaker has to show it doesn't. And then his cultural bit: Germans have turned obedience into a virtue so that they never have to be the one who decided.

Someone else at the table who'd hardly spoken said the one line that stuck with me: "you're each describing what happens when someone worse than you holds your view." I genuinely can't tell if that's deep or a way of dodging.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what, rather than just who wins? Three things I especially want: whether the fridge thing is even a good test case for what they ended up arguing about, whether the horror examples on either side do any real work, and whether the German/American framing is doing anything or they're just two flavours of one argument. I also have a feeling each of them is quietly assuming something different about who writes the rules and how much that person can be trusted, but I couldn't pin it down.
