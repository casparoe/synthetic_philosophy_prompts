I teach a 16-student upper-level seminar on social epistemology, one 75-minute session, one room, no screens during the exercise. I need a structured simulation that makes participants feel the difference between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber — and, more than that, feel why the second is far harder to escape than the first.

The distinction I have in mind, stated only so we are talking about the same thing: in a bubble, contrary evidence never reaches you, because the structure around you omits or filters it; exposure alone is largely curative. In a chamber, contrary evidence does reach you, but it arrives through sources the group has already trained you to discount, so the mechanism is a manipulated economy of credibility rather than a gap in your information. A chamber can therefore be punctured from inside only by a recalibration of trust directed at sources you have no independent way of assessing, and a member of a chamber can be reasoning carefully and in good faith the whole way through.

What I need from you:

1. A runnable simulation: concrete rules, roles, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes and 16 people. Both structures must be produced by the mechanics rather than announced — participants should first undergo non-exposure and discover that exposure cures it, then undergo discrediting and discover that exposure does not cure it. Neither term should be spoken until the debrief.

2. A wrinkle I have not managed to stage: I want participants to discover that a chamber's outputs can be true. In at least one phase the insulated, discrediting group should be right and the surrounding consensus wrong, so that what the exercise indicts is the structure of their believing rather than the falsity of what they believe — while still leaving them able to say what has gone wrong with them. This has to fall out of the rules, not out of anything I say.

3. A third phase on exit. Participants get a real choice about whether to leave and something small but genuine to lose by leaving; then each rational-seeming exit route — checking the outside sources directly, demanding stronger evidence, converting a trusted insider — should fail for a different structural reason. The failure of the convert-a-trusted-insider route should be the one that stings.

4. A debrief that maps specific moments onto specific features: the filtration mechanism, the credibility-inversion mechanism, the bootstrapping problem on exit, and the true-belief case. Give me the questions to ask, in the order to ask them, and the answers I should be listening for.

5. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — at least four failure modes with a fix for each. I am especially worried about (a) participants who refuse the role, (b) the exercise reading as propaganda rather than as structure, and (c) the sharpest student naming the mechanism halfway through and deflating the room.

6. An honest note on what the simulation cannot reproduce: the features of real bubbles and chambers that depend on exit costs, social ties, and identity investments, and that I will simply have to state at the end.

Keep the whole thing under roughly 1,100 words, and write it as something I can photocopy and run rather than as general advice about teaching the topic. Materials should be things I can make with index cards and a copier.

Lead with a two- or three-sentence bottom line that names the single mechanic doing the work and says why it produces the intended experience; then give the design. No preamble, and no restatement of this brief.
