I'm defending a job-market paper in a political theory workshop in three weeks, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. The paper is about how much rule-following a society should demand, and my thesis is that the question is asked at the wrong granularity: tightness and looseness, in the sense made familiar by the cross-cultural tightness–looseness literature associated with Michele Gelfand, are properties of institutional domains — commercial aviation, emergency medicine, undergraduate teaching, software release, child-rearing — and only derivatively properties of nations or cultures. Call it domain-local tightness. The claim I expect to be fought over is the displacement thesis: tightening a domain does not reduce the total error in it but reallocates it, converting errors of indiscretion, where someone departed from the rule, into errors of judgment, where someone followed the letter of the rule into a situation the rule was not written for, or did nothing because no rule licensed acting — and converting visible failures into invisible ones, because the same norm-enforcement that makes tightness effective also makes the second kind of failure costly to report. If that is right, part of the measured payoff of tightness, the accident rates and compliance statistics the literature leans on, is an artifact of what tight institutions count, and the normative upshot is a principle of differential tightness: be tight where errors of indiscretion are catastrophic and cheap to detect, loose where the residual errors are judgment failures no rule can pre-empt, and assign authority over how tight a domain should be to whoever bears the unmeasured costs rather than to whoever collects the measured benefits. The project began with a friend who flies narrow-body jets for a living and who described her cockpit as a place where nearly every action is rule-governed and where the hardest part of the job is knowing when to stop following the checklist, which is what first made me suspect the familiar contrast was being drawn at the wrong level.

The room will be about fifteen people and I can predict four kinds of interlocutor. Most of the department are analytic political philosophers, broadly liberal-egalitarian, who will care about autonomy, legitimacy, and whether a demand for rule-following can be justified to the people who live under it; they will want to know what my principle says when the bearers of the unmeasured costs are diffuse or absent, children, future patients, the residents of a flood plain. Two behavioral economists attend the workshop, and they will want to know what identifies a domain's optimal tightness, how I would distinguish displacement from a plain reduction in error, and whether the thesis is testable with the survey and archival measures the field already has or whether I have built a hypothesis that is unfalsifiable because its costs are by construction unobserved. One cultural anthropologist, who has said in earlier workshops that she regards nation-level tightness scores as reified composites of student samples, will attack the unit of analysis and may say that I have not dissolved the culture-level claim but only renamed it, since domains inside a single society correlate. And one colleague from the law school, who thinks in the vocabulary of rules versus standards, of ex ante precision and ex post discretion, of optimal standardization, will want to know whether my view is anything more than that familiar tradeoff with a psychological story bolted onto it.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, attributed to the kind of person who will make each one and phrased the way that person would phrase it: the economists' in the idiom of identification and measurement, the philosophers' in the idiom of justification and autonomy, the anthropologist's in hers, the lawyer's in his. Order them by how much damage they would do to the paper rather than by how likely they are, and flag separately which have real force and which I can dispatch quickly. For each, give a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a note on where the reply is soft, meaning the point at which I should concede rather than push. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything, because conceding it later will look like retreat; tell me which single reply has to land if the displacement thesis is to survive the hour; and tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks, with an honest note about what I should say if someone does. Assume fifteen minutes of questions, so triage matters. Please write the whole thing in American English.
