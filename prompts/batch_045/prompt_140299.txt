I'm writing a paper for a volume on closure and the skeptical argument — it grew out of a second-year graduate seminar I taught last spring on luck, method, and skeptical scenarios — and before I build thirty pages on top of the framework I'd like somebody to check whether the pieces actually hold together. Different colleagues have told me different things: one says the whole apparatus quietly collapses into a safety condition; another says I've reinvented contextualism while refusing the semantics; a third says my central innovation is just sensitivity with extra notation. I can't tell which of these, if any, is right, and I'd rather find out now than in the question period.

Here is where I've landed. (1) The core of the tracking account seems to me right: S knows p only if S's true belief tracks the fact — that is, it is sensitive (were p false, S would not believe p) and adherent (were p true, S would believe p) — with both counterfactuals evaluated for the method S actually used. (2) I want to replace binary sensitivity with a scalar notion I've been calling the *margin*: how far out along a fixed similarity ordering of worlds you have to travel, past the nearest not-p world, before you reach a not-p world in which S's method would still deliver the belief that p. Knowledge requires that the margin exceed a fixed threshold, so that merely barely sensitive beliefs — the ones that scrape by because the nearest failure world happens to be one where the method would go silent — don't count. (3) I hold only a restricted closure principle: a single competent deductive step from something S knows transmits knowledge to its conclusion provided the step does not shrink the margin. The step from *here is a hand* to *I am not an envatted brain* is precisely a margin-destroying step, so it doesn't transmit, and that is the whole of my reply to the skeptical argument. I don't claim unrestricted closure — I don't see how I could — but I'd be unhappy if the restriction blocked the ordinary run of one-step deductions in daily life and in science. (4) Ordinary perceivers in ordinary circumstances know the ordinary things they take themselves to know: that the cat is on the mat, that the department meeting is on Tuesday. And no one, including me, knows the denials of the skeptical hypotheses: not that I am not an envatted brain, not that the world was not created five minutes ago complete with my apparent memories.

(5) Two case verdicts I'm attached to. In the fake-barn case — the visitor driving through the county where almost every barn-looking structure is a façade, who happens to look at the one real barn — I'm happy to say he does not know. But consider this: I glance at the printed conference programme, which says the talk I want is in Hall A, and it is in Hall A; still, had there been a late room change the printer missed, I'd have walked to the wrong hall with exactly the same confidence. I want to say I know the talk is in Hall A, that the bare possibility of a printing error no more removes my knowledge than the bare possibility of a painted mule removes it in the zebra case (on which I'm inclined, less confidently, to say the zoo visitor does know). (6) The threshold in (2) is meant to be fixed, not contextually shifting: I don't want a semantics for *knows* on which its content moves with stakes. But I also don't want to say that ordinary speakers who withhold *knows* in the high-stakes bank cases are making a mistake; I'd rather explain that withholding pragmatically, as a matter of what it's responsible to say when the practical stakes are high. (7) Finally, a constraint I take seriously: verdicts shouldn't hinge on how finely we individuate the method in (1). I assume I can't manufacture sensitivity by redescribing what S did in ever narrower terms, and I take it as a cost of any version of the view if the margin in (2) turns out to be defined only relative to a choice of method description I get to make.

What I want is an audit, not a survey. Go through that set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, and which only look like they are — I expect at least one of the apparent conflicts dissolves once you look properly, and I'd like to know which. For each real tension, say what would have to give, what the cheapest fix would be, and what that fix would cost me elsewhere in the set: if, say, the fix is to move from sensitivity to safety, I want to be told plainly which of my commitments that eats. Then tell me whether there's a single revision that would clean up the most, and rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging them all as equally bad. Please assume I know the standard moves and the standard cases — no need to explain what sensitivity is or to rehearse the skeptical argument. I'm not asking to be converted to a rival account; I'm asking which of my own commitments is doing the damage. And don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I'll supply the references myself.
