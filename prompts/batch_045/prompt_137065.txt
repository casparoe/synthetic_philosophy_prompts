I convene the final session of a graduate seminar on the value of knowledge, and I would rather close it by handing out a poem than by summarizing the reading. Would you write one?

The target is the Meno problem in its value form: true belief and knowledge appear to get you to the same place — the traveller who guessed the road arrives at Larissa exactly as the one who knew it does — so what is the extra worth of knowledge supposed to be, and where is it supposed to come from?

Form: a villanelle. Five tercets and a closing quatrain, nineteen lines, two refrains alternating through the tercets and meeting in the last two. But I want the form to carry the argument rather than ornament it.

One refrain carries the practical-equivalence claim: the destination does not care how you came. It must return word for word every time — the road is indifferent, and so is the arrival.

The other refrain carries the claim about staying. What is true but untied does not stay put. It must return altered each time — one word replaced, never flagged, never apologized for — so that by the closing lines the reader can still hear the original sentence inside it but can no longer be certain. This is the statues of Daedalus: the belief leaves. Keep the refrain lines short, and keep line lengths near enough to one another that a listener hears a norm and therefore hears the deviations; the drift has to be audible as drift.

I would like the whole poem in the voice of a guide who has led parties to Larissa for years on guesswork and has never once failed to arrive.

The turn should not be the pious Socratic resolution. It should be the swamping worry, in the guide's mouth: if the worth of the tether is just that it keeps producing arrivals, then it borrows its worth from precisely those arrivals, and knowledge turns out to be a true belief plus something whose value is spent getting you where the true belief already got you. What, he wants to know, is he being asked to add?

Then the closing lines, where the altered refrain meets the unchanged one, have to say what is left. I am partial to the credit answer — the arrival is the same either way, but one of them is attributable to the person who got there — but it has to surface as something the poem has already been doing (a line that arrives by luck against a line that arrives because it was made to), not as a thesis stamped onto the last two lines. If you think the swamping worry really is fatal and no such close is honest, say so in a sentence before the poem and close honestly instead; but the worry has to be met, not stepped around.

No apparatus: no footnotes, no glossary, no naming of positions. It should read as a poem to someone who has never opened the dialogue, and as a correct one to someone who has.
