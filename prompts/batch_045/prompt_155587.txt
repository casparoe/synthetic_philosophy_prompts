I am Reader in Philosophy here and the philosophical member of the Interpretation Committee of the University Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology. The Committee reports to the Board of Trustees and meets on 12 March; I am expected to circulate a written opinion a week beforehand, and I would like your help drafting it.

The case before us is this. A deceased alumnus has left the museum £4.2m to endow a permanent gallery on the theme of civilisational collapse. The gift agreement requires the word 'collapse' to appear in the gallery's title, and the letter accompanying it proposes its own test: a civilisation may be said to have collapsed when, within three generations, its cities are abandoned and its script falls out of use. Our development office has advised that the title cannot be varied without the executors' written consent, that the executors are unlikely to be sympathetic, and that the alternative to compliance is returning the money — which the director regards as unthinkable. The three cases proposed for the gallery are the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BC, the Classic Maya southern lowlands, and Rapa Nui.

Three positions are already on the table. Our curators argue that in each case what ended was an institutional arrangement — palace-centred redistribution and long-distance exchange; lowland kingship together with its monument and calendar tradition; the standing-stone-building complex — while the populations persisted, so that calling these 'collapses of a civilisation' misdescribes the record. A liaison group representing descendant communities argues that being exhibited as a failed civilisation is itself a wrong done to the living, and not merely an offence to their feelings; there are, after all, Maya people alive in their millions today. Our education team replies that 'reorganisation' sells no tickets and moves nobody on climate, and that the visitor's question 'could it happen to us?' is the entire point of the gallery and depends on the harsher word.

At our last meeting I floated a principle I called the descendant veto: a past polity may be described as having collapsed only where no living community both claims descent from it and maintains any of its practices. I want the memo either to defend or to reject this, and in doing so to face two difficulties. First, the curators' objection that the veto makes the application of an ordinary descriptive term depend on contingent facts about present-day demography and self-identification, including who counts as a descendant community when several groups compete for the role. Second, a nastier implication I noticed afterwards: the veto would license the bare 'collapse' label precisely where there is no one left to be wronged by it, so that our bluntest language for failure would attach to the most complete destructions — the peoples we may most freely call failed being those least able to object.

There is also the question the donor most wants asked. The inference from these cases to us requires that the ancient cases be relevantly similar to ours, and they were regional subsystems embedded in neighbours who were often unaffected, whereas the unit we would be worrying about is planetary and has no unaffected outside. I would like the memo to say whether the 'could it happen to us?' framing survives that disanalogy and, if it survives, what exactly it is that is supposed to be at risk of happening.

Please draft the memo, of roughly 900 words, addressed to the Committee. It should: state what, in your view, 'collapse' is properly predicated of — a people, a polity, or an institutional arrangement — and why; recommend one course of action, choosing among accepting the gift as specified, accepting it while re-engineering the interpretive text so that every use of 'collapse' has an explicit subject and a stated measure of loss, seeking the executors' consent to a revised title with a named fallback if consent is refused, or declining the gift; set out the strongest consideration on whichever side you reject, in the form its proponents would put it; and close with a single sentence of proposed opening wall text that would implement your recommendation. Assume the Committee knows the archaeology only in broad outline: explain any term of art you rely upon, do not cite or recommend any reading, and let the reasoning stand on its own. Please write throughout in British English.
