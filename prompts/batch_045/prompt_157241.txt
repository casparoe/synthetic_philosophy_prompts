I'm 71, spent most of my working life as a population ecologist (fish stock modelling, lots of cost-benefit work, and a fair amount of signalling theory on the side), and since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university. They have a small pot of money for independent scholar projects — three months part time, deliverable is a paper of about 5000 words that I'd present to the group at the end — and they want a two page proposal. I need help writing it.

The topic I want is promising: what makes a promise bind. I've got a half-formed idea and I want you to hammer it into something fundable.

Here's the bit I keep coming back to. Almost every discussion I've come across uses as its stock case a promise whose cost to the promiser is known at the time of promising — pick you up at the station, lend you fifty pounds, that sort of thing. But a lot of actual promising isn't like that. Marriage vows say for worse. People promise to split whatever a joint venture brings in, to cover a friend's losses if the friend's business goes under, to co-sign, to take in someone's kids if the worst happens. Call these "lottery promises": at the moment of promising everyone knows the cost of performance is going to be drawn from some spread of outcomes, and only later does the draw happen and reveal whether keeping it was a trivial favour or something close to ruinous.

My question: does the moral force of the promise depend on how the draw comes out? And the reason I think it's a real question rather than a confusion is this. From the side of the function of promising — promising as a commitment device — insensitivity to the realised draw is the whole point. If you could reopen the deal after seeing how the coin landed you wouldn't have solved the commitment problem in the first place, and the promisee couldn't plan on you. But our ex post reactions, and the legal thinking about changed circumstances and ruinous cost, are extremely sensitive to the realised draw, and they're sensitive even when the bad draw was never a surprise — everyone knew the distribution. So either that sensitivity is defensible in its own right, or it's an outcome-bias artefact, moral luck leaking sideways into promising instead of into action.

What I want in the proposal:

- The question stated so it has a determinate answer, and split off from nearby ones I might be conflating it with.
- Why the existing families of answer don't already settle it. Roughly, as I understand it, there's the practice-or-convention family, the expectation-and-reliance family, the normative-power-and-rights family, and the view that there's no special duty of fidelity at all, just general duties about not misleading people and not disappointing expectations they were warranted in having. Say honestly which of these the case actually bites on and which one shrugs it off.
- The method, and why that method rather than the obvious ones. My instinct is a small parameterised grid of cases — vary whether the distribution was known, whether the draw came out high, whether the promisee's reliance was fixed, whether the promisee could have insured against non-performance, whether the relationship is ongoing — and then try to derive verdicts from each account mechanically instead of arguing from first principles. Make the case for that over (a) another attempt to derive promissory obligation from the ground up, (b) running questionnaires on people's intuitions about these vignettes, and (c) more conceptual analysis of what a promise is. I'd guess the survey route is a trap here, since a hypothetical in which the cost is randomly assigned is exactly the set-up that measures outcome bias, but argue it properly.
- What a finished result would look like, including a deflationary one.
- What would count as the project failing. I can think of two: that every account turns out to have a free parameter you can tune to match any verdict, in which case the discriminating-cases method just doesn't work and the honest product is a short negative note; or that the whole thing collapses into the general question about changed circumstances, in which case it's too narrow for three months. Tell me if there's a third failure mode I'm not seeing.
- A month-by-month plan, and one paragraph I can use on why this isn't just a rehash of the standard debate.

Two constraints. Don't put any citations or names of papers in it — I'll go and get those filled in properly once I know what I'm arguing, and I don't want to be caught citing things I haven't read. And write it so it would survive a sceptical philosopher on the committee, but don't build any step of it on my already knowing the literature; assume I've read a decent amount and definately less than I pretend to. Oh, and it's fine if the proposal says so explicitly: I'm an ecologist, not a philosopher, and I might of got some of this wrong.
