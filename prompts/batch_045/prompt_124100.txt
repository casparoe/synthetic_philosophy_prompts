My friend (she's a palliative care nurse) and I got into a massive argument last night, 1am, both slightly drunk, about whether there is any real difference between killing someone and letting them die. I ended up defending the difference and she said it was a superstition left over from religion, and the annoying thing is that every time I tried to say what the difference actually was, I ended up describing something else entirely. It got under my skin because my dad was in intensive care last winter and I have been chewing on it ever since. I'm not a philosopher, so please don't just hand me the textbook answer; I want to know what principle actually fits my reactions, and which of my reactions are just wrong.

Here is where I land, case by case, going on gut. (1) A runaway maintenance cart is going to hit five track workers; I can throw a switch so it goes down a siding where it will kill one worker. Throwing the switch: fine, obviously, I would do it. (2) Same five, but the only way to stop it is to shove a big guy off the footbridge into its path. Not fine, and I know the numbers and the outcome are identical to (1). (3) Hospital version: five patients will die of organ failure tonight; a man who came in for a routine check-up happens to be a match for all five; kill him, harvest, five live. Not fine, and it feels worse to me than (2): something about him having walked in for a check-up, and about what everyone else would then think when they walk into a hospital. (4) My dad: on a ventilator after a cardiac arrest, sedated, no realistic shot at recovery, we all agreed, the doctor switched the machine off and he died a few minutes later. Fine, I don't think anyone did anything wrong. And it feels exactly the same to me as if they had simply never put him on it, or hadn't re-intubated him when the tube came out: whether there was an action or an omission changes nothing for me here. (5) But: someone needs the last ventilator and will die without it, and the doctor just doesn't give it to them because they can't be bothered, or gives it to a less sick patient who arrived first. Not fine, and I'd call it letting them die, but I'd also say it is less bad than killing someone to get the machine, so the difference is real without being a free pass. (6) The one that makes me think the line isn't about moving your body at all: a parent who just doesn't feed their toddler and the child starves, I want to call that killing, even though it is pure omission; whereas the doctor switching off the ventilator in (4) is letting die, even though it is an action. So I think the killing/letting die line tracks what you owed the person, not what your hands did. (7) The pair I couldn't defend: dying patient in terrible pain, high-dose morphine to control the pain, everyone knows it will probably shorten his life by a few hours: fine. Lethal injection, same death, same motive, same request, arguably less suffering overall: not fine. She pounced on this and said it's where my position collapses, because I had just said the physical difference doesn't matter, so what difference am I leaning on here? (8) And then the numbers: I said I'd let five die rather than kill one. She pushed me: would I let fifty die? A thousand? A million, if killing one innocent person was definately the only way? I couldn't find a place where my answer flipped, which worries me, because either it flips nowhere, which seems insane, or it flips somewhere and the location is arbitrary, which means I'm just doing arithmetic with extra steps.

My own stab at a principle: the real line is not killing versus letting die, it is whether I am redirecting a threat that already exists or starting a new lethal sequence. (1) only redirects, (2) and (3) start new ones, (4) and (5) just stop resisting a threat that is already there, and (6) works because I owe the kid food but the doctors didn't owe my dad indefinite ventilation. But I think this breaks on (7), where the morphine looks like starting something new, and on (8), because a constraint like that shouldn't care how many people sit downstream of it. So: is there a principle that gets (1) through (6) and is honest about (7) and (8)? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause of it, tell me what it predicts for the two I keep going back and forth on — (9) one ventilator, already on patient A who might recover, patient B will die tonight without it and will definately survive with it, so do I take it off A? and (10) someone ventilator-dependent after a spinal injury, not dying, could live for decades, competent, has thought about it for a year, asks to be switched off, where I am certain in (4) and completely unsure here — and give me at least one case your principle decides that I haven't listed. Also say plainly which of my verdicts no decent principle can keep; I'd rather drop one verdict than end up with a rule with six epicycles bolted on.

End with a single machine-readable line, \answer{...}, giving the label of the principle you recommend plus the numbers of my verdicts it cannot save, like \answer{constraint on initiating new lethal sequences, no threshold; overturns 7 and 8}.
