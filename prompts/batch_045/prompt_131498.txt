ok so my friend and I spent like two hours last night arguing about sortition — replacing elections with random selection — and I think we were talking past each other. her version: the point of the lot is descriptive representation, the chamber should mirror the country, and once it does, the decisions get fairer because the people affected are in the room. my version: the real argument for the lot is that elections are a filter that selects for people who want power over other people, so swapping that filter out should change what gets decided, not just who is sitting there.

eventually we invented a diagnostic to settle it, call it the reshuffle test: take a sortition chamber, swap the entire random sample for a fresh one drawn from the same strata, run the same agenda, and see whether any vote comes out differently. if literally nothing changes, then the chamber was never the thing doing the work, and the mirroring was decoration. she said that's a bad test because good decisions ought to be stable under a reshuffle; I said no, stability under reshuffle just means the permanent staff is writing the laws and you've built a very expensive focus group. we didn't resolve it.

so: write me a mock consultancy audit report — big-four / ISO-quality-assurance style memo, header block, findings, a maturity score out of five — addressed to the clerk of a legislature that has just swapped elections for sortition plus hard two-term-and-out limits, assessing the new chamber against the Reshuffle Test. Deadpan management-consultant voice, bullet-pointed findings with invented metrics.

The joke has to be that the chamber scores perfectly: the sample is demographically exact to the decimal, and a full reshuffle of every member changes zero votes, which the consultants file under "outcome stability" and "selection risk eliminated" — while it's completely obvious to the reader that the unelected secretariat is writing everything, that the descriptive representation is pure symbolism, and that the term limits didn't remove incumbency so much as relocate it from the members to the staff. nobody in the memo may notice. no winking, no one breaking frame, no line hinting that this might be bad. include one recommendation that follows the logic one step further (something like extending rotation to the permanent staff, or packaging outcome-stability guarantees as a product). 600–700 words, and start with the memo itself, no preface or commentary before or after it.
