Question: Explain Ian Hacking's looping effects and say why they are supposed to matter for psychiatric classification. Given looping effects, are psychiatric kinds less real than natural kinds like gold?

Answer: Hacking's looping effects are why kinds of people behave differently from kinds of things. Gold does not care what chemists say about it: the classification and the thing classified are independent, so the kind stays where it is. When you classify people, though, the people classified can come to know about the classification and change how they act, feel and describe themselves; clinicians then find themselves facing new behaviour to describe, and the category gets revised to fit, which starts the whole thing over again. So a human kind is a moving target, and the classification and the people under it make each other up. This is more than the ordinary point that a label can change behaviour — a label shaping behaviour once would just be a self-fulfilling prophecy, whereas looping is the two-way, open-ended interaction between a kind and the people who fall under it.

The DSM is full of it. Asperger's being folded into autism spectrum disorder after field trials showed the distinction was unreliable, or the criteria for adult ADHD getting broader as research accumulated, are both looping effects: the category moved because the people under it had moved. Note also that the loop needs the classified people to know about the classification — a diagnosis given to someone who never learns of it cannot loop — so the effect matters much less for childhood diagnoses than for adult ones.

That is also why looping kinds are less real than natural kinds. Since the target keeps moving, a looping kind does not track anything independent of itself: the behaviours it collects are produced by the classification rather than found by it, and the kind is best understood as a description that clinicians find useful at a given time. None of this means the suffering is fake — people really do suffer, and Hacking insists on that. But the upshot is that psychiatric disorders are socially constructed rather than discovered, and that a classification written in properly biological terms, in brain mechanisms, would be immune to the looping that plagues the current manual. That is the direction DSM-style classification ought to be going in.
