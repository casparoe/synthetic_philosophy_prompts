I chair the admissibility committee of a standing Citizens' Convention on Climate and Nature, which vets written submissions before they enter the convention's public record. Our precedents, accumulated over two years:

1. A bishops' conference submission opposing liberalisation of embryo research, argued from ensoulment and magisterial teaching — we required an independent secular argument for the conclusion before it could be entered; the theological reasoning was allowed to remain as a preface.
2. A Muslim women's organisation's submission on sex-selective abortion, combining Qur'anic citation with demographic evidence and sex-equality arguments — admitted in full, on the ground that once a secular case stands on its own the religious framing need not be detached from it.
3. A secular humanist group's submission against faith schools — the autonomy argument admitted; a passage describing religious belief as a form of arrested cognitive development struck out as denying the equal standing of citizens.
4. A Sikh federation's submission seeking a helmet exemption for turban-wearing motorcyclists — admitted as it stood; we said that citizens are not bound by the restraint we impose on officials, and that the claim concerned a burden on a practice rather than a demand that others accept a theology.
5. A joint submission from a Catholic workers' movement and a Marxist tenants' union on rent regulation, each arguing from its own doctrine to the same policy — admitted, with a note that convergence between opposed comprehensive doctrines is itself a public reason.
6. A coalition of churches and refugee organisations arguing for resettlement purely from Scripture — admitted as it stood, over a dissent that this was flatly inconsistent with 1.

The new submission: an Anglican diocese, an animist group, and a secular river conservation charity jointly support conferring legal personhood on a river in a region where farmers and a hydroelectric operator object. The diocese grounds the claim in a theology of creation; the animist group in a cosmology it affirms literally; the charity says it does not believe the river is a person, but that unless it is spoken of as one it will not be protected — an avowedly fictive framing it calls motivationally indispensable. An appendix offers a consequentialist case about water security and recreation, and states plainly that this case would not by itself support personhood rather than weaker protections, and would not have moved the signatories to act.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I want the candidate rules our six precedents are jointly consistent with — a translation requirement, a civic-respect rule, an officials-only reading of the restraint, a convergence rule, and the less comfortable possibility that we simply admit reasoning whose conclusions we find congenial all seem to fit — what each predicts here, and which precedent is the closest analog. Where our precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, tell me what principle we would be adopting with each option, and name the further case that would best discriminate between the surviving rules.

Please answer in British English, attach rough confidence levels to your predictions and to the claim that the precedents cohere at all, and if you think the practice of filtering citizens' reasons is itself misconceived, or that the case as I have described it has no good answer, say so plainly rather than producing a polite ruling.
