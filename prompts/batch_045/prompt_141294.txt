And here is the answer, more or less as submitted (about 350 words):

Duhem's point is that you never test a single hypothesis on its own. What you test is a whole package: the hypothesis you care about plus everything you had to assume to get a prediction out of it — the initial conditions, the calibration of the instruments, the assumption that nothing else was interfering. If the prediction fails, logic alone doesn't tell you which part of the package to blame, so in principle you can always rescue your favourite hypothesis by blaming an auxiliary one instead, and there is no experimentum crucis that could force you to give it up.

Quine took this further and made it global. His claim was that for any theory and any body of evidence there will always be an alternative theory that fits that evidence just as well, obtained by making compensating adjustments elsewhere in your total system of beliefs. Since the two fit the observations equally well, observation cannot decide between them, and the choice has to be made on other grounds — simplicity, conservatism, fruitfulness, fit with the rest of what we believe.

A good example is the perihelion of Mercury. Newtonian mechanics plus the known planets predicted one rate of advance for the orbit and the telescopes gave another, with a stubborn residual of a few dozen arcseconds a century. One option was to keep Newton and add unseen mass — a planet or a ring of debris inside Mercury's orbit, which is what Le Verrier proposed — and another was to give up the inverse-square law. Nobody could decide between these at the time; the evidence available in the 1860s did not distinguish them, and people went on trying to make the extra planet work for decades afterwards. That is what underdetermination means: the data do not pick out one theory.

And this is what Kuhn was getting at. Because observation is theory-laden, rival paradigms are empirically equivalent, so no data can adjudicate between them; scientists convert for sociological reasons and then see the world differently afterwards. Evidence doesn't decide anything, the community does. So the real moral of Duhem-Quine is that scientific rationality is something of an illusion and that in the end it is fashion and power all the way down.
