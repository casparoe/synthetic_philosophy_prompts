I write novels — the literary kind, lots of interior monologue — and the draft I'm on has a scene I keep rewriting because I can't settle what my characters are entitled to say to each other, and my editor wants the manuscript in March. Setup: Marta is an archivist who is sure a letter in the collection is a forgery. She has good evidence and nothing much is riding on it. Then, unknown to her, a collector she has never met places a large bet on whether she is right; and separately, later, the letter turns out to be the thing that decides whether a colleague gets pushed out of the profession. One of the people in my writing group did a philosophy BA and keeps saying words like encroachment at me over pints, so I went home and tried to write down what I actually think, and this is the list. (1) Whether someone knows something partly depends on what is practically at stake for them — I'm convinced by the bank-type cases, and I don't think they can be explained away by saying the evidence gets worse as the stakes rise. (2) But I don't want to say that 'knows' means different things in different mouths: when two characters argue about whether Marta knows, they are disagreeing about one thing, not talking past each other. So the stakes business is a fact about knowledge, not about the conversation. (3) Justification is purely evidential — what the evidence supports doesn't move when the stakes move. My friend says (1) and (3) can't both be true; I don't see the problem, am I missing something? (4) Knowledge is the norm of practical reasoning and of assertion: you may treat p as a reason, or say p out loud, only if you know p. (5) Stakes are objective — what matters is how much really hangs on being right, not what Marta takes to hang on it or could work out if she thought about it. (6) Stakes transmit: if a great deal hangs on whether q and Marta is relying on p as her reason for q, then a great deal hangs on p too, so she can't dodge high stakes for the conclusion by pointing at low stakes for the premise. (7) Closure holds: if she knows p, knows that p entails q, and competently deduces q, she knows q. I'd give up most of the rest before I gave that up. (8) And in my scene I want to say the collector's bet is his business and Marta still knows the letter is a forgery, while the colleague's situation genuinely changes what Marta may say and do.

So: does that hang together? I'm after an audit, not a lecture. Rank the tensions by how bad they actually are rather than listing them as equally serious, tell me for each what would have to give and what that would cost me, and tell me which single revision cleans up the most. Also flag if I'm quietly using stakes in two different senses in different items. Be committed: tell me which item has to go and why, don't hand me a menu of options to pick from — and if one of my items is fine as it stands, say so plainly. I don't have library access out here, so please argue it rather than appealing to what so-and-so argues. Under 600 words, and I'd rather have it blunt than complete.
