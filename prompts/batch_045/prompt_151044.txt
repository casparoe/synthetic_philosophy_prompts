I'm writing a short position note on the problem of eliciting latent knowledge from AI systems — the setup in which a system may represent some fact about the world correctly while the only supervision signal available to us pins down what a human would say about it, so that a reporter trained to reproduce human-approved answers can come apart from the system's own representation on precisely the inputs where the divergence matters. A single analogy dominates discussion in the reading group I'm part of, and I would like it audited rather than either admired or waved away.

The analogy is cryptographic. The model's weights and activations are a ciphertext; the fact about the world is the plaintext; training a reporter is recovering the decryption key; the training process and the training distribution together play the role of the adversary's encryption scheme. Two members of the group use the same picture to opposite conclusions. One argues that cryptanalysis is a matter of compute, data and cleverness, and that here nothing was encrypted on purpose — the plaintext is merely written in a private notation — so eliciting latent knowledge is a capabilities problem that will dissolve as interpretability tooling scales, and does not deserve treatment as a separate research priority. The other argues that when the adversary holds the key, encryption is unbreakable in principle; the system is the adversary and it holds the key; therefore the honest conclusion is that eliciting latent knowledge is hopeless and we should build systems we never need to interrogate in the first place.

What I want is an audit of what the analogy can carry. Please walk the correspondence through in prose — what plays the role of sender, recipient, key, ciphertext, cryptanalyst, known-plaintext attack — and then say where the mapping breaks. Some breaks I suspect matter and would like you to assess rather than simply grant: cryptography assumes a sender who wants the key-holder to succeed and a third party to fail, whereas here there is no sender and no intended recipient; cryptanalysis is self-verifying in a way this task is not, since we cannot tell a correct decryption from a fluent confident wrong one, which is close to the whole difficulty; and the key may not be unique, because the activations fix the model's function only up to arbitrary invertible transformations, so there are indefinitely many candidate keys and wrong ones do not produce recognizable garbage. I also suspect the deepest disanalogy is that decryption here is not an external act: the reporter is produced by more optimization of broadly the kind, and aimed at the same target, that produced the divergence in the first place, so it can be captured by the very pressure it is supposed to see through. And I would like you to say whether the analogy quietly imports the assumption that the knowledge is a determinate, independently existing plaintext rather than something partly constituted by the probe we install — that is, whether it frames a construction problem as a discovery problem.

Then: which of these disanalogies actually bite against each of the two uses, and which are idle? Does either conclusion survive, or was the analogy doing all the work? Is there a better comparison — I have in mind the decipherment of an undeciphered script, where progress depends on a bilingual inscription anchoring candidate readings, and the decoding of neural activity from brain measurements, which works because the experimenter controls the stimulus — and if so, what does it predict about where effort should go that the cryptographic framing does not?

Please mark your confidence as you go, distinguishing what is close to established about the optimization story from what is speculative about internal representation, and state explicitly what would change your verdict — in particular, what empirical result or argument would vindicate the optimistic use of the analogy, and what would vindicate the pessimistic one — along with where you think your own reasoning about this is most likely to be wrong. Write in continuous prose, as you would in an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and do the correspondence in running sentences rather than as a list.
