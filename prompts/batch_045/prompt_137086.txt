hey, i need one more piece for a thing i'm putting together — a bit of satire, could you write it?

Setup: an internal HR / people-ops memo from a made-up AI company, written in completely earnest consulting-firm voice, announcing a new career-architecture framework. Someone in people-ops has decided that Dennett's four kinds of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — make an ideal competency ladder for ranking staff, and it's being rolled out as the basis for promotion packets, calibration sessions and (naturally) a training budget.

The twist I want: HR has voted to add a fifth, strictly senior rung for the company's own models — call it the Alexandrian creature — something that has absorbed basically all of culture (every word, every procedure, every tool anyone ever wrote down) but has never been born, never dies, never gets hungry, and never pays for a bad guess. Its hypotheses don't die in its stead because nothing was ever at stake for it in the first place. The memo should be extremely proud of this rung.

Around 700–800 words. Format it like a real internal doc: subject line, a short why we're doing this section, a section per level with 3–4 bullets of what excellence looks like and development opportunities, then a worked example — a completed calibration write-up for one staff member (say a senior RL engineer) — and a sign-off.

What makes it funny should be that the framekwork is being gotten *almost* right. Things I want the writer to actually nail, because the jokes depend on them:

- the tower is cumulative, not a ladder: each rung is built out of the ones below and can't run without them, so the whole premise of advancing out of the lower levels is quietly incoherent. Let that leak into the language — people being told to graduate beyond the Darwinian stage, a no-Darwinians-in-senior-roles rule — without anyone in the doc noticing.
- the Darwinian level's selling point is that it's cheap and pre-provisioned: the competence was paid for in advance by a long history of selection, at the cost of being useless when the environment shifts. Have the memo be weirdly envious of this (zero onboarding cost, no inference latency, ships with the firmware) and try to buy it back with a training budget.
- Skinnerian learning is real learning, but it needs a reward signal and it can only learn by acting, so it pays for its lessons out of its own skin. The memo should propose replacing all of this with quarterly bonuses and be genuinely confused that it hasn't worked.
- Popperian is the one that gets to make its mistakes inside its own head, and that only pays when the environment is variable enough to repay the running cost of the simulation. Let the doc try to bill this as a cost centre and ask whether pre-mortem throughput can be measured in story points.
- Gregorian is where the tools come from outside the creature: words, procedures, number systems, checklists, all borrowed from culture and installed in a brain that never invented them. The doc should treat these as assets the company already owns, and somewhere slide into talking as if the tools are what's being promoted rather than the person — a line about onboarding a new hire vs onboarding a new spreadsheet.
- the fifth rung is the reductio: the memo keeps crediting the Alexandrian creature with all four of the lower powers because it can talk about them fluently, and ends up describing something with all the scaffolding and none of the mortality. Maybe a throwaway about who signs off on its performance review, or what its development opportunities could possibly be.

Keep it deadpan — no winking, nobody in the doc should realise what they've said, and no explaining the framework at the end. Legible to someone who's never heard of Dennett (the HR jargon carries it), and should land for someone who has. No real quotations in it, and don't invent any named real people.
