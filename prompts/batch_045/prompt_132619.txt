In my decision theory seminar we read that paper on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL): in a big enough universe my decision is evidence about what other, causaly disconnected agents with different values are deciding, so there are gains from trade from cooperating with them, and the model says all cooperators have to maximize the same weighted sum of everyone's utility functions to hit a Pareto-optimal compromise, with the Nash bargaining solution as a Schelling point and dependency equilibria (not Nash equilibria) as the right equilibrium concept. Someone in my study group turned it into this argument and I cant find the hole:

(1) Under ECL, nearly all the expected value of anything I do comes from what it indicates about the actions of the many agents correlated with me, not from its causal effects inside my own light cone.
(2) So for practical purposes I should choose the action that fits the best group outcome, i.e. the one that maximizes the weighted sum the cooperators are all using.
(3) That weighted sum, and the disagreement point it's measured from, is a function of everyone's default action: what each of us would do if no compromise were reached.
(4) So I can move the compromise toward my own values by worsening my default action — credibly committing to something the others hate my doing absent a bargain raises my baseline and buys me a bigger share.
(5) And it doesn't help that the paper notes the gains shrink as your credence in any particular partner drops: in a large universe the number of candidate partners grows as fast as the credence per partner falls, so the incentive in (4) applies to all of them at once.
(6) So ECL doesn't just uncover gains from trade, it hands every agent a standing reason to degrade their own default behaviour — be nastier, more stubborn, quicker to walk away — since that's the cheapest way to improve your terms of trade; but a theory of rational cooperation that rewards making yourself a worse cooperator is broken, so something above has to go.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (3): the whole selling point of ECL is that correlation rather than causation does the work, and it really does look like where the compromise lands depends on the baseline. If forced I'd swallow (4) or even (6), but I want to know what I'm paying either way.

Which step do you reject, why does it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion.
