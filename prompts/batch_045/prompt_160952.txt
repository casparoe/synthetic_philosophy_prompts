I have been asked to contribute a short position piece to a graduate workshop on the ethics of migration, and before I commit the view to print I would like an audit of whether the parts fit together. My position is the following.

(1) Coercively keeping a peaceful person out of a territory is a serious moral act rather than a mere failure to aid; there is a strong pro tanto presumption against it, and the burden of justification lies with the excluder and must be dischargeable to the person excluded.

(2) I reject the club analogy and the joint-ownership analogy: whatever right to exclude exists cannot be derived from the associational choices of individual members, nor from a group's property-like relation to a territory.

(3) I nevertheless take freedom of association seriously as a non-instrumental personal-liberty right — there is something a person is owed in being able to decline association, including economic association — and partly for that reason I resist arguments that simply aggregate the interests of natives and of migrants into one impartial sum.

(4) Because (2) removes the individualist grounds, I want to locate any right to exclude in something collective: a people's interest in self-determination, understood as shaping the terms of their common life.

(5) I also want to allow that a state may give some weight to the interests of its own members over those of foreigners; partiality is not simply corruption.

(6) A distinction I have been trying to make work in my own writing: whether a given exclusion is permissible is not separable across time. Holding fixed both the applicant's claims and the present circumstances, a polity that has admitted very many people over the preceding decade may permissibly refuse the next applicant in virtue of what it has already done. Call this path-dependence in the right to exclude.

(7) Two case verdicts. A state whose own past extractive or colonial policies foreseeably produced an applicant's reasons for seeking entry has a diminished right to exclude that applicant. But I do not want this to generalize into a diminished right to exclude everyone in comparable material need.

Go through these pair by pair and tell me which conflicts are real and which only look like conflicts, ranked by how much damage each does to the package as a whole; for each real conflict, say what would have to be given up and what that costs me elsewhere in the set. I do not know myself where the fault line lies, so do not assume I have diagnosed it, and tell me whether the set can be repaired by a single revision or needs two. Argue from the commitments themselves — I am not asking who has defended which view.
