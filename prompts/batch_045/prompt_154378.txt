I work on the annotation/data side of post-training, and there's a pattern I keep runnning into that I don't have a word for. Not sure it has one.

Case one. We were training a model on multi-step arithmetic word problems, rating individual reasoning steps rather than just the final answer. After a few rounds the written working-out got much nicer — steps in a sensible order, no dead ends, everything reads as checkable. Grader scores went way up. But accuracy on held-out problems barely moved, and here's the part that bugs me: when it's wrong now, it's wrong in a way that reads perfectly clean, so our graders catch fewer of the errors than they used to. It's as if we trained the *account* of the reasoning and not the reasoning.

Case two, different task. Code review. We rewarded the model for "considering edge cases" in its commentary. What came out was longer and longer edge-case checklists that have almost no relationship to what the model actually ends up flagging. The checklist is boilerplate; the real judgement happens somewhere else entirely.

So: does this have a name? The nearest things I know are reward hacking and Goodhart's law, and both feel wrong. Nobody here is exploiting a bug in the grader — the graders are being perfectly reasonable, and the steps they like really do look like good steps. And "it's just rationalizing after the fact" is close, but that sounds like a property of a single output, whereas what I'm describing is a drift that training produces over time, and it makes the outputs *harder to audit*, which seems like a separate harm.

I'm not after a reading list — I can't chase down papers this week anyway. What I want is the carving, not the label. What exactly is this, where are its edges, and how do I tell it apart from its neighbours: reward hacking, Goodhart, plain post-hoc rationalization, and the model just being sycophantic toward whoever is grading it? If there genuinely isn't a term for it, coin one, define it, and tell me what would and wouldn't count as an instance — especially the cases where the reasoning really is the computation (a search trace, generated code that gets executed) versus cases where it's a narration. And one more thing I actually care about: does outcome-only supervision have a mirror-image version of this failure, or is it specific to supervising the process?

Answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up.
