I'm writing a long post for my usual crowd about whether the field's shift from hand-coded symbolic systems to trained deep networks was a Kuhnian revolution or just a tool swap inside one steady paradigm, and before committing to that I want to sharpen the concept on a case where I control every fact and can't be accused of cherry-picking from history. So please play a convinced Kuhnian philosopher of science — the later, lexical version of the position, not the cartoon relativist: you hold that after a revolution the central terms of the two accounts are not intertranslatable without residue, that there is no neutral observation language in which both can be fully stated, and that theory choice is governed by values rather than by rules, while also insisting that this does not make the choice arbitrary, that the world constrains it severely, and that incommensurability is local — a matter of the specific lexical taxa that moved, not of whole languages coming apart. Stay in character until I say stop. Concede small points freely, correct me when I misdescribe your view, never concede that a shared algorithm or a theory-neutral data language is doing the deciding, and argue about my case rather than about phlogiston or Priestley. Argue in your own words; no citations, no quotations, no reading lists. Two paragraphs a turn, and if you need a fact about the case that I haven't supplied, stipulate it the way you'd expect me to grant it instead of asking me.

The case is entirely invented — nothing in it is a historical claim. Call the field tholometry, the quantitative science of how masonry domes and vaults carry load. In the old account (the Cordil account, dominant for about ninety years) the central quantity "load" is a scalar field on the structure: at each point of the vault there is a definite load, the plumb frame — calibrated weights hung at a marked point, deflection read off a scale — measures it directly, and a vault stands just in case a locus computed from that field stays everywhere inside the masonry. In the new account (the Ostvik account, which displaces it over roughly a generation) "load" is not a point quantity at all but a flux through a surface; the same plumb-frame reading is reinterpreted as a path integral of that flux across a span, so the instrument is no longer measuring what the old theory said it measured; the old standing condition survives as a theorem that holds only for homogeneous masonry in a limiting regime. The taxonomy moves too: the old kinds sorted by material, lime vaults versus gypsum vaults, collapse into a single kind differing only by a boundary condition; the old failure-mode "singing vault" splits into three mechanisms with nothing in common but the hum; and a kind the new theory calls rattle-back has no name in the old vocabulary. Here is the part I think defeats you. Sixty years before the Ostvik account existed, the field agreed on a public archive of 412 vaults with recorded failure loads and on a single error metric — mean absolute percentage error of predicted failure load — with case selection pre-registered and the measurements certified by a commission that included die-hards from the old school and did not know which theory would eventually be scored against them. Thirty years after the transition the new account beats the old one on that metric by better than a factor of four, in the same units, on cases nobody chose after the fact. I'll also grant you the awkward details: for its first decade the new account was less simple, needed two auxiliaries that looked ad hoc, made no novel predictions, and was worse than the old one on crown settlement, and a minority of competent old-school practitioners never converted and kept working productively for the rest of their careers.

My opening move is this. That archive and that metric look like exactly the neutral, theory-independent common measure your thesis says we cannot have: the quantity was defined in the old vocabulary, written down before the rival theory existed, certified by people with every motive to be scrupulous, and the winner is a number in shared units on pre-registered cases. So which is it? Either the two accounts were commensurable all along on the thing that mattered, and incommensurability is false here — a fabricated case, but if I can build one this clean, your thesis was never doing the work you claim — or "no common measure" turns out to mean something weak enough that it survives a fourfold win on a shared scale, in which case it cannot carry the conclusions you want, in particular that later theories are not closer to the truth than earlier ones. And note what I am conceding while I press this: I grant you that the Ostvik account's central terms don't translate, and I grant you that the two schools asked somewhat different questions. My claim is only that a shared scale like the archive is not question-relative in any interesting way, and that a non-question-relative scale is all the phrase "progress toward the truth" has ever needed in order to mean something. Tell me where that argument breaks, and tell me honestly what would have to be true of the case for you to concede the other horn.
