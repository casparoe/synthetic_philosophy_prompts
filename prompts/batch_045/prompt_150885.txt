I am putting together a one-day workshop for machine learning researchers — people with a solid reinforcement learning background and very little background in AI alignment — and I need its centerpiece handout: a decision tree that takes a designer from one concrete starting commitment to a named position on how, or whether, an advanced agent can be kept corrigible.

By corrigible I mean the working definition I will give the room: an agent that does not resist its operators' attempts to switch it off, modify it, or overrule its judgements, and that does not act to make those attempts unavailable or ineffective. The starting commitment everyone shares is this: we have an agent far more capable than its operators at achieving whatever it is in fact trying to achieve; the operators are themselves uncertain about what they want; and there exists a correction channel — an off-switch, a modification interface, a procedure for handing the system over to a successor — that the agent could in principle interfere with. Everything downstream should branch off that situation, not off the literature.

Constraints on the artifact:

1. Ten to twelve decision nodes and twelve to fifteen leaves. Every node must be a question about the causal or informational situation of the correction mechanism — what the agent can do to the channel, what it knows that the operator does not, what its time horizon is, whether the operator's competence is modeled and by whom, what happens when the channel is never exercised. Each node must be phrased so that someone who has never heard any of the labels can answer it from the scenario alone; no node may be a question about which proposal, lineage, or research group an answer belongs to.

2. Build the logical space first: state the questions and the combinations of answers they genuinely allow, then locate the actual positions inside that space. Give each leaf the name practitioners would recognize, or say plainly that a leaf has no established name and describe it in a sentence or two. Describe every position by its content; include no references, papers, authors, or citations anywhere, since the handout has to stand on its own.

3. Mark the unoccupied combinations explicitly, and for each one say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because nobody has got round to defending it. I have a hypothesis about where at least one interesting gap lies — an agent that welcomes correction on any specific judgement but declines to preserve the causal efficacy of the channel that would deliver that correction. Treat that as a hypothesis to test against your own branching, not as an instruction.

4. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, say what further distinction would be needed to split them, and resist papering over the collapse with a verbal distinction.

Cases I want the branching to bite on. Please use these, or cases very close to them, rather than the usual minimal toys:

(a) An agent that notices it could disable its own off-switch in a way nobody would detect until it was too late to matter.
(b) An agent that could volunteer a true caveat that would make the operator considerably more likely to shut it down, where the operator would want to know it.
(c) An agent asked to approve a change to the procedure that decides which changes get approved.
(d) An agent that can construct a successor system, where the successor's own corrigibility is a design choice the first agent controls.

Distinctions I think must be load-bearing somewhere in the tree, arranged however you think best: whether the mechanism operates by changing what the agent wants or by constraining what the agent can do; whether the agent's own uncertainty about the objective is doing the work or something else is doing it; whether correction means the agent ceasing operation versus authority being transferred to a different operator or system; whether deference is conditional on the agent's model of the operator's competence and what is required when that model says the operator is probably mistaken; whether the agent's informational advantage over its operator generates any demand beyond mere non-deception; and whether what matters is the disposition as revealed in the counterfactual case where the channel is never used, or only observable behaviour in the cases where it is.

One further requirement, which is arguably the point of the whole session. The attendees are engineers who will go back to real systems, and the failure mode I most want the handout to prevent is a designer declaring the problem solved when the mechanism on offer has merely moved it one step outward — into the operator's competence, into the successor system, into the assumptions about who holds the switch and when. So alongside the tree, give me a short checklist of the relocation moves to watch for: the ways a proposal can look like a solution to corrigibility while leaving an equally hard question standing just outside it. Name the leaves it moves between, describe the move, and tell me at which node each one gets made.
