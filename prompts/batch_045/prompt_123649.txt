OK so I'm in a reading group on philosophy of physics and one of the postdocs put this on the board last week and I cant find the exit. I think it proves too much but I can't say where it breaks.

Setup so we're on the same page. Take a solution of GR: a manifold, a metric, some matter fields. Pick a region H and a smooth map of the manifold to itself that is the identity everywhere outside H but moves the points around inside H. Drag all the fields along it. Because the equations are generally covariant you get another solution, and it agrees with the first one everywhere outside H. So the world outside H is fixed and what happens in H isn't. Call a theory "holey" if it admits this trick.

The argument:

(1) If a theory is holey, then the complete state of the world outside some region fails to determine what happens inside that region.
(2) A theory for which that's true is indeterministic. That's just what determinism means.
(3) So holeyness implies indeterminism, for any theory whatsoever. Nothing in (1) or (2) mentions gravity, or the metric, or background independence. It's a schema.
(4) GR is holey. So — given that spacetime points are the kind of thing that can be the same or different ones across possibilities — GR is indeterministic.
(5) But look at what else is holey. Electromagnetism: add to the potential A the gradient of a smooth function that vanishes outside H. Same sources, same E and B everywhere, same A outside H, different A inside H. Holey. And Maxwell theory is the other theory everybody calls deterministic.
(6) And here's the case I actually can't get around, because it's a spacetime symmetry and not an internal one. Write Maxwell theory on a flat background but in generally covariant form: don't fix the metric as a background object, let it be a field that is required to be flat. Now take a hole map and drag the metric and the fields along it. Flatness is preserved, the equations are preserved, everything outside H is untouched, and the points inside H have moved. Holey. And this is just special relativity. Unlike the gauge case there's also no invariant that stays put — the metric and the fields inside H really are different tensor fields, so I can't say "same fields, different bookkeeping."
(7) Either EM and SR are indeterministic, which is absurd, or the schema in (1)–(3) is broken. And if it's broken, it gives me no reason whatsoever to give up substantivalism, because whatever mistake it makes in the SR case it makes in the GR case too, in the same order.

If I have to choose I'd rather keep "EM and SR are deterministic" than keep the hole argument, but I'd like the hole argument to keep some bite, and I don't want to just intone "diffeomorphic models represent the same world" as if that settled anything.

So: wich step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Concretely —

- Is (6) even legit? If I've cheated by quietly swapping special relativity for some other theory, say exactly what changed when I stopped treating the metric as fixed.
- If the right move is at (1) or (2) — something like "mathematically distinct models aren't automatically distinct possibilities" — then what am I committing to when I say that about spacetime points? I get why it's fine to say it about potentials. I don't get why it's fine to say it about points, or whether saying it is even different from giving up substantivalism, in which case the whole thing wasn't a reductio of the schema at all, it was just the original argument wearing a hat.
- And if I do pay that price, is there any interesting kind of substantivalist left for me to be, or is the "sophisticated" version just relationism in a costume?
