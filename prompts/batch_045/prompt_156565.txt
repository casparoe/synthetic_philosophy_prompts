I'm putting together a six-month, single-researcher project proposal for a small funder that has told us it will fund philosophy only when the result would change what an actual grantmaker decides in the next two years, and I want the project to be about whether the simulation argument should dampen fanaticism about the far future. How I got here: our reading group spent a term on longtermism and split badly over the fanaticism worry — roughly, the worry that expected-value reasoning over astronomical stakes plus very small probabilities licenses abandoning everything else we might fund in favour of a handful of long-shot, far-future bets. Someone floated the simulation hypothesis as a cure: if there is a substantial chance we are in a simulation, then the far future we are supposed to be safeguarding may be short, cheaply resourced, or liable to be switched off, so the astronomical numbers cannot be taken at face value and we can go back to ordinary, risk-averse prioritisation. I don't think that move works as stated, and I suspect the interesting version of the question is much more fine-grained. My supervisor thinks the whole topic is a gimmick, so the proposal also has to contain, in effect, an answer to her.

My half-formed idea is that the simulation hypothesis bundles at least four separate considerations that people run together and that pull in different directions. First, termination: a simulation can be ended or reset, so the expected duration of the future we can influence is discounted relative to what a straight longtermist calculation assumes. Second, value density: the number of minds per unit of underlying computation may be higher or lower than in a base-level future — and if simulations run simulations, with each deeper level cheaper in base resources, then the cheapest deepest level could dominate total mind-years, which would amplify the astronomical numbers rather than shrink them. Third, causal permeability: if the simulators can intervene, branch, roll back, or reward, then our far future may not be a single causal track, which matters both for whether extinction is irreversible from the inside and for how value should be aggregated across branches. Fourth, a regress or nesting effect: if our descendants will themselves run many ancestor simulations, then a large share of the value in our own far future is simulated value, and the value of what we do now partly depends on facts about a reality we cannot observe. My guess is that the dampening conclusion needs the first three to line up in a particular way, and that the second and third are at least as likely to reverse it — but I can't yet say what would settle the matter.
