I teach an upper-level seminar on transitional justice, and next week we're screening Campanella's The Secret in Their Eyes (El secreto de sus ojos) as the one work of fiction on the syllabus — everything else is the usual comparative material on South Africa's disclosure-conditional amnesty, Argentina's blanket amnesty laws and the later pardons, and Rwanda's gacaca. I want to use the film for more than atmosphere, but I also don't want to wring it into an illustration of a thesis my students could get out of a handbook, so I'd like a reading that goes in both directions. Practical constraints first: no bibliography, no case names, no citations — I'm teaching this from memory in a room without reliable wifi and I can't check anything, so please don't hang a claim on a source or a date you couldn't stand behind from memory either. And don't quote the film; paraphrase the scenes, since I'd have no way to verify a line and I'd rather not read a misquote back to my class. Assume I've seen it and skip plot summary. A friend in film studies keeps telling me the movie is really about the failure of narrative rather than about justice at all, and I half believe her, so feel free to tell me if she's closer to right than I am.

The two things I actually want to press on are distinctions I've been kicking around myself — they're my coinages, not terms of art, so don't go looking for them in the literature or tell me who made them first. First: call it the unsayable residue. A truth commission is a machine for turning wrongdoing into propositions — who did what to whom, when, where the body is — and its amnesty is usually conditioned on the perpetrator supplying those propositions himself. But some of what the victims and the investigators in this film know isn't propositional at all: it's read off a face in an old photograph, off who is looking at whom, off a pattern nobody can state as a fact without it sounding like nothing. That kind of knowledge is evidentially and morally loaded, but it's structurally inadmissible to a hearing, and a commission that ran perfectly would still leave it undischarged. Second: call it the acknowledgement hostage. Once the state stops being willing to punish, the one remaining good the victim might get — being acknowledged by the person who did it, having the wrong done named by him — is a good only the wrongdoer can supply, and only voluntarily, since compelled acknowledgement isn't acknowledgement. So amnesty doesn't merely forbear punishing; it effectively hands the wrongdoer a permanent veto over the victim's last remedy. In the film there's a third wrinkle I'd like you to name if it's worth naming: the state that owes Morales an answer doesn't just withdraw, it ends up employing the killer, so the addressee of the claim is captured rather than merely absent.

So: read the film through those. Where does it dramatize the residue and the veto, and where does it dramatize something my distinctions get wrong? I'd especially like you to sit with the ending — Morales keeping Gómez for decades in isolation and silence, rather than killing him, is a private substitute for a state that wouldn't answer, and it looks to me like a deliberate inversion of a truth commission: it forbears nothing, demands no speech, and withdraws address altogether instead of extending it. Is that the film's answer to my second point, or is it the film showing what happens when the state leaves a claim like that lying around, i.e., a cure that's worse than the disease and that we're meant to recoil from? Does the film's own formal apparatus — the photographs, the eyes, Espósito's inability to write the novel — actually argue for the first distinction, or am I reading my philosophy into a melodramatic device? And be honest about the places the reading distorts the film: Gómez is a lone obsessive rather than anyone acting on political orders, the case is set in 1974–75 so the impunity on screen is pre-transition and pre-dates the amnesty laws my seminar is about, and much of the machinery here is genre, not argument. Relatedly: would the film's complaint dissolve under a South African–style conditional amnesty (full public disclosure in exchange for immunity) or under something like gacaca, where the addressee is the neighbourhood rather than a court? My hunch is that it partly dissolves and partly doesn't, and I want you to say precisely which part is which.

One more thing on register, since this is going straight into a lecture. Please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on transitional justice — what the standard comparative story about amnesty actually supports, versus the claims that real specialists argue about — and mark your own reading of the film as the speculative thing it is. If a claim I've made above (that amnesty transfers a veto, that the residue can't be given in testimony) is itself a contested position rather than background, say so instead of letting me present it to students as where the field stands. If it helps, tell me what a good student who dislikes the reading would say, and finish, if there's anything to say, with what the film adds to this argument that the papers don't.
