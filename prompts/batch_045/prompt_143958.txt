I should say who I am before I ask anything. I have no academic background and no formal training in philosophy: I left school at eighteen, taught myself programming, and have spent roughly fifteen years designing, operating, and debugging distributed systems. I am fluent in that vocabulary at a working-engineer level — processes and nodes, local state, message passing, partial failure, consensus and quorums, logs and event ordering, happens-before and vector clocks and the fact that there is no global clock, merge functions and conflict-free replicated data types, levels of abstraction and coarse-graining, specifications versus implementations, observability from outside, and equivalence notions like bisimulation. I can follow an impossibility argument when I see one, but I have no logic beyond what the job required, and I may well be misusing the philosophical labels I have picked up on my own.

Please act as a professional philosopher of mind who is also comfortable reading computer science, and take this seriously rather than humorously: I am not looking for a popular-science gloss or for encouragement. Think carefully before you write, and begin with a short, visible brainstorming phase in which you list the candidate mappings between my vocabulary and the philosophical material, mark the ones that merely rename things, mark the ones that quietly smuggle in an assumption from my side, and say which ones you are going to keep and why. Then write the translation. Do not give me a bibliography, reading lists, or citations — I have no library access and I will look up names later. Refer to positions and arguments by description, and if I have mangled a label, correct it in passing without turning the correction into a literature lesson.

Here is what I want translated. Over the last couple of years, reading about panpsychism on my own, I have kept hitting the same wall. The view seems to get its initial plausibility from an argument about physics: that physical theory characterises matter only structurally or dispositionally — in terms of what it does, how it stands to other things, what an outside observer could in principle detect — and leaves the intrinsic nature of the stuff itself unspecified; that we know of one case of intrinsic nature directly, namely experience; and so the natural, non-miraculous conjecture is that the intrinsic nature of matter is experiential. But then the view owes us an account of how the many little experiences compose into the one big one, and that is the part I cannot get a grip on. On the page it looks like a well-specified problem. When I try to say it in my own vocabulary it sometimes looks like a confusion produced by mixing two levels of description. I cannot tell which impression is the right one.

So, as faithfully as you can, in my vocabulary and not in yours:

First, restate the motivating argument from physics. My rough attempt is that physics hands you only the interface and the state machine — the observable behaviour — and says nothing about the implementation, and that panpsychism is a conjecture about what the implementation is made of. Is that fair? Where does it bend the original?

Second, restate the composition problem itself, splitting it the way I have seen it split: why there is one subject rather than an enormous scattered plurality of them; how a very small repertoire of micro-level qualities could yield the rich and specific qualities I seem to find at my own level; and how micro-level experiences could come already structured in space and time in a way that lets them compose at all. Say which of these three has a clean counterpart in systems vocabulary, which has to be distorted to fit, and which has no counterpart.

Third, restate the main ways out. Brute or strong emergence, where composition simply produces something extra. The identity or constitution style of answer, where the macro experience just is the micro experiences arranged a certain way, so that there is no further thing to explain. The reverse direction, where the one big mind comes first and the problem becomes how it comes to be divided rather than how parts come to be summed. And the deflationary option, where nothing needs composing because the appearance of a single unified subject is itself partly an artifact of description.

Fourth, and this is the half-formed idea I most want you to work on: the thing I think of as the masking or overwriting worry. In my world, when I re-describe a swarm of nodes as a single service, the fine-grained description does not disappear. Both descriptions are true at once, neither destroys the other, the choice between them is pragmatic, and asking how many services there really are is close to a verbal question — the answer is relative to a purpose and to a set of re-identification conventions. So if macro-consciousness stood to micro-consciousness as the service stands to the nodes, I should expect both, and the familiar question of why we do not seem to find swarms of tiny subjects should have roughly the same status as why we do not find swarms of tiny services. But the writers I have read treat the absence of the tiny subjects as a genuine explanatory burden rather than as a fact about the coarseness of the description. Is the disanalogy in the direction of composition — macro-subjects are simply not abstractions of the kind services are — or in the direction of the evidence, or in the thought that being a subject is not a matter of degree or granularity in the way that being a service plainly is? Say where my own framing is confused, and do not be polite about it.

Then the residue report, which I take to be the interesting part. I want you to be explicit about three things. Whether the phrase that there is something it is like for a system can be expressed in my vocabulary at all, or whether everything I am able to say with it collapses into the claim that the system is usefully describable as an agent with states — and if it does collapse, whether that is because my vocabulary was deliberately built for third-person specification and excludes the first-personal by design, or because the philosophical phrase was never doing more work than the third-personal facts. Whether my vocabulary contains any way to state a fact of the matter about whether two processes are one process or two, given that all my re-identification criteria, failure detectors, and membership protocols are partly conventional — and, for each place where the conventionality is doing argumentative work in your translation, flag it rather than hiding it. And whether any feature of my vocabulary forces a choice among the options above rather than merely redescribing them: for instance, does thinking of composition as a merge function with a fixed algebra quietly push toward the identity answer and away from brute emergence? Whenever a mapping is really an argument in favour of one position, say so and separate it from the translation.

Finish with a short, honest paragraph on whether the composition problem is well posed, and one on what someone with my tools and my limitations could actually do with it: what question, stated in systems vocabulary, has a determinate answer that I could in principle reach, what would count as a real answer rather than a restatement of the puzzle, and what would count as the project failing. Aim for something I can read in fifteen minutes.
