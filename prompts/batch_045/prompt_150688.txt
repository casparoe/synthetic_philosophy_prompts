I work on interpretability and I want to know what my own field's routine methods commit me to. I'm not a philosopher, so please reconstruct the view rather than name it and move on.

Here are three things we do constantly. (i) Linear probing: freeze a model, take activations at some layer, fit a linear classifier for some property of the input — is this passage in French, is this board position a check, is the speaker being sarcastic — and treat high accuracy as evidence that the model "represents" that property, with the probe's weight vector identifying the direction that encodes it. (ii) Activation patching: run the model on a corrupted input, overwrite a small slice of the activations with the matching slice from a clean run, and treat recovery of the correct output as evidence that the patched site is where the computation "keeps" the relevant information. (iii) Dictionary learning: fit a sparse autoencoder to reconstruct activations as sparse sums of learned directions, call those directions the model's features, and go on to describe behavior — including interventions that steer behavior — in terms of them.

What puzzles me is that these practices sit badly with three findings, which I'll stipulate rather than ask you to check:

(a) Inside a single frozen model there seems to be no unique direction for a given feature. Probes trained with different seeds find many mutually non-parallel directions that classify the feature equally accurately and generalize equally well off-distribution, and directions that support a good probe for one feature also support good probes for features that have nothing intuitively to do with it. Dictionary learning doesn't settle the matter either: as the dictionary grows, features keep splitting into finer ones with no sign of convergence on a privileged basis, and when a coarse direction is available the finer ones go unused.

(b) Two networks trained from different random seeds on the same data can be stitched: insert a learned linear map from network A's activations at layer k into network B's layer k+1 and the composite performs as well as either network alone. Yet the direction each network uses for a given feature differs, and standard measures of similarity between the two activation spaces come out low.

(c) In case (b) the networks fail each other's probes — a probe trained on A's activations does poorly on B's, and vice versa — even though the two are behaviorally interchangeable in the stitching sense.

So: what view of representation does this cluster of practices presuppose? Does it require a causal-role view, on which an internal state represents F only because the system's downstream processing exploits it as F? Does it require only a covariational or informational view, on which carrying recoverable information about F is enough? Is it compatible with a view on which representation attributions are merely useful summaries of an input–output map with no further fact of the matter — and does that last view, if it is genuinely available, quietly rule out the realist story the methods are normally told under? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is underdetermined, i.e. where several incompatible theories would each license all three methods as we actually run them.

Then press the internal-consistency question, which is the part I care about most. Do (i), (ii) and (iii) presuppose the same thing? Probing is correlational, patching is interventional, and dictionary learning optimizes an objective the model itself never optimized — so which of them, if any, is the authority on what the network represents, and what should give if they disagree? In particular: can a causal-role view survive (a) by locating content in the whole activation vector rather than in any direction, and can any view that requires a unique privileged decomposition survive (a) and (b) together?

Finally, the practical payout. Suppose we adopted the deflationary view — that claims about what a network represents are answerable only to their usefulness for prediction, control and explanation-to-us. What would concretely change in how we run, report, or trust probes, patching experiments, and dictionaries? If the honest answer is that nothing would change, say that plainly and explain why; I'd find that as informative as the opposite.

Please keep the whole answer under 900 words. Lead with a single sentence stating the strongest commitment the three practices cannot jointly give up; then give the reconstruction of the implicit theory, the strict-versus-merely-compatible distinction, the internal-consistency verdict, and the consequences under the deflationary view. A short table is welcome. Do not cite papers or point me at a literature — I can't follow references, and I don't want them.
