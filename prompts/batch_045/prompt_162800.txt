I moderate a university colloquium series, and next month's session is a long-form interview with a political theorist who has spent years reconstructing Hannah Arendt's essays on truth and lying in politics into what she calls the "defactualization reading." I want questions that engage the machinery of that reading rather than have her restate it. Its moving parts, as she presents them, are these:

- Arendt's target is not the older lie that conceals a secret (which quietly presupposes the fact it hides) but image-making: a lie that proposes to replace a piece of the world wholesale, so that what erodes is not this or that belief but the fact/fiction distinction itself.
- The endpoint of sustained political lying, on this reading, is not popular credulity but a settled cynicism — the stance that everything is possible and nothing in particular is true — and it is this erosion of our bearings in the real world, not the falsehood of any proposition, that constitutes the political damage.
- Arendt is nonetheless read as optimistic: because a lie is unworldly, the liar must keep remodelling a reality he does not control, and the story eventually comes apart. Theorists of lying, on this view, have systematically overestimated deception and underestimated its built-in instability.
- Her own extension is what she calls saturation: a speaker who no longer needs any single story to be believed, because emitting many mutually incompatible accounts at volume removes the demand for coherence altogether. Saturation, she argues, is what disables Arendt's optimistic mechanism.
- Her remedy is institutional: factual truth, unlike rational truth, is contingent and cannot survive on persuasiveness alone, so it needs guardians whose authority is evidentiary and compulsory rather than argumentative — courts, archives, universities, independent presses.
- Applied to historiography, the framework distinguishes revision (dispute about significance conducted inside a shared record) from denial (an attempt at wholesale substitution of the record).
- Her stock deflection, delivered with some impatience, is: "that's a category mistake — you're treating factual truth as if it were rational truth."

Draft eight to ten questions for a forty-five-minute episode, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Include at least one question on each of the following, and make the sequence such that the category-mistake deflection becomes progressively harder to reach for:

1. Self-deception. Arendt's liar is defined as one who knows the truth he denies; ask what happens to the apparatus when the speaker is himself saturated and half believes his own output.
2. The cynic. Is the person who says that everything is possible really operating without the true/false distinction, or deploying it in a deflationary way? Press on whether "loss of the distinction" is a genuine psychological description or a metaphor doing argumentative work.
3. Novelty. Is saturation a genuinely distinct technique or merely the old lie at scale? Anticipate her answer that quantity now does the work consistency used to do, and follow up by asking why real regimes still police a single official line and punish particular reporters if coherence no longer matters, and what distinguishes saturation from noise that an audience simply ignores.
4. The institutional remedy against Arendt's own suspicion that truth's compulsion is despotic and alien to the political realm. Anticipate her distinction between the compulsion of a record and the compulsion of an argument, and press on where exactly the boundary falls — what makes a court's finding that something happened different in kind from a ruler's claim to know the truth.
5. The revision/denial boundary. Give her the hard case: a historian who concedes the documentary record in full but argues that the event's causal role and moral significance were manufactured afterwards, so that "what really happened" carries none of the weight the record is assumed to carry. Ask whether her framework classifies this as revision or as defactualization, and what follows for the classification if the two cannot be separated in practice.

End with one question asking what observation would count as evidence against her reading — what she would treat as disconfirmation, given that the outcome she predicts is a habit of mind rather than a measurable event — and close with a question the category-mistake move cannot absorb. Assume an audience that has read the essays; no biographical or "what drew you to Arendt" questions, and no requests for further reading.
