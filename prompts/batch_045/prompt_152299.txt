I'm a 4th-year PhD student, mostly formal epistemology with a bit of phil physics, and I've got something like two years of dissertation time left plus (fingers crossed) a first postdoc. I want to spend them on the problem of old evidence rather than on something safer, but I can't figure out which part of it is actually worth the years. The set-up I assume we agree on: you already know E, so P(E) = 1, so conditioning on E can't bump P(T) — and yet the perihelion of Mercury sure looked like it confirmed general relativity. Whatever the fix is, it has to be reachable by someone with probability theory, a bit of modal logic, and no real physics.

Here's my own provisional map, which might definately be junk. I think there are three different ways evidence can be old, and I suspect they're different problems wearing the same label:

(1) temporal oldness — you believed E before you ever had T;
(2) construction oldness — E was among the things you were staring at when you built T, so the fit is no surprise (the accommodation vs prediction thing);
(3) derivation oldness — you had T and you had E, and the only thing that's new is that someone worked out that T entails E.

On top of that there's an axis I'm much less sure about: whether this is a problem for a logic of confirmation (some relation between T, E and background) or for a norm of updating for agents who aren't logically omniscient. Those feel like genuinely different projects, and I think people slide between them.

What I actually want is an agenda, not a survey. Rank the open questions here for someone in my position — two years, formal toolkit, no physics — and defend the ranking: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable with the tools I have, which are neglected relative to how much depends on them, and which are traps that look deep but whose answer wouldn't change anything downstream. If my three-way taxonomy is the wrong way to carve the space, say what the right carving is and why the carving changes the order.

Two things I'd especially like weighed. First, my pet suspicion: that the puzzle is an artifact of reading P(E) = 1 as a credence you actually still have, when old evidence is really evidence you have a lossy memory of — so the right treatment might be a memory-or-recall model rather than a confirmation-theoretic one. Is that a research program or a dead end, and where does it sit in the ordering? Second, how much of the value here is internal to Bayesianism (patching a technical bug in a framework) versus exportable: the same structure shows up whenever a model is fitted to data and the same data is then cited back as evidence for it (double dipping, post-hoc theorizing in the replication-crisis literature), and whenever an analyst already has all the data on file and then comes up with a hypothesis that fits it. Should the export question drive the agenda, or is chasing applications a distraction from getting the core problem right?

And tell me what would move things: what result, argument, or empirical finding in the next couple of years would move a question up or down your list.
