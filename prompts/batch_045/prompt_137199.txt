I'm finally trying to write up a short paper on individual contributions to collective harms — the inefficacy objection, eating meat and flying and voting — and I'd rather have the holes poked now than after I've drafted 3000 words. It's for an upper-level undergrad ethics seminar, so it needs to be sharp but it shouldn't need a literature review. Don't give me citations or reading lists, I'll fill those in myself.

Here's where the argument is at.

Setup, which I'll compress: my skipped flight / skipped burger / skipped vote changes nothing, so the act looks morally free. The usual reply is that thresholds are uncertain, so in expectation my act nudges the probability of crossing one — tiny, nonzero — and in the voting case what I affect isn't the result but the margin, which gets read later by other people. I want to grant all of that and not spend words on it.

The case I actually care about is where the threshold move buys you nothing: continuous harms, no threshold, not a single person's experience changes even slightly. Expected-difference reasoning seems to win there, and yet the agent still feels that nothing they did mattered. My diagnosis: that leftover feeling was never causal. It's about answerability. Participate in a harmful practice and you become someone who can be asked why they took part; how big your causal footprint is has nothing to do with whether that question has an answer.

So the proposal, which I'm calling conditional exit. The duty you pick up by participating in a collectively harmful practice isn't a duty to abstain, full stop — it's a duty to make your level of participation contingent on other people's level, in a way they can see. The thought is that this turns a problem about individual causal impotence into a problem about assurance. My abstaining is pointless if nobody else's is coming, and rather then conclude that I'm therefore free to keep going, I should conclude that I have to arrange my participation so that others can condition on it: commit out loud to scaling down if and only if others scale down, put my effort into the scheme that has an actual trigger point, and so on. Consequence worth noticing: what the duty requires ends up depending on how legible your behaviour is to other people, which no causal-difference view predicts and which is an empirical question, not a moral one.

Biggest objection I need to handle, and where I'm least sure: sign uncertainty and leakage. Buy less meat, demand falls a hair, price falls a hair, someone else eats slightly more — my abstention may be not just ineffective but counterproductive. Same for the signalling version: loud conditional commitment can provoke backlash, or advertise that the practice is contested and worth defending. If I can't tell the sign of any of this, conditional exit starts to look like an expensive ritual. Tentative reply: the sign worry is completely general, it applies to every act with diffuse effects, so it can't be what explains the specific pull of the inefficacy objection, since that pull attaches to exactly the cases where the causal story looks clean. Not sure I believe this.

Also: the account gives a weird result for voting, where participation is all-or-nothing and can't be scaled. I think that's a feature — it explains why voting duties feel different in kind from consumption duties — but I haven't argued it.

What I'd like from you:

1. Is the answerability diagnosis doing any real work, or is it the causal claim in different clothes?
2. Which single premise in conditional exit is weakest, and what does the argument look like if you drop it?
3. Is my reply to the sign-uncertainty objection decent or am I cheating?
4. Does it over-generate? I'm in dozens of diffuse harmful practices; does this view put me on the hook for setting up conditional commitments about all of them, and is there a natural stopping rule?
5. The one objection you'd raise if you wanted to kill the paper — and whether it's answerable in a paragraph or whether it means the whole framing is wrong.
