I belong to a small, sporadically meeting reading group on the history of economic thought, and I have a friend — an engineer by training, with little patience for Austrian hand-waving — who is far more sympathetic to market socialism than I am. In our last session we became stuck on the socialist calculation debate, and my friend kept returning to the same analogy to make sense of Lange's reply to Mises. The analogy is that a socialist economy would be like one enormous, vertically integrated firm or, in an older image, like the whole of society organised as a single office and a single factory. Just as a large corporation replaces the market with hierarchy for many of the goods and services it needs — it does not make its design department bid against its procurement department in an open market, but instead issues internal transfer prices, budgets and managerial directives — so, my friend argues, a socialist commonwealth could replace the market with a central planning board. The board would do for the whole economy what a corporate board, or the hypothetical Walrasian auctioneer of general-equilibrium theory (the price-caller who adjusts prices until supply equals demand), is supposed to do for a single market: it would fix accounting or shadow prices for producer goods, watch inventories, raise prices when a good is in shortage and lower them when it is in glut, and repeat this trial-and-error procedure until the plan more or less balances.

The analogy is not decorative; it is meant to carry a substantive conclusion. It is supposed to make Mises's claim — that without private property in the means of production there can be no genuine monetary prices for factors of production, and therefore no economic calculation — look like a complaint about administrative inconvenience rather than an impossibility proof. If a firm can plan internally, the argument goes, why cannot the state plan for the whole? In our discussion my friend used the analogy to license the further claim that Lange's model answers Mises: because the planning board can simulate the market's price adjustments, the calculation problem is soluble in principle, and Hayek's worries about dispersed knowledge merely amount to the observation that central planning would be slow. The Hayekian side of the dispute is often illustrated by a passage in which he wrote that "The marvel is that in a case like that of a scarcity of one raw material, without an order being issued, without more than perhaps a handful of people knowing the cause, tens of thousands of people whose identity could not be ascertained by months of investigation, are made to use the material or its products more sparingly; that is, they move in the right direction." I would like you to audit the one-big-firm analogy and tell me how far it holds. Please give me an explicit correspondence table: what, inside a capitalist firm or corporation, plays the role of the market price system, of private property in capital goods, of profit-and-loss accounting, of the external market for factors, of managerial incentives, of bankruptcy or the threat of takeover, and of consumer sovereignty? Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the calculation debate from those that do not. For example, a real firm can compare its internal costs with prices for at least some inputs and outputs that are set outside the firm, and its internal transfer prices are ultimately validated by the external sale of the final product; a socialist commonwealth supposedly has no such outside. A firm's owners are residual claimants who bear liability and can go bankrupt, while its managers are monitored, however imperfectly, by capital markets and by the possibility of dismissal or takeover; none of these disciplines evidently survives translation into a Lange-style planning board, whose managers follow rules about inventory changes and whose prices are parameters in an administrative rule rather than terms of trade between independent owners. Please say which of these disanalogies are fatal to the analogy's intended use and which are merely accidental.

Finally, tell me what the analogy can and cannot carry, and whether a better analogy is available. If the one-big-firm picture is misleading, is the price system better thought of as a telecommunications network, a language, a distributed computing system, or something else — and does the better analogy change the verdict on Lange's trial-and-error board? In particular, does the board's procedure really put it in the position of the Walrasian auctioneer, or does the auctioneer metaphor itself illegitimately presuppose the very thing Mises denied, namely real bids backed by private ownership of the factors being priced? Does the firm analogy help us see why Hayek thought no board could reproduce that outcome, or does it obscure the point by making the economy sound like a single, centrally directed entity that merely needs better accounting? I do not want a bibliography or a reading list, and please do not rely on web access: simply work through the mapping, the breaking points, and the fate of the market-socialist conclusion. Please write your answer in British English.
