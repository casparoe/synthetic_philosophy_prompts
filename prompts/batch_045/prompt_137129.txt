After a long argument last night I need help working out what I actually think about mental causation. My friend ran something he called the exclusion argument: the neural realizer of my wanting tea is already enough, given the laws, to produce my reaching for the kettle; the reaching does not need a second, distinct sufficient cause; so either the wanting just is that neural state, in which case saying it caused the reaching is trivial, or it is something extra, in which case it is either powerless or a redundant second cause. And, he said, I am not allowed to appeal to redundant overdetermination here the way I do when two bricks go through a window, because the wanting is not independent of its realizer in the way one brick is independent of the other. I could not answer him. What I do have is a set of verdicts I give up only under pressure.

One. Two neighbours throw bricks through the same window, either brick alone would have sufficed, both hit. Both are causes. No hesitation.

Two. I want tea and believe there is tea in the kitchen, and I reach for the kettle. The wanting is a genuine cause of the reaching, and saying that I reached because I wanted tea is not loose talk standing in for a neural story I do not happen to know. Equally, I will not say that the neural state is the cause and the wanting is an idle passenger.

Three. Same wanting, same belief, same reaching: but a researcher has stimulated the exact neural realizer of that wanting, and the stimulation, not the wanting, produces the reaching; the wanting plays no role in the route by which the reaching comes about. Here I want to say the wanting is not a cause at all, even though the mental state and the behaviour match case two exactly.

Four. An emulated mind running on silicon, no neuron anywhere in it, wants tea and reaches. The wanting is a cause; the reaching has no cause describable in neural terms at all.

Five. My reaching for the kettle includes a tremor whose timing and amplitude nobody could have predicted from anything I wanted; my neurologist says it is motor noise. The wanting does not cause the tremor, though it causes the reaching of which the tremor is a part.

Six. Everything as in case two, except that a hardwired reflex would have produced the very same reaching at the very same moment had the wanting been absent. Here the wanting is not the cause; it has been preempted. This is the pair I cannot square: the realizer's sufficiency in case two does not trouble me at all, but the backup's sufficiency in case six seems fatal.

The principle I keep reaching for has three clauses: a mental event M causes a behaviour B only if (a) M and its realizer are related as determinable to determinate rather than as independent alternative causes, (b) B admits a description at the same grain as M, so that a change in M's value would change B under that description, and (c) nothing independent of M that would produce that same B under that same description is actually in play.

Please test that principle against the six cases and repair it or replace it. Is clause (a) even coherent, given that I am using the mind-brain relation's lack of independence to block overdetermination in case two while granting it in case one? Which clause is doing the work in case three, where the mental state and the behaviour are the same as in case two and only the route differs? And does the whole thing collapse on case five, where I allow the wanting to cause a reaching that contains a tremor it does not cause? Then tell me what the principle you end up with says about two cases I have not listed: a prosthetic that lets me set my wanting directly, leaving the neural realizer to settle itself however it does, and the question whether anything mental ever causes any maximally precise physical detail. Be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate.
