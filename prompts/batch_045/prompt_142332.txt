hey — reading group is doing perception next month and one of the guys in it (he's a disjunctivist-skeptic, i mean skeptical about disjunctivism) put this on the whiteboard after the session and i've been staring at a photo of it for two days. It concludes disjunctivism is false, but it also seems to conclude stuff nobody believes, and i can't locate where it jumps. Also I think it might be doing the same work as the argument from illusion but in reverse, which can't be right.

(1) Metaphysical disjunctivism: a veridical perception is not the same fundamental kind of event as a hallucination. In the good case the mind-independent particular you perceive is partly constitutive of the experience.

(2) If something is partly constitutive of an experience, then an experience lacking it is a different experience — in particular, it can't have the very same phenomenal character.

(3) So no hallucination has the same phenomenal character as the perception it is a hallucination as of. Seeing a dagger and merely seeming to see one differ in character, however alike you make them.

(4) Phenomenal character just is how it is for the subject. So if two experiences differ in character, the subject can in principle tell them apart from the inside, just by attending to how things seem.

(5) So a subject can always tell, from the inside, whether they are perceiving or hallucinating. Equivalently: a hallucination subjectively indistinguishable from a perception is impossible.

(6) But there are subjectively indistinguishable hallucinations — Macbeth's dagger, the drunk guy's pink rat, the bereaved person who clearly sees her dead husband at the kitchen table and only starts doubting it an hour later. In these cases the subject can't tell at the time, even attending as hard as she can.

(7) So disjunctivism is false.

The bit that makes me think it proves too much: run (1)–(5) on any state whose nature is partly fixed by how the world is. Take the view that knowing is itself a mental state, so that the difference between knowing that p and merely believing it truly is a difference in the state you're in and not just in your surroundings. By (2) and (4), you can always tell from the inside whether you know something or only believe it. Nobody thinks that — the standard case against the idea that you can always tell what mental state you're in is built exactly to deny it. Or take a representationalist who lets content be fixed externally: (5) then says my envatted duplicate and I can't be having subjectively identical experiences, which maybe some people accept, but it's a much bigger claim than anything about hallucination.

I want to keep (6) — it's the datum the whole literature starts from — and i'd like to keep (1), since the point of the reading group is that disjunctivism might be true. My friend just accepts (5): he says a genuinely perfect hallucination is impossible and that the cases in (6) are ones where the subject is inattentive, or is misremembering later, or is describing the case badly. That feels like cheating but I can't say why, and he has another move: replace can tell in (4) with there is a difference for the subject, so (5) becomes the claim that the hallucination is never exactly like the perception, and (6) only says the subject can't tell which one she's in — so no contradiction. Is that a rescue or a retreat?

So: which step do you reject? Is there an equivocation in (2) between constitutive of the experience and constitutive of the character? Is (4) a slide from different character to discriminable difference that even a non-disjunctivist should reject? Does the argument run metaphysical and epistemic disjunctivism together somewhere — I suspect a shift in (2) between experience as event and experience as state — or does it bear on both equally? And does the disjunctivist who denies (5) still count as a naive realist, or has she turned into something else?

Then, after the table, a short paragraph — 150 words max — on which disjunctivist package is left standing once the dust settles, and whether the version that bites the bullet on (5) can still explain what the hallucinator's experience is, as opposed to just what it isn't.
