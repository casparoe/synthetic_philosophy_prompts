Please act as a professional philosopher who also has a working knowledge of personality and social psychology. Before you settle on a verdict, brainstorm the ways the analogy below might come apart, and let that brainstorm shape the answer: I want the mapping and its failure points argued explicitly rather than a smooth verdict handed down.

I have recently been asked to co-chair the committee redesigning professionalism training at my teaching hospital, and we keep coming back to one analogy that is quietly doing all the work. Our director, an Aristotelian, wants to replace the current lecture-and-pledge format with repeated, coached practice of quite specific behaviors — delivering bad news, owning an error in front of a family, interrupting a colleague who is belittling a nurse. Her justification is that character is a skill, like any other skill you acquire by doing it, with feedback, until it becomes second nature.

A colleague who reads the social-psychology literature says this concedes the situationist critique rather than answering it. Whether someone stops for a stranger in distress, he points out, is predicted far better by how rushed they are than by anything we would call compassion; behavior tracks circumstances, not traits. So if virtue is a bundle of fine-grained, situation-specific competences, there is no such thing as a trustworthy person, only a trustworthy person-in-a-context — and the honest upshot of taking character to be a skill is that the budget should go to defaults, checklists and unit culture rather than to character.

She replies that this gets skills wrong. A good clinician is not consistent in the sense of doing the same thing in every setting, but reliable in the sense of tracking what matters, adjusting to the room, and holding up when tired or rushed; drilled competence is exactly what carries over into unscripted situations. On her view the skill analogy is what rescues virtue from the situationist findings, not what capitulates to them.

Please audit the analogy. Give me an explicit correspondence table — the coach, the drill, the feedback, the standard of success, automaticity, transfer, decay without practice, the learner's own grasp of what she is doing — and for each row say what it maps onto in the virtue case and how well it holds. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the work the analogy is being asked to do from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Three I care about in particular: a skill can be exercised to bad ends and still count as a skill, whereas a virtue seemingly cannot; a skill has success conditions checkable from outside the agent, whereas the point of a virtue seems to include her reasons and motives; and skills are domain-specific in a way that may leave no room for the domain-general capacity we appeal to when we call someone trustworthy in a situation we have never seen her in. On that last one: some of our residents are unfailingly gentle with patients and curt with the nurses who page them, and I would like you to say what the analogy predicts about whether this is one competence badly generalized or a failure of character.

Finally: does the director's conclusion — that we should keep investing in character rather than in systems — survive, or was it being carried by the analogy rather than supported by it? And if the skill analogy cannot bear the weight, is there a better one available from the neighborhood — craft, clinical judgement, practical expertise, fluency in a language — or is the honest answer that no analogy drawn from competences can do this work?
