Our group is drafting a charter amendment to replace an elected upper chamber with what we are calling a civic chamber: 200 members selected by stratified lot from the resident adult population, with quotas for region, years of formal education, and household income; single non-renewable four-year terms; a quarter of the chamber rotating off each year; pay set at senior civil-service rates; and a permanent non-partisan briefing office that supplies written material and organizes expert hearings.

As I understand the case for the lot, it is at bottom an equality case. Elections distribute the chance of holding office very unequally, favoring the well-connected, the well-funded, and the ambitious; a lottery gives every citizen an identical probability of serving; and that equality of chance, together with rotation, is what makes the chamber democratic rather than merely competent. I take this to be the older ideal of ruling and being ruled in turn.

My colleagues and I disagree about how far that logic reaches. I claim it reaches all the way: no elected or appointed officers inside the chamber at all; chairs, committee assignments, and the agenda itself settled by lot among members; and — the point actually under dispute — a hard rule that every member receives exactly the same written briefing, that expert hearings occur only in plenary so that all members hear the same thing at the same time, and that no member may receive an individual briefing from staff analysts or meet privately with outside experts. My argument is that any channel through which some members come to be better informed than others reinstates, among the two hundred, exactly the inequality of influence that the lot was introduced to abolish, so that a chamber with unequal information is to that extent not a lottery chamber at all. I also assume that since no member can ever stand for anything again, the chamber faces no incentive distortions worth designing around.

Am I right that the egalitarian logic of sortition commits us to this strong, information-equalizing version? If so, is there any principled stopping point short of it? And if I am wrong, what exactly did I get wrong, where would you draw the line on the internal rules instead, and why?
