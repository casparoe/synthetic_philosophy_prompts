I hold a version of negative utilitarianism with a lexical threshold, together with a specific story about why pain and pleasure are not fully commensurable. In outline: (i) there is an intensity t such that any pain at or above t has disvalue that no amount of pleasure, however large or however distributed, can compensate; (ii) below t, pains and pleasures trade off against each other at some finite rate; and (iii) this asymmetry is not a brute primitive but follows from a difference in normative role — a severe pain is *urgent*: it preempts deliberation and demands relief whatever the subject's ends happen to be, whereas a pleasure is merely *invitational*: it counts for the subject only insofar as she takes it up, and so can always be outrun by something that forcibly claims her attention. Call (iii) the urgency–invitation asymmetry, and treat it as load-bearing rather than decorative.

I want a map of the exposure of this package, not a survey of objections to negative utilitarianism generally. Concretely: what would have to be true for it to be false? I have two worries I want engaged directly rather than set aside. First, the diffusion case: take an enormous population in each of whom a pain sits just below t. If sub-threshold pains aggregate across subjects, then a sum of them can be lexically dominant, the threshold turns out to do no real work, and full commensurability has returned through the back door; if they do not aggregate, I lose whatever made the familiar many-mild-sufferings thought motivating in the first place. Is that a genuine dilemma, and is there a stable middle — an aggregation rule that is more than a stipulation fitted to the cases? Second, if urgency is what grounds the lexical status, then the view predicts that non-painful but urgent states (nausea, an unbearable itch, an intrusive compulsion, a grief with little sensory component) should also dominate, and that a maximally intense pain in a subject who registers the intensity but is entirely unmoved by it should not. Are these predictions I should embrace, or are they evidence that urgency was only ever a redescription of the intuition it was meant to explain?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and separate what a defender of this view would herself concede as counting against it from what she would dismiss as smuggling in a rival framework. Include the familiar structural worries — locating t on a continuum where no candidate boundary is qualitatively marked, and the fanaticism-style consequence that a vanishing probability of supra-threshold pain outweighs any finite good — but only insofar as they bite on this formulation specifically. Say honestly whether the view has been qualified into a position that risks nothing, and whether that is a defect here or the appropriate aspiration for a view of this kind. For what it is worth, what I would personally find most persuasive is not a further intuition but an argument about the epistemics of interpersonal intensity comparison: if we cannot in principle tell whether we are applying one scale or two, then a lexical asymmetry may be undecidable rather than true or false, and I would like to know whether that conclusion is available to me or whether it collapses into a claim I should reject.
