I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, though most of my reporting now is data work: last year my desk published a searchable database of roughly four thousand municipal water systems, each with a line saying whether that system exceeded the federal lead limit at any point in the past decade, and I've just finished a book that grew out of the project, containing about four hundred separately checkable factual claims. I've been asked to give a short talk to our standards editors next month on when a number is good enough to print, and I'd rather not stand up there with a list of intuitions and no principle behind them. My partner, who works in reliability engineering, points out that her field has formal machinery for exactly this — fault trees, common-cause failures, the fact that a thousand components each fail once in ten thousand times do not add up to a safe system — and I suspect the right answer borrows from that. But I don't want the whole thing reduced to her machinery, because what I actually have to decide is what sentences go on the page. So here are my verdicts, case by case, and I'd like you to find the principle I'm groping toward, tell me what it predicts about cases I haven't thought about, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save.

(a) A state lottery, ten million tickets, one winner, draw tonight. I'll say of any ticket you name that it will lose, and I'd sell you my ticket for a dollar. I have no evidence at all about ticket 437 beyond the odds. I won't say that every ticket will lose, and I will say that exactly one will win. (b) Our database: audits put each line at about 0.997; I'm happy to publish every line, and if a reader writes in about her own county I'll say yes, that line is right. I won't put on the landing page that every line in the database is correct, where the expected number of wrong lines is about twelve and the chance they're all right is roughly one in a hundred thousand. I will write that there is very probably a wrong line in here somewhere and we don't know which, and I feel no obligation to name one; once an audit flags line 2,847 I'll say that line is wrong, and not before. (c) Same for the book: I stand behind every claim in it and I also wrote in the preface that there is almost certainly an error in it somewhere. My editor finds this embarrassing; I think it is merely honest. (d) Now suppose I learn that all four thousand readings came off a single sensor calibrated once against a test strip that is itself maybe one percent likely to be wrong. The number attached to any given line still comes out around 0.997 on paper, and yet I pull the whole database until we recalibrate. So the per-line figure isn't doing the work on its own. (e) Now suppose two independent teams re-derive all four thousand lines by genuinely different methods and agree, so that per-line error odds are something like one in a hundred thousand and the chance the database as a whole is clean is ninety-six or ninety-seven percent. My gut still refuses the sentence that every line in it is correct, and I can't tell whether that is timidity or a real distinction. (f) Meanwhile, in ordinary reporting I routinely print conjunctive sentences whose joint probability is well below the per-line standard I apply to the database: that the official was in the building that night and the lab confirmed the sample was her son's, where I'd put the two parts at 0.95 and 0.97 and the conjunction near 0.92 — and I feel fine about that sentence while feeling not at all fine about any single database line at 0.92. (g) And here is the one that bothers me most: a cyclist is hit by a delivery van; nine hundred and ninety-nine of the thousand vans in the city belong to one company; there is no footage, no witness, nothing else. I will print that the odds are about a thousand to one that it was one of their vans. I will not print that it was one of their vans, even having just said out loud that I put 0.999 on it, and even if I imagine the stakes lowered from a lawsuit to a small administrative fine. Compare that with (a): in (a) I assert something about an individual member of a large class on nothing but a base rate about the class, and I'm entirely comfortable.

My tentative rule, which I'd like you to test against these cases and repair rather than replace outright, is: print a claim if, given everything I know including how the errors are correlated, its probability clears some threshold; let the threshold move with what is at stake and with how cheaply and visibly a correction can be made; never require me to add up what all my claims come to; and let a base rate over a class license a claim about a member of that class only when I have positive reason to think the member is exchangeable with the class in the relevant respect, so that there is no uneliminated way of carving up the class that would change the rate. I think that last clause is what separates (g) from (a), since the lottery mechanism guarantees exchangeability while the fleet does not. But I also think it may destroy (a), because I have no evidence whatever about ticket 437, and I don't want to give (a) up — and when I make (g) airtight by stipulating that vans are assigned to identical routes by a random dispatcher, I still don't feel right printing the flat sentence, and I want to know whether that residual unease is defensible or whether I should print it and be done. Please give me one or two candidate principles stated precisely, each clause tied to the case that motivates it; tell me what they predict for cases I haven't listed — whether I may print that every line is correct once the two audits agree, whether it matters if the errors are clumped rather than spread (say, one bad batch contaminating a random block of fifty consecutive lines, with the same expected number of wrong lines overall), and what should happen to my verdicts if a source sends me two hundred documents with a note saying exactly one is forged and offering to answer truthfully about any one I name, as I work through them one by one; and say plainly which of my verdicts you would overturn, whether (a) and (g) can both survive, and whether the principle you like is really a principle about what to print or only about what to believe, given that I believe every claim in my book and also that at least one of them is false without feeling in the least irrational.
