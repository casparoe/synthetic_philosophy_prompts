I am in my final year of school and I run the mathematics club: roughly ten people, one whiteboard, thirty-five minutes, nothing assigned between sessions, and a very uneven background — two of them do competitive programming, most have never written a proof, and all of them have seen some version of the popular video claim that some infinities are bigger than others. Next week I am meant to do a session on the continuum hypothesis. Please do not explain the continuum hypothesis to me; I want to be told how to explain it, and why the ordering you propose should be what it is.

The things I am unsure about, in no particular order.

Sequence. My instinct is that same size means you can pair the members off has to come before anything else, and that the diagonal argument has to come before I can even state the question, because nobody will feel the pull of asking whether there is a size in between until they accept that there are at least two. But I do not know whether to let them hunt for an in-between set — to try to build one and fail — before I tell them the question turns out not to be settled by the usual axioms, or whether to say that at the start. My worry is that announcing it early turns it into a fact about axioms rather than a question about how many points there are on a line, and that leaving it late means the hunt eats the session and nothing lands.

The carrying example. I want to run the whole thing on infinite sequences of coin flips rather than on decimal expansions, because I have watched people get derailed by the fact that some numbers have two decimal expansions, and I do not want that fight. Is that the right carrier? What does an example need to have and to lack here? I assume it has to be obviously infinite, obviously not pairable with the counting numbers, and yet not so obviously continuous that it invites the idea that what makes a set big is that its members are packed densely together.

Misconceptions, and where to handle each. The ones I expect: that all infinities are the same size, since infinity just means going on forever; that the rationals must be bigger than the integers because they sit densely between any two points; that the reals are the largest infinity there is; that an independence result shows the question was meaningless all along; and then, from the sharper members, either that we may simply choose whichever answer we please, or that adding a new axiom to settle it is cheating. On that last one I wonder whether the fact that mathematicians now routinely help themselves to an axiom that was once just as contested is the right reply or a distraction at this level.

What to leave out. I take it that forcing, the statement written with the alephs, and whatever large cardinals have to do with any of this should be named as deliberately excluded rather than quietly skipped. Tell me what else belongs on that list, whether I should even say the names of the two people who proved the independence results when I will not be explaining their work, and how to handle this: someone told me that asking whether there is a size between the counting numbers and the reals and asking whether the continuum is the very next size up are only the same question under a certain standard assumption, and come apart without it. If that is right I assume it is a footnote — but I would rather know whether it is a footnote or a trap, since two of my members will look things up afterwards whatever I do.

Finally, one question for the end that would show me the idea has actually landed rather than been memorised as a slogan. I was going to ask them what would have to be true of a set of real numbers for the hypothesis to fail, but I suspect that is too easy to parrot. Something harder, if you have it.
