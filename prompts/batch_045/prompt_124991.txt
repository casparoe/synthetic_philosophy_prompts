I am the senior editor of a technical report containing six hundred substantive claims, ten contributed by each of sixty specialists. From earlier volumes in the same series we have usable calibration data: the marginal error rate for a claim of this kind is roughly four in a thousand, and errors are not spread evenly — a contributor who makes one mistake typically makes three, all of them within a single section. Every claim in the present volume passed our review process, and I believe each of them. I also believe, on the strength of that same calibration data, that the report contains at least one error.

A colleague assures me that this is simply the lottery paradox with the tickets relabeled. In both cases, he says, I have a set of individually very probable propositions together with one further, very probable proposition to the effect that the set contains a falsehood; in both cases the trouble is generated by the same three assumptions — some threshold linking high probability to rational belief, closure of rational belief under conjunction, and the requirement that rational belief be consistent — and so in both cases I should identify a single fix, set it once, and let it settle the matter in the same form and at the same setting. I have three candidates:

1. Raise the threshold for outright belief until, in cases of this shape, belief is withheld somewhere in the inconsistent set — including, if necessary, withholding belief that the report contains an error.
2. Keep a moderate threshold but abandon closure, so that I may rationally believe each claim without believing their conjunction, and hence without contradicting my global doubt.
3. Keep both, but distinguish outright belief from acceptance: treat the six hundred claims as accepted for the purposes of publication while withholding belief, and express the global doubt only as a hedging remark.

I need to decide whether to print a caveat in the front matter — the report's equivalent of an author's acknowledgment that somewhere in the following pages something is wrong — so I would like your assessment of these three: which should I adopt as the uniform fix, and at what setting? If none should be applied uniformly, tell me which you would use for the report and whether it would serve equally well if I were holding a ticket in a six-hundred-ticket lottery.

I would rather have the truth than a tidy answer. If the question as posed cannot be answered well — if the two cases are not the same argument with the labels changed, if the clustering in my error data is not the incidental detail my colleague takes it to be, or if no single threshold does the work in both cases — say so plainly, and tell me what I should have asked instead. Note where your recommendations would change under a different error model, and where you take yourself to be going out on a limb.
