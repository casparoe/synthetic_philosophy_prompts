I've been doing mosquito and agricultural pest work for a county for about ten years, and I've never managed to say out loud what I actually think about killing insects. My gut has very firm opinions and I don't think they add up. I don't want reading recs or a name for the view, I want someone to try to find the rule I'm implicitly running.

Here's where I land, case by case.

1. Fogging the mosquitoes that are actually biting me and my kids in the backyard: totally fine, zero guilt.
2. Larviciding a wetland a couple of miles off so a town downwind doesn't get a dengue outbreak: fine, feels like the good part of my job, maybe better than fine.
3. Aerial spraying a whole region for a vector that isn't currently transmitting anything, just to keep numbers low in case: I'm uneasy in a way I'm not about 2, and I've never had a good account of why.
4. A gene drive meant to collapse a malaria vector population across a continent: clearly worse to me than spraying that kills more individual insects. My first thought is that it's the irreversibility, but honestly I think I'd still object if someone handed me a reliable off switch, so maybe irreversibility isn't the real thing.
5. Sterile male release: the local population collapses, just as many insects don't end up existing as would have died under spraying, and I'm completely fine with it, more than fine.
6. Ants in the kitchen with boiling water: fine. Same outcome through a baited growth regulator where the colony dies over about a week: I don't like it. Same deaths, worse method.
7. Non-target deaths bother me way out of proportion. I feel worse about the ladybugs and bees taken out as bycatch by a broadcast spray than about the target pests, even when the bycatch is a tenth the number.
8. Rat in the walls: I genuinely feel bad and want it done humanely. Ten thousand ants: shrug. Ten thousand rats: horrifying. I can't tell if I'm weighting a rat more than ten thousand times an ant, or if I stop aggregating insect deaths altogether while still aggregating mammal ones, or if it's just size bias I should get over.
9. Aphids on my roses: I do it, but there's a small twinge, more than I feel about the wasp nest I had taken out of the kids' playhouse, which is definately backwards if you think stinging matters.
10. Knocking down a caterpillar outbreak on soy here: fine. Doing the same thing to the same species in the part of its native range where it's food for everything else: uneasy, though I suspect I'm really reacting to it not being my business.

My best guess at the principle is something like: killing is fine when somebody has a real stake and the deaths are quick and targeted; what's genuinely bad isn't death as such but leaving a population or a species permanently diminished or gone. But that doesn't touch 7 at all, since bycatch bugs me far more than the headcount warrants, and it only half handles 4: if what's wrong with the gene drive is permanence, why does 5 feel so clean when the population ends up just as gone?

So: is there a principle that gets these, or nearly all of them? What does each clause of it come from? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and is there a messier principle that saves all ten instead? And what does whatever you land on say about the two I keep going back and forth on:

- engineering the vector so it can't carry the pathogen: nobody dies, the population stays exactly as abundant, but the species is permanently changed to suit us.
- making the pest dependent on something we supply, for instance a strain that can't finish its life cycle without a compound we put out, so the population could be switched off at will. This one feels worse to me than just killing it, and I can't tell whether that's a real difference or just a visceral reaction to the dependency.

If the principle you end up with leans on insect sentience or insect pain, say how much hangs on that, because I know enough to know it's contested and I'd rather not have my whole view rest on it.
