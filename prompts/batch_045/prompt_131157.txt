You are advising a small research group — five philosophers, three years of funding, no empirical social science, and a mandate to produce work that is conceptually rigorous but also usable by people who actually allocate resources. The group's topic is moral progress understood on the expanding-circle picture: the view that moral concern has, over history, widened to take in beings it previously excluded, and that this widening is the central thing we mean by moral improvement.

Before you can rank questions, I want you to work with three distinctions I have drawn myself, because I think the literature blurs them and the blurring distorts prioritization.

First, distinguish inclusion from re-founding. Inclusion is growth on a standard that was already correct: new beings are recognized as falling under a moral principle whose validity is not itself at issue. Re-founding is replacement: the standard changes, so that what counted as a reason before no longer does, or a new kind of consideration becomes salient that was not merely overlooked but unavailable. Some candidate future expansions look like inclusion (granting standing to animals is arguably recognizing them under an existing principle about suffering). Others look like re-founding (environmental concern, or concern for artificial systems, may introduce considerations that are not further instances of anything previously recognized). Call the hard case axis proliferation: a new moral axis appears, and it is unclear whether we have widened a circle or added a dimension.

Second, distinguish three framings the group could adopt: the descriptive question of what actually caused past expansions, the normative question of whether those expansions were improvements rather than mere changes, and the strategic question of whether anyone should try to cause further expansion deliberately. These are often run together, and I suspect they deserve very different amounts of the group's three years.

Third, consider what I call the unaudited present. We treat certain past moral outlooks as straightforwardly mistaken, and we do so from the only standpoint we have. We have no procedure for ruling out that our own outlook occupies, relative to our successors, exactly the position we now assign to the past. This is not the familiar worry that progress might be an illusion; it is the narrower problem that our confidence in judging the past is systematically unsupported by the kind of evidence our successors might have about us.

Given all that: what are the open questions here that most deserve this group's three years, and in what order? I want a defended agenda, not a survey of positions and not a canon of names. For each item, say what it is upstream of, whether it is tractable with conceptual tools alone in three years, whether it is neglected relative to its importance, and what kind of argument or result — arriving within the next two years — would move it up or down. Rank explicitly and tell me which items you are deliberately leaving out because their answers would not change anyone's behavior.

Two further constraints on your answer. First, tell me which of the three framings should get the largest share of effort, and why; I expect you to argue against at least one of them being worth the group's time at all. Second, identify the single consideration or crux such that, if it were resolved one way rather than another, the group should reorganize the entire agenda — and say what that reorganization would look like.
