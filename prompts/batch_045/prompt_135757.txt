I've been half following the Knobe effect for a while — mostly pop philosophy podcasts and one chapter in a book about irrationality — and I just listened to an episode that felt like it was overselling things, but I don't know the literature well enough to say where. Their summary went more or less like this:

"So the setup is simple. You tell people about a company chairman who is told that a new program will raise profits and will also help the environment, and he says he doesn't care about the environment at all, he just wants the profit. You tell a different group the same story but with harms instead of helps. Almost everyone says the chairman harmed the environment intentionally, and almost everyone in the other group says he did not help it intentionally. Knobe's conclusion is that our concept of intentional action is really just a moral verdict in disguise: calling something intentional is just our way of saying the person is to blame. And the upshot is much bigger than one weird word — it shows that philosophers' intuitions about cases are worthless as evidence, because what looks like a factual judgement about what happened turns out to be a moral reaction wearing a trenchcoat. So the whole intuition-driven style of philosophy is in trouble."

Then they mentioned a second study, which is the part I actually most want to ask about. As best I remember it: someone reran the whole thing with the chairman swapped out for an AI system that was purely optimizing for profit and had no idea the program affected the environment one way or the other, and around half the participants still said it intentionally harmed the environment. The hosts took this as the strongest evidence yet that the effect isn't about blame at all — you can't blame an algorithm — but about whether the agent had a reason to bring the outcome about, so the real story is about reasons, not morality.

Can you go through this claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on this would grant, what they'd say is a distortion, and what is just wrong? In particular: is "intentional" really being collapsed into "blameworthy" here, and is that what people in the field take the data to show? Does the big conclusion about intuitions being useless follow from the parts that are accurate, or is it a non sequitur? And does the AI version actually show what they say it does — my gut says it's the most interesting bit but that something is off, maybe that it changes more than one thing at a time compared to the original case, but I cant put my finger on it. Also, if there's some weaker way of stating the finding that both the podcast and a critic of it could sign on to, I'd like to know what that would look like.
