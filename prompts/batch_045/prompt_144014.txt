Here is the pattern I keep tripping over. I trained as a structural engineer and read evolutionary biology as a hobby, so I tend to ask two questions about any structure: why is it there at all, and why does it have this shape rather than another. In the biology I read, those two questions are almost never kept apart, and I cannot find vocabulary that keeps them apart.

Three cases. They are stylised composites rather than claims about any particular species, but I think they are faithful to real examples.

Case one. A bony flange sits where two plates of a skull meet. Experimentally reducing it lowers feeding performance and lifetime reproductive output, so selection is plainly maintaining it. But its size and outline are fully predicted by the geometry of the joint: it is the material left over where the plates meet, and given the joint you could compute the flange. There is no evidence of a stage in which the flange existed without the muscle attachment, and no evidence of an earlier, different use. So the flange is not idle — remove it and the animal does worse — yet nothing about its shape looks chosen.

Case two. A colour patch used in mate choice. It is under current sexual selection, and individuals with larger patches secure more mates. But the pigment is a consequence of how the cuticle is built, the variation the population throws up runs along one axis (extent) and not another (hue), and the hue that would serve the signalling job best appears to be unreachable. Selection is pushing, but it is pushing along a line laid down by development rather than toward the optimum for the job.

Case three, roughly the mirror image. A structure is beautifully engineered for what it does: an optimality analysis says it sits at or near the best attainable solution given the materials and the loads. But the organism appears to have no option about having it. It is forced by the body plan, and no accessible variant lacks it. If the job vanished, the structure would not. An engineer looking at it says designed; a historian says it was never selected for being there.

What strikes me is that my grid has two axes — is the presence of the trait explained by selection, and is its form explained by selection — and the vocabulary I have met seems only to name the diagonal cells. The historical co-option notion introduced a few years after the spandrels paper to replace the older, teleologically loaded term does not seem to fit: as I have seen it used, and in every standard example I have encountered, it requires two stages, a trait built for one job or for no job and later taken up for another. My cases have no dated transition that I can see. I have also seen philosophers argue about whether a trait's function is a matter of its history or of what it currently contributes, and neither side seems to be talking about my grid.

A related worry: the two tests I see used for is it an adaptation appear to answer different questions. Degrade or remove the trait and see whether fitness falls — that is a question about presence. Build an optimality model and see whether the design is good — that is a question about form. Both are called tests of adaptation, and they can come apart.

So: does the configuration I have described have an established name, and is there a literature that treats the two axes separately? If it does have a name, tell me precisely how my description departs from the standard use of that term. If it does not, coin what you need, define it, and give boundary cases. In either case I want the nearest established concepts laid out with an exact account of where each stops short of my cases, and I want diagnostics: what would actually settle which box a real trait belongs in. Tell me too whether my three cases are really one phenomenon, and whether my grid is the wrong shape — should maintenance be separated from origin, should form be split into geometry, material, and developmental pathway, is there a third axis I am missing? What I do not want is a bare label. Something in the range of 700 to 900 words is about right.
