I write and maintain backend services and data pipelines. I have no philosophy training, but I keep noticing that my team's everyday vocabulary rests on distinctions I can't state, and I'd like someone to state the view that has to be true for the way we talk to make sense.

The practice is our bug triage. Every day we sort the outputs of machines into four buckets, quickly and without argument: a correct output, a wrong output, a non-output (noise, corruption, not even a reading), and a correct output of something nobody wanted. We do this for systems that have no beliefs, no desires, and no idea what their outputs are for. Concretely:

(1) A temperature probe in a rack reports 23 degrees in a 25 degree room, because the firmware uses a slightly wrong constant in the voltage-to-temperature conversion. Every self-test passes and the hardware does exactly what its datasheet and its code say. We say the probe reports the wrong temperature, that the reading is false, and we file a bug.

(2) The same probe, with a loose connector, emits the top value of the analog-to-digital range. We do not say it reported an extremely high temperature. We say there is no reading at all, we drop the value, and we raise a hardware fault. So a wrong number and a meaningless number are different failures, even though both are just patterns of bits on a wire.

(3) A service is handed a byte string that is not valid UTF-8 and emits replacement characters downstream. We don't say the service garbled the text; we say it wasn't given any text, and the caller is at fault. Likewise when a caller passes a customer ID that no longer exists: garbage in, garbage out, and the blame stays with the caller. So whether a module's output is even assessable as right or wrong seems to depend on the provenance of what went into it, not just on what comes out.

(4) Now the case that actually puzzles me. The two-degree-low probe from case (1) was never fixed. It sat in the rack for six years. Alert thresholds, dashboards, capacity models, an on-call runbook, and three downstream services were all written around it, and the engineer who chose the constant left the company. Then somebody noticed, corrected the constant, and shipped it. The number became true. Every alert fired, the capacity model started making bad decisions, and the change was reverted within the hour, with a bug filed against the fix. Since that day we describe the original behavior as correct, and our runbook says that 23 means the room is getting warm. Nothing about the probe's physical dispositions changed when we flipped; what changed was what other systems had come to depend on. There is a well-known version of this in platform work: given enough consumers, every observable behavior of your system will be depended on by somebody, whatever the documentation promised. Notice that in this case we call a true output a bug and a false output correct, which suggests that our word wrong was never simply tracking truth.

(5) There is also a fourth move we make constantly. When the implementation matches the written specification, we close the ticket as working as designed, and we treat that as a defense. But we also sometimes override it: we say the specification itself was wrong, or that the behavior is spec-compliant and still wrong, and we change the spec. So the norm we appeal to is not simply what the original author had in mind.

So here is what I want reconstructed. What view of aboutness, of correctness, and of the difference between malfunction and mere noise does this whole practice presuppose? More specifically:

(a) What has to be true of a physical state for it to be about anything at all, so that the difference between case (1) and case (2) is a real difference and not just a difference in how much we like the number?

(b) What fixes what a signal says? In case (4) nothing physical changed at the moment we started calling the old behavior correct and the corrected behavior a bug. We appear to have appealed to history: what the output was built to do, and then what downstream consumers came to rely on its doing. Is that appeal strictly required by the practice, or could the same triage be reconstructed using only the state's current causal role in the running system?

(c) Is the should in this output should have been 25 the same should as in this reading is false, or are there two different norms here that we run together, and if so which one does the bug tracker track?

(d) Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one account of content would license exactly the triage I described? And if we swapped in a rival account, say one on which a signal means whatever its consumers take it to mean, or one on which it means whatever it reliably correlates with, what would my team have to stop saying? I suspect we would lose the ability to call a whole pipeline wrong, or the garbage-in excuse, but I'd like that spelled out.

(e) Finally, tell me what the reconstruction predicts for cases I haven't listed: an internal activation vector inside a trained classifier, a metric emitted for years that nobody has ever read, and a legacy wire format whose authors are gone and whose consumers are gone. For each, does the account say there is something the state is about, and something it can get wrong?

If the view you end up reconstructing has a standard name in philosophy, tell me what it is called, but what I actually want is the reconstruction, the argument that the practice needs it, and an honest statement of which of my cases it handles badly.
