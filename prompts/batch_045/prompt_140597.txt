I host a small, kind of scruffy philosphy podcast — just long conversations, no audience, no panel. Next week's guest is a philosopher I'll call Professor Ada Reinholt, who's written a cluster of papers defending something she calls the asymmetry view about higher-order evidence. Her rough idea: when the higher-order news says your faculties were malfunctioning — you've been slipped a cognition-wrecking drug, the hangar lighting is distorting the colours, the thermometer is broken — that defeats your first-order justification, and you should suspend on the first-order question (neither believe nor disbelieve), even though nothing you learned is evidence against p. But when the higher-order news only says you probably misweighted your evidence — you've disagreed with an epistemic peer, you were exhausted or annoyed when you made up your mind, someone's just told you you're a motivated reasoner — nothing at all happens to your first-order attitude. You keep your 0.9 on p. The only thing that moves is your self-trust: how much confidence you have in your own reliability on questions like this. That drops, and she says it should show up in what you're willing to do, not in what you believe.

She also has one stock move: whenever you hand her a case where the higher-order evidence obviously should lower confidence in p, she says "that's not higher-order evidence, that's first-order evidence about the world in disguise." Draft me 8–10 interview questions that make that move progressively harder to reach for, ordered so they build on each other, and never let her just restate the view. For each question give the follow-up keyed to whatever answer she's most likely to give.

Things I want definately covered:

- The bridge worry. If you should be less confident that you got this one right, why doesn't that itself push down your confidence in p? She can't just say "the levels are insulated" — make her say what insulation consists in.
- How her view is different from the plain "stick to your guns" total-evidence view, given that in peer disagreement cases she also leaves first-order credence fixed. (Rough glosses: the equal-weight view says split the difference with a peer; the total-evidence view says stay put if the first-order evidence still favours you.)
- A case where re-examining the first-order evidence isn't an option: a radiologist learns the hospital audit flags 30% of her reads as wrong, and the scans have been destroyed. Press on whether her view just collapses into suspension here anyway.
- Bias and ideology: if learning you're a motivated reasoner never moves your first-order beliefs, what's left of genealogical or debunking-style arguments in ethics or religion?
- The nasty cases where first-order evidence supports p and the higher-order evidence says your total evidence supports not-p — is she committed to rationality requiring a state that is irrational by first-order lights? Is "rational akrasia" coherent, or is it just a label?
- Calibration: can someone systematically mismatch their first-order and second-order attitudes every single time they get bad higher-order news, and still be epistemically fine? Is that stable if they notice the pattern?
- Action and speech: self-trust at 0.4, first-order confidence at 0.9 — do you bet at 0.9 odds, and do you still flat-out assert "p" to a colleague?
- Where memory cases go: you learn your memory of the one crucial detail was planted, but you still seem to remember it. Malfunction or misweighting? Why?
- One closing question about what single case would make her give up the asymmetry, plus one about whether this is a claim about what's rational or about what's epistemically virtuous/blameworthy.
