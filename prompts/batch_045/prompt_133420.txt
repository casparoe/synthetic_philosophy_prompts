I have been developing a contractual model of transitional amnesty — call it the bilateral-commitment model — and I want to run the Argentine sequence through it, because I suspect Argentina is the case that breaks it. I know the transitional-justice literature well enough; I do not want a survey of it. I want a mapping and then a verdict.

The model, stated briefly so that you need not guess at my terms. Amnesty is analysed as a device for solving a commitment problem at the handover of coercive power: the outgoing power holders require assurance against prosecution, the incoming government cannot give durable assurance because no legislature can bind its successors, and amnesty is therefore the price paid for a transfer that would not otherwise occur. Its structure is that of a bargain — a promisor, a promisee, consideration on each side, and third parties, namely the victims, who are the subject matter of the bargain rather than parties to it. The good the bargain is meant to secure is finality: an end to the contest over the past.

The facts, which I stipulate so that nothing turns on a detail you cannot check. The military government of 1976–83 enacted a self-amnesty shortly before handing over power, in the aftermath of the Falklands defeat; the incoming Congress annulled it almost immediately. Raúl Alfonsín took office in December 1983 and within days issued decrees directing prosecutions both of the nine officers of the first three juntas and of guerrilla commanders; CONADEP was created days later and delivered the Nunca Más report in September 1984, documenting some 8,960 disappearances, against the 30,000 claimed by human rights organisations. The Trial of the Juntas opened in April 1985 and produced convictions in December — life sentences for Jorge Videla and Emilio Massera, lesser terms for three others, four acquittals — with several hundred further prosecutions in train. Then came the military uprisings of 1987 and 1988, and the two statutes: the Full Stop law of December 1986, imposing a sixty-day deadline on new indictments, and the Due Obedience law of June 1987, establishing a presumption, not open to rebuttal, that officers below the rank of colonel had acted under orders. Carlos Menem's pardons of 1989 and 1990 covered convicted officers and guerrilla leaders alike. Through the 1990s, with punishment blocked, Argentine litigants and courts developed trials whose object was the establishment of the facts alone, and social movements developed the escrache as a form of public censure outside the law. Congress annulled the two statutes in August 2003, the Supreme Court held them unconstitutional in June 2005, and hundreds of prosecutions have followed; Videla died in prison in 2013.

What I want is this, in order. First, the disciplined mapping: at each of the three moments at which impunity was granted — the junta's own self-amnesty, the 1986 and 1987 statutes, the Menem pardons — identify the promisor, the promisee, the consideration, the third parties, and what would have counted as breach. Note, in particular, that the Argentine amnesties were granted by a democratic Congress to an institution that had already relinquished power, so that the consideration cannot be a handover; the only candidate is forbearance from a renewed coup. Second, what the model explains well: the lateness of the amnesty, its emergence under renewed threat, the fact that punishment preceded it rather than following it, and the symmetric treatment of the two sides, which a bilateral model may require even though the truth record denies the underlying symmetry. Third, where it misdescribes: 1983 was a collapse rather than a pact, the victims were not parties, and the self-amnesty has no second party at all.

Fourth, and most important, what the case does to the model. I have been toying with three revisions, and I want them tested against this sequence and, briefly, against the contrasting South African case of conditional immunity granted at the moment of transfer in exchange for public disclosure. One revision imports a doctrine of duress: a concession extracted under threat is voidable, and the Argentine statutes are the clearest possible instance. Another imports an intertemporal constraint: a polity cannot bind its successor, so no amnesty can deliver the finality it purports to sell, which should have made amnesty worthless to its beneficiaries and yet did not. A third abandons finality altogether and reinterprets amnesty as a moratorium — a purchase of time during which the balance of forces shifts against the beneficiaries, so that reopening two decades later is not a breach of faith but the expiry of the term. Tell me which revision is least ad hoc, what each predicts that the others do not, and whether any of them can be checked against what actually happened after 2003. And if the duress revision would invalidate nearly every amnesty ever granted, say plainly whether that is a reductio of it or a conclusion we should accept.

Finally, tell me where the model is silent. It has, so far as I can see, no vocabulary for the expressive and evidentiary work that went on in the 1990s — censure without punishment, findings of fact without sentences. Say so, rather than stretching the model to cover it; and then tell me whether a model with that gap can be the right account of what these measures are for.
