I'm on the awards committee of my professional society, and we've got a nasty one. Our annual medal goes, under the bylaws, to "the person or persons responsible for the advance," but in sixty years it's never gone to more than one person. This year: a group leader — call him M — conceived the experiment, ran the group, wrote the paper, gave all the talks. Three technical staff built the instrument, and their petition says his written protocols couldn't actually be built as specified and that they made hundreds of undocumented design calls, so what produced the result was their apparatus rather than his protocol; their line is "we were the hands, but the hands had to think." M's reply is that an instrument isn't an idea, the medal is for the idea, and that once you credit everyone who touched the object there's no stopping point. This week a machinist at another university who fabricated one custom part wrote in asking to be included, so M's no-stopping-point worry is now on our agenda as a real case rather than a hypothetical.

One member circulated a note invoking Hegel's master-slave dialectic: roughly, that M ends up dependent on the recognition of people whose standing he won't grant, and that the staff's labor on the object is what gives them something independent to point to. Half the committee rolled their eyes.

Draft the decision memo I'll circulate before we meet in two weeks. It needs a bottom line we can actually vote on — medal to M alone, to the three staff, to M plus some subset, or something else entirely — plus what the citation should say, and it should state the strongest version of whichever position loses rather than a strawman. If the Hegel framing really does work here, use it; if it's just fog, say so. American English please, a page or so.
