I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background and I've somehow ended up deep in the aquaculture animal-welfare rabbit hole. Please don't give me citations or reading lists, I can't check them anyway. What I actually want is a spec.

The purpose first, so adequacy has something to be relative to: I want a theory of welfare good enough to do three concrete jobs. (1) Decide whether some change at a farm is a real improvement or just a different-looking one — lower stocking density, swapping ice-slurry chilling for electrical stunning, stopping eyestalk ablation on the broodstock, whatever. (2) Compare across species, because someone will ask whether a kilo of shrimp is worse than a kilo of salmon and "it depends" is not shippable. (3) Be implementable as an actual check a person can run on a farm holding thousands or millions of animals they will never inspect individually.

So write the desiderata: what any theory has to deliver to count as adequate for that, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. The cases I want covered include: a shrimp whose eyestalk is ablated and then goes on eating and spawning normally; a fish with fin erosion and a deformed jaw that still feeds and grows at target rate; an animal that looks calm only because it's apathetic; the one individual in the pen who is doing terribly and will never be sampled; a whole life that lasts eight months; and whether death is a welfare item at all or only the stuff before it. Also the two I think are traps: welfare indicators that are tied to productivity (growth, feed conversion), so that a farm can score well while the animals are worse off; and breeding animals that no longer express the indicator — fish selectively bred not to thrash — which improves every measurement and maybe improves nothing.

Distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse: welfare versus moral status, how bad versus how many, state at a time versus over a lifetime, and the animal's actual condition versus the number the audit produces.

It also has to be consistent with the biology without leaning on anatomy these animals don't have — fish don't have the layered cortex mammals have, decapods are wired differently again — and it should say what experiment would settle the question and what result would count as evidence of feeling rather than of nociception alone.

Mark each requirement non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Flag the pairs you think can't be jointly satisfied; my guesses are species-specific evidence versus cross-species comparison, sensitivity to the worst-off individual versus only ever measuring distributions, and cheap enough to run versus tracking something internal to the animal. If the honest answer is that the full list is unsatisfiable and we have to pick which job we're building for, say that, that's a result too. And tell me which single requirement kills the most candidates.

Short scorecard at the end, a couple of lines each, no more: feeling-based views, preference- or desire-based views, functioning and natural-living views, and the operationalist view that welfare is just whatever the audit protocol measures. Score them against the list only.
