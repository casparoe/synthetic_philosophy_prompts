A reading group I'm in has spent an entire term on the substantivalism–relationism dispute about spacetime and has finished exactly where it began, and what I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

Here is where we are. We agree about the physics: same general-relativistic models, same commitment to diffeomorphism invariance, same unease about the hole argument and Leibniz equivalence, and same sense that Newton's rotating bucket and his two connected globes are doing real work without anyone being able to say precisely what. We agree that the hole argument does not touch the relationist, and that the substantivalist who identifies isomorphic models — the sophisticated version — appears to dodge it. We agree that the Machian hope, that the inertial structure of the world is fixed by its matter distribution, is at best unfulfilled. And then we stop. The moves that recur are: the substantivalist says the relationist can make no sense of an empty universe, or of a lone rotating sphere, or of the difference between a world and its uniformly shifted duplicate, and that the relationist's paraphrases end up stating the dynamical laws over a manifold anyway; the relationist says the surplus structure is undetectable even in principle and therefore idle, and that conceivability claims about empty worlds or absolute rotation are bare modal intuitions doing the work of arguments. Each move draws a reply that is not crazy, and nobody's credences move.

My hypothesis is that this is not a first-order disagreement about spacetime and not an empirical one. I think at least three distinct disagreements are running under one label: first, a methodological disagreement about where metaphysics gets its evidence, whether ontology is to be read off our best physical theories or whether distinctively philosophical criteria — conceivability, explanatory unification, parsimony about possibilities — are themselves data; second, a disagreement about what counts as an observation or a possible datum, specifically whether qualitatively indiscernible but numerically different possibilities are the sort of thing a theory is answerable to; third, a disagreement about which theoretical virtue is decisive, whether the substantivalist's apparent explanatory gain is worth its modal and ontological cost. I offer this as a hypothesis, not as something I've established.

To test it I gave the group a case we invented, which I'd like you to use as a probe rather than as a new argument to adjudicate on its merits. Call it the Hollow World. The entire material content of the universe is a single spherical shell; the interior is vacuum. Question one: is there a fact about whether a gyroscope at the centre is rotating? Question two: if so, is that fact fixed by the distribution and motion of the shell's matter? Now stipulate what the inhabitants discover. First, there is a determinate fact: the gyroscope readings at the centre are sharp and reproducible. Second — this is the part I care about — the interior inertial structure contains a component that is invariant under every redistribution of the shell's mass and every change in its state of motion: a residual, uniform inertial frame that no material configuration would remove or alter. Nothing here is an appeal to any real observation of ours; it is a stipulated test case.

I expected it to discriminate, and the way it failed is my evidence for the hypothesis. The Machian relationist looks refuted, but relationism has at least three retreats. One: reinterpret the result, deny that there is a fact about the interior at all, and treat the gyroscope readings as really reporting something about the shell. Two: allow primitive inertial relations to hold among events that need not be occupied by matter, which makes the view structurally very hard to tell apart from sophisticated substantivalism. Three: concede the actual world and hold relationism as a modal thesis about which worlds there are, so that no possible observation could ever count against it. Meanwhile the substantivalist observes that the second retreat is substantivalism in different vocabulary, and the relationist replies that the vocabulary is exactly what is at issue, because it tracks what the laws quantify over. So an apparently decisive empirical case turns out to sort versions of each view rather than the views themselves, and the live question seems to relocate into modality and into what a theory is answerable to.

What I want from you:

1. A diagnosis, not a survey. Use what each side actually says, and tell me whether my three-fold hypothesis is right, incomplete, or wrong, and if it is wrong what replaces it.
2. A straight answer on whether the dispute, or the parts of it, are verbal, substantive, or two different questions sharing a name — and if substantive, what kind of substance it has. Say explicitly whether the sophisticated-substantivalist versus sophisticated-relationist standoff is merely notational.
3. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction to be drawn, a formal result, an experiment that would actually bite, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
4. Two constraints. Do not give me a literature survey or a reading list; I have read the standard material and I am asking about the structure of the argument. And engage the Hollow World directly rather than treating it as an aside — tell me whether its failure to discriminate is real evidence about the dispute or an artefact of how I rigged it.
5. Be explicit about your own confidence. Mark which part of your diagnosis you would defend and which you would hedge, name the single consideration that would most quickly lead you to abandon or substantially revise it, and say where you think your diagnosis is most likely to fail. One clearly marked weak point is worth more to me than uniform hedging.

About 900 to 1,200 words.
