I need help with two things: what our appeals panel should decide next week, and what we have actually been doing for the nine years I have been on it.

I am not an academic. I am 46, I work in logistics, and I have been playing the same persistent online world — Alderhold, a sandbox fantasy game that has been running since 2006, with a main continent, a separate permadeath server, a player-run economy, and about forty thousand active accounts — for most of its life. I moderated for four years and have sat on the five-person volunteer appeals panel since 2016. We rule on disputes players cannot settle themselves, and we write a short reason for each ruling. Reading back through our own reasons recently, I noticed they pull in at least three different directions, and I cannot tell which one we have really been running.

Five of our rulings, as I remember them:

(1) Halberd Keep, 2015. A guild spent roughly two years building a fortified hall. Another player used an exploit to raze it in a night. The builders were furious, the keep was a landmark everyone knew, and the exploit broke the rules. We restored it from backup and suspended the attacker for thirty days.

(2) The valley fight, 2017. In the designated full-loot valley, a player killed another and took about a month of gear. The loser was upset, but he had clicked through the warning at the zone boundary, and the rules plainly authorize what happened. We ruled there was no harm we could remedy.

(3) The Marchmont grave garden, 2019. One active player maintained a garden of named stones for characters lost on the permadeath server. It had no mechanical function whatsoever — no storage, no buffs, no economic role. A builder bulldozed it to put up a shop, on land the rules allowed him to claim. We ordered the stones restored and the shop moved, and wrote that the garden "had standing in the life of the server." That ruling is why I think we have quietly given up the idea that only things with a mechanical or economic use are real.

(4) The sleeping account, 2021. A player who had not logged in for four years had his hoard taken by someone he had shared a password with. He never knew it happened; a relative wrote to us. We restored the items anyway and suspended the taker, on the ground that a harm does not stop being a harm because the person was not in the room.

(5) The siege of the painted hall, 2023. A player's museum of in-game paintings — again mechanically worthless — sat in a hall that another player captured through a properly declared and fairly won siege. We let him keep the hall, ordered the paintings returned to the collector as items, and said that winning the building did not give him the right to destroy what was inside it.

As far as I can tell, three rules fit all five: a loss is a real harm if someone's actual experience of the world is wrecked by it; or if it sets back what a player has genuinely put into the world and could reasonably expect to keep; or if it damages something the community's practices have actually made matter. They agree on every case above. The new case splits them, and I would like to know how.

The complaint is about an NPC. For three years a player called Ossuary has treated one of the stock "greeter" characters in the town of Marchmont — a woman named Fen, one of hundreds of identical greeters, no quest, no stats, three recorded lines — as a friend. Ossuary visits most nights, leaves a worthless crafted copper ring at her feet, and reads in-character letters aloud in the square to two or three people; about fifteen players have turned up at one time or another, and there is a thread on the unofficial forums where most of the replies are jokes. Another player, Grieve, read that thread, decided the whole thing made the server look ridiculous, and spent two months qualifying for a scripted plague event — a legitimate feature, open to anyone who finishes the quest chain — which permanently kills civilian NPCs in a chosen district unless players run a counter-quest within forty-eight hours. He triggered it during a holiday weekend, while Ossuary was away on a vacation announced in that same thread, so nobody ran the counter-quest. Fen died along with about thirty other NPCs. He broke no codified rule and sent no messages. Ossuary came back, has slept badly, has cancelled a subscription, and has asked us to undo it. The developers can restore Fen in about five minutes, and can also flag her immune to future events if we ask.

For context on the vote: of the other four panelists, two are on record saying NPCs are code and caring about them is the player's problem; one wrote the grave-garden decision and still thinks it was right; one has not said anything yet. Also relevant: the developers have announced that next patch the greeters will be replaced by versions that keep a short running memory of what players did to them, so a future Grieve could make Fen "remember" being abandoned during a holiday weekend.

What I want from you, and please do not send me to books or articles — I have no library access and I am not asking what anyone has published, I want the reasoning:

What are the rules that fit our five precedents, stated sharply enough that they disagree about Fen? What does each of them say we should do about restoring her, about granting her immunity, and about sanctioning Grieve? What do you expect us actually to decide, and what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of our five precedents is the closest analog, and what principle would we be committing ourselves to with each of the options? And what additional fact, or what future case, would settle it — for instance, would it change anything if two hundred players mourned her, if Ossuary had spent real money on the rings, if Ossuary had never told a soul, if Grieve had done it for loot rather than to hurt, or if the new memory-bearing NPCs are already in place when it happens again?
