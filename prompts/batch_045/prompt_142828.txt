I am in the lower sixth and writing my Extended Project on Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five, and I should be grateful for your help with the part of it that I cannot get straight by myself.

The bit I care about is the metaphysics the novel gives Billy Pilgrim through the Tralfamadorians: the creatures who keep him in a zoo on their planet tell him that every moment of time has always existed and always will, that they can see all of it at once, and that this is why death is not worth fussing over. I have been reading about the Rietdijk–Putnam argument in special relativity — roughly, that because simultaneity is relative to a frame of reference, events I should call future are simultaneous with my present in some other frame, and that if the argument works it gives you a block universe in which all events are equally real. I want to read the novel through that argument rather than hang the argument on the novel as decoration, and my supervisor has warned me off both of the obvious failure modes: flattening the book into an illustration of a view I have read about elsewhere, and bending the physics until it says what the book wants it to say.

Two things are blocking me.

First, the Tralfamadorians seem to be asserting three things as though they were one: that all times are equally real; that the future is fixed and cannot be changed; and that it therefore does not much matter what anyone does, which is the register the novel's own refrain about death runs in. I suspect these come apart. I need the separations made precisely — why the block universe is not the same claim as determinism, and why determinism is not the same claim as fatalism about agency. I also want to know about a further thesis I have started calling temporal flatness: the thought that the difference between anticipating something and remembering it, or between deliberating and reviewing a decision, is metaphysically second-rate, a mere feature of where a creature happens to be located. Please tell me whether temporal flatness is genuinely entailed by the block universe or whether it is something I have invented, and if it is not entailed, what would have to be added to the argument to get it. I think the Tralfamadorians are living by temporal flatness rather than by the block universe, and I want to know whether that is a real distinction.

Second, I am unsure how much the argument actually proves even if it succeeds. A presentist is supposed to be able to reject some premise of it, but I cannot work out which one, or how violent the rejection has to be: is the disputed premise about physics, or about what it means for something to exist? And does it matter that the Tralfamadorians do not arrive at their view by physics at all, but by claiming to perceive the whole of time from outside it? If the novel's characters reach the same-sounding conclusion by a different route, does the argument vindicate them or only resemble them?

Then the reading. Please work through three moments — Billy's first episode of coming unstuck in time, the zoo on Tralfamadore, and the firebombing and the slaughterhouse cellar — and at each one say what the block-universe lens makes visible and where it misfires. I want at least one place where the honest answer is that the physics does not deliver what the scene wants it to. I should also like your view on whether it is the Tralfamadorian speeches that carry the metaphysics or the shape of the narration itself, since a book told by someone unstuck in time may be arguing for flatness in its form even where its characters argue badly.

Lastly, the consolation. The Tralfamadorian view reaches Billy as comfort, and it seems to me to be comfort bought at some cost: if every moment simply is, then it is hard to see what is left of the thought that what happened at Dresden was a wrong done to someone. Does the block universe, honestly understood, underwrite that comfort, or does it leave grieving and blaming exactly where they were? And what does the novel know about the experience of time — trauma, recurrence, being unable to leave one event behind — that an argument about what exists cannot reach?

Around a thousand words, addressed to a capable sixth-former rather than to a first-year seminar, would be ideal.
