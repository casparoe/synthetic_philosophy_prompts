I'm the TA for a third-year seminar on Schopenhauer this term — mostly philosophy majors, plus two composition majors who are by now pretty sick of The World as Will and Representation — and the last session is on the music chapter in Book III. I want to close it with a poem instead of another summary of the reading, and since the reading has worn everyone out I'd rather the form do the arguing than the footnotes.

The set-up, in case it helps: for Schopenhauer every other art copies the Platonic Ideas, and the Ideas are themselves only grades of the objectification of the will, so painting and poetry speak (he thinks) only of shadows; music skips the Ideas altogether and is a copy of the will itself, which is why it gets under our skin so much more effectively, and why he says the world could as well be described as music made flesh as will made flesh — even, he claims, that music could to some extent still exist if the world did not. The analogy he draws inside a single piece is what I want the poem built on: the deep bass is the lowest grade, the dumb mass of the planet, gravity and stone; the middle voices fill in the plant and animal world; the high leading melody is the human business of striving, wandering off into dissonance and coming home to the tonic, with the satisfaction at the cadence lasting about a second before the whole thing wants again.

So: a villanelle, nineteen lines, iambic pentameter, in which the two refrains are the bass line and the melody line. I don't want to write them for you, but here's what they have to be able to do. They should be roughly the same length and metre so you could in principle sing them simultaneously, one as a descant over the other as a ground, and they must not paraphrase, answer, or contradict each other — the point is that they're two copies of the same original, not a copy and the thing it copies. Keep the vocabulary of feeling out of the melody refrain entirely: no grief, no longing, no yearning; do it with verbs of motion and wanting. Let the tercets climb through the grades (inorganic, then plant, then animal, then human) so the bass keeps coming back underneath while the upper voice gets more restless, and let the strictness of the returning lines be the answer to the objection one of my composition students keeps raising — if music copies the will directly rather than the Ideas, why should it have any architecture at all, keys and cadences and voice-leading, instead of being one undifferentiated surge? The poem should make you feel that you can only hear the surge because of the scaffolding. In the final quatrain I want both refrains together, arriving, but not resting: the cadence's satisfaction is momentary and the willing picks right back up, so the last line shouldn't close anything, it should start something. It also has to work for someone who's read none of the book — no theory vocab, no name-dropping, and ideally readable at once as a poem about a piece of music and as a poem about a landscape, without ever settling which of the two is the original — while still being something I could hand to the composition majors without them wincing. Definately don't add a note explaining the argument at the end; I want to hand it out cold.
