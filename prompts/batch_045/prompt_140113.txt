American English, please.

My thesis: some normative reasons are unowned. There are reasons that are no one's reasons -- not reasons for anyone to do anything -- and they are still reasons, not merely values or bad-making features. Call them free-floating rationales. They aren't idle: they fix the deontic cost of states of affairs, and agents pick them up by a relation I call proxy uptake. When you acquire causal access to a state of affairs, you don't create a reason; you inherit an address for one that was already floating there.

Two steps plus a payoff. Step one, the Sealed Valley case: a valley is sealed by a rockfall that predates every agent; inside it an animal suffers for years; no agent ever could have helped and none ever will be able to. There is, I say, a reason to relieve that suffering, and it doesn't depend on anyone's being able to act. Step two, inheritance: when an agent later acquires causal access to a state of affairs, the free-floating rationale gets addressed to her rather than generated in her. Payoff: this explains why learning that no one could ever have helped doesn't make the cost go away, and it explains why newly-possible duties feel like discoveries rather than decisions.

The room: roughly half are reasons-first non-naturalists of the broadly Scanlonian sort, for whom a reason is essentially a triadic relation between a fact, an agent and an action -- they'll say an unowned reason is just a value wearing a hat. Five or six are Kantian constructivists: reasons are norms of practical reason, a norm with no addressee isn't a norm, and they'll press the guidance-and-motivation point hard. One expressivist who'll ask why I'm not simply reifying my own attitude of "there's something to be said against that." And a decision theorist at the back who will want to know what difference any of this makes to what anyone should do anywhere.
