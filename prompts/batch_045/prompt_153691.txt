I would like an audit of an analogy rather than a verdict on a puzzle, because the analogy is doing all the work in an argument I keep losing.

The set-up first. A casino offers the following. A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads. If the first heads falls on the nth toss you are paid (−1)^(n+1)·2^n/n dollars — plus $2 if the first heads is on toss one, minus $2 if on toss two, plus $8/3 if on toss three, minus $4 if on toss four, and so on, the nth case having probability 2^(−n). Weighting payoffs by probabilities in the order the tosses come gives 1 − ½ + ⅓ − ¼ + …, which converges to ln 2, about 69 cents. But it converges only conditionally: list the same terms in another order and you get a different total, and by choosing the order you can get any total you like. So the usual verdict is that this game has no expected value at all, and hence no fair entry price — a quantity that depends on the order in which you happen to list the outcomes is not really a quantity.

What my friend says — he works in quantitative finance, and I have since watched the same move made in a comment thread — is that an expected value is nothing but the long-run average: what you get per play if you play the same gamble again and again. On that reading, the fair price is about 69 cents, the verdict above is pedantry about a convention for summing an infinite list, and the long-run average cannot be order-dependent, because adding up a list of numbers never depends on the order you add them in. He uses this to conclude that the game is worth its naive sum, that the no-expected-value people have confused a definition with a fact, and that I should be willing to pay 60 cents a play for as many plays as I like.

I tried to check this by simulation, and the results did not look like the clean convergence he predicts. In blocks of ten thousand, a hundred thousand and a million plays, the running average never sits still: it wanders in a band roughly between 0.3 and 1.1, it lurches by visible amounts when a very large payoff lands, and the spread between blocks shrinks far more slowly than it does for an ordinary gamble — going from ten thousand plays to a million barely narrowed it. He says that is just slow convergence and the limit is still 69 cents.

Then I tried something he did not address. I wrote down variants in which the same list of payoff magnitudes (2, 2, 8/3, 4, 32/5, …) is attached to the same list of probabilities (1/2, 1/4, 1/8, …) in a different pairing. No magnitude is added or removed, no probability is changed; only which magnitude goes with which probability is different. The naive sum changes, as the rearrangement point predicts — some pairings give a total above a dollar, some below zero — and when I simulated those, the running averages seemed to be heading for those other numbers rather than for 69 cents, and in the more aggressively reshuffled pairings they did not seem to be heading anywhere at all. Yet it is hard for me to say what about the game has changed: no payout is larger or smaller than before, no outcome is more or less likely, and a single play looks no worse.

So I want the analogy audited, not just the arithmetic. Please give me the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — between the source domain (repeated sampling, an average over a list, the law of large numbers) and the target (a single play, the payoff-weighted sum, the expectation): what plays the role of a sample, of the sample size, of the estimator, of the quantity being estimated, of convergence. Then the places the mapping fails, sorted by whether they matter for the use my friend is making of it: I suspect some of them are harmless and some of them are the whole ballgame. In particular I would like to know what the long-run-average picture imports that the game does not have — that the average exists, that it is order-free, that a package of n plays is worth n times one play, that repetition is available to a person offered the game once — and which of those imports his 69-cents conclusion actually rests on.

Three things I would like addressed head on. First, whether the long-run average for the original game converges at all, and if it does, whether it converges because of a law of large numbers or for some other reason; a mathematician I asked muttered something about a condition of the form "n times the probability of a payoff exceeding n has to go to zero", which I could not follow, and said the comparison class is not the Cauchy distribution, whose sample mean never settles, but something with slightly thinner tails. Second, what to make of the pairing variants: if the long run does deliver a number, and that number tracks the pairing, in what sense is the number a property of the gamble rather than of a description of it, and what does that do to the claim that the long-run reading dissolves the problem? Third, whether the conclusion survives at all: is the game worth 69 cents, is there no fair price, or is the truth that you get a definite answer only after switching from one decision rule to a weaker one, and if so what that weaker rule costs you — what it stops you being able to say, which comparisons it forbids, whether it still lets you price a bundle of plays by pricing one play.

If the long-run-average picture is the wrong thing to reach for, tell me what a better one would be: expectation as a centre of mass, as a limit of truncated expectations, as a price derived from a decision rule rather than read off a sum, or something else — and say what that alternative makes vivid that the average picture hides.
