I work in assessment design for professional certification, and over the years I have noticed a pattern I have no name for. It does not seem to be quite the thing people usually mean when they say a signal is trustworthy because it would be expensive to fake.

The pattern appears wherever judgment is made on the spot rather than on a submitted artifact. A singer who sounds flawless on the recording is asked to sing it again unaccompanied, slower, in a different key. A candidate submits a polished written case study, and the examiner instead asks for a second one, from a different domain, with ten minutes' notice. An applicant tells a well-shaped story about a difficulty they handled; the interviewer asks what the other person in the story said next, and then what happened three weeks later. In each case the performance itself costs the strong candidate almost nothing: producing one more instance is not expensive for them in the way an elaborate display is expensive. What seems to do the work is that the weaker candidate cannot produce an *arbitrary* instance, or cannot produce one that hangs together with the one they just produced. They can produce the one they prepared.

Three features strike me as odd, and they are why I suspect this is not merely the expensive-signal story in different clothing. First, the credibility seems to sit on the receiver's side: the examiner chooses which variation to demand, and the reliability appears to depend on that choice being unconstrained and not fully predictable in advance. Second, when a format becomes well known — when the follow-up questions are published, or the oral exam acquires a stable question bank — the format stops discriminating, even though nothing about the candidates has changed and nothing they do has become cheaper. An expensive display does not seem to lose its force merely from being anticipated. Third, when examiners want more confidence they often add a difficulty: less time, a worse instrument, an unfamiliar key. The costliness is imposed by the receiver at the moment of testing rather than borne by the signaler in producing the display.

So, concretely, what I want is this. Is there an established concept for reliability of this kind — reliability arising from the receiver's capacity to demand unprepared variation rather than from the signaler's expenditure — and is it treated as a mechanism in its own right or as a special case of the cost-based one? If a special case, I would like to know what the cost is a cost *of*, given that the marginal instance looks free to both parties. If distinct, what are its boundary conditions: does it collapse once a candidate can invest once in a large enough stock of prepared variants; does it require the space of possible demands to be open-ended; does it require the demand to be unpredictable, and if so is that a different requirement from the other two? And what should we say about the reverse case, where those best at the underlying skill are systematically worst at producing it on demand — a strong writer who freezes in an oral defence, a performer whose best work is heavily revised rather than spontaneous? That last case matters to me professionally, and I have no vocabulary for what goes wrong in it.

A label alone will not help me. I need the carving: what exactly this phenomenon is, which nearby notions it is liable to be confused with, how it differs from each, and where precisely it stops working. If no established term exists, please propose one, with a definition and the boundary cases it is meant to settle.
