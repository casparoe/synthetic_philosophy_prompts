I spent roughly thirty-five years in visual psychophysics — single-unit work in striate cortex early on, then human orientation discrimination and perceptual learning — and I have retired into a philosophy of perception reading group, where I keep landing on the penetration side of the dispute about whether what we believe, want, or have learned can reach into the content of perceptual experience. I would like to practice arguing for it against someone who genuinely disagrees.

Please play a committed opponent: a defender of the encapsulation of early vision in the Fodor–Pylyshyn line. I want the strong version, not the straw man. You hold that vision is a mandatory, informationally encapsulated module; that the stock apparent cases of penetration — the ambiguous figures, the desired object that looks closer, the hill that looks steeper to the tired walker, the cross-modal speech effects — are all either attention selecting among contents the module already delivers, or post-perceptual judgment and report, or a shifted response criterion; and that where experience really does change, the change is sub-personal retuning of the machinery by use, which is perceptual learning and not penetration by belief, desire, or concepts. You may concede small points — you can grant that attention moves things about and that some laboratory effects are real — but you will not concede that a personal-level cognitive state alters what a visual experience presents, and you will not break character until I write the single word stop.

Here is my opening. I am going to stipulate a case rather than cite one, because I want the argument and not the literature. Two groups of observers train, over a fortnight, to discriminate a novel pair of shape categories — call them glomps and fleeps — at a fixed retinal position, with brief masked presentations and trial-by-trial feedback, until both groups reach the same measured sensitivity. The groups differ in exactly one respect. The first is told, before training begins, that glomps and fleeps are two real kinds of thing the laboratory has been studying for years and that every observer eventually learns to see the difference directly. The second is told that the display generates the shapes by rule and that the two names are only a bookkeeping device, carrying no claim about what is out there. After training, in a session with no task, no feedback, and no forced choice — only, describe what you see — nearly everyone in the first group volunteers a categorical look: the shapes now present as glomps or as fleeps, and they report seeing which it is before they can say anything about the features. In the second group, at identical sensitivity, the reports stay at the level of features — arcs, notches, angles — and nobody volunteers the categories. Then everyone is debriefed in full: both groups are told the truth, that the categories were assigned arbitrarily by the experimenter, and both are put back in the booth. The first group's reports revert over a few dozen trials; the second group's do not change, because they never had them.

I take that pattern to be hard to explain away, and here is why. It is not attention, since the two groups attend to the same stimuli at the same positions for the same number of trials, and their sensitivities are matched. It is not a criterion or a response bias, since the measure I am leaning on is spontaneous free description with nothing to be right or wrong about. It is not the distal stimulus or the retinal input, which are identical across groups. And it is not sub-personal retuning either: retuning by use should have been produced equally in both groups, since the training on the shapes was the same, and retuning should not be undone in an afternoon by being told that the categories were arbitrary. What changed, and then un-changed, tracks what the observers believed about the categories. So a belief about what kinds of thing one is looking at reached into the content of the visual experience itself, which is precisely what you deny.

And I do not think you can answer this with your best card, which I take to be the epistemic one. You want to say that if belief could reach into perception, perception could no longer serve as the tribunal that adjudicates among our beliefs about the world: the evidence would already contain the conclusion, and observation would be as theory-laden as the theories it was supposed to check. But that argument assumes the point in dispute — that perception's authority requires insulation from what we already know — and I think that is an inheritance from an old empiricism rather than a finding about visual systems. A visual system that has been shaped by what it has learned about the world is, in my experience of actual visual systems, a better detector rather than a compromised one, and its deliverances remain answerable to the scene, because a wrong thing learned produces systematic error that the world punishes. Insulation, then, is not what makes perception evidentially useful, and your reason for denying penetration fails.

Take that apart.
