I am writing a novel set in the 2080s in which a statutory body, the Nomic Standards Panel, maintains a public register of laws of nature, and its entries have teeth: under the Act no patent may be granted for a device whose operation would require a registered law to be false, and no public money may go to a project premised on a registered law's being merely locally true. A whole chapter turns on the ruling the Panel is about to give, and I want that ruling to be one a careful philosopher of science could actually have arrived at, rather than one that merely sounds authoritative to a lay reader.

The precedents in my file are these. The Panel refused to register "no sphere of solid gold exceeds a mile across", on the ground that it is a limit on how matter happens to be distributed rather than on how it can behave. It refused "every coin in the Bank's vault on 1 January 2079 was silver", calling that an inventory rather than a law. It has a standing rule against entering any generalization whose terms are fixed by reference to a date or a named object, under which it refused "everything sampled before the Change is green and everything sampled after is blue". It refused "a bound planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times", placing it instead on a separate list of derivative regularities, because it holds only given more fundamental laws together with how the system happened to start. It registered in the highest grade "no causal influence outruns light in a vacuum", "every electron carries a charge of one negative unit", and "the total energy of an isolated system does not change". And it registered, in a lower, domain-relative grade, "every cell is the offspring of a cell", which it said holds in every biologically possible history rather than merely in ours.

The application now before it concerns a quantity my future physics calls the Ashgrove constant, κ: a single dimensionless number, measured to twelve decimal places in eleven independent kinds of system, never derived from anything deeper, and, the Panel is advised, able coherently to have taken any of a range of nearby values — though worlds with those values would contain nothing capable of storing information. The Ashgrove Institute wants to build a device that would work only if κ could be varied locally; a rival party has applied to have κ's constancy entered not as a law but as a contingent matter of fact. What I need from you is this: a prediction of how the Panel rules on κ — law, contingent fact, or something on the derivative list — on the assumption that it wants to be consistent with itself; which of its precedents is the closest analogue and why; an account of the principle it is implicitly running (a best-system account, a relations-among-universals account, a powers-or-essences account, a governing account, an invariance-under-counterfactual-variation account, or something I have not thought of) together with the other principles that fit its precedents just as well but come apart on this one; a best guess among them with your reasons for preferring it; and a description of the one further application that, if I put it before the Panel next, would best separate the candidates that survive. Please also say how confident you are in each part of what you tell me, and single out the claim you would most expect a professional philosopher of science to reject. I should prefer the answer in British English.
