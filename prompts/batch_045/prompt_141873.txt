For thirty-odd years I worked on replication and repair enzymes, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a reading group in the philosophy department that is currently working through cultural evolution. I arrived sympathetic to the Darwinian approach to culture — the idea that ideas, techniques and stories form lineages with heritable variation in their own transmissibility has the right shape to me — and one of the other retired people in the group is an enthusiastic defender of treating memes as a genuine second replicator. At the last session I presented the argument below, which I had put together partly to needle him, and I have since become suspicious of it. Not because I can find a step that is clearly false, but because I think it proves considerably more than I am willing to accept, and I cannot locate the mistake precisely enough to say what I am giving up.

(1) A Darwinian explanation of why some trait recurs in a population requires more than recurrence: it requires heritable variation in fitness. Copies must resemble their sources in whatever respects affect their probability of being copied again.
(2) Cultural transmission does not deliver that. Nothing stored in one head is transferred into another; a learner observes or is told something, infers a representation, and later generates behaviour from it. In experimental diffusion chains the content is visibly transformed within a few steps, and the "same" variant in two different people is identified retrospectively, by similarity of what they say or do.
(3) If what passes between people is a reconstruction rather than a copy, then the resemblance between one person's version and the next person's is not descent with modification. It is the repeated generation of a similar product by a shared generative mechanism — the same relation that holds between a genotype and the phenotype that development builds from it.
(4) So what explains the recurrence and the population-level distribution of cultural items is the structure of that generative mechanism: the psychological and ecological factors that make some versions easier to reconstruct than others, which the anthropologists in the group call attractors. Selection among cultural variants is then a redescription of the population-level output, not the causal process behind it, and treating memes as replicators adds nothing that the study of those biases does not already give us.
(5) But nothing in (1)–(4) depends on anything peculiar to culture. An organism is not a copy of its genome either. Every generation rebuilds the phenotype from DNA together with the machinery of the cell; development is sensitive to context, is channelled onto a limited set of outcomes, and "the same" trait in parent and offspring is identified retrospectively by similarity of phenotype.
(6) So if steps (2)–(4) are sound, the parallel argument shows that natural selection on genes does not causally explain the recurrence of adaptive phenotypes — only the structure of development does — and that talk of genes as replicators adds nothing. That I cannot accept: whatever else is true, selection on genes is the paradigm causal explanation in my old field.
(7) There is also a sharper, quantitative version from my own area that reaches the same conclusion. Quasispecies theory gives an error threshold: the amount of information selection can hold together in a lineage is bounded by the accuracy of copying per unit copied, up to a factor depending on the selective advantage, and without repair the limit is reached very quickly. The copying fidelity reported for cultural transmission chains looks worse than that, so by the threshold criterion no complex cultural item could be maintained by selection on transmission; it would have to be regenerated. Yet my colleagues routinely treat RNA viruses — whose copying fidelity per nucleotide is far worse than anything I have seen quoted for diffusion chains, and which lack the repair machinery that DNA genomes have — as evolving by selection on their sequences. So the raw fidelity number cannot be doing the work I wanted it to do.

What I would like from you is a diagnosis: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I would prefer to keep (1) in some form, and I do not want to abandon the claim in (2) that much of cultural transmission is reconstructive at the micro-level, because I find that empirically well supported; nor do I want to concede that every domain of culture is handled equally well by the same story, since cumulative technology feels quite different from urban legends or religious concepts. My suspicion is that I have slid between two different notions of heredity, or between "the item is not copied" and "the item is not heritable", but I cannot make the distinction sharp enough to say where (3) stops being a good inference, and I would rather not simply pick whichever step saves the conclusion I like. If more than one step will do the job, tell me which is cheapest and what each of the alternatives commits me to about the cases in (6) and (7).
