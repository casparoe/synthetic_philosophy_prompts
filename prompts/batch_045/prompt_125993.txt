I have no academic background in any of this — I got into it because of an argument at work about whether test scores are "genetic," and I've been reading on my own for about a year. I think I've got the basic idea now, but I want someone to check whether the package I've ended up with actually holds together, because I keep getting the feeling it doesn't. Here's where I've landed:

1. Heritability is a population statistic: a ratio of variances, in one population, at one time, under whatever range of environments that population happens to have. It doesn't tell you why any particular person turned out the way they did, and it isn't a measure of how biological or fixed or innate a trait is.

2. So something can be very heritable and still be completely fixable. If everyone in a town eats the same deficient diet, the differences in height within that town could be nearly all genetic, while a change in diet would still make the whole town taller. I think this is the single most important thing people get wrong.

3. I've started splitting this into two numbers in my head, because I couldn't find a name for the second one. Heritability is how much of the *spread* is genetic. The other one — I call it the lift — is how much a given person's value would change if you moved them from the worst environment anyone in that population actually has to the best one, holding the person fixed. I think those two numbers are basically independent of each other: you can have high heritability and a big lift, and you can also have low heritability and a small lift (say most of the differences are just random developmental noise, so moving people into a better environment doesn't do much for anybody).

4. On policy, though, I part company with the people who say heritability tells you nothing about what's changeable. I think it gives you a ceiling: if a trait is 80% heritable, then no realistic policy can take away more than about 20% of the spread in it. That feels to me like it follows straight from what the number is, and I think the "says nothing about malleability" slogan has been oversold.

5. On methods, I don't put much weight on twin studies. I'd rather have DNA-based estimates, and I especially like designs that compare siblings to each other, on the grounds that holding family background fixed is the only way to be sure you're not just measuring the neighbourhood somebody grew up in.

6. Related to that, I think the evidence pretty clearly says the family you grew up in has little lasting effect on how you turn out as an adult — the shared environment part keeps coming out near zero for adult traits.

7. I think heritability within a group tells you essentially nothing about differences between groups, and it annoys me when people use the first to say something about the second.

8. Three things I can't get straight. (a) I think measurement error means heritability gets *under*estimated, since any sloppiness in measuring the trait ends up counted as environment; but I also think the equal-environments assumption in twin studies means it gets *over*estimated, since identical twins being treated more alike gets counted as genes. I feel like I shouldn't be able to believe both. (b) I think heritability is defined using an additive decomposition, so when genes interact with each other or with environments the number is partly a fiction; but I also think it's the right number to use when you want to predict how much children will resemble their parents. Those seem to fight each other. (c) I think the fact that outcomes run in families is one of the most important facts about inequality, and I also think heritability being low would be perfectly compatible with that. But people talk as if those two thoughts are at war.

One more thing that may or may not belong: I've read that average test scores climbed a lot over the last century while heritability stayed roughly where it was, and that used to bother me and now I've decided it doesn't. I'm not sure I decided that for a good reason.
