The question (parts (a)–(c) of question 4, marks as indicated):

Kant holds that an action has genuine moral worth only if it is done from duty (aus Pflicht) rather than merely in accordance with duty (pflichtmäßig).

(a) Explain this claim, and say whether it follows from it that an action has moral worth only when the agent performs it unwillingly or against her desires. (5)

(b) Two volunteers staff a crisis line overnight. Amara does it because she finds the work absorbing and is glad of the company; the thought that she might be required to do it has never occurred to her. Bo does it although he finds the work draining and unpleasant, and he does it only because he is convinced that, given how few people take these shifts, he is required to take them. On Kant's doctrine, whose action has moral worth, if either? Does the doctrine give the right verdict here? (10)

(c) Amara then reads Kant, comes to believe that she is required to volunteer, and resolves to act on that belief. Suppose that on the nights in question what in fact gets her to the phone is the same spontaneous interest in the work as before, and that her belief that she is required plays no part at all in moving her to act. Does her action have moral worth on Kant's view? What does your answer suggest about whether moral worth can depend on features of an agent's motivation that she could not avow or introspect? (10)

The student's answer:

Kant's distinction is a distinction about the ground of the will, not about observable behaviour. An action is in accordance with duty when it is the action that duty prescribes; it is from duty when the agent's reason for performing it is that duty prescribes it. The same outward act can be one or the other depending on what moves the agent, which is why Kant can say that the shopkeeper who gives correct change because honesty is good for business, and the person who helps others because doing so gratifies a naturally sympathetic temperament, both do what duty requires without their actions having genuine moral worth. Worth is therefore invisible from the outside, and Kant himself remarks that we cannot be certain even in our own case that an action was done purely from duty.

It does not follow that worth requires reluctance. Kant's requirement is that duty be the agent's motive, not that inclination be absent, and his own example of the person who helps others while weighed down by private grief and indifferent to their suffering is meant to show that worth shows up most clearly when inclination is missing, not that it requires this. Worth is also not simply a matter of acting unwillingly: Bo's reluctance does not by itself give his action worth, since a person can act grudgingly from fear of disapproval or from a desire to think well of himself, and in that case duty is not what moves him even though the act is unpleasant.

So in part (b) only Bo's action has moral worth. Because worth requires that duty be the agent's motive, the presence of any inclination in favour of the act rules out worth: Amara likes the work, so however she conceives of what she is doing her action cannot have moral worth. This is not a consequence Kant shrinks from. Having said that such actions are amiable and deserve encouragement, he nevertheless holds that Amara's volunteering is, strictly speaking, not a morally permissible action, since an action done from inclination alone fails to satisfy the only condition on which an action can be morally right. That is the counterintuitive part of the doctrine, and I think the objection in the question is successful: any view that classes willing, cheerful beneficence as impermissible, while giving full moral credit to the grudging volunteer, has got things backwards.

In part (c) Amara's action does have moral worth. Acting from duty just is acting on a maxim that one can will to be a universal law, and having read Kant she now wills her maxim under that description; the fact that she continues to enjoy the work is irrelevant, since the universal-law test does not ask what caused her to act. It is also lucky that this is the right answer, because Kant holds that we can never be sure what our own motives are, so he cannot make worth depend on the causal role of a motive that introspection cannot reach. What she can avow is that she acts because she is required to, and that is what makes the action hers in the relevant sense.
