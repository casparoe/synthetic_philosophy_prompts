I am trying to say, precisely, when a non-human thing deserves to be written into a historical narrative as an actor rather than left in the scenery as a condition, and when citing one tips over into the environmental determinism I want to avoid. I have been going by my gut on individual cases; below are the verdicts. Alongside them is a principle I have been testing, which I suspect is either too strong or simply the wrong shape. I do not need the historiography surveyed, and I am not looking for sources; I want the principle.

The principle I have been using has three clauses. First, plan-revision: X is an actor in an episode only if X's onset is datable inside that episode's own timescale and forced the human agents to abandon and remake plans they had already made. Second, direction: a non-human factor may be cited to explain what agents were responding to, but not substituted for what they decided. Third, invitation: where the arrival or the magnitude of X is itself the downstream product of human choices, X is an 'invited actor' — a third thing, neither pure actor nor pure condition.

The verdicts. The eruption of Mount Tambora and the crop failures that followed across Europe and New England: full actor, and no uneasiness at all about letting a volcano shape migration and grain prices. Coal in Britain: condition, not actor, and 'coal caused the Industrial Revolution' strikes me as lazy — though I do not object to saying that the location of the coalfields helped fix where the mills went. The potato blight in Ireland: the blight is an actor, but I resist saying it caused the deaths; grain kept leaving the country and relief was organized badly, and I want to call the blight a trigger and the policy the cause of the mortality — even though the blight is by far the more abnormal ingredient. The Black Death and the subsequent scarcity of labour, rising wages, and weakening of serfdom in Western Europe: fine, the bacterium is an actor, and I am happy to let a germ carry a large social change. The Antonine and Justinian plagues invoked to explain the spread of Christianity or the failure of the Mediterranean reconquest: suspicious, and it feels like hand-waving of exactly the sort I have just endorsed in the Black Death case. Mosquito-borne disease in the Atlantic world, which made some coasts lethal to Europeans and survivable to others and so helped fix what kind of colonies and what kind of labour systems took hold: fine, no determinism worry, and I am content to call the mosquito an actor. The same style of argument at millennial scale — continental orientation, domesticated animals, Eurasian germs killing the overwhelming majority of the indigenous population of the Americas: acceptable for the demographic collapse, but I bristle when it is extended to explain why the West rather than anywhere else, or used as a master key for the whole rise of Europe. The North Sea flood of 1953: actor. The bare fact that much of the Netherlands lies below sea level: condition — and yet explanations of Dutch water management that begin from that fact seem to me entirely legitimate, while explanations of Dutch commercial primacy that begin from the same fact seem to me determinist and I would rather not use them.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the Black Death against the Antonine and Justinian plagues: in both a pathogen is invoked for a large social outcome, and my clause about mechanism and my clause about direction do not obviously separate them. I also cannot get comfortable about the coalfield verdict, since the plan-revision clause would seem to make every slow geography a mere condition forever.

What I want from you: a precisely stated principle, or two candidates if the choice is genuinely open, with the case that motivates each clause; a version simple enough that someone holding only a written narrative could run it as a test; its verdicts on four cases I have not listed — a monsoon that fails to arrive, so that the actor would be an absence; a storm that a city had anticipated and built against, which then arrives as forecast; a flood whose height was nobody's doing but whose damage curve is entirely a function of where people were permitted to build; and the exhaustion of a resource that the agents always knew was finite. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and whether the right response is to drop the verdict or to add a clause. Give me also the simpler rival that overturns one of my verdicts, so I can see what buying simplicity costs. And tell me what all of this says about the case I keep reversing on: whether the 1918 influenza pandemic belongs in the causal story of how the First World War ended, or whether that is precisely the kind of explanation I claim to distrust.
