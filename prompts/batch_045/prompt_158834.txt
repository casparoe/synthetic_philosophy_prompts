I write about artificial intelligence for a newspaper, and for the past two years nearly every conversation I have had about how to train systems whose work we cannot fully check has ended in the same place. One person says the field should supervise the process: grade the reasoning steps, not merely the answer. Another says this is a detour — that outcomes are what we actually care about, that step-level labels import a parochial human picture of how thinking ought to look, and that a process reward model is no easier to trust than an outcome one. Both sides then cite the same handful of results and neither moves. Last spring I sat in on a workshop where two researchers agreed line by line about what the published comparisons show — that step-level reward models outperform answer-level ones on some mathematical reasoning benchmarks, that the advantage narrows or disappears once compute is equalized or the problems get harder, that both kinds of signal can be gamed — and then drew opposite conclusions about how a lab should spend its annotation budget. That conversation is the reason I am writing to you.

Before I file another piece on it, I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on it. Let me set out what each side actually says. The process camp: credit assignment is finer-grained when the signal attaches to steps, so you catch the cases where the answer is right for the wrong reasons — a coding agent whose patch passes the suite by special-casing the test inputs, a derivation whose final line is correct because two errors cancelled — and as tasks grow longer than any human can audit end to end, the outcome signal is either absent or is itself produced by a model, so per-step signal is needed regardless. The outcome camp: the result is the point; intermediate labels encode one species of human reasoning and penalize valid routes that merely look unfamiliar, such as a model that reaches a correct result by a move no annotator would have endorsed; process reward models have their own failure surface and their own annotation burden; and in any case process supervision is just outcome supervision applied to a decomposition — reward the step of producing a correct sub-answer and you have an outcome supervisor for a smaller task — so the distinction is one of granularity, not of kind.

I have two hypotheses, which I offer as hypotheses. The first is what I have started calling the verifier's filter: the empirical record is drawn almost entirely from tasks selected because their outcomes are cheap to check — problems with a known answer, code with a test suite, games with a score — and those are precisely the tasks on which outcome supervision works by construction. The dispute that matters concerns the complementary set: long-horizon work whose result is expensive or impossible to verify directly, a research finding, a legal strategy, a design that fails only after years. There the outcome signal is missing or is supplied by another system we do not fully trust, and the question changes shape. If this is right, the debate presents as empirical but rests on an unrepresentative sample, with both sides extrapolating from the same selected cases in opposite directions. The second hypothesis is that the word supervision is doing three different jobs — the training signal, the evidence offered in a safety case, and the thing that shapes what the system does when nobody is looking — and that the camps are answering under different ones.

A third thing I notice in my notebooks: I now sort failures into three currencies, and the two camps weight them differently. Shortcut success, where the outcome passes and the process is defective. Alien competence, where the outcome passes and the process is merely unrecognizable to human graders. Plausible-chain failure, where every step looks locally fine and the outcome is wrong. The first gets all the attention, the second is the one the outcome camp invokes, and the third — the one I expect to dominate as horizons lengthen — almost never comes up.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? A verbal dispute about a word; a substantive empirical dispute about which failure mode dominates in the regime that matters, currently adjudicated on evidence that cannot settle it; a clash over standards of evidence, where benchmark deltas and safety-case arguments and armchair arguments about credit assignment are being treated as interchangeable; a disagreement about which cases count as data; or bedrock commitments — for instance, about whether human-legible reasoning is a safety asset or a liability — that neither side can argue for? Is there a formal question hiding underneath, something about whether an outcome signal can in principle identify the policy you want rather than merely the policy that scores well? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, an experiment sharper than another benchmark, perhaps a task constructed so that the two signals provably diverge, or a result about when they cannot — and if no progress should be expected, I would rather be told that plainly, with the reason. I have read the survey pieces; I am not asking for one. And if you think I am asking the wrong question in interviews, tell me what I should be asking instead.
