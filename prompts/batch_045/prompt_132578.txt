i'm in this hodge-podge working group — two historians, a political scientist, and a philosopher of science who keeps saying things like "modal robustness" — and we've spent a whole term on one question: were the big outcomes of history inevitable? Our running example is the summer of 1914 and whether a general european war was already locked in once the assassination happened, or whether it was a cascade that hinged on a handful of decisions, any one of which could have gone the other way. And we are completely stuck: after a term literally nobody has changed their mind, and I dont think anybody has even changed their reasons.

Here's roughly the shape of it:

- The "forces" people: given the alliance structure, the mobilisation timetables, the arms races and the domestic pressure on each government, something like a general war was arriving within a few years regardless; 1914 was the occasion, not the cause. Pushed, they say any counterfactual that averts it has to be huge — you have to delete the railways, or delete nationalism, or delete the previous decade.
- The "forking paths" people: the same outcome depends on a chain of individually avoidable decisions — a blank cheque given or not given, a mobilisation order delayed two days, a different foreign minister — so it was fragile. And they point out that the forces people's story would have worked just as well for 1911 or 1912 or 1913, which they take as a sign that it explains nothing.
- And then our third member, the archivist, who thinks the whole exercise is theatre: history has exactly one run, counterfactuals have no data, and "inevitable" applied to a one-off macro-event is a word with no cash value.

We tried to fix it with a gadget, and I want to know whether the gadget is the problem. Call it the Latest Divergence Test: an outcome is inevitable to degree n if the latest moment at which a *small* change could still have averted it is n units before the outcome — where a small change is one that doesn't itself directly produce or directly prevent the outcome, so no deleting nationalism and no time travellers. Small divergence, placed late = fragile. Big divergence needed, or the last divergence point sitting decades back = robust. We thought this would settle things. Instead each camp just restated its own position in the new vocabulary: the forces people say the latest admissible small change is years too early to matter, the forking-paths people say there are admissible small changes in the last week of July, and then they just stare at each other about what counts as admissible and how small is small. Someone else in the group pointed out a second problem, that the test is asymmetric: we audit the actual outcome for robustness but never audit the alternatives we invoke for the same fragility, even though the alternatives are chains of decisions too.

So: what is this disagreement, actually? My own hypothesis — talk me out of it if its wrong — is that the first-order historical question has quietly been replaced by a metric question, about how you measure distance between possible histories and which neighbourhood of the actual one you get to look at, and that the neighbourhood is interest-relative, so nobody can lose. But there might be something else hiding underneath: whether the causal structure of political history is more funnel-shaped — lots of small causes, few big outcomes, self-buffering, attractor-ish — or more branching, sensitive-dependence, chaotic. That sounds like a genuinely empirical question which our single actual run can't settle, because both structures generate exactly one deterministic sequence and so both fit the record equally. Or maybe the archivist is right and the question is just ill-posed.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on 1914: what kind of disagreement is this — verbal, evidential, a fight about which cases count as data, bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for, different questions under one label, a hidden formal or empirical question, or a badly formed question — and how much of the stalemate is coming from the gadget rather than the debate? Then, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Is there a distinction, a formal result, or a kind of evidence — comparative cases, simulation work, natural experiments — that would move it, or should we expect no progress, and if so is that a defect in the question or just the shape of the terrain?
