My city's land-use process has a shape I cannot make philosophical sense of, and I would like its implicit theory made explicit rather than argued for or against.

Here is how it works, as I understand it. Most residential land is zoned single-family. Putting two or more units on a lot zoned single-family requires a discretionary permit: the city mails notice to owners of record within three hundred feet, holds a public hearing before the planning commission and then before the council, and issues written findings. Incompatibility with what the staff reports call neighborhood character, added traffic, loss of curbside parking, and shadow are all accepted grounds for denial, and none of them has to be quantified or shown to reduce anyone's property value. Opponents may also sue under the state environmental-review statute on the ground that the city's analysis was inadequate, which routinely adds a year or more; the delay is part of what opposition buys. By contrast, demolishing a house on the same lot and replacing it with one very large single-family house is approved ministerially: no notice, no hearing, no appeal, even when the replacement generates more traffic and more shadow than the duplex would have. Meanwhile the same council is under a state mandate to plan for several thousand additional homes over the next decade, and fair-housing law forbids the city, and largely forbids sellers and landlords, from selecting occupants by race, by family status, or, in some states, by source of income. The neighborhood therefore cannot say who may live on a street, though through the permit process it can determine roughly what the housing there will cost. Last year the council added a second track: projects within a quarter mile of a frequent bus line, and projects in which at least a fifth of the units are income-restricted, are now approved ministerially, with no hearing and no appeal, while everything else keeps the full process; and in the older streetcar-era neighborhoods near downtown it simultaneously adopted an overlay that strengthens the hearing requirement and adds design review. Nobody is compensated when the council approves over objection, and nobody pays anything when it denies.

Five features of this package strike me as pulling in different directions. First, standing is set by parcel and radius rather than by stake: someone who bought last month and a family that has lived on the block for fifty years have identical standing, the tenant next door is not notified at all even when the project would demolish the building she lives in, and the people who would occupy the new units are not parties, are not counted, and are not notified, despite being more numerous and, by any ordinary measure of intensity, more strongly affected. Second, the intensity of opposition is itself recorded as evidence of harm: findings recite how many letters arrived and how many residents signed up to speak, and a large turnout counts in favor of denial, while no comparable tally is taken on the other side and intensity is never weighed against intensity. Third, the veto reaches the form of other people's property but not its occupancy or its ordinary use: a neighbor cannot be stopped from painting the house, keeping several cars on the property, or selling to anyone at all, but can be stopped from adding a second unit. Fourth, the same objection now has no force two blocks from a bus line and full force three blocks away, and is treated as defeasible by transit proximity and by subsidy while being hardened in the overlay districts. Fifth, the process contains no symmetric affirmative power: residents can block a great deal and compel nothing.

What I want is the view of a person's claim on a place that this bundle presupposes, and I can think of five candidates. The first treats homeownership as an undiversifiable stake in a local collective asset whose value depends on the built and social character of the immediate area, with the veto as the protection of that stake. The second treats residence as membership in a self-governing community with a collective right to determine the shape of its common life, a right to the city as a right to shape rather than merely a right of access. The third treats the process as the protection of legitimate expectations formed under an earlier set of rules about school capacity, parking, quiet, and outlook. The fourth treats the hearing as an information-eliciting device, on the assumption that nearby residents know local facts that planners do not. The fifth says the practice is not the expression of any view at all but the predictable result of concentrated benefits and diffuse costs.

For each of these, tell me whether the practice as I have described it strictly presupposes the view, is merely compatible with it, or actually conflicts with some feature of it; and be explicit about where my description underdetermines the answer, in the sense that two or three of the theories would license exactly the process I have described, and about which single change to the process would best discriminate between them. Then tell me what would have to change if the city replaced the presupposed view with one of the others: what a process built on the investment-stake view would look like as opposed to one built on the membership view, what a process built on the protection of expectations would substitute for the veto, and what a process honestly designed to elicit local information would do with a roomful of angry residents and a petition.

I am less interested in whether the neighbors are right than in what the city commits itself to by running things this way. In particular: is the two-track reform an admission that the veto was never a right but only a defeasible privilege, and what principle is the city adopting when it removes that privilege near transit while reinforcing it, through the overlay, in the neighborhoods that are oldest, most central, and most able to afford lawyers?
