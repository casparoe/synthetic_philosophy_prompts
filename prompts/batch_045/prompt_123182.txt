I'm a graduate student in something completely unrelated (medieval land law) and I've been sitting in on a reading group on computation and the mind, so I'm the least qualified person in the room and I've probably been noticing something everyone else already has a word for. But I keep seeing the same move, and I'd like to know what it is.

It goes like this. Someone wants to show that some human capacity — understanding a sentence, or planning a route, or telling a morally relevant difference — can't just be a matter of running a mechanical procedure. The evidence offered is a complexity result: formalized in a reasonable-looking way, the task comes out NP-hard or worse, so no efficient procedure does it in general. Invariably someone points out that the hard instances are cooked up: enormous, gnarly, stuffed with structure no person ever actually encounters. And the reply, which I've now heard in three or four versions from three or four different people, is: sure, but the subset of instances that actually matter isn't something you can pick out efficiently either — there's no fast way to tell whether a given instance is one of the benign ones — so the difficulty hasn't been explained away at all, it has just moved into our inability to carve out the good cases.

The same shape keeps turning up elsewhere. Someone argues that if P were equal to NP then mathematical creativity would be finished, since finding a proof would be no harder than checking one; you object that "polynomial" isn't "feasible" when the exponent is huge, and you get told that the exponent is beside the philosophical point, which is only about whether an algorithm exists. Or: someone argues that the brain can't be doing Bayesian inference because exact inference is intractible; you point out that variational and sampling methods do fine in practice; you get told that those carry no guarantees, so they aren't really Bayesian inference, and the intractability result stands. In each case what strikes me is that the conclusion keeps all its force while the content that made the result interesting gets conceded away, and what's left standing is a claim about our access to the problem — we can't recognize the easy instances, we can't bound the constants, we can't certify the approximation — rather than about the problem or the capacity itself.

So: does this move have a name? I dont think it's just the usual complaint that hardness results are only worst-case, though it sits right next to that; and I don't think it's the thing where someone defends a strong claim by retreating to a weaker one under pressure and then quietly resuming the strong one, though it sits next to that too. It also feels close to something about non-constructivity — the gap between an algorithm existing and our being able to say which algorithm — but not identical. I'm not after a bibliography or citations I can't check; I want the carving. What exactly is the move, what are the nearest named things and where does it differ from each, does one of them fit best or is this genuinely unnamed, and if it's unnamed, what would you call it, how would you define it, and what are its boundary cases — in particular, when is relocating the difficulty into our own access actually legitimate (a real reduction of the philosophical claim to a claim about unrecognizability, or about missing certificates) and when is it a swindle? One last thing that bugs me and that I'd want any definition to handle: in the first case, "the instances that matter" look definable only by appeal to the very capacity we were trying to say something about, so the relocation may be circular rather than merely weak.
