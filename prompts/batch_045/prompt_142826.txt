I convene a short graduate reading group on collective action and game theory, and our final session is on what people in my circles call Moloch: multipolar traps, races to the bottom, the situations in which defection is dominant no matter what anyone else does. My students arrive with the tragedy-of-the-commons framing, and I have been trying to shake them out of it, because in the cases I care about the individual cost of restraint is not merely small — the benefit of unilateral withdrawal to anyone, including the person withdrawing, is close to zero, so that refusing to play is indistinguishable from simply losing, and what gets burned is a stock that was never anybody's to sell. I would like to close the term with a poem rather than with a summary: something that gets the mechanics right, rather than something that amounts to saying that greed is bad.

A sestina strikes me as the right container for this, and I would like the constraint to be real. Six stanzas of six lines plus a three-line envoi, the end-words rotating in the usual retrogradatio cruciata, so that the word closing one stanza is the word opening the next — I want that permutation carrying weight. Whatever the speaker is left holding at the end of one scene should be the first thing in her hands in the next; the scene should change while the vocabulary stays the same, which is rather the point about a trap that reappears in a new industry wearing new clothes. Take these as the six end-words unless you can defend a better set: Moloch, defect, cheap, burn, tomorrow, hands.

Give me six stanzas in six different domains — an advertising-saturated medium, a fishery, an arms race, the citation or prestige economy of academic publishing, an attention market, and one of your own choosing — and let the poem be spoken by a participant rather than by Moloch himself or by a prophet denouncing him: someone who can see the trap perfectly and feels the pull anyway, whose reasons for joining are locally sound. A game theorist in the room should not wince, so please do not collapse a multipolar trap into a two-person prisoner's dilemma, do not imply that mutual restraint would be stable if only people were nicer, and do not offer the consolation that recognising the trap is halfway to escaping it. The envoi, carrying all six words two to a line, is where I would like you to commit yourself: either the speaker gets out, in which case name the mechanism that actually changed the payoffs — a sovereign, a binding commitment, a coordination device, an exit that costs everyone something — or she does not, and the form closes on her where she stands. Aim for something that can be read aloud in about two minutes, and please write it in American English.
