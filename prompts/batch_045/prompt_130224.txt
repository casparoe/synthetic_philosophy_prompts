I teach moral philosophy, and over the past year I have been reading, as a sideline, about proposals for getting artificial agents to avoid side effects: the thought is that an agent's impact can be curbed by penalizing how far the world it brings about departs from how the world would have gone had the agent not acted at all, or had it never been switched on. I have become convinced that one structural feature keeps generating the difficulties these proposals run into, and I cannot find that the feature itself has been isolated and named. I would like to know whether it has.

The feature is easiest to see in a pair of cases.

First case. A household robot is carrying a load across a room. A vase stands on a shelf that is going to give way at noon whatever the robot does. At 11:59 the robot's arm knocks the vase; the robot could have steadied the shelf, and does not. In the comparison world the vase breaks as well, so the penalty works out at zero. Yet something has plainly gone wrong here — and, more to the point, we do not want a scheme under which an agent can shed a penalty by making sure the damage was going to happen anyway.

Second case. Same room, same shelf. This time the robot steadies the vase and prevents the breakage. Now the world differs from the comparison world in exactly the way it would have differed had the robot smashed the vase deliberately, and the penalty is at its maximum. Rescue and vandalism are indistinguishable to the measure.

Two further wrinkles, which I have not seen addressed. The verdicts in both cases swing with how finely states are described: describe outcomes finely enough and what happens is that a vase is broken at 11:59 by a robot's arm, which the comparison world does not contain, so the penalty reappears; describe them coarsely and it disappears. The measure appears to have no way of fixing its own grain. And with more than one penalized agent the same structure recurs collectively: if two agents each bring about a bad state that either of them alone would have brought about, each can point to the other and the penalty collapses to zero for both, while charging each with the whole thing counts the harm twice.

I do not think this is merely the familiar point that causing something and making a difference to it come apart in cases where a backup cause is waiting in the wings. Two things seem different here: the agent can arrange for the backup itself, and the quantity in question is not an after-the-fact ascription of responsibility but a term the agent optimizes against, so it is available for gaming. Nor is it quite the point about proxies being corrupted by being optimized, since what is gamed is a constraint rather than a stand-in for what we actually want. There is also something close to it in the free-will literature's cases where an agent could not have done otherwise because someone was standing ready to step in — except that there the conclusion drawn is that the agent is responsible anyway, whereas these measures return zero.

So: does this cluster have an established name, or a literature of its own? If it does, I want the nearest concepts and an account of how each differs from what I have described. If it does not, I want a term and a definition tight enough to settle the borderline cases I have sketched — the rescue case, the case in which the agent supplies its own backup, the case of fine-grained descriptions, and the two-agent case. A label on its own would be of no use to me; the carving is the point. I am not asking for references or further reading. Please answer in American English.
