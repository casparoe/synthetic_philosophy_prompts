I'm giving a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on historical evidence next month and I want the question period prepared rather than the paper. My thesis is that the slogan about history being written by the victors misdescribes the mechanism: victors rarely need to falsify or suppress, they out-reproduce. Every copying, reprinting, anthologising, cataloguing, citing and digitising event is a filter, and a lineage with even a modest per-generation advantage in reproduction — better-funded scriptoria, a canonical author who gets quoted by everyone who follows, a language that gets catalogued and OCR'd first — can come to hold almost the entire surviving record over enough generations without a single act of censorship. On this picture power leaves its trace not in the content of surviving sources but in the shape of the archive, so the methodological payoff is a survival test: before relying on a claim about what a period believed or did, ask what pattern of differential loss would have had to occur for the surviving record to look the way it does if the claim were false, and whether that pattern is more or less credible than the claim itself.

The room will be roughly twenty people: mostly empirically minded archival historians of early modern Europe who think source criticism already handles bias and who distrust general claims about evidence; one digital humanist who builds large text corpora and already corrects for survivorship and text reuse; one archivist who knows that acquisition, arrangement and catalogue metadata are themselves selective; and two philosophers of history inclined to say that if the archive is a selection effect then there is no fact of the matter left to recover.

Draft my prep sheet: the eight objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do, each tagged with who raises it and phrased in that person's vocabulary rather than mine, with a two-minute reply to each and a note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me which one or two points I should concede in my first two minutes to keep the room, which objection I should decline to answer gracefully, and which question I am hoping nobody asks. Finally, give me a compact numerical illustration of compounding attrition I could put on one slide — a per-copying-generation advantage compounding over generations — but choose the parameter values yourself so the illustration is honest rather than rigged, and tell me plainly at which values the effect becomes large and at which it does not, since I expect the digital humanist to check.
