I teach philosophy at a small liberal-arts college, and this spring I am running an upper-level seminar on social epistemology: 24 students, one 90-minute session a week, and a three-week unit in which I want them to get genuinely confused about majority rule before I tell them anything. The unit is nominally about Condorcet's jury theorem, but it happens to fall in the same term in which my department is rewriting its hiring procedure, and the argument in the faculty meeting is a live one: our chair wants four subcommittees of six, each reporting a single recommendation up to a full meeting that then votes on those four recommendations, on the grounds that this concentrates expertise; a second colleague wants the whole department in one room, discussing together and then voting; a third wants secret ballots and no discussion at all. So I would like this session to be an experiment in which the students test these procedures rather than a lecture in which I evaluate them. What I need is a runnable game, sized for my room, that leaves them having felt two things in their bones. First, that a majority of individually feeble signals is strikingly more reliable than any single member of the group, including its strongest member. Second, that this reliability is not a gift of numbers but a gift of error being independent, and that two quite different mechanisms — a shared cause standing behind everyone's evidence, and people deferring to what earlier people said — destroy it in ways that are measurably different from each other. I do not want the words independence, correlation, or Condorcet used in front of them until the debrief; the mechanics should make the failure arrive before it has a name.

What I have so far is this: a coin, two opaque bags of poker chips, one with a red majority and one with a blue majority, the coin deciding which bag is in play, each student drawing one chip with replacement as a private signal of which bag it is, and the class voting by secret ballot on index cards; afterwards I compare the majority verdict with the verdict of whichever student was individually most accurate across the round. Beyond that I am stuck, and especially on the arithmetic: I need you to fix the chip proportions, the number of trials per condition, and the group sizes so that the effects are actually visible with n = 24. My worry is that a straight majority of 24 will be right almost every time and there will be no headroom left in which a later condition can look worse. I would like four conditions in the same session: the baseline just described; a common-cause condition in which errors are correlated without anybody copying anybody (my thought is that students draw in pairs, each pair being shown a single chip, so that the number of draws halves while the number of votes does not — tell me whether that is the right instrument and what it does to the numbers); a deference condition in which votes are cast in a fixed public order and later voters see the running tally; and, because of the hiring dispute, a two-tier condition in which the class is split into four juries of six that each return a verdict, the final decision being the majority of those four verdicts. Scoring has to be in points toward something trivial, since I have no budget and would rather not hand out money, but it has to be enough that students care whether they personally are right. I also want one deliberate wrinkle I have been turning over and cannot get to work: somewhere in the sequence, let students who believe their own signal is especially strong declare that and have their vote count double, so that the class discovers for itself why self-reported confidence is not the same thing as competence. Tell me whether that belongs in, where, and what it will do. Please give me the materials, the roles (a tally clerk, an announcer, whatever else), a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes including setup and teardown, and the exact instructions to read aloud — including what to say to a student who asks, halfway through, what the point of all this is.

Then the part I most need: a debrief that maps specific moments the students will have lived through onto specific features of the theorem — the competence condition, the independence condition, the difference between a shared cause and deference, and whatever the two-tier result really shows about my chair's proposal. I want to be told honestly which of the four conditions is most likely to fail to produce its effect in a single session, and what I should do on the spot if the numbers come out backwards; how to handle the fact that by the third condition the students will have worked out that they themselves are the subjects of the experiment; and what to do if one student's private accuracy is so high that the group looks bad beside them. And I want a frank note on what the game cannot show: I take it that it cannot reproduce anything asymptotic, and that because my signals travel between students as bare verdicts rather than as reasons, it will make discussion look worse than it is — say clearly what I will have to supply in words at the end instead. No citations or reading list, please.
