I'm putting together the syllabus for my upper-level seminar on privacy for next spring, and I want to screen Hitchcock's Rear Window in week two, before we get into the readings proper, as a kind of intuitive warmup. But I taught it once before and the discussion went nowhere — everyone said "it's about surveillance, watching people is bad," and we were done in fifteen minutes. So I want to come in with a sharper frame this time, and I'd like you to help me build it.

Here's the rough idea. The accounts we'll be reading treat privacy as control over information about yourself (Westin, Fried) or as limited accessibility of a person (Gavison), and the standard deflationary move (Thomson) says privacy talk is dispensable: every "violation of privacy" can be paraphrased as some other wrong plus a bit of property or bodily stuff. What strikes me about the movie is that Jefferies never actually *sees* anything anyone would call a secret. He sees a man carrying a heavy trunk, a wife not being where she usually is, her purse and jewelry left behind, a saw and a knife wrapped in newspaper, dirt disturbed in a flowerbed, and eventually her wedding ring on the wrong woman's hand. Nothing on that list is private in the informational sense, the Thorwalds never lose control of any one item, and yet by the end he knows his neighbor killed her. That's structurally the same worry we argue about with data brokers and the mosaic theory — the harm shows up at the level of the pattern, not the datum. So my first question is whether the film is best read as an argument that control accounts underdescribe the wrong, or whether it's arguing for something else entirely.

My actual hunch, and please push back: the film is defending something more like an *attentional* account. What privacy protects isn't what's known about you but what's *attended to*, and what makes Jefferies' watching creepy isn't the information he ends up with but the posture — sustained, one-directional, uninvited, pleasure-taking, and from someone who has standing to look (he's a photographer, he's a neighbor, he's stuck in a cast and immobilized) but not standing to look like *that*. On this reading Stella's running moral commentary about peeping Toms is the thesis rather than comic relief, and the dog's death scene, where the whole courtyard rushes to its windows and one woman's scream turns everyone into a witness, is the film showing the *good* version of mutual attention. Is that anything philosophers have really argued for, or is it a metaphor dressed up as a theory? And does the film support it, or does it undercut it, given that the watching is what catches a murderer?

Two more things if you can. One: where does the film resist the reading? I don't want to be the guy who turns a thriller into an ilustration. Jefferies gets a moral alibi the plot hands him pretty cheaply, the genre needs him to be right, and we never get Thorwald's side at all — so maybe the honest thing to say in week two is that this movie isn't really about privacy and I should screen something else. Tell me if that's what you think. Two: what does the film add that the papers don't? My sense is the literature is weirdly bad at the phenomenology of watching and being watched — the pleasure, the boredom, the way it fills time — and that a movie can do something there that an argument can't. But I might just be rationalizing a screening.

Give me something I could hand to grad students as a framing note: a reading, the two or three scenes you'd point them to, and where you think I'm wrong.
