I've been reading one of those pop-science books about consciousness, and it sums up Dennett on the color phi illusion roughly like this:

"You flash a red dot at one spot, then a fraction of a second later a green dot a few inches away. You don't see two dots, you see one dot gliding across, and it seems to turn green partway along the path — even though the switch looks like it happens before the green dot has been shown at all, so your brain seems to be inserting a change it didn't yet have the information for. Dennett says there are two stories you can tell. On the Orwellian story, you're conscious of the sequence as it arrives and then your memory of it is rewritten, the way the Party rewrites history after the fact. On the Stalinesque story, nothing gets presented until the editing is finished: the fix is made backstage, in advance, like a verdict written before a show trial. But the finished version is identical either way, so the two hypotheses predict exactly the same behaviour, and no experiment could ever tell them apart. Dennett's conclusion is that the question of when the subject really became conscious of the change has no answer, because there's no single place in the brain where experience gets shown to an audience — no moment of consciousness for the revision to come before or after. The timing of experience is therefore permanently hidden from us, and you can't really trust what people say about when they experienced something. Some researchers argue that later work on how the visual system fills in missing information has since tipped the scales toward the Stalinesque story."

Two things bug me. The "permanently hidden from us" bit makes it sound like an epistemic limitation, when I thought Dennett's claim was stronger than that; and the bit about who "won" sounds odd if the whole point was that the question isn't well posed. But I can't tell where the fair summary stops and the sloppiness starts.

Relatedly: a friend who does psychophysics says the whole thing is easy to settle. Define the time at which a content becomes conscious as the earliest time at which it can still control a speeded forced-choice response, probe the subject mid-trajectory with a quick red-or-green judgment, and jitter the probe time across trials. He thinks that if the probe says "red" whenever it falls before the green flash, while the end-of-trial report still says "changed midway", that's evidence for the Orwellian story, because there was an earlier experience that later got overwritten.

Can you go through the book's summary claim by claim and tell me what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what a defender of Dennett's view would actually object to? And is my friend right that his probe makes the Orwellian/Stalinesque question empirical again, or does it just smuggle the thing Dennett rejects back in under a new name? If it's the latter, what would have to be true for the distinction to become a real, testable one?
