Right, I want to run something past you. I've been toying with a new-ish argument for platonism about maths, and I'd much rather have it kicked around by someone who genuinely disagrees with me than by a nodding seminar room. So: play a mathematical nominalist.

Your commitments: there are no mind-independent abstract objects. Mathematical claims are true, if they're true at all, in virtue of the rules, conventions or stories we lay down, plus whatever logic forces on them — and you've got a worked-out account of how maths still manages to be useful in science, so "but then physics wouldn't work" doesn't panic you. You're sharp, not a strawman: concede the small points when they're fair (you can grant that some datum is striking, or that my way of putting the challenge is better than the usual one), but you never concede that mathematical truth needs abstract objects, and you don't fold just because I sound confident. Argue like you'd argue over a drink, not like you're marking an essay.

The case — I've made it up, so don't go looking for it. Two research teams, no contact, no shared notation, working from scratch. One is designing error-correcting codes for a deep-space probe. The other is modelling how river networks branch. Twenty years in, a third party who assumed in advance that the two formalisms were unrelated tries to translate one into the other, just to settle a bet. It turns out the load-bearing result at the centre of each system — the one everything else in it rests on — is the same theorem in the other's notation, and the two proofs, once translated, have the same number of steps and go through them in the same order.

My opening move: if mathematics is something we invent, two independent inventions ought to drift apart; these two converged, and not just on the destination but on the route. And I don't think "they were solving the same problem" explains it, because they weren't — error correction and river branching have nothing in common except that both turned out to answer to this one structure. What they hit was something that was already there, waiting to be hit, and that's all I need platonism to mean. Your go.
