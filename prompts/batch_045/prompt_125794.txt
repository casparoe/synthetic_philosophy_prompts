After a reading-group dinner last night I ended up in a three-hour argument with an old friend — a syntactician by training, and temperamentally hostile to anything that smells like prescriptivism — about Douglas Hofstadter's satirical essay from *Metamagical Themas*, published under the pen name William Satire. You probably know the piece: it stages an English in which the unmarked third-person pronoun for a human being is the one reserved for whites (with a separate marked form, *ble*, for blacks, complete with its own possessive and reflexive), and it hands the defence of that arrangement to a pompous narrator who insists the unmarked form is simply inclusive when the context calls for it, who jeers at the reformers' proposed replacements as ugly and unpronounceable, and who treats the entire programme as an attempt by fanatics to cure a social problem with a dictionary. The twist, of course, is that our own default-masculine usage appears inside the satire as the absurd alternative that no sane speaker would ever accept. My friend admires the essay and thinks it proves nothing. His position was that reforming pronominal usage cannot do any real work, and when I asked him to say what he meant he fell back on a one-liner he repeated perhaps a dozen times: you can't change the world by changing the words. He used it twice over — first to dismiss the case for reforming defaults, and then to dismiss the satirical method itself, on the grounds that laughter at a fictional racist default is a cheap rhetorical win that shows nothing about the one we actually live with.

I want to hand him something more articulate than a stare, so what I need is a disambiguation rather than a verdict on the whole dispute. The readings I managed to sketch at the table were these, and I suspect several of them are either trivial or confused: (1) a *cost-reallocation* reading — the burden of a default falls asymmetrically on whoever is not the default, who must repeatedly work out whether an utterance includes them and must mark themselves when they want to be seen, so the point of reform is to move a small recurring cost, not to change anyone's beliefs; (2) a *practice* or *rehearsal* reading — uttering a form is repeatedly performing a way of carving up the world, and the mechanism is habit and attention rather than doxastic change, so a slogan about what words can change may simply be aimed at the wrong psychological variable; (3) an *availability* reading specific to satire — defamiliarisation does not argue you into a conclusion but alters which contrasts are representable for you, turning an invisible default into a visible option that can subsequently be accepted or refused; (4) a *diagnostic* reading — the fact that the joke lands on you is evidence that the default was there, which is not at all the same as evidence that abolishing it would help; (5) an *expressive* or *social-meaning* reading — what is changed is what speakers conventionally signal about the norms of their community, which is a change in the world only in the thin sense that conventions are part of the world; and (6) a strong constitutive reading, of the sort where a language's categories fix the categories available to thought, which I take to be false but which is probably what he thinks I believe. There is also a worry I could not shake about the method: the satire works by mapping our dispute onto an axis where my friend and I already agree, so its force may be entirely borrowed from a prior moral judgement, in which case it transfers nothing and could in principle be run in reverse. Please separate these into precise theses, tell me which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false — and, most importantly, tell me which reading his one-liner would have to be expressing in order to do the work he wanted it to do, both against reform and against the satire, and whether that reading survives scrutiny. I do not want references or suggestions for further reading; I cannot check them and neither will he.

Please structure your reply as follows. First, **The menu** — roughly 700 words: the distinct precise readings, each with a one-sentence statement, the causal or normative mechanism it posits, and a verdict (trivial, substantive and defensible, or false), set out so that I can carry them into the next argument as a list rather than as an essay. Second, **What the slogan had to mean** — about 250 words: which single reading would actually support both of his dismissals, whether that is plausibly what people mean when they say it, and what happens to his position if he is read as asserting only the weaker readings. Third, **Pump, proof, or intervention** — about 300 words: whether the satirical method is best understood as an argument, as evidence that a default exists, or as something that changes its audience, and whether the borrowed-force worry about orthogonal mapping defuses it. Finally, **The strongest version of my side** — about 200 words: the reading I should have led with, the most damaging thing that can be said against it, and what kind of evidence would settle the matter between us.
