I have been asked to circulate a short position paper ahead of a graduate workshop on aggregation and the separateness of persons, and before I do I would like an audit of the set of commitments I mean to defend. My sense is that at least two of them are pulling against each other, but I cannot locate the fault line; I also suspect that at least one of the apparent conflicts is not a conflict at all, and I would rather be told that plainly than have every item flagged as equally problematic.

The commitments:

(a) I accept the core of the separateness objection: classical utilitarianism extends to the social whole a principle of rational choice that is at home only within a single life, and in doing so it treats the boundaries between persons as if they were merely the boundaries between the phases of one life. I want to say instead that justification is owed to each person severally, and that a loss concentrated on one person is not thereby cancelled by gains scattered thinly across many.

(b) In the bare rescue case — five strangers down one corridor, one stranger down the other, no special ties, everything else equal — I think I am required to rescue the five, and I think both the thought that it makes no difference whom you save and the thought that I should give each of the six an equal chance are evasions.

(c) I think I may save my own child rather than two strangers' children, and that this is not merely permissible but something it would be a kind of failure on my part to decline.

(d) I take intrapersonal aggregation to be entirely unproblematic: I may accept a decade of chronic pain for a career that makes my life as a whole much better, and I see nothing amiss in a person prudentially discounting her own distant future.

(e) Behind a veil of ignorance in which I have an equal chance of occupying anyone's position, I would choose by maximizing expected utility; maximin seems to me unable to handle small probabilities of large losses, and I do not want a decision rule that forbids every policy carrying a one-in-a-million risk of catastrophe.

(f) I accept the ordinary regulatory practice of trading small risks of death against large diffuse benefits, priced at a value per statistical life, at least where the risks and the benefits fall on one population over time.

(g) I hold a person-affecting restriction — nothing can be better or worse unless it is better or worse for someone — and I also want to say that a high-consumption, high-emissions policy wrongs future people even in the cases where the policy affects who comes to exist and those lives are worth living.

What I want is an ordering of the genuine tensions by severity, not a list of equal-sized worries. For each pair that really is in tension, say what has to move, what the least costly revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. For each pair that only looks conflicting, say why it dissolves. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and — since I would be reluctant to surrender either (a) or (b) — whether holding both is genuinely available to me once the rest is tidied up, or whether the tidied version is no longer the view I thought I was defending.

One constraint: I am not asking for a bibliography. I know the literature well enough; what I cannot see is the shape of my own position.
