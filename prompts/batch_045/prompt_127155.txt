I'm in a small reading group on constructivism in mathematics — two type theorists, a constructive analyst, and me (my own background is computability theory, so my instinct is always to ask which program computes the thing in question). One participant, a logician I'll call R., has been unusually crisp about which classical moves she will and won't accept, and I've started writing her verdicts down: she denies that we are entitled to assert that Goldbach's conjecture is either true or false, while allowing that we can assert that this disjunction is not itself refuted, i.e. not-not-(G or not-G); she happily asserts, uniformly in n, that n is prime or n is not prime, and the same for any decidable predicate; she accepts not-not-not-P implies not-P but rejects not-not-P implies P in general; she rejects the limited principle of omniscience, the claim that for every binary sequence either it is everywhere zero or it is not; she accepts countable choice, on the grounds that it only spells out what it means to say that for every n there is an m; and she rejects the step from not-not-there-is-an-x-such-that-P to there-is-an-x-such-that-P when P itself involves unbounded quantification, without ever having said anything about the case where P is primitive recursive. She has also twice remarked in conversation that she has no principled objection to the thesis that every total function from the naturals to the naturals is computable — but she has never endorsed it, and the constructive analyst insists the remark was about type theory rather than about mathematics.

The case we want to put to her next has three parts. (i) Let A be a primitive recursive predicate on the naturals — say, n codes a solution of the Diophantine equation E — and suppose we have a proof she accepts of not-not-(there is an n such that A(n)). May we then assert that there is such an n? (I think this is what's called Markov's principle.) (ii) Suppose a program emits rationals together with a computable modulus of convergence, thereby presenting a real number x, and suppose we have a proof she accepts of not-not-(x = 0). May we assert x = 0? (iii) Is she obliged by anything she has already committed herself to answer (i) and (ii) the same way, or could she coherently accept one and reject the other? One of the type theorists expects her to say yes to both and thinks her whole stance is really Russian-style constructivism with a computability thesis quietly smuggled in; the constructive analyst expects her to say no to both and thinks she is some variety of Bishop-style constructivist; I can't tell, because as far as I can see her verdicts so far are compatible with several different packages of principles at once.

What I'd like from you is an explicit statement of the two or three candidate rules that fit her track record — the packages of principles she might be endorsing — what each of them predicts about (i), (ii) and (iii), and your best guess at her answers together with the reasons for preferring that rule over the others. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so plainly, and tell me which further question I should actually put to her that would separate the surviving options: I would much rather walk into next week's meeting knowing which single case to raise than come away with a survey of the constructivist schools. Please also say, for each part of your answer, how confident you are in it, and single out the claim you are least sure of — in particular whether the real-number question really does stand or fall with the number-theoretic one, since that is the step I most suspect I have wrong.
