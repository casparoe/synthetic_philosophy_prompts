I host a tiny philosophy podcast out of my department — just me and one guest, 45 minutes, and the audience is half grad students who've read the literature and half people who listen to philosophy podcasts while doing dishes. Next week's guest is a philosopher named Marisol Okonkwo-Reyes, with a new book called The Room That Learned, defending what she calls the apprenticeship reply to the Chinese Room.

Rough version of her view: she thinks the intuition pump only works because the room is frozen. The rulebook is just given; nobody in the setup ever had to acquire Chinese; nothing is at stake if the answers come out wrong; the system never changes. On her account, understanding isn't a matter of what a system does with symbols at a time but of how it got to be able to do that — a system understands only if its competence was acquired through a history of being corrected against a world and against other speakers, where the corrections mattered to it. That's supposed to explain why the Room doesn't understand and why a fluent human does, without denying that the processes doing the work are computational.

I need eight to ten questions. What I definately don't want: anything biographical, anything where she just restates the view, and any cheap gotchas she could reasonably refuse to answer. What I do want:

- One question pressing on whether her condition just collapses into a causal or teleological theory of content — and, more pointedly, whether Searle, who thinks consciousness is a biological phenomenon with real causal powers, could simply accept that acquisition history matters and say he never denied it, in which case has she won anything or only changed the subject?
- One on composition: if the history is what does the work, and the rulebook in the room was distilled from a system that did learn by being corrected against real speakers, whose history counts, and does the room now understand? She's said on a couple of other shows that she bites this bullet, so I want the follow-up to that.
- One on whether the history is constitutive or merely evidential, built around the case of someone who learned Chinese fluently as a child and then suffered retrograde amnesia wiping out any memory of learning it. If she says the history still does its work even when it's unremembered and behaviorally inert, I want the follow-up: what is the history doing there beyond naming whatever the underlying substrate is doing?
- One on whether current large language models, fine-tuned with human feedback, already satisfy her criterion. If she says no, I want her to say exactly which part of the apprenticeship is missing, and if she says yes, I want her to sit with that on air.
- One that a non-specialist listener can follow without the bottom falling out of it. Half my audience hasn't read Searle and won't know the systems reply or the robot reply from the brain-simulator reply, and I don't want them to feel like they missed a step.

Structurally: she answers almost every objection with some version of "that's the intuition pump talking" — she's very smooth about it and its a trick I want to close off. Can you order the questions so that move gets less and less available as we go, and is basically unusable by question seven or eight? Give me a follow-up for each of the last four, keyed to what she's most likely to say back.

She also keeps claiming in interviews that this debate has become empirical and could in principle be settled by experiment, so end with one question about what evidence would count against her view. Thanks — this would save me most of a Sunday.
