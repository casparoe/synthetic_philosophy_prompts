I'm one of two staff attorneys in the interpretations office at my state's department of environmental quality — I'll invent a name and call the state Corbin so this doesn't turn into a dig through somebody's real code. Roughly twice a week a permit writer or a field inspector emails us asking what some sentence in our statutes actually means, and whoever picks it up writes a two-page guidance memo that gets posted on the intranet, gets treated as binding by about forty field staff, and maybe three or four times a year ends up stapled to a brief when a permit denial gets appealed. Right now my colleague and I do this by instinct and we disagree constantly: he thinks the only thing that matters is what the words conveyed to a careful reader back whenever the thing was passed, I keep reading the findings clause and asking what disaster the legislature was actually reacting to. We need something we can both run where the answer doesn't depend on who opened the email.

One wrinkle that might help or might make the whole thing harder: Corbin has a codified rule of construction, call it the Administrative Interpretation Act, which says (paraphrasing) that we have to give effect to the meaning the words would have conveyed to an ordinarily attentive reader at the time the provision was adopted, taking account of (1) the words themselves, (2) how the provision fits with the rest of what was enacted alongside it, and (3) the mischief the enactment was aimed at — and that if after all that the meaning is genuinely unclear, we have to pick whichever reading least expands the reach of any penalty, fee, or prohibition. So it's a hybrid that isn't cleanly either of the two things people argue about. Constraints: ten business days per question, two lawyers, no budget for archival legislative-history digging (our pre-1990 files are full of holes), the output has to be a bright line a non-lawyer inspector can apply in the field without calling us, and it needs the general counsel's and the program director's signatures. We can't answer it depends very often or the inspectors stop asking us anything.

Three live disputes, so you can see the shape of the problem. (1) One provision says no person shall operate a motorboat on any watercourse classified as Class I or Class II wild water, and the definitions section defines motorboat as any vessel propelled in whole or in part by machinery, whether or not that machinery is the vessel's primary means of propulsion. The enacted findings talk about preserving the quiet character and natural shoreline of the state's remaining wild rivers. Question: does the ban reach a canoe with a 55-pound-thrust electric trolling motor, and a battery-powered hydrofoil board? Our public brochure has told people for years that it doesn't, on the theory that electric motors are quiet, and I think that brochure is definately wrong. (2) A permitting provision used to require every applicant to publish notice in a local paper three weeks running and then file proof of publication; a 2019 amendment exempted small withdrawals from the publication requirement, but nobody touched the sentence requiring the applicant to file proof of publication within thirty days of the application being deemed complete, or the sentence in the next section saying an application is incomplete until everything required by the permitting section is filed. So: can we deny a small-withdrawal application because the applicant never filed proof of publication of a notice they were never required to publish? Text says yes, purpose screams no, and the tie-breaker about penalties is sitting right there — I want to see how your procedure actually orders those against each other. (3) A revocation provision says the department may revoke any permit upon finding that the permittee has violated any provision of this chapter or any rule adopted pursuant to it. A small town's only wastewater plant missed three monthly monitoring reports during a staffing crisis: no exceedance, no harm, just missing paperwork. The program director wants a compliance order instead of revocation, and so do I, and I have a suspicion any honest procedure is going to tell me that reading any provision as any material provision isn't interpretation at all, it's us quietly declining to use a power we plainly have, and that we'd have to own that out loud. If that's where it lands, say so plainly.

What I want is an ordered procedure: the gates in sequence, what counts as closing each gate, what kind of evidence or argument is even admissible at each one, where it stops with a hard no or an undetermined, and where it stops and hands the question to a named human because it's a policy call dressed as a meaning call. It should be runnable in a morning by someone with a law degree and no theory background, it should output something an inspector can act on, and it should be honest about which steps are just judgment in a lab coat. Flag which steps come out the same no matter whether the person running it is a textualist or a purposivist, and which ones smuggle in that choice. Then run it in order on all three, including the one whose answer I'm not going to like, and say where it declines to answer at all. Please don't give me citations or a reading list — I have no library access from the office and can't check them. Do tell me how confident you are in each of the three applications, and single out the one step in the procedure you're least sure about, the one you'd expect a reviewing court to throw out.
