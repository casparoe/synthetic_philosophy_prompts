I'm a senior doing an independent reading project on moral dilemmas, and I keep running into one pattern that I don't have a word for. My teacher says this pattern is one of the things people argue over when they ask whether genuine moral dilemmas — cases where you do something wrong no matter which option you take — are even possible. So I'd like to know whether it has an established name in the literature. More than the name, though, I want the carving: what exactly the thing is, what it isn't, and where its edges are. A bare label won't survive my teacher's first question.

Here are the instances I keep coming back to.

1. Two friends asked me to drive them to separate events at the same time, in opposite directions, and I could only take one. I drove the one who would otherwise have missed something that mattered more to her. I'm as confident as I am about anything that I chose correctly, and the friend I didn't drive said she agreed. But afterwards I wanted to apologize to her, and the apology felt like something I owed rather than something merely polite. Had I shrugged and said "I picked right, nothing to discuss," that would itself have been a small failure. Yet I don't think I did anything wrong.

2. I noticed the same shape in a novel: a character breaks a promise in order to do something urgent and plainly more important, is certain he was right to break it, and then spends years trying to make it up to the person he broke it against — who by then is dead and never learned about the promise at all. That struck me as the strangest part. Whatever is left over after the justified choice seems able to survive the person it's directed at.

3. And here is the bit I can't fit into anything. It seems to matter enormously whether the person passed over was in a position to notice. If my friend had never found out that I could have driven her, and nothing had changed for her, I think I'd have felt almost nothing — but the claim I supposedly failed seems to be exactly the same claim in both versions. It also seems to matter whether I chose at all: had the matter been settled by a coin flip or by which car started, the leftover is different in a way I can't spell out.

The other thing I want to name: the leftover behaves like a debt rather than like a feeling. It seems to give me an actual reason to give that person priority next time, and when I do, it goes away, as though it had been paid off. That isn't ordinary sadness, and it isn't guilt in the usual sense either, since I don't believe I did anything wrong. It also seems to shrink when the choice was obvious and grow when the two claims were nearly evenly matched, which is odd if it's really a debt — debts don't get smaller because paying was easy.

So: is there an established concept for this — the directed, apparently reason-giving, apparently dischargeable leftover that survives a justified choice between competing claims? If there is, I need to know how it differs from the nearby things it might be confused with: plain regret, guilt, the general unpleasantness of having had to choose, the kind of sadness a bystander might feel. If there isn't, or if the established concepts only cover the version where the person passed over knows about it, I'd like a coined term with a definition and some boundary cases I can test it against. And finally: does a pattern like this actually support the claim that some dilemmas leave you in the wrong either way, or can everything I've described be explained away by someone who denies that?
