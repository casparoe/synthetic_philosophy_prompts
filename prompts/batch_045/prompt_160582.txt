ok, slightly odd request but here goes. Two people in my reading group won't stop going at it about predictive processing and the free energy principle. One is a full-on unificationist — perception, action, attention, even the sense of being a self are all prediction-error minimisation under a hierarchical generative model, and the FEP is the deep principle under that, applying to anything that keeps itself together. The other is an ecological-enactive critic: PP is mostly redescription, a nice engineering vocabulary stuck onto things we already knew, resting on a principle so general it covers teapots.

I want the document they'd produce if they actually ran an adversarial collaboration instead of sniping at each other in the group chat.

1. Start with a numbered agreed-claims section — the things both can genuinely sign. Not diplomatic mush like "the brain is complicated". Real content: what the formalism commits you to, which phenomena are the ones to be explained, where the burden of proof sits, what would count as PP being vacuous, and what would count as the enactive alternative being untestable.

2. Then a disagreements table. Columns: the crux; the unificationist's own wording of their position on it; the enactivist's own wording of theirs (each phrased so that side would actually say it, not a straw version); one case or argument both agree would bear on it; what the unificationist predicts it will show; what the enactivist predicts. Rows for at least:

- the triviality worry: if it applies to anything with a Markov blanket, is the FEP unfalsifiable, or just very general? Test: take one simple non-neural system and ask whether the FEP gives a quantitative prediction that no rival description gives you.
- the dark room problem: does bringing in something like expected free energy plus built-in priors dissolve it, or does that just smuggle back the agency the theory was supposed to explain?
- the ledger test, which I invented, so tell me if its fair: make any PP explanation state, non-circularly, (a) what the generative model is, (b) what counts as prediction error and how you'd measure it independently of the behaviour you're explaining, and (c) how precision gets set. If (c) can only be filled in by pointing at the data being explained, call it a redescription. Both sides have to say up front whether they accept the ledger as a fair test, and what they'll do if their own account fails it.
- the residual test: take one showcase case the unificationist likes (I'd go with why you can't tickle yourself, but pick whatever both will grant) and ask whether the enactivist can re-derive the same quantitative pattern from sensorimotor coupling alone, without adding a surplus parameter of their own. If the re-derivation needs one, the unificationist gets to claim the work is being done by the thing they posited.
- whether "inference", "model" and "representation" are being used in a technical, information-theoretic sense, and what survives once that's pinned down.

3. Finish with a short section on which parts of this fight you think are verbal, which are genuine, and which one crux would move the most if it got settled — one line of reasoning each, no hedging.

Write the predictions as if nobody knows yet how any of it comes out. Under 900 words for the whole thing, and skip citations or a reading list — I dont need paper names.
