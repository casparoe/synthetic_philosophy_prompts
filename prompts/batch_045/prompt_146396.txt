I'm in the final year of a joint philosophy and physics degree, and I'm the student member of my university's Interdisciplinary Research Fund panel. We have one award left to make this round (about £8,000, to be spent within twelve months), and I've been asked to circulate a written recommendation before Thursday's meeting. I'd be grateful if you would draft it.

The application is from a postdoctoral researcher in experimental photonics and a PhD student in philosophy. They want to build what they describe as a physical realisation of an accelerating processor: an optical switching element whose nth computational step occupies 2⁻ⁿ minutes, so that infinitely many steps complete within the first sixty seconds of a run, with a further sixty seconds reserved for read-out. Their target is Goldbach's conjecture — that every even number greater than two is the sum of two primes — which has been neither proved nor refuted. Because a counterexample, if one exists, would be found after finitely many steps, while a proof is not available, they say the device settles the conjecture in one minute of laboratory time: it either latches or it does not.

They anticipate the obvious objection and reply to it at length. The lamp case, they say, is the wrong model of their set-up. Their read-out is a bistable element that is armed at t = 0 and, if and only if a counterexample is found, flips once and stays flipped; because it can change state at most once, its state at t = 60 s is fixed by the history however many internal steps the processor has yet to perform. So, they argue, we never have to ask what the machine is doing 'at' the limit instant, and the supertask has a determinate outcome even though the processor's internal state does not.

They concede two difficulties. By roughly the hundred-and-fiftieth step each interval is shorter than the Planck time, and the switching element would need a response bandwidth with no finite upper bound, so power draw diverges as t approaches sixty seconds. Their answer is that the divergence is a property of the idealisation, and that all we need is fidelity up to the step at which an answer is latched; a breakdown before that point shows up as 'nothing latched', which they distinguish from 'conjecture true'.

One panellist, a philosopher, says the proposal is not coherent enough to fund, on the grounds that there is no fact of the matter about the reading. Another, an engineer, says the Planck-scale worries are a prejudice about what is practical rather than an argument about what is possible, and that this is simply a hard engineering problem. Our terms of reference permit awards only for work that is tractable within the grant period and capable of yielding a result, and I expect the argument on Thursday to turn on which of those conditions, if either, this fails.

Please draft the memo I will circulate, addressed to the panel and under 900 words. Recommend one of three options — award in full, award a reduced feasibility study (say, validating the read-out discipline on Π₁ sentences whose truth value is already settled, and mapping where the reading stops being interpretable), or decline — with reasons the applicants could reply to, any conditions if you recommend funding, and an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the side you are rejecting. No literature survey: two panellists work in this area and will skim it.

Two further requests. First, close with a short note marking which of the memo's load-bearing claims you hold with confidence and which are contestable judgements a reasonable philosopher of physics might reject; I would rather send something hedged in the right places than something that sounds more settled than it is. Second, if you think the decision as I have framed it cannot responsibly be made — for instance if the latch manoeuvre changes the subject rather than answering the lamp objection, if the asymmetry between 'nothing latched' and 'no counterexample exists' is fatal in a way I have not seen, or if 'capable of yielding a result' does no work here — say so plainly in a paragraph before the memo instead of giving me the document I asked for.
