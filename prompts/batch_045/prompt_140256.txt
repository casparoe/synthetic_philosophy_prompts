hey — i got roped into writing the practice exam for my school's philosophy/econ club and i need one essay question on why some countries are rich and others arent. context: everyone in the club is 16-17, nobody has read any actual papers, and the room we sit in has no wifi and no library books. so the question and the model answer both have to be totally self-contained: no real-world examples, no "as economist X argued in year Y", no reading lists. everything has to come from the case i give plus reasoning. 45 minutes, 20 marks total.

the problem is that everyone has absorbed the same one-word answer off the internet — "institutions" — and i can already see six identical essays coming: a paragraph on institutions, a paragraph on geography, a paragraph on culture, conclusion "its mostly institutions", 14/20 for everyone. i want a question where that gets you like 6.

so i drafted a made-up case to build the question around. use it, fix it if its badly designed, or throw it out and build a better one:

Meridia is an island, one language, one mix of farming and fishing, fairly even rainfall and soil, no known gold/coal/oil. In 1891 a colonial governor splits it in two with a straight line drawn on a badly printed map; because of the misprint the line lands about 40km north of where he meant it. Nothing real changes as you cross it — same soil, same crops, the same families end up on both sides. One governor, one legal code, one school system until independence in 1960, then two countries, North Meridia and South Meridia, with word-for-word the same constitution, the same elections every five years, the same laws about property, contracts and taxes on paper. Today income per person in North is about four times what it is in South. Assume these are all true:

(a) In North a road contract normally goes to the lowest bidder. In South it normally goes to someone the minister is related to. In both places over 90% of people tell surveyers that corruption is wrong.
(b) In 1970 a big oilfield was discovered in South — the poorer half. Most of the money appears to have ended up in private bank accounts abroad.
(c) On survey questions like "do you trust your neighbours", "is hard work important", "how religious are you", North and South give basically the same answers.
(d) In North every district office has kept a written, public register of who owns which piece of land, continuously since 1893. In the South nobody does. The reason it started in North: the district officer posted there in 1893 happened to be a man who liked filing things, trained two clerks, they trained two more, and the habit stuck. In the South the officer posted that year was transferred after three months.

the kinds of things im hoping the question forces people into, if theyre any good (use your own if youve got better ones): whether "institutions" here means the rules on paper or the rules actually followed, and what would have to be true for "institutions did it" to be false rather than just definitional; the fact that (d) makes the institutional difference itself something that needs explaining and traces it back to a small fluke, so "the cause" might be a contingent chain instead of a factor; whether the causal claim is supposed to be "in general, elsewhere, in other cases" or just "here", and what one case can and cant establish; whether "why is North rich" and "why is North richer than South" and "what should we change" are even the same question; and how (b) messes with a simple resources story.

i was also thinking of adding a part b where you imagine re-running history from 1891 a hundred times and asking what the pattern of outcomes would tell you — no idea if that helps or just confuses people, you decide.

what i want back: the actual exam question (one question, part b if you think its worth it), a model answer of about 600-700 words, and a marking scheme with 4 or 5 must-hit points, how many marks each is worth (20 total), and one line each on the mistake people will actually make that loses them those marks. then a short note on what separates an A (17+) from a B (13-16) — a specific move the A answer has to make, not just "more detail" or "neater writing". and tell me if my case is broken in some way that lets a memorizer off the hook.
