I had this huge argument with a friend last night about how much people should be able to hide from the state, and I think we were mostly talking past each other, so I want to know what's actually being assumed underneath each position.

The thing that bugs me is the pattern across three practices that are all just normal where they operate. In Germany, the constitutional court basically invented a right to informational self-determination, you can get old news reporting about yourself delisted once the public's interest in it has faded, and criminal records aren't public — but the same country requires you to register your address with the authorities and tell them whenever you move. In the US there's no general right to be forgotten, arrest records, mugshots and court filings are public by default, and private sites repost mugshots and charge you a fee to take them down (some states have tried to stop them) — but there's no national ID and no residence register. In China, courts publish blacklists of people who refuse to pay judgments — name, ID number, sometimes shown on screens in public, with travel restrictions attached — and you come off the list once you pay.

My friend's view: being published is itself a punishment, so it needs a sentence length, and every record should have an expiry date after which it's nobody's business. My view: that's not quite it either, because visibility isn't the punishment, it's a background condition of living among other people, and the state just redistributes a gaze that was always there. To break the tie I came up with something I called "gaze symmetry" — the idea that openness is only legitimate when the seeing runs both ways, so the citizen should be able to read the state as well as the state reads them, and that what's creepy about the Chinese setup isn't the publicity but that it's one-directional.

So: what does each of these three arrangements actually presuppose? About whether being seen is a harm at all, about what a person is such that a record about them can belong to them, about time and whether a past stops being yours, about whether the state is trustworthy enough to be the one holding the file. Which of those commitments are strictly required and which just fit comfortably? Is the German combination really inconsistent or does it only look like it? And if you took gaze symmetry seriously, which of the three practices would have to change and how — or does my distinction collapse into something else once you push on it? Please also say how confident you are in what you're claiming and single out the point you're least sure of; I'd rather know where the argument is fragile than get a smooth answer.
