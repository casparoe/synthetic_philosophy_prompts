I am in the lower sixth and I run my school's philosophy society: about fifteen students aged 16–17, one 55-minute lunchtime slot, and a decidedly mixed group — a couple of them take economics or psychology, most have never argued about anything philosophical outside my sessions. I have no budget, money may not change hands under school rules, and the only rewards I can credibly offer are things like chocolate, five extra minutes of break, or house points. One member has a nut allergy and another avoids sugar, so whatever the stake turns out to be, there has to be a version of it that does not depend on food.

What I want is an activity in which the group runs into the gap between what you judge best and what you actually do, and then has to argue about whether that gap is irrationality at all — or just a change of mind that looks like one from the outside. They should meet the problem before I name it: I'd rather the words "weakness of will" and "hyperbolic discounting" did not appear until the debrief, and ideally not until someone in the room has produced their own description of what happened.

Here is the apparatus I have been sketching in my notes, which you should use, correct, or throw away as you think best. Please decide, rather than offering me alternatives.

First, a "cold" choice between a smaller reward now and a larger one later, then a "hot" choice between the same two things later in the session, with the smaller reward physically present in the room the second time. I am worried that compressing a week into fifty-five minutes makes the whole thing trivial, so tell me whether the cold choice has to be taken the week before in a sealed envelope — I do have the previous meeting available if so — and how you would keep it a genuine choice rather than a formality.

Second, what I've been calling the endorsement probe: at the moment of the hot choice, each person privately ticks whether they still think the larger-later option is the better one. My hope is that this separates two things I can't keep straight on paper — a preference that reverses over time, and a judgement you act against while you still hold it. Build the slips so that this distinction shows up in the tallies the group can see.

Third, a commitment device offered at a price: before the hot choice, anyone may pay part of the larger reward to have the immediate option removed from them for good. I suspect almost nobody will take it even after predicting that they will fold, and I want that fact arranged so the group has to confront it, including the people who take it purely to look disciplined.

Fourth, a hunch of mine I'd like tested in the room: that people predict their classmates' reversals better than their own. Have everyone privately forecast both their own hot choice and the class's reversal rate before the tray comes out, and tell me how to display the comparison honestly.

Fifth, a short written exchange: each person writes a note from their earlier self to their later self, then replies as the later self. I want that to feed a debrief on whether the earlier self has any authority over the later one at all.

Please give me: the exact wording of any slips or cards; a minute-by-minute timeline; a payoff scheme that costs me nothing; how to tally results on the board without anyone feeling exposed; a debrief that maps specific moments onto the descriptive question (did preferences reverse?), the normative question (is reversal irrational?), and the question of what an explanation in terms of steep short-run discounting would actually dissolve; an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each; and a frank note on which part of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to state myself.

Commit to a design. Make the definite choices — stakes, timings, wording, what gets revealed when — and give your reasons, rather than laying out options for me to choose between. Where you think my framing is muddled or my hunch is wrong, say so plainly and tell me why. Please also mark which parts you are confident will actually happen and which are educated guesses, since I will have exactly one attempt at this. I can't get hold of journal articles and I don't want a reading list or citations, just something I can run next Thursday.

Please write in British English.
