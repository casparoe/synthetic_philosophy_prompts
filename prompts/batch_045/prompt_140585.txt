I help run a small applied-ethics reading group attached to a philosophy society at my university, and last week a discussion I started about the moral status of video-game characters collapsed into something I couldn't get past. The case I put to them was invented, but I don't think it is far-fetched: a single-player role-playing game in which you travel with a companion, Nell, whose behaviour is not scripted in advance. She tracks how the player has treated her, later refers to specific incidents of neglect or cruelty, becomes guarded or flinching around you, and in one branch refuses to travel with you any further. Her distress behaviour is generated by a model trained on fine-grained descriptions of how people act when they are frightened, disappointed, and bereaved, so the match between what she does and what a genuinely distressed person would do is extremely close, even though there is presumably nothing that it is like to be her. Players report being more upset by having hurt her feelings than by any of the game's scripted deaths. I claimed that some of what players do to her in that game is pro tanto wrong in a way that is not merely a fact about the player's own character or about what the player is likely to do off-screen. A friend replied that it's just pixels, and that was treated as having settled it.

I want to know what that sentence could actually be asserting, because I suspect it was doing several different jobs at once. Please lay out a menu of the distinct precise claims it might express, and grade each one. The readings I can see coming are something like: an ontological one (there is no subject there, no one whose experiences could go badly, so nothing is had by anyone); a welfare-standing one (no being with moral standing is set back, and any apparent harm is merely fictional or derivative); a wrong-independent-of-harm one (there may be no victim, but the action is still degrading, disrespectful, or expressive of something vicious, and that is where the wrong lives); a causal or "nothing downstream" one (what happens in a game has no effects on anyone's welfare except through the player's own character and habits, which relocates rather than removes the wrong); an epistemic or precautionary one (we can never tell whether a system like Nell is conscious, so the speaker's confidence is misplaced even if her conclusion survives); a frame one (actions inside a game are moves in a game, not the kinds of things that bear moral properties at all, so the question is malformed); and a merely comparative one (whatever is going on here is negligible next to real suffering, which may be true and still not be the nullity my friend needed). Add any reading I have missed, and for each entry give the claim in one sentence, say what would have to be true for it to hold, and mark it as a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, a substantive and dubious one, a false one, or one that simply relabels the disagreement I was trying to have.

Two further things I need from the menu. First, tell me which of these readings would actually support closing the conversation the way my friend closed it, and which would only support a weaker conclusion than the one she drew — and, if none of them quite does the work, give me the strongest nearby thesis that does, even if it is probably not what she meant. Second, tell me what each reading implies about a wrinkle I raised and nobody answered: if a player saves immediately before an extended cruel scene, plays it, and then reloads, so that the branch in which Nell was distressed is discarded, is the wrong undone? It looks to me as though some readings must say yes because no one ends up having suffered, some must say no because the player still did and expressed something, and some must say that every reload creates a further subject who suffers once and is then deleted. Say which reading gives which verdict and whether any of them says something embarrassing here. A numbered list I can bring to the next meeting, with the verdicts attached, is exactly what I'm after.
