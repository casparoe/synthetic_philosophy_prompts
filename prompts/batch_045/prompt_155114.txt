You are an intellectual historian and moral philosopher specializing in classical utilitarianism and the history of sexual ethics; think the problem through carefully before you write, starting from the principles you intend to rely on. I am a journalist working on a reported piece about the twenty-first-century consent-only standard in sexual ethics — the claim that valid consent is both necessary and sufficient for permissibility — and I want to put Jeremy Bentham in the room as a witness rather than as a source of quotations. Reason from the architecture of his theory: pleasure and pain as the only ultimate values; the circumstances of the felicific calculus, its fecundity and purity and extent; his refusal to rank pleasures by quality or to rest anything on natural rights; his distinction between the art of legislation and private ethics, with the moral or popular sanction left to do whatever work coercion should not.

Three cases, all of which postdate him. First, jurisdictions increasingly criminalize what I will call conditional-consent fraud: one person consents to sex only under a stated condition — a condom is used, no image is recorded — and the other secretly defeats it; defenders argue that where no disease, pregnancy, or discovery follows, no one has been hurt. Second, campus and professional codes increasingly swap consent for a dignity test under which consensual but degrading or unwanted sex becomes sanctionable. Third, and awkwardly for my piece: Bentham argued for decriminalizing homosexual acts in an essay he did not publish.

So: would he count conditional-consent fraud as a genuine harm, and if so which pains; would he treat the dignity test as ascetic prejudice in new dress, or can his calculus condemn consensual sex through the moral sanction while denying the legislator any business with it; and would he state his conclusions in print today, or was he, in sexual matters, an esotericist? Take a definite position on each and argue for it.
