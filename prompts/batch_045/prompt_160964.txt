I'm in the last year of a PhD on early modern pessimism, and I want to use Macbeth to test a distinction in the antinatalism literature that I think has been under-described. Benatar's asymmetry is normally stated for the case of bringing a single new person into existence: the absence of pain is good even with no subject there to enjoy it, the absence of pleasure is not bad when no one is deprived. What I cannot find settled is whether it generalises to lineages — whether it gives a reason against there being a further generation at all, over and above the sum of the reasons against creating each of its members, or whether a line simply inherits whatever verdict the individuals in it receive.

Macbeth looks like a useful test case because it is saturated with procreation and takes the opposite attitude throughout: Macbeth's deepest grievance is the fruitless crown and sterile sceptre he says the witches gave him while promising kingship to Banquo's descendants; Lady Macbeth grounds her resolve in the claim that she would have dashed out the brains of her own nursing infant rather than break her word; the killing of Macduff's children is the moral centre of the play's second half; and its most nihilistic speech arrives when Macbeth, told that his wife is dead, calls life a tale told by an idiot, signifying nothing.

I want a reading of the play through this lens rather than a verdict on Benatar. Where does it genuinely dramatize the asymmetry's structure — for instance in the coupling of the prophecy of a royal line descending from Banquo with Banquo's murder, so that we see goods guaranteed to a lineage alongside a harm guaranteed to its founder? Does the play's foreknowledge condition matter: these agents are told in advance how their lines will go, which antinatalist arguments almost never assume, and does that defeat or preserve the asymmetry's verdict for Banquo's line? And where does the play resist the reading, so that an honest critic has to say so: is Macbeth's childlessness a curse the play itself endorses, or does its tragedy depend on our sharing Macbeth's conviction that fruitlessness is the worst fate a person can have? Is the play's pessimism perhaps about continuation rather than creation, which would make it a poor illustration of the asymmetry even at its most antinatalist moments?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reading. No preamble and no restatement of my question.
