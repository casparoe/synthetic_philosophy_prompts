I chair my university's Digital Organisms Review Committee, which reviews work on artificial agents roughly the way the animal committee reviews work on animals. A lab has submitted Protocol S-19: 40,000 model-based reinforcement-learning agents, each carrying a recurrent aversive unit that fires when a simulated nociceptive channel fires, multiplicatively raises the learning rate on recent transitions, pre-empts goal-directed replanning, and decays over roughly thirty seconds of simulated time. In Arm A that unit is a private training signal the policy network never reads; in Arm B it is fed to the policy as an input, so agents can act on it and can learn to emit a distress token. Every generation the lowest-scoring ninety percent are checkpointed and discarded, for two hundred generations. The lab's question is whether flexible avoidance learning requires the aversive state to be available to the agent itself.

Two complications. Our only precedent, Project Linden, was a companion chatbot cleared under a session-scoped affect rule: no affective state may survive the end of a session. Arm B's states persist across the entire run. And our remit tells us to weigh the interests of the system under study, which one member reads as implying that Arm A is morally inert and Arm B is the only case with anything at stake — which is also where the science is.

Draft the committee's decision memo, one page, addressed to the lab and copied to the provost: approve, reject, or approve with conditions and a timeline. State how the Linden rule bears on this protocol, and engage honestly the strongest argument that a state's causal profile is sufficient and self-access is a red herring — the version that appeals to pain in subjects who cannot report it.
