I have no academic background in philosophy or neuroscience; I've been reading about the commissurotomy cases on my own for a couple of years, and I've got into an argument in a reading group that I now suspect I was winning for the wrong reasons.

The analogy I leaned on — and which a lot of the popular writing on this seems to lean on as well — is that a split-brain patient is like a pair of conjoined twins sharing one body: two subjects, each with its own private experience, one of whom happens to do most of the talking. I used it to argue for a strong conclusion: that the unity of consciousness is a wiring accident rather than a fact about subjects; that severing the callosum does not create a second person but exposes one that was there all along; and hence that an ordinary human being is really two conscious subjects fused by a thick cable, rather than one subject with two specialised halves. Someone in the group pushed back that the analogy is rigged, because conjoined twins are two whole organisms that happen to share plumbing, whereas two hemispheres are halves of one system that never had separate arousal, separate affect, or separate bodies — and that a better comparison would be two specialist departments inside one organisation, one of which also runs the press office.

What I'd like is an audit rather than a verdict on who is right. Please give me the correspondence table explicitly for both pictures: hemisphere ↔ twin, corpus callosum ↔ whatever plays the role of the twins' channel, shared brainstem and body ↔ shared body, left-hemisphere language dominance ↔ one twin being the spokesman, the interpreter's confabulations ↔ what, intermanual conflict (one hand buttoning a shirt while the other undoes it) ↔ what, the classic case where one half is shown a snow scene and the other a chicken claw and the patient picks a shovel and explains it by reference to the claw ↔ what. Then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually bear on the conclusion I want and the ones that are decorative. In particular I'd like to know what each picture quietly imports that the target lacks: a viewer or homunculus for the two streams, a moral and legal notion of a person, a self-model in each half, the assumption that a hemisphere is the sort of thing that could have a point of view at all.

To keep this from being a rerun of the standard moves, I'd also like both analogies tested against three cases of my own, which I think isolate what each picture is really claiming. First, the dimmer: instead of cutting the callosum outright, imagine its bandwidth could be turned down continuously, from full to nothing — does the twins picture have to say there is a moment at which one subject becomes two, and if it can't name one, does "two people" survive as anything but a façon de parler? Second, the mood channel: the link is intact but transmits only arousal and affect, no content — one unified emotional weather, two streams of thought; is that one subject, two, or something the analogy has no vocabulary for? Third, the cases I've read about where someone loses or never had a whole hemisphere, or is born without a callosum at all, and is apparently one ordinary person: does that count against "one hemisphere, one person", and if it doesn't, why not?

Finally: does the strong claim — that we are each two subjects held together by a cable — gain any support from the analogy, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? And is there a better analogy, or is the honest answer that nothing in ordinary experience is remotely like this? Please write in British English, and please don't give me references or further reading: I have no library access and can't check them, so argument rather than sources is what I need.
