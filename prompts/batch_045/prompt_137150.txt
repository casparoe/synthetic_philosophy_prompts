hey — I need a third thing for a reading-group session I'm running on how the phrase "Western values" actually gets used in arguments. I've already got the serious handout, so what I want now is a parody to put next to it.

Write it as a call for tenders / request for proposals, issued by an invented EU-adjacent foundation, commissioning a consultancy to produce one authoritative, court-usable list of Western values to be attached as a schedule to something legal — a migration directive, a school curriculum, whatever. Numbered clauses, and at the end a short scoring rubric plus a "questions recieved from bidders" Q&A. About 700-800 words. Full procurement voice: "the Contracting Authority", "deliverable 3.2", "pass/fail criteria", "comfort letters", deadpan throughout.

The jokes have to come from real features of how the phrase works, not invented ones. The document should quietly commit itself to at least these:

- The list has to do exclusionary work: the values must be ones a named non-Western region obviously fails while no member state of the Authority obviously fails. Ask bidders to state their method for the awkward fact that most candidate values — rule of law, toleration, free inquiry, the dignity of the individual — are also endorsed all over the world and were fairly recently failed at at home. Bidders must say whether they'll define "the West" geographically, by descent, by civilizational inheritance, or by assent to the values themselves, and the document should not notice that the last option makes the whole exercise circular.
- A provenance clause: every value must be traceable to at least two of {Athens, Rome, Jerusalem, the Enlightenment, 1989}, with a provision that no value may be credited to a source that would embarrass the Authority, and that any borrowing from elsewhere be described as transmission rather than origin.
- A clause on the phrase's two jobs. The Authority wants a single list usable universalistically in one paragraph (these are just human values, anyone may sign up) and particularistically in the next (these are *ours*, and *those people* don't share them), with no visible seam. Bidders must specify how they'll manage the switch.
- A "conversation-stopper" requirement: invoking the list must be able to end a discussion rather than open one, i.e. shift the burden of proof onto whoever is uncomfortable. Bonus points if the phrase can be made to do real work in a dispute that is actually about gas, housing, or a trade deal, without anyone registering the change of subject.
- A pass/fail criterion excluding any value whose main effect would be to criticize current Western policy — freedom of movement was floated in an earlier tender and withdrawn after legal review. Let the bureaucrats be completely straight-faced about it; that should be the funniest clause in the document.
- A rubric weighted roughly: 30% distinctiveness (does the list pick out *us*), 30% flattery (is it a list of what we already do), 25% deniability (can a critic quote it without being called an ethnonationalist), 15% historical accuracy. It should be obvious from the weights how the thing will actually be scored while the document insists all four matter equally.

Constraints: no named living people; no real organizations except as a genre reference; invented spokespeople are fine and you can put words in their mouths — but everything inside quotation marks should be made up, since I don't want to fact-check real speeches. Keep the irony deadpan: nobody in the document should ever notice that the category might be empty or that the list is just a list of things we like. A reader who thinks the phrase is fine should be able to read it as a reasonable procurement document; a reader who's suspicious should be able to feel the reductio. And keep it readable for someone who's never thought about the category once — no in-jokes that require having read a specific book.
