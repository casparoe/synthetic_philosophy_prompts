I'm a junior at a fairly large public high school, and I've started going to school board meetings this year because our district has been arguing about how to tell whether its own programs work. I'd like your help figuring out what our district's reporting rules actually assume. The argument has stopped making sense to me, and I think the part I'm stuck on is conceptual rather than statistical.

Here is what the district does. Every spring we take a common end-of-course algebra exam. Students are placed into four cohort bands, Band 1 being the lowest, using their score on the previous year's exam, and the bands are re-cut every year so that each one always holds a quarter of the students: they are percentile bands, not fixed score ranges. The district publishes the percentage passing within each band, and it also publishes each school's overall pass rate, which is the number the local paper and the state report card use to rank schools. When the district evaluates its algebra support block, an extra period that students below a cutoff are placed into, it compares this year's pass rate inside each band with last year's pass rate inside that same band; it refuses to compare students in the program with students not in it, and it refuses to compare the district-wide rate from before the program existed with the district-wide rate after. When it defends the program to the board, though, it shows a slide of the district-wide rate before and after. At any school where a band has fewer than twenty students, that band's rate is suppressed entirely, and the school is still ranked on its overall number.

The year the program was expanded, ninth graders who used to take algebra in tenth grade were moved into it, so the pool of test-takers changed. Every band's pass rate went up and the district-wide rate went down. I've since read that this kind of reversal has a name, but the explanations I find just say to control for the confounding variable, which doesn't settle anything for me. My worry is the percentile bands themselves. Because they are re-cut annually, this year's Band 2 and last year's Band 2 are not the same kind of students: if the cohort as a whole scores higher on the earlier exam, this year's Band 2 is made of students with higher old scores than last year's Band 2, so improvement within bands across years looks like it could be mostly bookkeeping. The district's own principle is that you must compare like with like, and re-cutting the bands seems to break that principle while also being what makes the reversal possible. On top of that, the quantity being held fixed, last year's score, is itself something the program is supposed to raise over time, which feels like holding fixed the very thing you are trying to measure.

So what I want is not a verdict on whether this is good statistics. I want to know what the package of rules presupposes. What view of what a rate is, or of what it is for a program to make a difference, or of what makes two groups comparable, would someone have to hold for these rules to be the right ones? Does the district have to think of a rate as a fact about a population rather than about a school or a program, and of causation as difference-making rather than association, or could some quite different view license exactly the same rules? Which parts of the package follow from that view, and which are just habits that happen to sit well with it, so that several views would permit them? I'd especially like to know whether the package contradicts itself, since the district ranks schools on the unadjusted number while refusing to judge the program on it, or whether that only looks contradictory because the two numbers answer different questions. And if the district decided that the only honest comparison is each student against their own earlier score, with no bands at all, what would it lose by that change, and would the loss be real?

Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, the way you would write a long email. Assume I know no statistics beyond averages and percentages and no philosophy beyond what I've said; if a term like confounder or counterfactual is carrying weight in your answer, explain it in a sentence instead of leaning on it.
