I have been going round in circles in the comments of a blog I have read for years, and I have ended up defending an argument whose conclusion I do not want. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) Reductionism about testimony: a hearer is justified in believing p on the basis of a speaker's say-so only in virtue of reasons the hearer already has independently of that say-so — perception, memory, and inference from a track record — for taking the speaker to be reliable about matters like p, together with the absence of undefeated defeaters. The bare fact that someone asserted p does no justificatory work on its own.

(2) Bayesian gloss: let r be the hearer's expected reliability for the speaker on matters like p, conditional on everything except the say-so. The say-so raises the hearer's credence in p by an amount that increases with r, and leaves it untouched when r is at chance level.

(3) The hearer's estimate of r comes from a finite track record: some number of the speaker's past assertions, or of assertions by relevantly similar speakers, whose truth the hearer could check by non-testimonial means. Under any reasonably spread prior, a finite record puts positive density on every value of r and leaves E[r] strictly below 1; and for speakers in novel domains — the stranger on the train, the specialist whose field I cannot check at all — the checked record is empty.

(4) Most of what I believe about history and science reaches me through chains: A told B, B told C, and so on down to me, with few or no links that I can check non-testimonially.

(5) If each link transmits with expected reliability strictly below 1, and the links' errors are independent, then the evidential force of the chain decays geometrically in the number of links, tending to no confirmation at all as the chain grows.

(6) Hence: for almost every proposition I hold on the basis of what others have told me, my credence should be barely distinguishable from what it would have been had nobody told me anything. Testimonial knowledge is impossible in practice, and the same reasoning applied to the empirical and historical claims that any epistemology of testimony relies on undermines itself.

(7) I do not accept (6). But I would rather give up almost anything else than become an anti-reductionist, and my interlocutor's only reply — that a reductionist needs merely the absence of defeaters, not positive reasons — strikes me as changing the subject rather than answering it.

Which step is doing the illegitimate work? Tell me where the argument breaks, why that step looked sound, and what rejecting it commits me to — in particular, price the alternatives of abandoning (1), abandoning (3), and denying (5). If more than one step is suspect, say which is cheapest to lose. Please answer in British English.
