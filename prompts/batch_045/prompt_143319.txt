Hi — I'm a GP by background and I sit on our regional health system's individual funding request panel, the thing everyone here calls "exceptional cases". We meet twice a month, get through about twenty-five cases a session, ten or fifteen minutes each, and the panel is me, a paediatrician, someone from finance, and a lay member who is formidably sensible and has no medical training at all. Our founding document says we fund "restoration or maintenance of normal bodily and mental functioning" and we don't fund "improvement of function beyond what is normal". That sentence read as perfectly clear for about a fortnight, and then real cases started arriving and it's been coming apart in our hands ever since. I've done some reading on the therapy-versus-enhancement distinction in my own time and I get the general shape of the arguments, but everything I found stops right at the bit I need: somebody has to actually decide, in fifteen minutes, and write one page of reasons that has to survive an appeal and be readable by the person who applied. I was complaining about this to my brother, who teaches, and he said the same thing comes up with exam extra time and whether a quiet room is a treatment or an advantage, which is basically the same problem wearing a different hat.

So: please write me a decision procedure my panel can actually run, with the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and returns a "no" on its own, and where it has to hand the question to the four of us because it's a values call and not a factual one. Some constraints. We can't put terms like "species-typical" in the decision letters — the lay member rolls her eyes and the applicants definitely won't know what it means — so every question needs an askable-in-plain-English version. We get a twenty-page submission from the applicant's clinician and we can't commission anything ourselves, and maybe forty per cent of the time the submission just doesn't contain what we need, so the procedure has to tell us what to do when the answer is "unknown" rather than pretending we can look it up. We're also meant to be consistent with what earlier panels did without being strictly bound by it, so say how to record a decision so the next one can follow it. Two things I want built in because they've wrecked us twice already: one, some of these benefits are positional — they only work because most other people don't have them, and if we funded everyone the benefit would just evaporate — and I don't know whether that counts against funding or whether it's a fairness question we should be voting on separately. Two, my colleague pointed out that if we'd built schools around moving around instead of sitting still, a chunk of what we fund for attention problems would count as enhancement under our own rule, which makes me think "normal functioning" is quietly being measured against a world we invented; I'd like a question that catches cases where the demand being met is one our own institutions created, and I'd like you to tell me honestly if that question opens a hole we can't close.

Then run it on these. (1) A cochlear implant for a 45-year-old man who has been profoundly deaf since birth, fluent signer, active in the Deaf community, otherwise healthy; half the panel thinks this is obviously treatment because he's deaf, half thinks it's enhancement because there's nothing there to restore. (2) A request from occupational health on behalf of night-shift nurses for a wake-promoting drug — the papers just call it Drug W, it's licensed for narcolepsy, these nurses don't have narcolepsy, they have our rota — with data showing measurably higher error rates on nights. (3) Growth hormone for a twelve-year-old boy on the second percentile for height, no deficiency, both parents short, predicted adult height about 5'3", and the referral letter mentions bullying. I've a feeling at least one of those comes out somewhere the panel won't want to go — my guess is the growth hormone one, since we've always informally treated short stature as cosmetic, but if it's a different one that should bother us, say so plainly instead of letting me fudge it. If you've room, sketch the case that keeps coming up and that I personally can't get straight: a 58-year-old surgeon whose memory is completely normal for her age and not for her job. And tell me which distinctions I have to keep for the procedure to stay honest, which ones I can drop without it going wrong, and where it will give me an answer that's consistent and defensible but just isn't right.
