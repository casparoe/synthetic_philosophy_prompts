Hi — I'm one of five people on the investment committee of a small (~$180m) college endowment. We look at maybe 25 opportunities a year, meet monthly, and every memo we write ends in a number: a probability of hitting the target return, which we then feed into a position size. I've become convinced this is broken in a specific way: it makes us systematically favour whatever is easiest to model, and it lets a manager with a nice backtest of a brand-new strategy outcompete a boring fund with fifteen years of actual data, because the backtest produces a cleaner number. Someone pointed me at Keynes's Treatise on Probability — the bit about probability being a degree of rational belief grounded in evidence, and especially his separate notion of the weight of an argument (how much evidence you have, as opposed to how favourable it is), plus his claim that for a lot of real decisions there just is no numerical probability and people fall back on a convention that the future will look like the past, or on what everyone else is doing.

Can you turn that into a procedure we could actually run in a committee meeting? I mean: the questions in order, what counts as a real answer to each, when the procedure spits out a no, and when it has to hand the question back to us as a values call. Three things I care about most. (1) A test for whether a number in a memo is grounded in an actual class of comparable cases or is really just model output dressed up — I want the memo to say which. (2) Some rule connecting weight to position size that doesn't collapse into "be more conservative when you know less." (3) What we're supposed to do when there genuinely is no probability: Keynes seems to say falling back on convention is just what everyone does, but I want to know when that's fine and when it's self-deception, and whether "hold cash and wait" is ever an honest output we could defend to a board that hates cash drag.

Then run it on three cases: (a) re-upping with a private credit manager we've been with through two downturns and who has 15 years of quarterly data; (b) a first-time fund doing something genuinely new with a beautiful backtest and three months of live trading; (c) our single biggest allocation, which we've rolled over for years mostly because its never given us a reason to stop — I have a feeling the procedure is going to tell us something about that one that we don't want to hear. And please say plainly what the procedure can't do for us.
