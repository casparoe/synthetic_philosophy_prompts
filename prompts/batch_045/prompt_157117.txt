I am writing a novel, and a scene in it has stalled on a question of moral mechanics rather than of prose. Two sisters: Hannah, who ten years ago conducted a long affair and lied about it repeatedly to people who trusted her, and Miriam, who has for two years been quietly concealing a serious financial deception from her husband. Hannah discovers the second deception and blames Miriam for it — accurately, proportionately in tone, and with real grief. What I want the narration to convey is that Hannah's blame is somehow off: not mistaken about the facts, not mistaken about the wrongness of what Miriam did, but off in the register of who is speaking. The reader is meant to feel that Miriam is entitled to a certain kind of resistance, of the sort we gesture at with the question who are you to talk. When I try to say precisely what that entitlement consists in, I keep producing an argument that lands somewhere I cannot accept. A philosopher I met at a residency has tidied it into steps, which has not helped:

(1) To blame someone is to address them as answerable to a norm — to present that norm as one the addressee had sufficient reason to comply with and can properly be called to account for flouting.

(2) One can coherently address another person on behalf of a norm only if one is oneself committed to that norm; blame uttered by someone who does not accept it as applying to her own case is not moral address at all but scolding, posture, or the exercise of power.

(3) A person is committed to a norm only if she has complied with it or, having failed to, has acknowledged and repudiated the failure; commitment that survives violation untouched is not commitment but lip service.

(4) Nobody complies with every norm she professes, and — this is the step that does the real damage — for any fault in another person that I might blame, there is some description under which I have done the same kind of thing myself: everyone has deceived, everyone has ranked her own interests above someone she owed something to, everyone has failed to give help she could have given.

(5) So virtually no one ever has standing to blame anyone for anything.

(6) Which is absurd, and not only in the abstract: it strips the standing of the deceived wife who has herself once deceived, of the wrongdoer who long ago repented and was forgiven, and arguably of anyone enmeshed in institutions that produce the very wrongs she condemns. It seems to leave blame a category with a handful of instances, all of them saints.

My friend says the whole thing is the tu quoque move dressed up, and that I am confusing a vice of the blamer with a defect in the blame; but that diagnosis makes the scene's central intuition into a confusion, which I am reluctant to accept. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather keep (1) and (2) than accept (5), and I would rather keep the claim that Hannah's blame is defective in some way than keep the claim that nobody can ever blame. Please tell me which premise looked fine but is not, why it looked fine, and what each of the standard ways out — that hypocrisy is merely epistemically relevant to blame, that blame needs no standing condition at all, that standing is a matter of equal moral position rather than of clean hands — has to pay. I have also sketched a patch of my own: standing, I want to say, is not a matter of clean hands but of reciprocal exposure — a blamer retains standing if she could accept being blamed in turn, by the person she blames, for her own failures of the same kind, and loses it precisely when she claims an asymmetry she would refuse in reverse. Does that escape the argument, or does it prove too much in its own direction? Rank the moves by what they cost the rest of my thinking about blame, and say what, in the end, the narration should make explicit for the reader to see what Miriam is entitled to.
