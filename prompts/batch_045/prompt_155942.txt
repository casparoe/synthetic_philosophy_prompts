Next week I'm presenting at our policy school's political theory workshop and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis: when someone is uncertain between moral views, she should sometimes take a safe Pareto improvement over the option her own favourite view ranks highest. I define a safe Pareto improvement as an option that (a) no moral view with more than, say, 5% of her credence ranks below the status quo, and (b) stays at least as good under every parameter setting the evidence leaves open, even scored maximin-style rather than in expectation (so robust along two axes at once: value-robust and evidence-robust). The argument is aimed against maximizing expected choiceworthiness across moral views; I say intertheoretic comparisons are too shaky for that, and that my rule also beats plain dominance because it bites under empirical uncertainty too. Case study: a large cash transfer program for people in extreme poverty, financed by a small tax on a narrow base. I claim it passes both tests even though there are identifiable losers and even though the prioritarian in me thinks a differently targeted, bigger program would do more good.

The room: two welfare economists who live in cost-benefit analysis, QALYs and distributional weights; one luck egalitarian; one operations researcher who does robust decision making under deep uncertainty; one deliberative-democracy theorist who thinks any move of the form we don't need to say which moral view is right is technocracy in disguise; assorted grad students.

Give me the seven or eight objections I'll actually get, ordered by likely-times-damaging, each phrased the way that particular person would put it (say who's asking), with roughly a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include what I should concede in the first two minutes, the one reply that has to land for the proposal to survive at all, and the objection I'm praying nobody raises.
