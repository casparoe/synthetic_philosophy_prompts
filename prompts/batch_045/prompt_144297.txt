In the decision-theory reading group I run, we have spent the better part of two years on the hitchhiker case Parfit made famous: you are stranded in the desert, a driver will carry you to town if he is confident you will hand over $1,000 once you are safe, he is a near-perfect judge of character, and there is no way to pay in advance. Nobody has moved anybody, and I want to know why.

The two positions are familiar to us and I will sketch them only to fix reference. One side says paying in town is what you rationally ought to do, and takes the case as a refutation of causal decision theory: your handing over the money changes nothing causally, but it is superb evidence about whether you were the sort of person who got the ride, and the evidential, functional, and timeless variants of decision theory all converge on paying. The other side says that once you are in town the money is spent for nothing, so paying is straightforwardly irrational, and that the interesting claim is the further one that grew out of the case: it can be rational, back in the desert, to arrange to become the sort of person who pays, that is, to bring it about that you will later act against the balance of reasons as you will then see them. Everyone in the room agrees about the causal structure, the payoffs, and the driver's accuracy rate. The disagreement is not empirical in any obvious sense and it is not ignorance of the arguments.

Here is my own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis. I have started sorting the things we say into three audits: a momentary audit, which assesses the choice in the town against the agent's evidence at that moment; a diachronic audit, which assesses the plan adopted in the desert across the whole episode; and a procedural audit, which assesses the rule or algorithm that produced the behaviour and counts every instantiation of it, including the instantiation running in the driver's head as he decides whether to stop. My suspicion is that the two camps are not disagreeing about the case at all but about which audit is authoritative, and that talk of rational irrationality is a symptom of choosing under one audit while being assessed under another. A secondary suspicion: part of the apparent depth is manufactured by the setup, which strips the agent of every commitment device, so that what looks like a normative conflict may in large part be a shortage of commitment technology.

I have three probes, and I want you to tell me which of them actually discriminates between the candidate diagnoses and which is a distraction because both sides can absorb it. First, the registered promise: at the kerb, for a dollar, a notary can register a debt that will be enforced in town. Now everyone says sign, and the desert deliberation is over before it starts. Does that unanimity show that the original case is an engineering problem rather than a normative one, and if so which camp is left with a real thesis? Second, the legible self-blinder: the driver's accuracy extends to your attempts to alter your own later dispositions, so he correctly predicts both whether you will hire someone to make you a payer and what that person will make of you. Can the rational-irrationality route survive that, or does it enter a regress of meta-manipulation, and does the answer change in any way that matters if the driver is 90 per cent accurate rather than perfect? Third, the amnesiac payer: you knew in the desert that on arrival your memory of the promise and of the driver's face would be erased, and that the $1,000 leaves your account unless you say one word. Does the reason to pay depend on something amnesia destroys, such as a standing intention, a relationship, or a settled trait of character, or is all the work done by the structure of the case?

Write me roughly 900 words. Begin with the diagnosis in a single sentence I could disagree with: is this a verbal dispute about the word rational, a substantive dispute about whether rationality is a success notion or a coherence-with-your-evidence notion, a hidden empirical question about whether agents can in fact self-blind and whether predictors can see them trying, a case of two questions answering to one label, or bedrock disagreement that neither side can argue for? Then give the reasons for it, drawn from what each side actually commits itself to rather than from what it is often accused of saying, and give the strongest objection to your own diagnosis. Then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a finding about self-blinding and its limits, a change of question. Or argue that no progress should be expected, and say why. Do not survey the literature, do not cite or name-drop authorities, do not appeal to how most decision theorists come down, and do not close by splitting the difference.
