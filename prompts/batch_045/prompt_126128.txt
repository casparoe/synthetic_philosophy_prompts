I'm on the faculty senate this year (long story, nobody else would take it), and I keep watching the same shape of thing happen and I have no word for it. About ten years ago the senate passed a rule that any change to the general-education requirements needs a two-thirds vote plus a full academic year before it takes effect. The stated reason was explicit and a little sheepish: we knew we were prone to passing curricular reform in a May fit of enthusiasm and regretting it by September, so we were tying ourselves to the mast. Fine — that's just a Ulysses contract, nothing to see. What I didn't expect is where the thing has ended up. The rule has never once been used to slow down a reform anybody was enthusiastic about. Every proposal now gets routed through a subcommittee that drafts it as a "pilot" or a "temporary concentration requirement," which doesn't trigger the rule at all; everybody knows this and says so cheerfully and on the record. Meanwhile the rule bites constantly, but on things nobody was ever worried about — right now it is the single reason we can't fix a two-credit lab requirement that everyone agrees is broken, because no one can scare up two-thirds for the fix in the year they think of it. So the precommitment is formally intact, is still warmly described by everyone in the room as *our* precommitment, and is still doing real work, and none of that work is the work it was adopted to do, while the work it was adopted to do proceeds at full speed through a side door. I've now seen the same shape twice more: a state-level supermajority requirement for raising taxes, adopted to restrain a legislature's own spending impulses, which two decades later gets invoked mainly by a minority to block fee and licensing changes that have nothing to do with the original temptation, while the spending everybody was worried about happens through bonded debt that nobody counts as spending. And a friend who put a one-day delay on her phone's app blocker to stop doomscrolling in bed; a year later she doesn't use the phone in bed at all, she uses a cheap tablet she bought for the purpose, and the blocker now functions mostly as an obstacle when she wants to look up a recipe in the kitchen.

So: the binding survives, in force and in everyone's self-description, but its bite has migrated onto a different decision or a different party than the one it was aimed at (and often onto parties who had the least say in adopting it), while the original temptation gets satisfied by a route the binding doesn't cover. Is there an established concept for that, or am I going to have to name it myself? I've poked around the rational-choice literature on self-binding — Schelling, Elster, that crowd — but they seem mostly interested in whether and why binding works at all, not in this slow sideways drift. It isn't weakness of will or ordinary akrasia, because nobody in these stories is failing to do what they judged best; they're complying scrupulously, in public. It isn't hypocrisy or bad faith either: the workaround is announced out loud and nobody thinks they're cheating. It's not the dead-hand objection, since no one feels ruled by the past — the present reaffirms the rule every time it comes up. It isn't desuetude, since the rule is cited constantly and shapes how everything gets drafted. It isn't Goodhart's law, because the indicator isn't being gamed, it's being honored. And "function creep" doesn't feel right either: the function is still blocking, exactly as designed; it's the *target* that moved.

I don't want a label to drop into a footnote, so please don't just hand me one of those near misses. What I want is the carving. If there's a real concept here, tell me what exactly it is, how to tell it apart from each of its neighbours, and where it stops; if there isn't, coin something and define it properly, with boundary cases. Three cases I can't settle myself: does the phenomenon require that the device bit correctly at first, or is it still an instance if the rule was always somewhat off-target? Is it an instance when the device was adopted partly to *signal* resolve to outsiders rather than to restrain anyone, i.e. when the binding was never really aimed at the temptation in the first place? And is there any test that distinguishes genuine from migrated binding from the inside — my only candidate so far is something like "if this rule were repealed tomorrow, would the people now bound by it pass it again, and in the same form?" I'd also like to know whether the single-agent version (the tablet) is really the same thing as the collective version, or whether the collective case needs something extra, like the fact that membership turnover means literally nobody bound is anybody who bound themselves.
