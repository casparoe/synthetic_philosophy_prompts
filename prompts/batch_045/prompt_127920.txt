I am a second-year PhD student in machine learning, currently taking a graduate seminar on the moral status of artificial systems, and I would like a systematic translation of one argumentative move in that literature into the vocabulary I actually work in. I am fluent in that vocabulary, so please do not stop to explain it to me.

The move I have in mind is this: the claim that whatever matters morally attaches to *computations* rather than to the physical stuff that realises them, combined with the follow-up dispute about how finely computations are to be individuated. On the face of it, the claim lets the same computation survive radical changes in substrate while a single physical process hosts many computations (or one computation is spread across many processes), so that counting systems and counting the things that matter come apart — which then raises the attribution question: if some candidate system has moral status, which of the processes going on in and around it are the ones that carry it, and how much of the training, the prompting, and the surrounding infrastructure belongs to the thing being counted?

Please restate that package in my vocabulary: architectures as maps from weights to functions, trained models as single points in weight space, a forward pass as a trajectory through activation space, weights versus activations versus in-context state, functional and behavioural equivalence between models, floating-point precision and run-to-run kernel non-determinism, batching and shared KV caches, speculative decoding and other decoding-time search, quantisation, pruning, distillation, fine-tuning, in-context learning, chain-of-thought as extra computation at inference, mixture-of-experts routing, and the tools of mechanistic interpretability (circuits, features, ablations, activation patching) as ways of isolating which parts of a pass do the causal work.

Two things about that vocabulary matter to me and I would like you to press on them rather than paper over them. First, in my own notes I have been distinguishing three candidate bearers of whatever the position cares about — the architecture, the trained model, and the individual forward pass — and I cannot tell which one, if any, the position needs; a translation that quietly substitutes one of the three for *computation* has smuggled in a decision, not made a translation. Second, two cases keep breaking my attempts: (a) a production forward pass is rarely a clean single-input process but a fused operation over a batch of hundreds of sequences with shared caches, so the boundary of the individual computation is a scheduling artefact; (b) a model and its quantised or distilled near-twin are behaviourally near-identical while differing substantially in activation-level causal structure, so it is unclear whether they host the same computation or different ones.

The part I most want is the residue report. Go through the claims one by one and say which translate cleanly, which translate only under a stipulation about grain that my field fixes by convention rather than by argument, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all — in particular whether *how many* computations a process hosts, or whether two runs are the same computation, are questions my vocabulary can even pose. Then give me your verdict on the residue: is it a defect of the machine-learning framework, or a sign that the original claim was doing less work than it looked like it was doing? If you think the translation fails somewhere specific, say where and why, rather than repairing it silently.
