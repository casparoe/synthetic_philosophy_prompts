In discussions of scalable oversight, one camp argues that debate between two AI systems lets a weak human judge supervise a model much stronger than herself, because adjudicating between two competing arguments is often far cheaper than producing the correct answer unaided, so the judge's inability to solve the problem need not limit her ability to pick the better case. The other camp replies that this asymmetry is contingent and can invert: a debater can bury a single false step inside a branching structure of individually plausible sub-claims, force the judge to choose which branch to spend her limited attention on, and thereby make the cost of exposing a lie larger than the cost of telling it. What frustrates me is that the two sides agree on the formal setup, agree that the asymmetry holds for some tasks and fails for others, and then draw opposite conclusions; the argument has been in this shape for years and nobody has moved.

My hypothesis is that they are not talking about the same gap. Distinguish three: a verifiability gap, the difference in cost between producing a correct answer from scratch and certifying one that is handed to you; a refutability gap, the difference in cost between making a false claim survive and driving a wedge into one; and an evidence gap, where the facts that would settle the question are not available to anyone in the room, so no amount of argumentative compression helps. I suspect the optimists' considerations really concern the first gap in domains where certification is cheap, the skeptics' really concern the second gap under adversarial conditions, and that neither side's arguments touch the third, where debate may simply be the wrong instrument.

Please diagnose the impasse on that basis rather than adjudicating it: is this a substantive disagreement about a contingent empirical question, namely how these gaps scale as models get stronger relative to the judge; a verbal dispute carried on under a shared word like verifiable; a clash of standards of evidence between people who want worst-case guarantees and people who will accept average-case experimental results; or two groups answering different questions, one about near-term oversight of systems that are only modestly superhuman and one about oversight of systems whose advantage is large enough that the judge cannot even track the sub-claims? Then, given your diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a measurement, a taxonomy of task types, a reformulation of the question — or argue that the question is ill-posed and that no such result should be expected. I want the structure of the disagreement and the reasons for your diagnosis, not a survey of the literature.
