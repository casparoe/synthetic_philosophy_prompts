I'm in a graduate reading group on reference and modality this term, and our leader — call her Dr. Voss — has spent the semester ruling on cases rather than expounding a theory, so I've started writing her verdicts down because my own paper depends on anticipating her. So far: she says the Hesperus–Phosphorus identity is necessary a posteriori; that the standard-meter case yields a contingent a priori truth; that in the Gödel–Schmidt case the name still refers to Gödel and the sentence is simply false; that on Twin Earth our word for water does not apply to XYZ and the water identity is necessary a posteriori; that in the Madagascar case current speakers' uses refer to the island rather than to the stretch of mainland the travellers' word originally picked out; that names like Jack the Ripper and Jonah fail to refer if the associated stories are fiction, so reference is never secured by whichever candidate happens to fit the description best; and — the closest thing I have to a real precedent — that a museum label reading the Aldridge Skull continues to refer to the original skull even though the original burned in 1931 and every measurement, cast, and photograph in the subsequent literature descends from the plaster replacement. She describes herself as a causal-historical theorist who thinks the natural and formal sciences are permitted to fix reference by convention. A friend in the group thinks Madagascar is obviously the governing precedent for the case I care about; I think the Aldridge skull is, and we've agreed to put the question to you.

The case is this. In 1959 the botanist Vandermeer pressed a sheet of material from a tree in a valley, wrote in his notebook that he was naming the species of this specimen, published the name with a description, and deposited the sheet as the name-bearing specimen. The sheet turned out to be a mixed collection: it contains foliage from two closely related but genuinely distinct species, which I'll call S1 (the left-hand fragment) and S2 (the right-hand fragment). Vandermeer never noticed. His description is compatible with both species but mentions one character, a leaf-margin type, that only S1 has. In 1994 a reviser, following the convention that a name whose original specimen turns out to mix taxa must be pinned to a single piece of it, designated the left-hand fragment as the type element — so the official mechanics of botanical naming attach the name to S1. But by 1994, and still today, every field identification, every illustration in the field guides, and every sample working botanists actually handle traces back to S2; S1 is extinct and known only from that fragment. The name also appears on an endangered-species schedule, where the population actually being protected is S2's, though I doubt Voss will let a downstream consequence decide a question about reference.

What I need: (1) Say what she will and should, for consistency with her own track record, say that the name refers to — S1, S2, neither, both (a homonymy), or indeterminate — with the reason, and tell me whether on her view there is a fact of the matter here at all. (2) Extract the two or three rules about reference determination that her verdicts are jointly compatible with, since I take it they are compatible with more than one; show what each predicts about this case; give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it; and adjudicate the Madagascar-versus-Aldridge-Skull disagreement, saying which is the closer analog and what exactly turns on the difference between them. (3) Tell me what she will do with the modal and epistemic classification. Supposing the name comes out referring to S2, is the sentence that attributes S2's distinctive seed-coat type to the species necessary a posteriori in the way the water identity is supposed to be, merely contingent a posteriori, or something a botanist could know a priori from the conventions of nomenclature? And if the answer is contingent a posteriori, is that because the term is not rigid or because the trait is not essential to the species — does her track record tell us which of those moves she will reach for, or does it leave her free? (4) Design one further variant of the case that I could put to her in office hours to separate the rules you think are still live — varying, say, the timing, publicity, or uptake of the 1994 designation, or the identity of the two species — and tell me what each rule predicts about it. Please argue the case out rather than reporting positions from the literature; I have the reading list and don't need another one, and what I really need to know is where my example is genuinely unsettled by commitments she has already made rather than settled by a theory she has never signed up to.
