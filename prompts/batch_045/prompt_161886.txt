I am putting together the second meeting of a graduate seminar on skepticism, and I want a comparison I can hand to students as a scaffold for discussion rather than a survey of the literature. Please do not give me a bibliography or a catalogue of names, titles and dates; what I need is the argumentative structure made explicit, with the contested joints flagged, since I will be asking students to take sides on each of them.

The two hypotheses I want compared are these.

H1, the solitary vat. A single brain is kept alive in a vat and connected to a computer that stimulates it so as to produce a coherent stream of experience. The subject was embodied until some time t and continues, experientially, in what looks like the same life. There are no other envatted brains. Every apparent person is part of the stimulation programme; there is no genuine social interaction and no other mind in the subject's world.

H2, the shared vat. Many brains are envatted and wired to one computer running a single common simulation. Because the brains are coupled through the machine, their experiences are correlated in the way that the experiences of embodied agents are: what one subject does affects what the others undergo, disagreements get resolved, promises get kept and broken. There is, on any ordinary test, genuine social interaction and there are genuine other minds. None of these brains has ever been embodied, and the physics of the simulation differs in its fine structure from the physics of the vat world that hosts it.

My questions:

1. Which is the stronger skeptical hypothesis, and by what criterion? I want the criterion argued for, not assumed: indistinguishability from the subject's own point of view, the proportion of our ordinary belief set each hypothesis falsifies, the number and cost of the auxiliary assumptions each needs, or something else. If the ranking flips depending on the criterion, say so.

2. Does the move from H1 to H2 change what skepticism is threatening — the existence of the world, its independence from us, the existence of other minds, the match between appearance and reality, the reliability of our cognitive faculties? Identify where the two hypotheses come apart and where they do not, and say which of these targets is the one the traditional deceiving-deity scenario was after.

3. Run the closure schema against each hypothesis: if a subject knows some ordinary proposition, and knows that it entails the denial of the skeptical hypothesis, then the subject knows the denial of the hypothesis. Take as the ordinary proposition something like having hands, or being in conversation with another person. Does the antecedent survive better under H2 than under H1, and if it does, does that weaken the skeptical argument or merely relocate it? Does the argument from ignorance and the argument from hallucination behave differently across the two cases?

4. The familiar semantic reply holds that a subject in the skeptical scenario cannot so much as think the thought that it is in it, because its words would not refer to the relevant kinds of thing. That reply turns on the subject's causal situation. In H2 the subject is causally connected, through the computer, to other brains and to the hardware itself, and the simulation may depict neuroanatomy, vats, and even the hypothesis H2 as a hypothesis discussed within the simulated world. Does any of this restore reference to real brains and real vats? Does the reply survive unchanged, or does it have to be restated in a form that H2 escapes? What exactly turns on whether the computer counts as part of the causal chain running from the subject to a real brain?

5. Is there a non-question-begging way to decide whether computer-mediated causal coupling between brains counts as interaction in a shared world, or is any answer to that question just the skeptical question re-posed? If the latter, what does that tell us about what the contrast between H1 and H2 was supposed to isolate in the first place?

6. One final wrinkle. In H2 the simulated world may itself contain vats, brains, and computers running further simulations, and those in turn may contain others, indefinitely. Does such a hierarchy collapse — so that H2 is H1 at every level, or is no longer a vat scenario at any level — or does each level inherit the problem intact? Where in the regress, if anywhere, does the skeptical force get generated?

Please keep the whole answer under about nine hundred words, organise it by my numbered questions, and wherever you make a judgment state the contested commitment it rests on: I want the students to see the choices rather than receive a verdict. Close with a short paragraph saying which of the two hypotheses you would put at the centre of the session and what you would concede to someone who put the other one there.
