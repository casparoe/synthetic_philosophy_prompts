hey — I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy background at all, so please don't assume I know any of the terminology: if there's a term of art I should be using, name it and define it in half a sentence. I'm applying for a tiny artist-run microgrant (something like $8k, four months, realistically 8–10 hours a week since this is nights and weekends) and what they want back is a public repo plus a short writeup, not an academic paper. So I need a two-page-ish research proposal, and please skip any bibliography or citations entirely — I'll add those myself later or leave them out, and I'd rather the space go to the actual argument.

The topic is the thing that gets called the Letter Spirit problem: what makes every 'a' an 'a', given that the 'a' in a Didone serif face, in blackletter, in a 3x5 pixel bitmap font, and in my five-year-old's crayon scrawl share basically no measurable geometry. I've been messing with variable fonts and SVG path interpolation on weekends, and two things keep bugging me. First, naive interpolation between two outlines is fine geometrically but produces total garbage semantically: morph an 'a' into a 'u' and the midpoint is neither. Fixing it requires deciding which point on one outline corresponds to which point on the other, and the only way I know to do that is by thinking in terms of parts — stem, bowl, shoulder, terminal, aperture — which starts to feel like the letter's identity is carried by a part structure plus qualitative relations (how many enclosed counters, which way the aperture opens, what's attached to what, what's above what) rather than by shape. Second, I got into an argument with a friend who does ML and insisted the whole thing is just clustering: embed a bunch of outlines, and 'a' is a region in that space. But that seems to miss that a Latin 'a' and a Cyrillic 'а' can be pixel-identical and still be different letters, because they belong to different contrast sets — and conversely that someone could design a brand-new glyph with no causal descent from any scribal tradition and it would instantly be an 'a'. My half-formed idea, which I want turned into a real proposal, is to stop asking what all the a's have in common and instead ask what has to be held fixed while everything else is allowed to vary — with the twist that there are two directions of variation: hold the letter fixed and vary style, hold the style fixed and vary the letter, and maybe the category is just whatever sits at the fixed point where those two things cross.

So here's what I want the proposal to do. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer — I'm tempted by something like: is there a finite specification in a small "anatomy language" of parts and relations such that every recognizable instance of a letter satisfies it and no instance of any other letter does? — but you should push back if that's the wrong sharp version, or if the interesting claim is negative. Say why the two obvious answers leave it open: the prototype / family-resemblance / cluster story, and the historical-or-causal-chain story, and be specific about the case each one dies on. Then the method, month by month, for four months at ~10 hrs/week: I'm imagining month one hand-building the spec language and hand-specifying a deliberately nasty subset (a, o, u, n, h, d, b, p, q, g — the rotation and reflection pairs, because that's where any purely relational spec should break), month two writing a little generator that randomizes the metrics while holding the spec fixed and getting maybe twenty people to classify a few dozen of the outputs, month three running the minimal-edit experiments where I hunt for the smallest geometric change that flips which letter people see and ask whether the flip always coincides with some discrete structural event (a counter opening, a stroke detaching) or whether there are continuous paths where the category changes with nothing discrete happening, and month four doing the held-out test — given twenty-five letters in a style the system hasn't seen, generate the twenty-sixth from its spec and see if readers accept it as belonging. Also: I want an honest failure section. I think the project fails if every letter's spec turns out to need at least one arbitrary metric threshold to keep other letters out, because then "structure" has quietly become prototype-plus-thresholds; and it also fails if the human data shows no sharp boundaries anywhere and everything is graded mush. And tell me, plainly, if ten letters is already too ambitious for the time I have and what you'd cut.
