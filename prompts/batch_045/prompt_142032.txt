A friend and I have just spent a long evening arguing about a case we both know. Some years ago a local landlord systematically withheld security deposits from tenants who could not afford to sue, lied to them about repairs, and made their lives steadily worse; he was never charged, never repaid anyone, and died last year after a slow and painful illness. My friend said, twice, "what goes around comes around." I first took this as a piece of folk sociology: people who treat others badly accumulate enemies, grudges and risks, and over a long enough life that tends to catch up with them. He says that is not what he meant. He meant that the illness was in some sense his, that he had it coming, and that there was something fitting about it — something we were licensed to be quietly glad about. When I pressed him on the mechanism by which any of that could be true, he said "karma," which struck me as relocating the puzzle rather than solving it.

I would like a disambiguation rather than a verdict on who won the argument. Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that one-liner could be expressing in that exchange, ordered roughly from the thinnest to the most ambitious. Several I can only half-formulate, and I want them separated and sharpened rather than confirmed:

a descriptive claim about how repeat defectors are treated by the people around them, and about the ordinary risks that a life of cheating creates; a claim about the self-destructiveness of the vices themselves, such that the traits that make someone a wrongdoer tend to consume him; a claim that wrongdoers deserve to suffer and that their suffering is good, or at least non-bad, quite apart from any deterrent or compensatory effect; a "ledger" claim, where wrongdoing generates a quantity of moral debt that subsequent misfortune somehow settles; a fittingness claim, that suffering is the apt response to wrongdoing and that being glad about it is the apt response to that; a "discharge" claim, that the suffering alters what is still owed — to the victims, or by way of remaining blame; and a strong cosmological claim, that the world contains a mechanism of moral causation which tends to arrange this, perhaps across lives.

For each reading I want: what exactly it asserts; what would have to be true for it to be true; whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or something false or incoherent; and whether it can be held by someone who believes in no cosmic order at all, or whether it requires more machinery than my friend ever supplied.

Most importantly, say which readings would actually do the three jobs my friend wanted the slogan to do: making his death fitting rather than merely unfortunate; making my friend's quiet satisfaction an appropriate response rather than indulged spite; and making a difference to what remains owed to the tenants he never repaid. My suspicion is that most of the readings deliver the first two only if a desert claim can be made sense of when nothing is owed to anyone in particular, when no one who was wronged is compensated or even aware, and when the misfortune is causally unrelated to the wrong — so please say whether desert needs a recipient, a beneficiary, or a currency that matches.

Two test cases, if they help discriminate. First: imagine a device that, with perfect accuracy, arranges painful but non-lethal accidents for wrongdoers in proportion to their wrongdoing, which no one can detect, which compensates no victim, and whose work no one ever learns about; and then a second device that does the same but inflicts the suffering on someone the wrongdoer loves. Which readings imply it would be a good thing to switch these on, and does the difference between them tell us anything about whether the slogan is about the wrongdoer or about the balance? Second: suppose the landlord had spent his last decade anonymously repaying every tenant, had become a genuinely different person, and his victims were entirely indifferent to his fate. Which readings still say the illness was fitting, and does the fact that nobody ever says "what goes around comes around" when a decent person prospers tell against any of them, or is it merely an artefact of when the sentence gets used?

Please present the result as a list or table I can take back to him, with the readings in one column, your verdict on each in another, and a third saying which of the three jobs it actually performs.
