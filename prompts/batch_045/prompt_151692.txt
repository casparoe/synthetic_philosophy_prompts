hey — please take the role of a historian of environmental history who also cares way too much about causal inference, like the kind of person who'd argue about DAGs at a conference hotel bar. I've been arguing with a friend in a small ACX-adjacent reading group about whether germs, weather, and volcanoes can genuinely be "historical actors," or whether that phrasing is just a stylish way of smuggling determinism back into narrative history. Everyone keeps landing on "well, sometimes," which is useless, so I want to force some precision. Here are two tests I made up, and I want you to apply them rather than swap in your own framework (though see the note at the end):

1. The Replacement Test. Swap the non-human cause for a different non-human cause of roughly comparable severity — a drought instead of a pathogen, a cold decade instead of an eruption. If the *same specific outcome* still falls out more or less on schedule, then the original cause was only doing generic "bad stuff happens, societies suffer" work, and it deserves little credit for the shape of what actually happened. If the outcome changes its character (timing, direction, who wins), the cause is doing specific work.
2. The Channel Test. Trace the causal chain from the germ/ash front/rainfall anomaly to the outcome and count how many genuine human decision points it has to pass through. If it bypasses every decision point — people just die, or just starve, and the outcome falls out mechanically — you've got determinism wearing a lab coat, and the score should be penalized even if the causal claim is true. If it has to route through at least one choice that a well-informed contemporary could reasonably have made differently, the cause is acting *through* agency rather than instead of it.

So: score these six episodes for "honest causal credit" on a 0-100 scale, then put them into four tiers (something like S: load-bearing and not deterministic, A: strong but partly generic, B: real but mediated to hell, C: mostly vibes), with one sentence on why each lands where it does and a one-line trace of the chain for each. The six: smallpox and the fall of Tenochtitlán; the Black Death and the collapse of labor scarcity in England in the decades after 1348; Tambora and the Year Without a Summer; potato blight and the Irish famine; yellow fever and the French expedition in Saint-Domingue; the 1918 influenza pandemic and the end of the First World War (including the "Wilson was sick at Versailles" version of that story). Use the consensus version of the facts and don't look anything up — you have no web access, and I'd rather have your honest priors than invented precision; if you're shaky on a detail, flag it and say how much your score would move if the detail goes the other way.

My own hot take, as seeding: I think the 1918 flu is massively overrated as a historical actor and belongs in tier B or C, because the Replacement Test kills it — remove the flu and you still get a Central Powers collapse in 1918, you just get it a bit later, whereas remove smallpox from 1520 and the whole shape of the conquest is different. I also suspect Saint-Domingue scores higher than people expect because the disease got there *through* a decision (seasoning, troop timing, who got sent where), so it passes the Channel Test in a way famine cases often don't.
