Hey — I co-host a small, loosely edited epistemology podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends what I've started calling counterfactual reductionism about testimonial warrant: the claim that a hearer's justification for believing what she's told doesn't require her to actually have positive reasons bearing on the speaker's reliability (not occurrent, not even stored in memory), but does require that she be in a position to produce such reasons if she were pressed — roughly, reasons about the speaker's competence and honesty in the relevant domain, plus the usual base-rate/track-record material. So the requirement is modal rather than occurrent.

I want eight to ten questions that go past asking her to lay the view out. She handles the standard hard cases very smoothly by saying that's just a shift in the baseline of what the hearer could come up with, and I want a sequence that makes that move progressively harder to lean on, ending with something about what would actually count against the view. For each question I'd like the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, plus a one-line note on why the question bites.

Things I definitely want covered:

How the could gets spelled out. Is it ability-on-reflection, ability-after-five-minutes, ability-with-help-from-others, ability-with-a-search-engine? If help from other people is allowed, the requirement looks like it stops being a condition on the individual hearer and becomes a distributed one — press on whether she's willing to swallow that, and then on whether a distributed version still counts as reductionism rather than as the anti-reductionist's default entitlement with extra bookkeeping.

The squeeze. If the counterfactual condition is weak enough that nearly every competent adult satisfies it, the view predicts nothing and is hard to distinguish from the default/entitlement views she's arguing against; if it's demanding, it seems to over-generate skepticism about the ordinary epistemic division of labour (my dentist, my mechanic, the air-traffic controller). Her likely move is to tighten the ability so that it has to be exercisable without special assistance — so I want the follow-up to target that specific tightening.

The novice cases, where I expect her to say that kids and first-language learners do have crude evidence (caregiver track records, sincerity cues, the fact that adults mostly get things right). The follow-up should press on whether evidence that crude could possibly underwrite the *content* the kid acquires — that invisible organisms cause disease, that the earth is very old, that words mean what they mean — rather than merely underwriting the bare claim that this speaker is worth listening to.

The case that looks like free money for her, and I want it asked so it doesn't stay free: outputs of systems that aren't speakers. A translation app, an automated weather readout, a chatbot, an aggregated database. No one is offering assurance, no one is taking responsibility for what's said, nothing you could trust in the interpersonal sense — but there is a reliability profile the hearer could in principle sample. Reductionism seems to glide over these while trust-based and assurance-based default views have to say something awkward. Ask it, let her have the point, then follow up: does her view end up saying that warrant from a well-calibrated machine is in *better* shape than warrant from a colleague's assertion, and is the difference between the two merely pragmatic? And then: what if the system's reliability is genuinely opaque to the user — proprietary, black-box, something no amount of querying would let her estimate? Does the could-marshal condition fail there, and is that the result she wants?

Content-based versus speaker-based reasons. Her requirement is naturally read as reasons about the speaker. But suppose the only thing the hearer could produce if pressed is a reason about the content — it fits her other beliefs, it's the sort of thing that would be widely reported if false. If that's enough, the view has quietly stopped being reductionism. I want the question, and I want the follow-up to her almost certain answer that speaker-reasons can be partly generic.

One question on whether the whole debate has gone verbal on us: reductionism as a thesis about where warrant *comes from* (testimony transmitting rather than generating it) versus as a norm for how hearers ought to manage their beliefs. If it's only the metaphysical thesis, what's the cash value — does it leave us with anything to say to a student who believes everything she reads?

And please, no questions of the form so how does your view relate to Hume's: she gets that every single time and it derails into twenty minutes of exegesis. Nothing that requires her to summarize literature or name who argued what either — I want everything keyed to the actual machinery of the position, not to who is on which side of the debate.
