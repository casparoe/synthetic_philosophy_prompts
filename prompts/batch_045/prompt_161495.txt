I'm a retired biologist (35 years of insect flight biomechanics, mostly) who's drifted into philosophy since I stopped teaching, and my old university has a small 'senior scholar' scheme that lets retirees run a one-year independent project with a faculty mentor. They want a three-page proposal and I need you to turn my half-formed idea into one.

Here's the idea. Everyone talks as if Darwin's inversion and Turing's inversion are the same move run twice: first on the appearance of design in organisms, then on the appearance of understanding in minds. I dont think they are the same move. Darwin's swaps the explanation while leaving the thing to be explained untouched — the appearance of purpose is real, the cause is different from what it looks like. The Turing one, at least in the version that gets used on minds, seems to need something stronger: not just that comprehension is caused by competence, but that it is made of competences. And as far as I can tell nobody says explicitly which of the two they are doing.

So the question I want to propose: split comprehension into three grades — bare competence; monitored competence, where a system can reliably tell whether it just succeeded; and portable competence, where a system can take what it does and redeploy it, teach it, debug it, apply it somewhere else. Which of these grades can be built out of the grade below without comprehension sneaking back in, and at which grade does the building stop working? My guess going in is that grade two comes cheap out of grade one and that grade three is where the inversion either goes through or breaks, but I want the proposal to say what would settle it either way, not just announce my hunch.

For method: I'd rather do three worked cases than wade through the general literature — one biological, one control system from my own old field that is plainly competent and plainly not representing anything, one from current machine systems — plus the armchair work of sharpening the three grades. Say why that beats the obvious alternatives, because I'll be asked: a proper survey of the consciousness literature would be a second career, and arguing head-on about whether today's large models understand anything looks to me like the same intuition pump going round forever.

Also give me: what a finished result would look like, what would count as the project failing rather than just succeeding boringly, and an honest assessment of scope — one year, part time, me alone with a mentor for an hour a week. No citations or reading lists please, I'll get those from my mentor. And write it so a committee of philosophers who've never met me can tell whether this is a real project or a retiree loose in the library.
