I'm building an agent runtime and I want to know whether the architecture I've drawn on the whiteboard is secretly a theory of consciousness, or whether I've just reinvented one half of somebody else's distinction. Here's the design (call it Kestrel):

- About twenty modules: some perception things, a vector memory, several action proposers, a planner. They share no memory and can't call each other directly. The only channel is one totally-ordered log; modules publish events and subscribe to topics.
- Writes go through a quorum. An event counts as "global" once a majority of modules have acked it. Before that it sits in the publishing module's local buffer and can be evicted under memory pressure — it may never reach the log at all.
- The log is bounded. Under load I sample: events below a priority score get dropped instead of committed, and the producing module is never notified.
- There's a "narrator" service that reads a sampled subset of the log and generates the answer you'd get if you asked the runtime "what's going on, what did you notice, why did you pick that action." It's lossy and it runs a couple hundred milliseconds behind the bus.
- There's also an audit sink: full fidelity, every event, but write-only during runtime. Nothing subscribes to it. The only reader is me, hours later, during a post-mortem. I've started calling these orphan events — published, durable, zero consumers.

What I want from you: translate the access vs. phenomenal consciousness debate, and what global workspace theory is actually claiming, into this vocabulary as carefully as you can.
