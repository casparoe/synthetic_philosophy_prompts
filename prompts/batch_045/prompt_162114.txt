I'm a journalist working on a feature about how consciousness gets studied in the lab these days, and I want to get one methodological dispute straight before I file. A long magazine profile I read recently described Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology roughly like this:

Dennett's heterophenomenology is the proposal that a science of consciousness should confine itself to third-person evidence: what subjects say about their experience, plus everything else an experimenter can measure about them and their surroundings. The investigator treats a subject's report the way an anthropologist treats an informant's account of a ritual — as material to be recorded and then interpreted alongside neural and behavioural data — deliberately suspending judgement on whether the subject is right. What gets reconstructed from the reports is not the subject's experience itself but the subject's "heterophenomenological world": the world as the subject takes it to be, populated by whatever items they claim to encounter there, including such things as the afterimage they say they are currently having. Because the truth of the reports is suspended rather than granted, the method carries a metaphysical commitment: there is no further fact of the matter about what a subject's experience is really like beyond what shows up in their reports and behaviour, which is why Dennett concludes that qualia, in the traditional sense, do not exist. The method is thus a liberalised behaviourism — verbal reports simply count as one more variety of behaviour — and it follows that any creature producing exactly the reports and behaviour of a conscious human being would be conscious, with nothing further to ask. The investigator's own introspection is likewise supposed to drop out, the entire point being to take first-person data out of the science. And where a subject's report collides with their own performance, as when blindsight patients insist they see nothing while guessing at well above chance, the report has to win, since it is the only route to experience there is.

I don't know this literature well enough to know what to trust. Two things make me suspicious. First, I'd have thought the methodological decision not to grant the truth of a report is compatible with holding that there is a truth of the matter about the subject's experience that the report may miss; but Dennett does deny that qualia exist, so perhaps the profile is right to run them together. Second, I've been told that Dennett himself resists the behaviourism label, which makes me doubt that clause even though the rest of the paragraph reads as though it were written by someone who knows the material.

There is also a complication from my own reporting. Several labs now try to read the contents of experience out of brain activity under so-called no-report conditions, in which subjects are given a task but never asked to report anything; as I understand it, the decoding models are trained on trials in which subjects did report, and then applied to trials in which they do not. A neuroscientist I interviewed put the problem to me like this: if reports are the only bridge to experience, then every experiment that drops the report has stopped studying consciousness, and every experiment that keeps it is studying the report rather than the consciousness. That sounds like a genuine dilemma, but also like something a defender of heterophenomenology would refuse to accept, and I cannot tell whether the no-report work is a counterexample to the method or merely an application of it.

Could you do two things? First, work through the profile's description clause by clause and tell me what a philosopher sympathetic to Dennett's project would accept, what they would wince at as a simplification or a term used loosely, and what they would call simply wrong — and where the profile presents a substantive thesis as though it followed from the method alone, identify the further commitment it actually depends on. Second, tell me whether my interviewee's dilemma holds, and specifically whether the fact that no-report decoders are calibrated on report-labelled trials settles the question in one direction or the other, or whether the calibration point leaves the interesting part of the objection standing.

Please answer in British English, and write at the level of a well-edited magazine piece: gloss any technical term you need, but don't flatten the distinctions that are doing the work.
