I've got to give the eight-minute speech at the opening of the Aldermere tidal barrier next Saturday — the one with the plaque. My dad's firm designed the gates thirty-odd years ago and he never stopped going on about it, so the family think it's a lovely full circle that I'm the one speaking; I inhereted his job, more or less, after he died, and now I'm the person who signs off the annual exceedance numbers. In the room will be: the engineers who actually built the thing, about sixty residents of the estate that went under in the floods of my childhood (some of them still angry, a couple of them friends of my parents', one of whom will definitely buttonhole me afterwards), two people from the reinsurance syndicate that underwrote a chunk of it, the local MP who will say something about protecting the community for generations, and my own teenage kids, who think the whole thing is a wasted afternoon.

Here's my problem, and it isn't stage fright. Everything a speech like this naturally wants to say about risk is something I don't believe, or believe only in a form I can't say out loud to sixty people who lost their ground floors. If I say "the barrier takes the annual chance of flooding from one-in-seventy-five to one-in-five-hundred", I'm quoting a long-run frequency in a series of years that aren't like each other — the sea level's rising, the catchment's changed, the whole point is that the series isn't stationary — and anyway nobody in that room wants to know about an imaginary run of Aldermeres, they want to know about this barrier, this winter, their house. If I say "one-in-five-hundred is how confident we are", I've turned my team's careful uncertainty into a mood, and the syndicate will hear that as: it's a price, and prices are negotiable. If I say "the barrier will keep you safe", I've said something that appears nowhere in the model, and my dad used to tear strips off anyone who did that. What I actually think — and I've never managed to say it in a room like this one — is that the number is a propensity: a real feature, out there, of the barrier-plus-estuary-plus-storm-climate set-up, the way a weighted die has a tendency to land six, which is neither the frequency with which it lands six nor how sure I am that it will. My old professor had a discipline for this that I've carried around ever since: you never get to utter a probability without saying what set-up it is a property of — chance of what, under what conditions, over what window — and he reckoned most public fights about risk are somebody quietly swapping the set-up in the middle of a sentence.

So write me the speech. It has to do genuine work: explain to the engineers and to a woman whose photo album went under water in the same flood, what kind of thing the number on the plaque is, and what it isn't, and let the engineers hear that I know propensities are single-case while also letting the residents hear why that doesn't get them an answer about their own front room. Don't sneak in a promise, don't make risk into a feeling, and don't hand the insurers a way out — but don't turn it into a scolding either, and don't let me imply that my dad's life's work was a number I can't cash out. Two more things I'd like you to respect: I must not use the phrase "once-in-a-lifetime", since that's precisely the confusion, and I must not talk as though the chance of a storm surge given a flood is interchangeable with the chance of a flood given a storm surge — I know those come apart for propensities and I'd like the speech not to depend on confusing them, even in plain English. Warm rather than lecture-y, funny at most once, roughly eight minutes spoken, and if there's a point where the honest thing is "I don't know", put it in rather than papering over it. British English throughout, please.
