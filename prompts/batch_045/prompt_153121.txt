I'm trying to work out what I actually think about moral worth — when a right action actually has it — by going with my gut on cases rather than by reading around the literature.

- Shopkeeper who gives correct change, and the only reason is that cheating would be bad for business: no worth, even though the action's right.
- Kid raised to believe handing over a runaway is a duty, who helps the runaway anyway out of pity while thinking he's doing something wrong: worth, and I'm tempted to say more worth than the person who does it calmly from a sense of duty.
- Donor who gives only because a mate nagged him and he feels guilty: no worth at the time. But if the same guy is still giving five years later out of genuine concern, I want to give those early gifts some worth retroactivly. Meanwhile the person who gives for years out of real concern and then turns bitter and stops caring: I don't want to take worth away from the earlier gifts. I know that's asymmetric and I cant say why.
- Surgeon who operates partly to impress her boss and would have done it for that alone, though she also takes it to matter that the patient lives: some worth, but less.
- Man who visits his friend in hospital mainly because it's what you do, not out of affection: worth, but something's off in a way the runaway case doesn't have — and I don't think "off" means less worth.
- Guy who gives to whichever charity a dart lands on, and it happens to be an effective one: less credit than someone who checked, but no blame; and if the dart had landed on a dud I'd blame him less than someone who didn't care at all.

My stab: an action's worth tracks how robustly the good reason would still have moved you in nearby cases where the selfish or easy reasons are taken away — call it robustness or whatever you like. Test that against my verdicts and repair it: does one version get them all, and if not which verdict has to go? Which clause is doing the work for which case, and is worth a matter of degree or all-or-nothing on your account? Then tell me what your principle says about two cases I havn't listed: an AI assistant that refuses a harmful request only because it was fine-tuned on polite refusals, and someone who does the right thing for a reason they'd disavow if they stopped to think about it.
