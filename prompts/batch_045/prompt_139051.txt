I've been chewing on Pascal's mugging again and I want to stop doing it in the abstract. Take a real case: the runup to the LHC switching on around 2008, when people were saying it might make micro black holes or strangelets and eat the planet. (There was at least one lawsuit in US federal court trying to enjoin startup — I think it got tossed without ever ruling on the physics, but please don't hang anything on the procedural stuff, I don't remember it well and it's not the interesting part.) The physics community's answer, as I remember it, was basically that cosmic rays with way more energy than anything we can build have been hitting the Earth and the Moon and much denser objects for billions of years without anything blowing up, so whatever the probability is, it's constrained by the fact that this experiment has already been run a colossal number of times.

My working definition of the mugging, which I suspect is too broad: tiny probability times astronomically huge payoff swamps the certain cost, so hand over the wallet. Fix that if it needs fixing — I think something about who controls the payoff and how cheap the claim is to make is doing work I'm not capturing.

Then the actual ask, in both directions. Map the case: who the players were (the objectors, CERN and the physicists, the funders, and everyone else who had no seat at the table but ate the risk), what the real option set was (proceed, delay, run at lower energy first, commission more safety work, shut it down), what the payoffs are on each branch, and what counted as evidence. And say where the mugging lens explains what actually happened vs. where it misdescribes it — my pet theory is that the interesting variable isn't the size of the numbers at all but whether there's a way of getting traction on the probability that doesn't route through the claim itself, so the LHC thing was never really a mugging and a stranger with a napkin is. Is that right? Can't the mugger just scale their number until even a cosmic-ray-anchored 10^-15 loses?

Then what the case does to the theory. Three things I've been poking at, use them or kill them:

1. Bond-posting. What actually makes the classic mugging nasty isn't that the claim is unverifiable, it's that the mugger can't post a bond — there's no payment up front or payout later that would let their real credence show up in anything you can observe. If the claim were contractible you'd just say put your money where your mouth is and the whole thing dissolves into ordinary mechanism design. So is the mugging secretly a special case of decision problems where no price can be posted? And if so, which of the usual fixes (bounded utility, probability floors, leverage penalties, just shut up and multiply) are even aimed at the real thing?

2. Insider vs stranger. The 1942 worry that the Trinity test might ignite the atmosphere had the same shape, and it came from the people building the thing. Does the standard story treat that differently from a guy on a street corner, and should it? My guess is that the ignore-strangers move is load-bearing in the classic setup in a way nobody admits, and that it's the wrong axis to be leaning on.

3. Correlated muggings. A million agents getting structurally identical claims: paying is fine for each and catastrophic in aggregate, and paying also selects for more mugging. How much of the one-guy-on-a-corner analysis survives once you add the population and the dynamc?

Last thing, the one I actually care about: whatever you land on, does it leave the astronomical-stakes argument for working on x-risk standing? Every move I can think of for killing the mugging seems to blunt longtermism too, and I want to know if that's a real tension or if I'm conflating two things that just look alike. And please say explicitly where the theory is silent rather than making it talk — no reading lists, I want the analysis.
