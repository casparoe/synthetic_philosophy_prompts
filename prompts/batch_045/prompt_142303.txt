I'm in the final year of a PhD in philosophy, working in social epistemology with enough formal training to handle finite probability spaces, partitions, and simple fixed-point arguments, but I am not a microeconomic theorist. My department awards a nine-month dissertation-completion fellowship, and I need a two-page proposal for it.

Here is the dialectic as I understand it, and the half-formed idea I want turned into a real project. The agreement result says that agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree: common knowledge of their posteriors forces those posteriors to coincide, and from this one gets the no-trade conclusion that purely informational differences cannot by themselves motivate a trade. Since actual markets exhibit large and persistent volume, something in the assumptions has to give, and the dominant move in the debate has been to give up the common prior. My dissatisfaction is that this move is unfalsifiable: for any observed volume one can always posit priors that would generate it, so the argument settles into a standoff between people who find heterogeneous priors plausible and people who do not, with no conceptual or empirical purchase. The route I want to try instead is to keep the common prior and the common knowledge of posteriors intact and give up something nobody seems to treat as an assumption at all, namely that agents can communicate their posteriors to arbitrary precision. In practice announcements are coarse ('likely', 'nearly certain', 'a toss-up'), and where a price rather than a sentence carries the information the exchange imposes a minimum increment, so the price channel rounds as well. Once posteriors are reported on a finite grid, the agreement argument can at best deliver agreement up to one grid step, and I would conjecture that the residual gap supports a positive expected gain from trade that vanishes as the grid is refined.

What I want sharpened into a proposal: a precise statement of the question, with the finite model specified well enough that the question has a determinate answer; why the standard positions leave it open; why attacking the precision assumption is a better bet than attacking the common prior; a month-by-month plan for nine months; the range of possible conclusions and what would decide among them; and an honest account of what would count as the project failing. Two things I specifically want the proposal to address rather than smooth over. First, my suspicion that the interesting quantity is not the size of the residual gap but its location: if coarsening is uniform in probability the residual disagreement concentrates in the middle of the distribution, whereas the reporting practice people actually seem to have is fine near certainty and coarse in the middle, which would relocate the gap to the region where an asset's payoff is least sensitive, or possibly the reverse. I cannot tell which, and the proposal should treat it as a lemma to be established rather than a premise. Second, whether the result would support a diagnostic: my thought is that granularity-driven trade should shrink when the channel is enriched and should be roughly insensitive to the size of the stakes, while prior-driven trade should scale with the stakes and be insensitive to the channel, so that one could in principle tell the two explanations apart instead of merely redescribing observed volume. Please don't give me a bibliography; I will add citations myself. And if the honest verdict is that this is not tractable in nine months, or that the central bound is something any theorist would write down in an afternoon, put that in the risk section rather than quietly scaling the project down.
