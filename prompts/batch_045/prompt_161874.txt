I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on democratic theory, and I've been asked to lead one 80-minute session for about eighteen classmates on the boundary problem — the difficulty that any vote presupposes a settled answer to who is entitled to vote, while every proposed answer to that seems itself to have been settled by somebody, and so on down. I want them to run into it rather than hear it from me: no lecture up front, and the name of the problem shouldn't be said until the debrief.

My rough plan, which I'd like you to design properly or replace: the group has to make one genuinely binding but small decision (how we use the final seminar, plus roughly twenty-five euros of leftover course money for food), and before that they must decide who counts, by a vote held under a franchise I assign arbitrarily and in advance — say, only students whose surnames fall in the first half of the alphabet, or only those dealt a marked card on arrival. They should then propose and vote on a principled rule of their own and only discover afterwards that any rule they accept has to be applied to the choice of the rule. Partway through I'll hand them a second, wider decision — one that would bind the whole department or next year's cohort — so that they have to recompute the franchise from scratch and notice that the demos seems to shift with the decision rather than being a fixed group of people.

One principle of my own that I'd like the activity to put pressure on: membership graded by whether the decision changes what a person can do, the set of options available to them, rather than by how well off they end up. I want that tested against the two familiar ones — everyone whose interests are affected, and everyone who will be subject to the decision's enforcement — including the cases where it diverges from them.
