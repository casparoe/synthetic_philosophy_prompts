I am a retired control engineer and systems physiologist, and for the past four years I have sat as external examiner for a final-year undergraduate option in philosophy of mind. Philosophy is a late and largely self-taught interest of mine, so please do not assume I know the literature's shorthand: if you use a term of art in anything addressed to me, define it. The convener has asked me to set one essay question this year on the nature of the emotions, and I would like your help drafting it.

The constraints are these. The paper is closed-book and unseen; the question is to be answered in a single hour; it is marked out of twenty. It must be self-contained: I do not want a question whose answer depends on knowing who said what, or on being able to cite anything, since my students will have no access to texts in the exam room. The topic is the familiar three-way division — emotions as judgements or judgement-like states with correctness conditions; emotions as perceptions, or perceptual presentations of evaluative properties; emotions as feelings, that is, as bodily or affective states. What I want is a question that cannot be answered by laying out that taxonomy, attaching a label to each box, and announcing a verdict.

My own instinct, formed over a career spent around instruments, is that the three proposals may not be genuine rivals at all. I keep wanting to compare an emotion to a measurement. There is a quantity the device is meant to track; a physical change in the device itself by which it tracks that quantity; a readout in which the value is presented to a user; and a verdict or report that the user might then endorse, act on, or override. All four of these can be discussed without contradiction, and it is not obvious to me that each of the three theories is doing more than claiming that one of the four is the whole story. So I would like the question to press on whether that comparison is genuinely clarifying or merely relabels the dispute — and, if it is clarifying, whether it reveals the dispute to be to some extent verbal.

In particular, I want candidates to be made to do the following.

First: say what each of the three families of theory is in the business of explaining, and locate the point at which — if at all — they genuinely conflict rather than talking past one another.

Second: deal with correctness conditions, using two cases. The ordinary recalcitrant case, where someone sincerely judges that flying is far safer than the drive to the airport, flies without hesitation, and is nonetheless afraid: which theory is embarrassed here, and can the embarrassment be dissolved by distinguishing the quantity the emotion tracks from the quantity the agent judges? And a stipulated case of systematic mis-calibration: consider a creature whose fear response fires reliably in the presence of some feature F, where F was a dependable proxy for danger in the environment in which the response was tuned, but is only weakly correlated with danger in the environment the creature now occupies. In virtue of what, if anything, is the response a misfire? What is it a misfire about? Which of the three theories has the resources to say, and which is forced to concede that nothing is going wrong at all?

Third: deal with a stipulated dissociation pair. Subject P shows the full bodily and behavioural signature of fear, reliably discriminates dangerous situations from safe ones, and plans and acts accordingly, but reports no felt valence whatever and denies being afraid. Subject Q has episodes of the same bodily signature in situations she herself classifies as safe, forms no new intention and changes no plan, and reports the episodes as not being about anything. Does P have emotions? Does Q? For each of the three theories, say whether the answer it gives is a cost to be explained away or an entailment to be welcomed — and then say what follows for the question whether a creature without concepts, a rat or an infant, could have emotions at all.

Please deliver the following.

1. The question as it would appear on the paper: self-contained, including the stipulated cases, in no more than three hundred words.

2. Two or three sentences, for me rather than for the candidates, on what the question is designed to discriminate — what the merely competent answer does that the first-class answer does not.

3. A model answer of about seven hundred words, written in the voice of a strong first-class script rather than as a summary of a debate. It should take a determinate position: are the three theories rivals; is the dispute partly verbal; and if the measurement comparison is on the right lines, which of its four elements is indispensable to any adequate account of emotion and which is optional. Please also flag, in a sentence or two after the model answer, any place where the model answer depends on a contested rather than a neutral characterisation of one of the three views.

4. A marking scheme, out of twenty, as a table with exactly these four columns:

   - Criterion — a short label for what is being rewarded.
   - Marks — the marks available for that criterion; the column must sum to twenty.
   - First-class move — the specific thing an answer must do to earn full marks here.
   - Failure mode — the particular mistake or evasion this criterion exists to penalise. I want these to be real mistakes a student would actually make, not generic remarks about irrelevance or lack of clarity.

5. A second table, on where the model answer is most likely to be wrong, with exactly these four columns:

   - Load-bearing claim — the premise in the model answer that the verdict depends on.
   - Strongest objection — the best argument a well-prepared candidate could mount against that premise.
   - Defeater — what the candidate would have to establish for that objection actually to succeed, stated as a condition: this is the consideration that would, if made out, change the model answer's verdict.
   - Credit — how many marks a genuinely successful objection of this kind should attract, and whether that credit should be capped, with a one-line reason.

If you judge that the three requirements above cannot be done justice in an hour of examination writing, say so at the end and tell me which you would drop and why.
