Analyze the July Crisis of 1914 as a commitment race. By that I mean a situation in which several parties each face pressure to be the first to make an irreversible, observable move — here, an order for general mobilization — because once one party has visibly locked itself in, everyone else is reduced to accommodating or to fighting at a disadvantage, and because mobilization was slow, hard to recall, and ran on railway timetables, delay was itself a cost.

Hold these facts fixed: Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia, issued with a German assurance of support; Serbia's near-complete acceptance; the Austrian declaration of war; Russia's shift from a partial mobilization directed only at Austria-Hungary to a general one; the German demand that it be halted, followed by German declarations of war on Russia and on France; and the German plan's requirement that France be struck first, through Belgium, so that a war with Russia carried a war with France along with it.

What I want is the mapping and then the audit. Who exactly were the players, and which of the parties were players rather than pieces? What counted as a move, and what counted as the others finding out that a move had been made? Where did the race actually run — in cabinets, in railway schedules, or in each government's beliefs about the others' schedules? Then: what the commitment-race model explains about July 1914, what it misses or describes badly, whether any state had a move that the model says was available but that no one in 1914 appears to have contemplated, and whether the timetables made the outcome overdetermined regardless of what anyone decided. Close by asking what the case does to the model: does it confirm it, or does it expose a missing distinction — for instance, between a commitment that has actually been made and one that the other side merely believes has been made?
