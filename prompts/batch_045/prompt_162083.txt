I work on research policy in a system that funds and regulates animal experiments in neuroscience, and there is an inconsistency in our practice that I have never been able to get straight. The practice is roughly this: protocols must be prospectively assigned a severity band and justified by expected benefit to humans, and the entire preclinical pipeline — analgesia testing, anxiety and depression models, behavioural neuroscience generally — runs on inferences from rodents, and sometimes from zebrafish or fruit flies, to human minds. Within the same institutional framework there are also sharp lines: a mouse's pain behaviour is read as evidence of pain, attributions of anything like belief or recollection to a fish or an insect are treated as far more doubtful, the question whether a cerebral organoid or a simulation has mental states at all is treated as not worth asking, and — the part that puzzles me most — the more a result depends on a specific mechanism, the more reviewers insist on identifying the conserved circuit and its molecular details, as though reproducing the causal profile were insufficient.

What I want is the implicit philosophy of mind reconstructed. My own guess is that the practice is functionalist, and committed to something like multiple realizability: the same mental state can be realized in very different neural hardware, so a rat can be in pain or be anxious, and what does the evidential work is causal role and behaviour rather than substrate. But I want you to be exact about whether the practice strictly presupposes that or whether something weaker would license it — for example a view on which mental states are individuated partly by their realizing mechanisms, or a resemblance view on which phylogenetic and structural nearness rather than functional sameness carries the evidential weight. In particular, does the severity side of the practice, which reads affective states off behavioural proxies including coded facial expressions even in animals we regard as distant from us, pull against the mechanism-hunting side, which discounts a behavioural match unless the underlying circuit is homologous? Is the institution saying two inconsistent things, or is there one theory on which both sides make sense?

To sharpen it, consider a reform I have been arguing about with colleagues, which I will call the homology rule: a finding in a non-human model counts as evidence about human psychology only if the mechanism subserving it has been identified and shown to be homologous to the human mechanism, and a match in input-output profile or causal role carries no evidential weight on its own. Is that rule an application of the practice's own logic, or a betrayal of it? My sense is that it is what an identity theorist, or anyone who thinks realization details are partly constitutive of mental-state identity, should want — and that adopting it would push the pipeline toward human-based preparations such as organoids, microphysiological systems, and computational models, at which point the practice has to say whether what it has built is a model of a mind or a mind, and whether it has any resources for deciding.

Then tell me what would have to change under the alternatives. If we were straightforward identity theorists about the mental and the neural, what would we have to stop doing? If we were functionalists all the way down — applied consistently to insects, to organoids, and to artificial systems as the functional profile alone dictates — what would we have to start doing? And which parts of the current practice are genuinely underdetermined, such that functionalists, identity theorists, and intermediate views would all license them, so that they are not evidence of any commitment at all?

Finally, on epistemic status: separate what is settled or close to unanimous among philosophers who work on this — that multiple realizability is coherent and is a standing problem for type-identity theories, say — from what is contested, such as whether our actual everyday and institutional practice of attributing mental states to animals confirms functionalism, whether the best functionalist accounts can accommodate the pull of the homology intuition, or whether the appeal to homology should be understood as an evidential rather than a metaphysical constraint. Mark where you are giving me your own view rather than the consensus, and mark where the disagreement bottoms out in empirical questions about neural conservation that neither of us can settle here.
