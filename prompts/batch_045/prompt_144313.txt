I teach a twelve-week upper-level seminar on concepts and categorization. The students are third- and fourth-years, roughly half philosophy majors and half cognitive science; they have already worked through the classical theory, prototype accounts, and exemplar accounts on the usual natural-kind-ish cases (water, gold, tiger). The session I need planned is the fifth, eighty minutes, seminar table and whiteboard, no projector — anything I use has to be drawable by hand in a few strokes. It is the pivot of the course: the point where we move from natural kinds to artifacts and notations, and I want to spend the whole hour on one question — what makes every 'a' an 'a'? — the question behind Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project. Please do not explain the question to me; I want a plan for teaching it.

What I want from you:

1. The order in which the components have to be introduced, with the dependency made explicit: what has to be in place before the question even looks like a question to them, and which step collapses if an earlier one is skipped or reversed. In particular, tell me whether the three-way distinction between the letter-type, the typeface or hand, and the individual variant should be handed to them at the start as vocabulary or extracted from them at the end, and what is lost each way. The claim I want them to be able to state by the end is that sameness of letter across its guises is neither sameness of intrinsic shape-properties nor a matter of stipulation, and that style is the name of what varies while letter-identity does not — but tell me if that is the wrong thing to aim at in eighty minutes.

2. The one example to carry the session, what it must make vivid, and what it must keep out of the way. My candidate is a typeface — call it Rotunda — in which every lowercase glyph is the shape standardly used for the next letter of the alphabet, so that a page set in Rotunda looks like entirely ordinary, well-made text and cannot be read. I have also considered a face in which only the 'a' and 'o' shapes are exchanged and everything else is left alone, and a set of real 'a's from a serif face, a geometric sans, a blackletter, and a shaky hand. Which of these should carry it, or is there a better one? I suspect the failure mode is an example that lets them say it is obviously a code and stop thinking in the first two minutes.

3. The misconceptions, the order in which they will actually arise, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. The two I am confident about are the template picture — they will think sameness of letter is sameness of some stripped-down shape — and the shrug that since writing is a convention there is nothing more to say. I would guess there are three or four more, including one about the relation between being an 'a' and being read as an 'a', and one specific to the cognitive scientists in the room, who will reach for a learned similarity space and think the philosophical question dissolves.

4. What to leave out at this level and say out loud that I am leaving out. I do not want to describe the Letter Spirit program's own machinery, and I do not want to reopen the general question of whether concepts are prototypes or theories. Tell me what else I should refuse to explain.

5. A check that discriminates. At the end I would like to put a script I have invented in front of them: five letters, of which one is a small closed round bowl with a vertical stroke descending from the right of the bowl and a short horizontal bar crossing the stroke about two-thirds of the way down; a second is the same figure without the bar; a third has the bowl open at the lower left; a fourth has the bowl replaced by a triangle. Then a fifth figure — the first one, with the bowl broken and the bar reduced to a stub — and the question: which letter is it? Tell me what else the set has to contain for that question to have a determinate answer rather than being a matter of taste, and what an answer would have to cite in order to count as having got the idea rather than having been told it.

Two last things. Mark the step in your sequence that you are least confident about, and the point where the plan depends on a substantive claim about perception or about conventions that you would expect a colleague of mine to reject. And if something in my framing is confused — if the distinction I want them to end up with is less stable than I am assuming, if the letterform case cannot bear the weight I am putting on it, or if the hour cannot hold what I have asked for — say so plainly instead of politely building me a plan around it, and tell me which of my stipulations to drop.
