Hi — I'm 71, retired about four years now. Before that I spent some thirty-five years in earthquake seismology, mostly at a national survey: instrument networks first, then catalogues and statistics, that sort of thing. Since stopping I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the public library — six of us, a retired lawyer, a couple of teachers, and one PhD student who more or less runs it — and this term we're crawling through Popper's Poverty of Historicism. The book is dedicated, so the student told us, to people who fell victim to the belief in inexorable laws of historical destiny, and it argues that you can't forecast the course of history, largely because you can't know today what people will know tomorrow.

Last week I stuck my neck out. I said that in my own field most of the things we happily call laws are nothing like the exceptionless universals the group seems to assume a law has to be. The Gutenberg–Richter relation is my stock example: the count of earthquakes falls off as a power of their size. It's a regularity about populations of events, not about any individual quake; it won't tell you when the next one comes; it holds only as long as the background conditions hold — same tectonic setting, roughly the same heat flux driving the plates — and change those and the relation changes with them. Nobody concludes that seismology therefor has no laws. So, I suggested, maybe there could be laws of history in that same sense: regularities over ensembles and within a regime — a given mode of production, or a given level of energy capture — that rule some futures out without picking out any single one. Things like how long a political arrangement tends to last before it comes apart, how the sizes of the biggest cities spread out, how often big wars come per century. You could even imagine the regularity being one of scaling across periods rather than identity between them, so that history looks statistically similar at different scales, the way a power law has no characteristic event size.

The PhD student shut this down with a line I've since heard twice more: history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes. Everyone nodded as if that ended it. Maybe it does, but I can't tell what it was supposed to mean, and it seemed to be doing two different jobs at once — telling me my statistical regualrities aren't laws, and telling us that there are no laws of history full stop.

So: can you set out, as a list I can take to the next meeting, the different precise claims that one-liner might be making, from the trivially true to the substantive? Which are platitudes, which are actually defensible, which are false or just confused? And — the bit I care about — which of them, if any, would really sink the kind of law I was proposing, and which leave it untouched? I'd also like to know where the line sits relative to Popper's own argument: my hunch is that his objection is about forecasting a unique process, and about trends not being laws, which is narrower than what the rhyme line is being used for, but I may be reading him wrong.
