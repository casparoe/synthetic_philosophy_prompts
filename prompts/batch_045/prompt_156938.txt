I teach an upper-level seminar on the unity of consciousness, and I am writing this year's take-home final. I need one essay question on the split-brain evidence that cannot be answered by rehearsing the familiar standoff — either the commissurotomy splits one subject into two, or it merely divides a single subject's access to its own states — and that instead forces students to do the disambiguating work themselves.

Build the question around a case they have not seen in the readings, which I would like you to compose. Call the patient N. She is several years post-callosotomy and shows the standard picture: she cannot name what is presented to her left visual field, her left hand sorts and matches objects she cannot name, and so on. Two features of her case are unusual, and they pull in opposite directions. First, she acquires novel bimanual skills — say a two-handed piano passage she has never practised — at close to normal speed, which sits badly with two independent centres of motor control. Second, on trials where a written instruction delivered to her right hemisphere conflicts with a spoken instruction to her left, her two hands each carry out their own instruction, her physiological response registers conflict (skin conductance, facial affect), and yet her verbal report mentions only the spoken instruction, confabulates a rationale for what her left hand did, and registers no conflict at all.

The question should require students to separate at least three claims that the two-minds-in-one-skull slogan runs together — that there are two subjects of experience; that there is one subject whose experiential contents are not all co-conscious; that there are two centres of agency sharing no access — and to say which, if any, N's case supports, and why the case gives evidence in both directions at once. Then require the crux: whether the question how many subjects is well posed here, what evidence could in principle settle it, and why N's own report cannot settle it either way. Finish by making them hold their verdict steady across the operation: if they say two subjects, they must say why the pre-operative patient was not already two, and if they say one, they must say what the operation actually changed.

Deliverables: the question as I would hand it to students (90 minutes, 800 words maximum); a model answer of about 600 words; and a marking scheme of five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, each with a one-line note on the mistake strong students typically make on that point. The model answer must commit: it should take a definite position on whether the counting question is well posed and defend that position with reasons, rather than surveying the options and leaving the verdict open. No citations, reading lists, or references to named authors anywhere in the packet — the answer has to stand on argument alone.
