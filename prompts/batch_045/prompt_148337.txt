The set-up. A rich guy who likes these puzzles invited 100 people to a contest. Each invitee can push a button once within 24 hours, there's no communication between them, and everyone got the same letter saying all 100 were picked because they've read the same Hofstadter piece on superrationality. If exactly one person pushes, that person gets $1,000,000. If two or more push, the prize is void and nobody gets anything at all. If nobody pushes, everyone gets $10,000 — the organiser fixed the consolation at exactly one hundredth of the prize, which is the bit that set the two of them off. Sam and Yuri both got letters; I didn't, so I've got no stake in it.

Move 1, Sam: "The consolation settles it — everyone should just not push. If all 100 of us roll a hundred-sided die and push on a 1, each of us is worth something like seventy-three hundred dollars once you count the runs where nobody's die lands and we still collect the ten grand. Ten thousand for certain beats that, so refraining is the superrational answer and there's no need for dice at all."

Move 2, Yuri: "Maybe, but notice you're leaning on the consolation being exactly a hundredth of the prize: at that level the margin is basically nothing, since pushing with some tiny probability instead of zero costs you pennies. And the bigger problem is that pushing weakly dominates — if nobody else pushes, pushing is a million dollars instead of ten thousand, and in every other case pushing and not pushing both get you zero. So a causal decision theorist just pushes."

Move 3, Sam: "Weak dominance is exactly the reasoning these puzzles were built to break: in the original version, with no consolation, the same argument tells everyone to push and everyone walks away with nothing, which is plainly not the superrational answer. And the organiser told us all 100 read the same piece, so my button isn't independent of yours in the way your dominance argument needs it to be."

Move 4, Yuri: "Knowing we read the same essay makes your push evidence about mine, not a cause of it, and evidence about what other people will do doesn't stop me from taking a free million once I'm standing at the button. Besides, the classic superrational fix is everybody rolling dice — so your side needs my decision to be perfectly correlated with yours and needs those dice to be independent, and I don't think you get to have both."
