I am putting together a diagnostic handout for a final-year undergraduate seminar in metaethics, and I need a decision tree that sorts students into positions on what, if anything, follows from a set of descriptive premises to a normative conclusion. The handout is meant to disentangle three things that textbooks routinely run together: Hume's point about the move from is to ought, Moore's charge of the naturalistic fallacy, and the open-question argument. I want the instrument to treat these as three distinct claims, and to be built from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a list of positions with authors attached.

Constraints on the instrument:

Begin from a commitment every student in the room already shares — that some particular first-order moral judgment is true (say, that torturing a child for amusement is wrong). No node may ask whether moral facts exist or invite the respondent to self-classify; every node must be a question about a case or about the logic of a derivation, phrased so that an intelligent student with no metaethics background could answer it without knowing where it leads.

Use at most ten branch nodes and produce between nine and eleven leaves. The leaves must include: non-cognitivist projectivism; quasi-realist expressivism; error theory; reductive or analytic ethical naturalism; non-reductive (synthetic, a posteriori) ethical naturalism; Moorean non-naturalism; neo-Aristotelian naturalism built on the evaluation of living things against their life-form; a constructivism that grounds reasons in a practical standpoint or in what the agent values; and a view on which the gap is only apparent because some descriptive facts are already partly normative (institutional facts, or the norms internal to a practice or a function).

The splitting work should be done by distinctions like these, though you may add or substitute ones that do better: whether any 'ought' is categorical or whether all of them are relative to some end, desire, or standpoint the agent might lack; whether normative predicates stand for properties at all; whether normative properties are identical with, constituted by, or merely strongly supervenient on descriptive ones; whether a bridge premise must be analytic and a priori or may be synthetic and a posteriori; whether a derivation is licensed by a constitutive standard internal to an artifact, a role, an institution, or a species; whether the gap is logical, semantic, or merely epistemic; and whether naturalness, as opposed to being a descriptive property, is what is supposed to do the offending.

To keep the tree honest, it must be tested against six short probe derivations that I will hand out alongside it. Each has purely descriptive premises as written and a normative conclusion:

1. This blade was designed and marketed for slicing bread; it shreds the crumb. Therefore it is a bad bread knife.
2. Amara said the words I promise to pay you Friday, in a setting where promising is an established practice. Therefore she ought to pay you on Friday, other things being equal.
3. Viren wants to be in Leeds by six; the last train that arrives before then departs at four. Therefore he should take the four o'clock train.
4. Human offspring are born helpless, acquire language only from sustained interaction, and fare badly without years of care from adults. Therefore parents ought to care for their infants.
5. No society could persist in which sworn testimony carried no expectation of truthfulness, and every known society has some such expectation. Therefore one ought not to lie under oath.
6. This procedure would inflict prolonged conscious suffering on a being that has done nothing and will receive no compensating benefit, and no one desires it for its own sake. Therefore it is wrong to perform it.

For each probe, say which node of your tree it reaches, whether the derivation is treated as valid, as a valid enthymeme hiding a premise the view endorses, or as invalid, and why. Each leaf should come with a note listing which probes that leaf must accept and which it must refuse, and one sentence on the leaf's most awkward commitment.

Two further requirements. First, at least one combination of answers that your tree permits should be left unoccupied: name the empty cell and say in one sentence whether it is vacant because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Second, flag at least two pairs of leaves that your tree cannot separate, explaining what further question would separate them and why that question is unsuitable for a ten-minute classroom exercise.

Finish with a routing table giving, for each leaf, the pattern of answers that lands there, plus one worked path traced from the top of the tree to a leaf.
