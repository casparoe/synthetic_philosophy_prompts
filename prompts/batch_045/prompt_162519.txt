I teach an undergraduate ethics unit on punishment, and it ends with a module on informal social sanction: boycotts, shunning, and deplatforming. I want to use Hawthorne's *The Scarlet Letter* as the case that carries that last module — as something the students read against the arguments, not as a period illustration bolted onto a conclusion I have already reached. So I need a reading that is faithful in both directions: one that does not flatten the novel into an example, and does not bend the philosophical problem to fit the book.

The debate as my students will meet it has this shape. One side says that withdrawing attention, association, or a platform is simply the exercise of one's own rights: nobody is entitled to an audience, to other people's labour, or to be insulated from being told what others think of them, and a boycott is speech rather than penalty. The other side says that when the withdrawal is coordinated and targets someone's access to the shared infrastructure of speech and livelihood, it is a punishment in everything but name — and punishment owes its target proportionality, a hearing, and someone answerable for it, none of which a decentralized sanction supplies. I take both sides seriously, and I do not think the impasse is verbal.

Here is what makes me want the novel, and the hypothesis I want tested. Contemporary arguments almost all assume that the harm of social sanction is *exclusionary*: you lose a platform, an audience, a venue. Hawthorne's Boston inflicts something close to the inverse. Hester is put on an actual platform, elevated and held still, and the punishment is compelled visibility — she is made permanently legible to everyone, unable to control what she is taken to mean. My suggestion is that a good deal of what gets argued under the heading of deplatforming is better modeled as status-degradation or forced legibility than as silencing, and that the novel lets us see why the silencing frame is incomplete. I would also point at two structural features the literature handles badly: the sanction is authorless (each townsman does something small; no one has standing, or a duty, to calibrate the total; severity is a function of collective uptake rather than of anyone's intent), and it is errant in its target (the man who is, on the novel's own terms, more culpable is not sanctioned at all, and the person who organizes the sanctioning is the worst figure in the book). Boston is also a single venue with no alternative audience, which is precisely the condition under which exclusionary sanction does its damage — a condition that is empirical and contingent, and whose absence is the usual reply to the deplatforming critic.

What I would like is a reading that says what the novel shows about social sanction — compelled legibility as a harm distinct from lost audience; the scaffold as the town's only platform and the night scene on it as the private, non-uptake version of the same act; the embroidery of the letter and its drift in what the town reads it as meaning; the final scaffold scene as the point where the sanction arrives at the right person by confession rather than by the town's mechanisms; and Hester's return, of her own accord, to the mark she could have shed. I want you to say what that return is: reappropriation, internalization of an unjust penalty, or a claim that a self can outrun what it has been made to signify.

I also want the resistance stated plainly. Hawthorne is not a liberal theorist of associational freedom; his machinery is guilt, conscience, secrecy, and exposure before God, and much of the book's force depends on Hester's sanction being in some sense deserved and on Dimmesdale's un-sanctioned torment being self-inflicted. And the analogy breaks in ways I want named: a face-to-face town of a few thousand is not a network with amplification and anonymity, and the mechanisms that make modern sanction explosive — and modern exit possible — are absent. Tell me which scenes you would put in front of students, where the analogy misleads, and, if there is one, what the novel contributes to this argument that no paper in it does.
