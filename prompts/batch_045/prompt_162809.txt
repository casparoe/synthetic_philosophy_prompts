I'm taking a seminar on computational models of creativity, and I've been asked to close the last session, which is on Hofstadter's knob-twiddling picture: a creative act as a variation on a theme, where you inherit a style space with implicit axes (register, tempo, how literally to take a word, how far to let one thing stand in for another), and the crux of creativity is perceiving which few knobs matter and by how much to turn them, with occasional conceptual slippage letting one knob do another's job. I don't want a summary of the reading; I want a poem, and I want the form itself to carry the idea.

Write a sestina. Fix the six end-words as six items in that picture — tune, knob, slippage, space, key, ear — and let the rotation do the arguing: the same six words returning in a new arrangement each stanza is literally what variation on a theme is, so each stanza should be a genuinely different variation on one carrying scene (a musician improvising on a borrowed tune) rather than six passes at the same gesture. Then the part I actually care about, which is the objection nobody in seminar answered.

Knob-twiddling explains movement inside a space, not the arrival of a new axis: a real leap isn't just a new setting if the dimension being turned was not on the console before someone reached for it, and if some leaps add a knob, the model owes us an account of what turning a knob that isn't there yet could even be. So break the form in the tornada: let a seventh end-word from outside the six enter and take a slot, and let the closing lines register both that this is what a genuine leap consists in and that it is precisely the part the knob picture cannot model. Standard length, no footnotes or epigraphs, no naming Hofstadter inside the poem; it has to stand up for people in the room who haven't read the chapter, and it shouldn't flatter the model — one of my classmates thinks the whole framework is empty, and I'd rather the poem be fair to him than win the argument.
