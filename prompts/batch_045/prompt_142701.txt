Me and my mate were up till 2am arguing about whether you can seperate the work from the person who made it and got nowhere, so here's her track record and I want to know where she lands on a new case.

She still plays a band whose singer did time for assault ('the songs haven't hit anyone'); she won't watch a stand-up special by a comic who turned out to be vicious off stage, because the act is basically just him talking; she'll read a novelist's fiction but not his essays, since the essays actually argue the vile stuff; she listens happily to a composer who took money from a fascist arts programme; but she won't read a set of mountain poems by a poet once she found out they were written to order for that same regime, even though there's nothing political in them. She also loves a poet whose best work came out of a breakdown and doesn't think that's a mark against it.

New case: a novel, The Drowned Summer, by a man whose daughter drowned in the bath while he was down the pub. It's about grief, it's apparently astonishing, and every review says its whole power is that the grief is completely real — but he never quite admits in it that it was his fault, and readers come away having quietly forgiven him.

Would she read it? More usefully: I can see at least two rules that fit those six verdicts and split here — one like 'reject when the bad thing is in the work, or is the making of the work, rather than just causally behind it', the other like 'reject when the work only works if you take up, or take pleasure in, something morally off'. Tell me what each predicts about the novel, which one you think she's really running and why, and what one further case would pry the two apart. Under 400 words, British English, no citations or reading lists — just the argument.
