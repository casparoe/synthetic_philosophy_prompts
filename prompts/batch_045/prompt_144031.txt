I am a novelist, and I am trying to build a scene in which a standoff between two people is structural rather than psychological: not two temperaments colliding, but two things they both genuinely accept turning out to be impossible to honour together. The skeleton I have in mind is Sen's liberal paradox, the result that no way of turning individual rankings into a collective decision can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain, the Pareto condition (if everyone ranks one outcome above another, the second is not socially admissible), and even a minimal liberalism condition in which at least two people are each decisive over some single pair of outcomes. Whenever I try the classic illustration on friends — two readers, one copy of a novel, one of them eager and one of them scandalised — the objection comes back immediately: it only works because both characters are busybodies with preferences about what someone else does in private, and people who minded their own business would never generate the cycle. I want a case that survives that dismissal, and I want it in a register I can actually put on the page: two adult siblings, say, with one odious errand between them over a long weekend, or any comparably ordinary setting you can make work.

What I would like you to design is a case built against the busybody objection, to these specifications. Two agents, and no more than four feasible outcomes — three if you can manage it, and if a fourth is unavoidable, tell me why three cannot be made to work. A household, a workplace, a shared errand: nothing futuristic, no lifeboats or runaway trolleys or other thought-experiment furniture. All preferences strict, so that nobody is indifferent at any load-bearing point. Each person's decisive pair must differ only in that person's own conduct, so the right being exercised is genuinely theirs and not a licence over someone else's business. And no one in the story may want to control, forbid, or disapprove of another person's private conduct: no prudes, no moralists, no one who is pleased by another's going without. The preferences should be the ordinary, even attractive ones — not wanting to be the one who does the thing, wanting to spare the other person, being willing to do it if nobody else will.

After the scene, do the demonstration. Set out the profile, then derive each forced collective ranking one at a time, naming the condition that forces it — this link by the first person's right, this one by the second's, this one by everyone's agreeing — and end with the cycle, or with an empty set of admissible outcomes, whichever is the honest description of what happens to a social decision function here, and tell me whether it matters that I am asking for a single outcome to be chosen rather than a full ordering of all of them. My own suspicion is that what carries the case is indivisibility: that the thing at stake can be done at most once, or done jointly, or left undone. Tell me if that suspicion is wrong and something else is doing the work, and then remove the feature you identify and show me the case going consistent. Finally, the part I will use most: how would someone determined to keep minimal liberalism attack your scene? Which of my constraints would they say is secretly doing the illegitimate work, what repair would they propose — restricting each right to that person's self-regarding preferences, perhaps, or reconceiving rights as side constraints on action rather than as inputs to an aggregation — and is there a version of the paradox that survives that repair? If any two of my specifications cannot be jointly met, say which one gives and why. And tell me honestly whether any of this can be spoken out loud by my characters, or whether it has to remain structure, felt as an impasse and never named.
