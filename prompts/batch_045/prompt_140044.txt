I'm a journalist — I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and right now I'm trying to put together a 1,200-word explainer on the repugnant conclusion for a general audience, the kind of reader who has never opened a philosophy paper but will happily argue about it in the pub. My editor's complaint about the standard presentation (a small population of ecstatic people versus an enormous population of lives that are barely worth living) is that the numbers are so astronomical that readers just shrug: nobody can picture those populations, so nobody feels the pull of the problem. I want to do it with one single, small, checkable case instead of the usual sci-fi scaling-up, and I want that one case to do three jobs at once, so I need it built to order rather than lifted from a textbook.

Here's the brief. Build me a three-option choice in a realistic institutional setting — a health ministry or a big foundation picking between three five-year programmes that differ in how many children are born and how well-off those children end up, something like that — in one paragraph, with integer welfare numbers and population sizes a reader can do in their head (no billions, no galaxies, no utility monsters). It has to be a pure different-people choice: in every option, everyone who exists has a life that is clearly, uncontroversially worth living, nobody is miserable, and no option works by making anyone worse off, so the case can't be smuggling in suffering, harm, or rights violations to do its work. Then the verdicts I need, all generated from numbers you show me rather than stipulated: the plain additive total view has to come out for option C; the average view has to come out for option A; and a critical-level view (add up welfare above a fixed positive threshold, the standard move for dodging the repugnant conclusion) has to come out for option B — the option neither of the other two ranks first. The threshold you use has to be one a critical-level theorist could plausibly have settled on for reasons of their own, and you need to say what those reasons are; gerrymandering the threshold to make B win is exactly the kind of cheating I want to avoid, so be explicit about the range of thresholds that deliver the verdict and how much room there is.

After the scenario, walk me through each theory's arithmetic, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work (I have a hunch it's the gap between the threshold and the welfare of the largest, least well-off group, but tell me if I'm wrong), and then perturb it three ways and show me the new numbers each time: set the threshold to zero, raise the welfare of the biggest group, and raise the welfare of the smallest group. I'd also like an honest note on what a defender of the critical-level view would say to make choosing B look principled rather than silly — and, since this determines how I frame the whole piece, a committed verdict from you on whether B really is an absurd result or whether the case has accidentally revealed that B is the option we should have wanted all along. Pick a side, give me your reasons, and tell me how confident you are and where you might be wrong. Finally, flag anything in the case that you think would misfire or just confuse a non-philosopher reading it on a Sunday. Please write in British English, and don't cite anything — I'll add the names and references myself.
