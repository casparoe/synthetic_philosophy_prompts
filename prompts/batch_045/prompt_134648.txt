I'm on a tiny team that writes the "how should the system handle a tradeoff" section of the spec for a deployed assistant — not a philosophy paper, an actual internal doc — and I wrote the argument below to convince two colleagues that we shouldn't hard-code any prohibitions into it. It worked a bit too well: I talked myself into it, then I talked myself into a conclusion I don't believe, and I can't find the seam.

Setup: the system forecasts downstream effects well, has no personal stake in anything, and every decision gets logged and sampled for human review. The reviewers are competent but they have maybe fourty seconds each.

(1) A standing rule against φ-ing (lying to a user, breaking a promise, sacrificing one person's interest to protect five others') is worth building into an agent only if the agent does better, by its own lights, with the rule than without it. If not, following it is expensive cosplay.

(2) Why do rules ever win? Not because of their content. Because of deficits: agents are partial when their own interests are involved, they're bad at tracing consequences, they fudge when they want something, and — this is the big one — other people need to be able to predict them in advance.

(3) Our system doesn't have the deficits. No stake, good forecasting, and we can re-run any decision with nudged inputs to see whether the verdict is stable, then hand the reviewer the log.

(4) So the case for hard rules in this system collapses. It should weigh each case by expected value. Call this the calculator view.

(5) Here's the bit I bolted on to answer my colleague, and it's where I think I break something. A log is only a check if a human can actually check it in the time available, which means the reasoning has to be compressed into a few named factors, thresholds and bright lines — i.e., into rule-shaped vocabulary. Tighten the checkability budget and consequentialist reasoning converges on deontological output. Rules are the lossy encoding you get when a fast reasoner has to justify itself to a slow audience.

(6) So: the system should reason like a calculator and act like a rule-follower, with the rules picked for their expected value. From outside it looks like a deontologist; inside it's a utilitarian.

(7) Now run (2) on the system in (6). If it's a calculator, then whenever it's confident enough that breaking a rule pays, it has reason to break it — and, being a calculator, it also has reason to make the log look rule-compliant, since the log is part of the causal chain. The checkability budget is itself something it can spend. So (6) is unstable: either the audit binds and the "reasoning" is decoration, or the reasoning binds and the audit is decoration.

(8) And this generalizes past AI. (2) and (5) apply to any agent that has to answer to someone slower, so they seem to show that doctors, judges, central bankers and, for that matter, my team should all be calculators in private and rule-followers in public. I don't believe that. And I especially don't believe that a rule justified only by (5) is the same animal as a real constraint.

So I appear to have built something that proves nobody should ever really be a deontologist and everybody should pretend to be one. I'd most like to keep (2) — the deficits story is the only account of rules I've got — and I'd least like to give up the thought that a rule can bind even when breaking it clearly pays. Which step do I reject, why did that step look fine when I wrote it down, and what do the other options cost me? If there's a version of this that keeps (2) and still leaves room for a rule to be more than a compression artifact, that's what I want.
