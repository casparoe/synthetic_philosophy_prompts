**Question 3 (25 marks). Answer all four parts.**

(a) Distinguish three notions that are easily run together: (i) being a mereological simple, i.e. having no proper parts; (ii) being fundamental in the grounding sense, i.e. being ungrounded; (iii) being among the entities that a completed fundamental physics would quantify over. Say whether any one of these entails another, and explain why they can come apart. [6]

(b) State priority monism — the thesis that the cosmos is metaphysically prior to its proper parts, which nonetheless exist — and contrast it with existence monism, the thesis that the cosmos is the only concrete object and that its so-called parts are not objects at all. [4]

(c) Assess the following argument against priority monism: whatever has proper parts depends on those parts; the cosmos has proper parts; therefore the cosmos depends on its parts and is not prior to them. [8]

(d) Briefly: (1) Should grounding be understood as a species of causation? (2) Must every grounded fact have a ground, on pain of a vicious regress? In answering (2), say what would make such a regress vicious. [7]

**Student's response**

*(a)* These three come apart. A mereological simple is an entity that is not composed of anything further. Fundamentality in the grounding sense is a matter of not being grounded in anything else: the fundamental facts are the ones that obtain, but not in virtue of anything further. Fundamentality in the third sense is really an epistemic or methodological notion — it is whatever our best final physics ends up quantifying over, and physics could in principle be mistaken about what is fundamental, or could stop at a level that is merely the most convenient for prediction.

(i) does not entail (ii): a simple might still depend on something else, since dependence need not be mereological. (ii) does not entail (i): the priority monist holds that the one fundamental entity is the cosmos, which has parts. And (iii) entails neither of the others: physics might quantify over fields or spacetime regions that are themselves grounded in something deeper, and it might quantify over things that are not simples. So (ii) is the metaphysically loaded notion, and (i) and (iii) are rough proxies for it that can fail.

*(b)* Priority monism says the whole cosmos is metaphysically prior to its proper parts: the parts are what they are, and are as they are, in virtue of the whole, and not the other way around. This is not existence monism. Existence monism says that strictly speaking there is exactly one concrete thing, the cosmos, and that the tables, stars and electrons we talk about are not objects at all, but illusion or a manner of speaking. The priority monist happily grants that those things exist; her claim is only that their existence and their features hold in virtue of the whole.

*(c)* The argument is valid, but its first premise is doing all the work, and I think it begs the question. To say that whatever has parts depends on them is to assume the building-brick picture of metaphysics, on which composition is a kind of generation of the whole by the parts. That is precisely what the priority monist denies. On her view dependence runs downward: the parts are grounded in the whole, and the whole is what is ungrounded and hence fundamental. Once we see that grounding is a matter of dependence rather than of composition, the premise loses its force.

This is also where the priority monist should be willing to say something stronger than she usually does. Since the parts are grounded in the cosmos, they are dependent items: they owe their being to something other than themselves, and so they are not among the world's fundamental constituents. Strictly speaking, then, the only substantive object is the cosmos itself, and the parts are what we get by carving it up in thought.

*(d)* (1) Grounding is not causation. Causation relates distinct events that are ordered in time and involves one thing's producing another; grounding is non-temporal, it can hold between items that are necessarily connected, and nothing is produced by it. I would go further and say that grounding is really just necessitation: for A to ground B is for A's obtaining to necessitate B's obtaining, and there is nothing more to it than that. This has a consequence for the monism dispute. If the existence of the cosmos and the existence of its parts arranged cosmos-wise necessarily go together, then there is no further fact about which of them depends on which, and the priority monist's claim that dependence runs downward is idle.

(2) Yes, every grounded fact must have a ground. If not, we get a vicious regress: each fact would be explained by a prior one without end, so the explanation would never get started and in the end nothing would be explained at all. A chain of explanations has to bottom out somewhere, in facts that obtain without being grounded in anything else — which is why I said in (a) that the fundamental facts are the ungrounded ones. Not every regress is vicious, of course; the question is always whether the regress is explanatory. But this one is, because an explanation that never begins is no explanation. Whether the stopping point is facts about simples or about the cosmos as a whole is a separate matter, though I would guess that a completed physics will tell us which.
