I'm retired now — thirty-odd years as a field ecologist, mostly alpine plant communities, and about four years ago I started going to a philosophy reading group down at the library, which is how I got talked into this. My old department has asked me to give a forty-five-minute session to this year's incoming PhD students in the autumn, and the brief they gave me was basically "tell them why they shouldn't trust p-values." Most of them will have had exactly one stats course and come out of it thinking a p-value is the probability the result was a fluke, or the probability the null hypothesis is true, and I don't think a slide of definitions will dislodge that. So: don't write the talk for me — I want a plan for the talk. What order do the pieces have to come in and what depends on what, given that I think the load-bearing distinction is between the probability of the data given the hypothesis and the probability of the hypothesis given the data, and that I want to get from there to the replication crisis without ever suggesting the crisis is mainly about fraud or sloppy people? And I need one example to carry the whole thing. My candidate is a colleague who swears his sourdough rises faster when he plays Bach to it — I like it because the prior is obviously low, the outcome is continuous rather than yes/no, and anybody can see how you'd keep checking the dough until it looked like a win. Is that the right example, or does it lack something that, say, a fertilizer trial on an alpine sedge would have? Please don't give me the disease-screening / mammography base-rate thing or Fisher's tea-tasting lady, they'll have seen both in their first-year notes and it'll feel like a rerun.

The part I want most help with is the misconception map, because I've sat through enough of these to guess what goes wrong. I expect (a) the p-value-as-probability-the-null-is-true one, (b) the idea that if you peek at your data and stop when it looks good you've done something *dishonest* rather than something that quietly invalidates the number, (c) "just run more replicates and it fixes itself," and (d) the mirror-image error that the students will overcorrect into: that Bayesian methods would have saved us, as if priors can't be tuned the same way stopping rules can. Where in the sequence do I head each of these off — before it forms, or after they've said it out loud? I also want to build toward something I've been chewing on myself and can't quite state cleanly: my suspicion is that even perfectly honest researchers who never peek and never hack will still produce a literature full of exaggerated effect sizes, purely because journals prefer surprising results and surprising results are the ones that happen to land far from the truth when the effect is real but small. I saw someone online call these magnitude and sign errors, which sounds right, but I don't want to lean on the name. I'd like the session to land there rather than land on "p-hacking is bad," and I'd like to know what has to be in place earlier for that point to feel inevitable instead of cynical. Oh — and there's one thing I can do live: every student has a laptop, and I can have them each simulate a fake experiment on a coin they know is fair and see how many of them get under 0.05 anyway. Where does that belong?

Finally: give me one question to put to them at the last five minutes that would tell me whether the distinction actually landed rather than whether they memorized a phrase, one thing I should flatly refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing (I'm guessing likelihood vs. Bayesian proper, or maybe why priors are supposed to be subjective), and a line for the inevitable hand at minute forty asking "so should we just stop using p-values?" — because I honestly don't know what I think. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, please, the way you'd write an email to a friend: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
