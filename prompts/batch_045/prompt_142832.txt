I did thirty-odd years as a research scientist, mostly numerical weather prediction and ocean-atmosphere stuff, and now that I'm retired I've got into reading philosophy and I've got stuck on Dutch book arguments — I can't tell whether they actually underwrite anything real, or whether they're a cute bit of maths that sits off to one side of the practice I used to live inside.

The practice I keep coming back to is this. Services that issue probability-of-precipitation forecasts grade their own forecasters, and sometimes set pay or promotion partly on it, using a strictly proper scoring rule: a rule with the property that if your genuine confidence that it rains is p, then your expected score (long-run average over many days) is best if you announce p, and strictly worse if you announce anything else. The Brier score is the one I knew, but there are others, and forecast tournaments and prediction markets run on the same basic idea. So this is a funded, institutionalised, boringly normal practice: we build an incentive system around the assumptions that a person's confidence in a proposition is a single sharp number, that the number is well defined even for one-off events like tomorrow's weather, and that reporting it honestly is what we want. My questions:

What does the practice actually presuppose about degrees of belief, and how much of that is the Dutch book story doing the work? In particular, does it strictly presuppose that credences satisfy the probability axioms — the thing the sure-loss argument is supposed to deliver — or does it only presuppose that honest reporting is incentive-compatible, which looks like it might be a different and weaker claim? Could you still justify scoring forecasters on a purely long-run-calibration basis (of the days you said 30%, did it rain on about 30% of them) with no talk of credences at all, and if so does that show the practice never needed the Dutch book in the first place?

I also want to know whether the practice quietly rules out views it looks neutral about. Suppose a forecaster's confidence is mushy in this sense: she'd pay up to 40 pence for a ticket that pays a pound if it rains tomorrow, but she wouldn't sell such a ticket for less than 60 pence — there's a gap, no single number there. Does running her through a proper scoring rule just force a number out of her anyway, in which case the practice is compatible with that view, or does the practice only make sense if there's a real number to be extracted? Relatedly, I read somewhere that there are Dutch-book-flavoured arguments that push the other direction, i.e. that seem to tell against always having one sharp probability, and I'd like to know whether the forecast-scoring practice is even in a position to adjudicate between those.

Here's a variant I made up because I couldn't find it discussed, and I want to know whether it's coherent. Take a proper scoring rule and add what I'll call a sharpness bonus: on days when you announce something above 0.8 or below 0.2 and you turn out right, you get an extra bump, and on days when you announce 0.5 you forgo the bump, even if 0.5 really is your confidence. Now the score-maximising report is no longer your true credence. Is the resulting institution incoherent, or is it coherent but doing a different job — rewarding the production of decision-useful information rather than grading anyone's rationality? That seems to me to split the practice into two aims that might not need the same philosophy, and I want to know which aim the actual practice is serving and what each one presupposes.

And here's the bit that really puzzles me, because it's the same people doing both. The scientific culture that is perfectly happy to score a 30% chance of rain will not put a probability on a hypothesis. In my old field we reported p-values and error bars and wrote "consistent with" or "no evidence for", and no one would write "the probability that this model is correct is 0.7". There's a related convention in assessment reports of using words like likely and very likely with a rough percentage range attached in an appendix, instead of numbers in the text. What view of probability does that half of the practice presuppose — that probability is a property of procedures and long-run frequencies rather than of anyone's confidence, or just that assertion has a much higher evidential bar than having a degree of belief? Can both halves be true at once, or is the weather office and the journal quietly committed to different theories?

One last thing, because it seems to me to be where the action might be. Can a Dutch book be run against a literature rather than a person? If five separate papers each establish things that jointly cannot be more than about 0.3 probable, is there an argument that the community is incoherent even though every individual paper passed its own referees and every individual author satisfies probabilism on their own? Does the scoring practice help with that at all, or is it only ever about individuals, and what would have to change in how we publish or how we assess fields if we took the community-level version seriously?

I'm less interested in the maths of the arguments than in which of these commitments the practice really needs and which it merely fits nicely with, so please flag anywhere the practice is underdetermined and several different theories would license it. And I don't want a reading list — I can't get to a library easily and I'd rather have the argument.
