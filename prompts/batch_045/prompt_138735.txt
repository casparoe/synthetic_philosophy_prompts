I'm in a philosophy of science course this term, and we've just finished the unit on Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes — hard core, protective belt, the negative and positive heuristics, progressive versus degenerating problem shifts, novel facts versus facts accommodated after the fact. What struck me in seminar is that the framework looks beautifully diagnostic in the retrospective case studies and almost unusable in real time: nobody can tell, in the present, whether an auxiliary modification is a bold theoretical growth or an ad hoc rescue, and Lakatos deliberately refuses to put a clock on how long a programme may degenerate before it should be abandoned. Our TA basically conceded this and said the framework is a rational reconstruction, not a decision procedure. I think that concession is much funnier than it sounds, and I'd like to write something that exploits it.

So: write a mock internal document from a university's research-governance bureaucracy that has tried to operationalize Lakatosian methodology as an actual administrative procedure — say, the Research Programmes Office's annual problem-shift review, issued as a memo with numbered guidance notes and a worked appendix. Make it deadpan committee prose, around 650 words. The test case should be a plainly fictional, mildly absurd programme that has spent about a decade absorbing failed predictions: I'd suggest something like the Coherence Cascade Programme in organizational psychology, whose hard core holds that groups dissipate a measurable 'cohesion energy' in a law-governed way, and which has met every anomaly by adding auxiliary hypotheses about measurement conditions. The office must apply the Lakatosian criteria scrupulously correctly — it should never misdescribe the framework — and the joke should be that doing so gets it nowhere: every criterion it applies turns out to be satisfiable by any programme at all, and the form can generate an endless series of deferrals without ever being able to declare degeneration. Please include (a) at least one dissent note from a committee member who is an exasperated falsificationist and wants a time limit, (b) a clause or footnote in which the office must assess whether its own methodology is a progressive or degenerating programme, and (c) a genuinely unexpected, independently confirmed finding that the Coherence Cascade people did predict in advance, which the office has no vocabulary to credit properly. Don't let the document collapse into winking or state the moral; a reader who has never heard of Lakatos should still follow the absurdity, and someone who likes the framework should feel the satire is aimed at institutionalizing it rather than at a strawman version of it.
