I run a small type foundry, and we are finishing a low-contrast humanist text family — a roman with a true italic companion, meant for continuous reading at 8 to 11 point. We build candidate glyphs with a generative tool that perturbs stroke skeletons and proposes variants; my collaborator M. is the one who rules on them. For the lowercase a she says simply "that's an a" or "that's not an a of ours," and I have never been able to reconstruct the rule she is applying. I would like to know how she will rule on the proposal now on the table, and what rule would make her verdicts cohere.

Her verdicts so far on candidates for the roman:

1. A variant with the bowl's weight shifted toward the shoulder, the terminal shortened and cut at a shallower angle, the aperture between bowl and stem opened wider, and the stem's foot serif simplified — accepted, and she said it was better than what we had.

2. A variant in which the aperture was closed, leaving a closed ring with the stem attached on its right and no terminal — rejected. Her words: "that's an o with a stick glued to it."

3. A variant in which the stem was moved from the right side of the bowl to the left, everything else unchanged — rejected. "That's a d."

4. A variant in which the bowl and stem were left as they are but the joint between them was broken, so that the stem stands a few units clear of the bowl — rejected. "The a has to be one animal, not two." At 9 point in running text most readers still read it as an a, and we told her so; she did not change her mind.

5. For the italic companion, the tool proposed a one-storey a — a round bowl sitting on the baseline with a straight stem at its right, no arch over the bowl and no terminal — accepted. "In the italic that's the only honest a." Pressed on why the same shape would not do in the roman, she said only that "the roman hand and the italic hand are not the same hand," which I do not think answers the question.

6. A candidate that is, stroke for stroke and unit for unit, the accepted roman a, but which the tool reached not by editing our a and not by copying a reference, but by extrapolating from the family's o, d, and g — rejected. "That one is an a by accident." We pointed out that no reader could tell it apart; she said readers are not the tribunal.

The new proposal is a one-storey a for the roman regular: a round bowl on the baseline, a straight stem at its right rising to the x-height, no arch and no terminal, the stem cut flat at the baseline with no foot serif, and sidebearings, width, and x-height identical to the accepted two-storey a. Crucially, the tool got there by deleting the arch and terminal from our own accepted roman a and redrawing the bowl as a full circle — the same kind of edit that produced items 1 and 4, not the extrapolation procedure she objected to in item 6. So the shape is the italic's shape but the derivation is our own glyph's. We set 9 point paragraphs with it: no reader misread a word and nobody noticed anything until we pointed it out, though two of them said afterward that the page looked "cleaner but less like us." She has also been known to say that "an a isn't a shape, it's a settlement with the o and the d," which I have never been able to cash out.

What I want from you: predict her verdict, and identify the rule she appears to be running. Please set out the other rules that fit all six of her earlier verdicts equally well, say what each of them predicts about this new case, and give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it. Then tell me which single further case would best separate the surviving rules from one another — e.g. some variant of the same shape reached by a different route, or proposed for a different cut or optical size — and say what each rule would say about it. Finally, if consistency with her own record commits her to rejecting this glyph, I want that stated plainly, together with the principle she would thereby be adopting: is a letter identified by its shape as read in context, by the arrangement of its parts, by the gesture or procedure that produced it, or by the slot it occupies among the other letters of this family?
