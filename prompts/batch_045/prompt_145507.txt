I am setting the final examination for a final-year seminar on social choice, rights, and collective decision, and I need one essay question together with a model answer and a marking scheme. Students will have 90 minutes for it, can write at most about 1,200 words, and it carries 20 of the paper's marks.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a question. Any candidate who has done the reading can recite Sen's argument that no social decision function satisfies unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, and a minimal liberalism condition, and can reproduce the two-reader case that generates the cycle. I do not want to reward that. What I want is for students to test whether the standard repair — dropping the demand for a social ordering and instead giving each person a choice set in a game form, so that rights are powers over one's own sphere rather than entries in a collective ranking — actually solves anything, or whether it merely relocates the conflict.

My suspicion, which the students should be made to work out rather than be handed, is this: the repair dissolves the formal inconsistency but not the normative one, because the outcome produced when everyone exercises their rights can still be Pareto-dominated by a different outcome that is also reachable by some permissible exercise of everyone's rights — it just requires someone to exercise their right differently. Call that Pareto-improvability-under-rights, PIR. The further wrinkle I want them to confront is a unanimous-waiver clause, UWC: any right-holder may waive her right over her protected pair, and a right is defeated only if every right-holder waives, in which case the pair is decided collectively. Does UWC get the society out of the PIR failure, and is the resulting regime recognizably liberal or is it paternalism with extra steps — especially given that each person may waive only conditionally on others waiving, and that some rights may be non-waivable? I suspect the honest verdict is that it depends on whether rights are waivable at all and on what the point of a right is taken to be, but I want the question to force a determinate verdict rather than an invitation to discuss.

Please produce:

1. The examination question itself. It must be self-contained: state unrestricted domain, Pareto, and minimal liberalism in full; set out the two-reader profile explicitly (the prude most prefers that nobody read the book, but would rather read it himself than let the lewd reader read it alone; the lewd reader most prefers that the prude read it, and prefers the prude reading alone to reading it alone himself); define PIR and UWC in the question; and phrase the whole thing so that it has a determinate answer and cannot be discharged by surveying the standard responses.

2. A model answer of no more than 600 words.

3. A marking scheme worth 20 marks: five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and one line on how candidates typically get each one wrong. The scheme should separate an A answer, which distinguishes dissolving a formal inconsistency from dissolving a normative conflict and says what would count as each, from a B answer, which handles the machinery correctly but only catalogues the familiar moves.

Do not cite or name any papers, authors, or dates anywhere in what you write; the exam is closed-book and I will supply references myself if needed. Keep the entire response under 1,100 words.
