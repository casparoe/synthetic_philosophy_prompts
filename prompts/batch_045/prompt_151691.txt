I clerk for the Franchise Commission of the Free City of Mirenne, a semi-autonomous port city where roughly one resident in six is not a citizen, and I have been going back and forth with a colleague about how the Commission should rule on a petition that is coming up next month. To make sense of it I need to reconstruct what rule the Commission has actually been running, because its record so far is consistent with several, and they come apart on this petition.

The record, as I have it: in 2011 the Commission lowered the municipal voting age to sixteen, reasoning that sixteen-year-olds may work, pay local taxes, hold a tenancy with a guardian's countersignature, and are answerable under city by-laws; it declined at the same time to lower the age for national elections, and in 2020 it refused a petition for a voting age of fourteen on the ground that below sixteen political judgment is not yet formed independently of household and school. In 2016 it granted the municipal vote to non-citizens with at least five years of lawful residence and an indefinitely renewable permit, while refusing it to seasonal, employer-tied and short-term permit holders and to anyone short of five years' residence even where they paid local taxes and had children in city schools; it has never extended any franchise beyond municipal elections to non-citizens. On prisoners it has held that those on remand and those serving under two years keep the vote, while longer sentences suspend it, on the view that a serious and sustained breach of the terms on which the community admits someone to a share in its decisions can cost them that share. And in 2019 it recommended compulsory attendance at the polls for citizens aged eighteen to seventy-five — not compulsory choice: the ballot carries an explicit none-of-these-candidates line and blank or spoiled papers are recorded as abstentions — exempting residents over seventy-five, adults under plenary guardianship, and citizens who have lived abroad more than fifteen years and keep no property or pension in the city. When it enfranchised sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds it declined to compel them; when it enfranchised non-citizens it explicitly left the compulsion question open as not properly before it.

The new petition asks the Commission to make attendance compulsory for the non-citizen municipal electorate created in 2016, on the same terms as for citizens: same administrative fine, same abstain option, same exemptions by age and capacity. The figures attached to the petition are these: turnout among that electorate was 74 per cent at the last municipal election, against 79 per cent for citizens overall and 61 per cent for citizens aged eighteen to twenty-nine; about half of them have been entitled to naturalize for years and have not applied, while most of the remainder cannot naturalize at all, since their state of origin forbids renunciation and Mirenne does not permit dual nationality. What I want from you is the following: set out the two or three rules that could jointly generate all four lines of precedent — some kind of two-tier view on which rights may reach beyond citizenship while the duties of membership may not, some kind of compulsion-follows-enfranchisement view with carve-outs for age and capacity, some kind of representativeness view keyed to turnout levels, or another you prefer — and say what each predicts about this petition and about the decliners-versus-ineligible split inside it; give your best guess with the reasons for preferring it; identify the closest analog among the Commission's own precedents; and if you think the record genuinely underdetermines the matter, name the further case that would settle it (I had been wondering whether the revealing one is a later petition to compel sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds now that their turnout has slid, but tell me if you think that case is unhelpfully ambiguous, and why), and say which principle the Commission would be committing itself to either way. Please keep it fairly tight and finish with a single machine-readable line: \answer{extend compulsion} or \answer{do not extend compulsion}.
