hey — I'm drafting a short philosophy piece on the statue-and-the-clay puzzle and I'd really like some comments before I take it further. Rough notes below; it's messy on purpose.

THE NOTES

Setup: Goliath the statue and Lumpl the piece of clay fill the same space and are made of the same matter, but they seem to differ: squash the clay into a ball and the lump survives while the statue doesn't; swap out a small part and the statue survives while the lump arguably doesn't. Standard conclusion: constitution isn't identity, there are two coinciding things here. The rival options I know of: two-thing views (constitution without identity, or the temporal-parts/overlap version), one-thing views (the object survives the squashing and merely ceases to be a statue), and eliminativism about artifacts.

My first claim: the modal argument is weaker than it looks. The differences it points at are differences in which counterfactual continuations we're willing to count as "the same thing going on", and what we're willing to count is fixed by sortal and institutional conventions, not by the object's intrinsic makeup. Evidence: the Proclaimed Lump. A vitrine contains an unremarkable lump of clay. On tuesday a curator, acting under some statute, signs a certificate saying "the clay now in Vitrine 4 is hereby a work of statuary titled Lump". Nothing intrinsic changes: same matter, same shape, same causal powers, just paperwork. Yet afterwards there really does seem to be a statue there, and it seems newly fragile in a way the lump wasn't — squash it now and you've destroyed a work, where before you'd have reshaped a lump. So at least for artifacts the "extra" modal profile is conferred by social facts, which means the modal data doesn't discriminate between the one-thing and the two-thing views.

Second claim, and this is the bit I think is new: consider revocation. On wednesday, before anyone touches the clay, the curator withdraws the certificate; on thursday someone squashes the clay and apparently destroys nothing. So whether a given purely intrinsic event (the squashing) counts as an object's ceasing to exist is sensitive to a social fact that can be flipped at will. The two-thing theorist can say: fine, the statue came into existence on tuesday and went out of existence on wednesday, untouched and unmoved. But then we have objects popping in and out of existence in virtue of paperwork, which feels like an embarrassment rather than a result — or at least it needs to be compared honestly with what the one-thing view has to say about the same sequence.

Where I land so far (shakily): one thing in the vitrine, and every "ceases to exist" claim is implicitly indexed to a sortal-institution — "ceases to be a statue", "ceases to be that piece of clay". Cost: the number of things in the vitrine depends on which institutions are in force, which is uncomfortably close to saying existence is up to us. I'm not sure how bad that cost is.

WHAT I WANT FROM YOU

a) Is the revocation case actually a new problem, or is it just a re-run of something the two-thing views already handle in their sleep? If it's a re-run, tell me which move defuses it and I'll cut the section.
b) Poke holes in the first claim. In particular: is it true that the modal differences don't discriminate between the one-thing and two-thing views, or is there a version of the modal argument that survives the Proclaimed Lump?
c) Is the ending interesting or a dead end? I half suspect it collapses into the eliminativist option, in which case I should say so explicitly instead of pretending I've got a third view.

No need for references or citations, and please do tell me if any section is just boring or obviously confused — I'd rather cut than pad.
