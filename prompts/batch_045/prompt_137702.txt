Hey, I need a hand with something for a reading group. Two friends of mine have been going in circles for about a year over whether everyday conventions — tipping, queueing, jaywalking, that sort of thing — are actually moral obligations, and in particular whether a visitor has to follow the local version. One of them I'd call an accomodationist: conventions are locally maintained public goods, you owe it to the people who keep them up to fall in line, you owe it even when the convention is completely arbitrary, and if you can't manage it you should stay out of the relevant situations. The other holds something like a portability view: your reasons travel with you, an arbitrary convention generates no obligation at all, only prudential reasons plus the general duty not to set people back, so a visitor may stick to home conventions when the local one is arbitrary, and the sting locals feel at being deviated from isn't itself a reason for the visitor.

Write the joint statement they should have produced instead of all that arguing. Numbered agreed-claims section first — claims both can sign, including about what a convention *is*, about which of these cases count as easy, and about what doesn't settle anything. Then a disagreements table: the residual disagreements stated precisely, and for each disagreement give it twice, once in the wording the accomodationist would accept for their own view and once in the wording the portabilist would accept for theirs. No weaselly "it depends" — the whole point is to make exactly what it depends on explicit.

Then the cruxes, with concrete cases. Please use these three, which I've set up so both of them already agree the case bears on something:

- Counter-tipping. In Marlow Bend restaurants there's no tipping and leaving cash on the table is conventionally read as implying the server is begging; servers describe it as demeaning. Visitors from tip countries tip anyway, generously and kindly.
- Invisible jaywalking. In a city where literally everyone, including the police, crosses on a red when no car is coming, a visitor waits at empty intersections. Drivers slow down warily for the person standing still, and traffic is very slightly worse.
- The queue of objects. At bus stops in another city people queue by putting a bag or umbrella down in a line and standing nearby. A visitor who has no idea this is a thing walks past the bags and boards first.

For each crux say what sub-question it turns on and what each side predicts about the case, written down in advance. I suspect at least three cruxes: whether the meaning an action has for the local community partly fixes its deontic status; whether making yourself illegible or mildly unsettling to others counts as setting them back; and whether an obligation requires that the visitor could reasonably have picked the convention up, or whether not knowing just changes what you're blameworthy for. Add any others you think are load-bearing.

Two more things. First, I've got a hunch part of this is verbal: obligation might be doing three separate jobs — there's a reason, you're blameworthy if you don't, others may enforce or coercively demand it — and my friends may be agreeing about one and fighting about another. If you think that, say exactly which part is verbal and why, and rewrite that bit so it stops looking like a disagreement. Second, stipulate this shared background for the whole document so it doesn't sprawl: in all three cases the convention is arbitrary in the sense that its opposite would coordinate about as well, the visitor causes no material harm and isn't trying to make a statement, and the locals' reaction is real but small. If a claim only holds when that background is dropped, say so rather than quietly relying on it.
