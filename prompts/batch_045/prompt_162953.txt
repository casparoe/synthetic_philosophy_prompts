I'm a journalist — mostly science-and-ideas features — and I'm working on a piece about whether the content of what people say and think is fixed by what goes on inside their heads. I've read the usual material and I can write the meaning-isn't-in-the-head paragraph myself. What I can't do, and what I want from you, is a case that does something sharper than the standard one.

Here's what I need. Build me a Twin-Earth-style scenario in which two versions of externalism about reference assign *different referents* to the same word, in the same speaker's mouth, at the same time. The two views:

(1) The historical-chain view: a term's reference comes from an original baptism or grounding and is passed along by a chain of communication and use, so what the chain traces back to settles the reference.

(2) The causal-regulation view: what settles reference is whatever is actually causally responsible for the speaker's tokenings here and now — the stuff their use of the word currently tracks, or is regulated by, in their present environment.

I want the case to make (1) say the word refers to the Earth substance and (2) say it refers to the Twin Earth substance. So the disagreement has to stay *inside* externalism: its not just externalism versus descriptivism, and ideally a descriptivist would have a clear answer too rather than a shrug. Both views should have something real to point at in the story.

Constraints:

- One paragraph, and it has to be a paragraph I can drop into a magazine piece. My editor will kill me if it needs a footnote. So: no brain surgery, no amnesia, no switched-at-birth, no characters who are philosophers explaining themselves, and nothing exotic beyond the twin planet itself.
- Everyone in the story is epistemically normal: a competent layperson's or a working scientist's ordinary grasp of the relevant substance. Nobody is weirdly ignorant, nobody is omniscient, and nobody is confused about which liquid is in front of them.
- The split has to fall out of the setup, not be stipulated. No one in the story gets to announce what the word refers to, and the word's use shouldn't be idiosyncratic in a way that just begs the question.
- The case shouldn't secretly depend on microstructure. The same structural situation should be reproducible with any pair of superficially indistinguishable kinds, and if yours does depend on chemistry, tell me.

Then show me it works:

- State the word, the speaker, and what each of (1) and (2) assigns as the referent, and the reason each gives.
- Name the single feature of the case doing the work, and show the case collapses without it: what do both views say if you remove the historical link, and what do both say if you remove the difference in the current environment?
- Tell me how a defender of whichever view comes out worse would push back — the cheapest way for them to bite the bullet or redescribe the case without giving up the theory.
- Say what my reader is actually supposed to learn. Does the case show that externalism is two theses people run together, or something weaker than that?
- And a sanity check: if one of my two views as I've described it isn't really a version of externalism, or isn't the version anyone holds, say so and fix the description before you use it.

Bonus question I keep going back and fourth on: does the twin planet do any real work here? Is there a terrestrial version — one community, one word, two superficially similar substances, no second planet — that would do the job just as well for a general reader? If you think the twin planet is essential, tell me what it adds.
