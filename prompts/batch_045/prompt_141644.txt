For most of my working life I was an experimental physical chemist, and the part of the job I never stopped worrying about was calibration. A number on a printout is worth something only because there is an unbroken chain of physical contact — specimen, transducer, reference standard, instrument, hand — running from the thing measured to the figure written down. Break the chain anywhere and you still have a number; you simply cannot say what it is a number of. So when I took up philosophy in retirement and came across the problem of how symbols come to be about anything at all, I recognised the shape of the worry at once, even though the literature discusses it in vocabulary I am still learning.

I have agreed to give a twenty-minute talk on it to my local philosophy group in the spring, and I would like to open with a poem rather than with a summary of the argument, because the point is one that assertion tends to flatten. I am asking you to write that poem.

The form I want is a sestina: six stanzas of six lines, unrhymed, with the same six end-words recurring through the standard rotation, closed by a three-line envoi in which all six words appear again. The situation inside the poem should be the familiar one of a learner — a person, or something like a person — who possesses a complete and correct dictionary of a language written entirely in that language, and nothing else: no pictures, no pointing, no speaker, no world. Choose six ordinary words for concrete, perceivable things as your end-words, and let every explanation the poem offers of any one of them be made out of other words. Never let the poem gesture at something outside itself.

Two things matter more than the rest. First, accuracy: the failure on display must not be that the learner is missing a definition. It has all the definitions, and every relation between its symbols is correct; that is precisely what makes the case. Someone who has actually read this literature should be able to see that the poem is about the difference between a symbol's meaning being exhausted by its relations to other symbols and its meaning being fixed by contact with what it is about — and should be able to tell that it is not a poem about scepticism about the external world, not about the indeterminacy of translation, not a re-run of the Chinese room's point about understanding, and not the claim that machines lack feelings. Second, the rotation has to do philosophical work: the six words should keep arriving in an order they did not choose and cannot alter, so that by the last stanza the reader feels that everything is in its proper place and that nothing has arrived anywhere. The envoi should not solve the problem. I would be content with its conceding that a causal chain like the one I spent my career maintaining looks necessary, while leaving it open whether such a chain is sufficient or only moves the question one step back.

Please avoid all technical vocabulary in the poem itself — no talk of symbols, semantics, or grounding; I will supply those words in the talk. I would also rather the speaker not be a machine addressing its programmer, which has been done to death. Most of the people in the room will have never opened a philosophy book, so it has to stand as a poem for them first and as an illustration second, and I would like no footnotes, epigraph, or explanatory coda: the explaining is my job.
