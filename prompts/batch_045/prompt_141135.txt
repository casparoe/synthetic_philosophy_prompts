I had a long argument last night with a friend — a structural engineer — about Goodman's new riddle of induction, and she has since sent me the passage below, which she copied from a popular philosophy website and says settles the matter. Something in it strikes me as unfair to Goodman, but I cannot locate what.

The passage reads: "Philosophers used to worry about whether induction can be justified at all. Goodman sharpened that worry into a proof that it cannot: for any body of evidence, there are always infinitely many hypotheses that fit it perfectly and yet contradict one another about the future. Say that 'grue' means green if examined before 2050 and blue if examined after. Every emerald examined so far has been green, so the evidence supports 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' equally well, even though after 2050 the first predicts green emeralds and the second predicts blue ones. Nor is there any principled way to prefer the first: a thinker who had grown up with 'grue' and 'bleen' as basic colour words would say exactly the same thing about our word 'green', which to them looks like a gerrymandered, time-indexed mongrel. Goodman's own answer is that we simply fall back on the most entrenched predicates, meaning the words we have used most often — 'green' wins because it is a more familiar word than 'grue'. The moral is that nothing beyond habit and linguistic fashion separates a good projection from a bad one, so every prediction from past evidence is exactly as reasonable as any other."

My friend adds two points of her own. First, that this is precisely why extrapolating the safety of a bridge from thirty years of satisfactory service is arbitrary: 'it will hold until 2050 and then fail' is only grue in another vocabulary. Second, that the riddle has nothing essentially to do with time, so a predicate such as 'grack' — green if the stone weighs under a carat, blue if not — raises exactly the same difficulty.
