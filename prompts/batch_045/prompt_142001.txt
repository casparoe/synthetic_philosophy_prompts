I'm in an undergraduate seminar on early modern philosophy — we finished the *Meditations* last week, with bits of the *Discourse* and the *Principles* assigned alongside — and I've gotten into an argument with a friend in the math department who works on formal epistemology about whether the cogito can serve as the Archimedean point Descartes says he needs: the one firm and immovable point from which the rest can be moved. I've ended up convinced that it cannot, but she says I've conflated two separate questions and I can't see where, so I'd like you to go through my reasoning and tell me which steps are straightforwardly wrong, which are genuinely contested among people who know the texts, and which are right but don't entail what I take them to entail.

My reasoning runs in three steps. First: whatever Descartes says, "I think, therefore I am" is an inference, and as such it needs a suppressed major premise of the form "whatever thinks, exists" — so it can be no more secure than that general claim. I know that in his replies to the objections Mersenne collected he insists the point is not derived by any syllogism but is recognized by a simple intuition of the mind; that strikes me as a dodge, since either it is an inference, in which case it needs the premise, or it isn't an argument at all, in which case it's hard to see how it *proves* anything. Second: the needed premise is itself a clear and distinct perception, and the reliability of clear and distinct perception is what Descartes secures — by means of clear and distinct perception — only after he has proved that a non-deceiving God exists, in the Third Meditation. So the Cartesian circle eats the Archimedean point itself: "I am, I exist" is only as good as a rule that isn't certified until later, and so it can't be what gets us there. Third, and here I think I'm on firmer ground: look at the actual wording. The familiar Latin formula doesn't appear as such in the *Meditations* at all (it first shows up, as I understand it, in a marginal note to the *Principles*); what the Second Meditation gives us is that this proposition, I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind. But that guarantee holds only in the moment of thinking it, and Descartes has to carry the result forward while he's occupied two meditations later — and certainty about what one merely remembers having established is precisely what the divine guarantee is supposed to underwrite. So even on his own terms the foundation exists only on the instant, and can't be built on.

So here is my repair, which is the part my friend says is weakest. Drop the "I" and drop the inference: what is genuinely indubitable is a token-reflexive, self-verifying thought — any occurrence of the thought that this very thought is occurring now is true, and no demon could make it false, since making it false would require it to occur, which would make it true. That, I claim, gives Descartes the one unshakeable certainty he wants without smuggling in a subject, and it also answers the objection (I believe it originates with Gassendi) that all Descartes was entitled to conclude was that thinking is occurring, not that *he* thinks. My friend's reply was that this "proves" nothing capable of bearing the weight of the physics, because Descartes needs a thinking substance whose essence is thought, not a sentence that verifies itself — and I don't see why the latter can't be made to do the former's work.
