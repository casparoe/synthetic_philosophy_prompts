A friend and I argued until well past midnight about whether there is any fact of the matter about what exists in a domain when the evidence that would settle the question is, in one way or another, beyond reach, and we kept talking past each other because neither of us ever said what a good answer to that question would have to accomplish. So before we resume, I want the specification written down rather than the verdict.

The area is this. An agent is embedded inside some domain and has to adopt one of several candidate inventories of what there is, in order to plan, predict, and talk to other agents about it. Some of the differences between candidate inventories are invisible from where the agent sits. I want to distinguish three grades of invisibility, and I would like you to keep them separate throughout. Call a gap penumbral if some finite extension of the agent's access would close it, asymptotic if no finite extension closes it but an unending sequence of probes would converge on an answer in the limit, and absolute if no extension of access of any kind closes it. Call a gap reflexive if whether it closes depends on which inventory the agent adopts, because one candidate says the probe that would discriminate is constructible while the other says it is not. I care about the reflexive ones most, since that is roughly where our argument stalled.

Write me the desiderata: what any adequate account of ontology identification under inaccessible information must deliver. Give me the cases it has to get right, including at least the reflexive case above, the case of two agents with disjoint but individually penumbral access who disagree with each other, and the case of candidates that agree on every probe anyone can actually run but disagree about what would happen under a probe only one of them permits. Give me the distinctions it must not collapse: invisible versus merely unobserved, gaps that are the agent's fault versus gaps in the world, identification for one agent versus identification for a community that pools its access, and settling a question versus merely deciding how to act while it is open. Give me the results it must not contradict, in particular that permanent underdetermination can happen and that a verdict reached only in the limit is not a verdict an agent ever has on hand. Behind each requirement, say why it is a requirement and whether you think it is non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp. Fix the purpose as follows: the account is supposed to be usable from inside the domain, by a bounded agent acting in finite time, and it is supposed to tell two disagreeing agents whether their disagreement is substantive or merely verbal.

Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidates. I have in mind the realist who lets identification outrun evidence, the verificationist who ties ontology to accessible difference, the pragmatist who weighs the costs of getting it wrong, the structuralist who identifies only up to structure, and the learning-theoretic picture that settles for identification in the limit; a sentence or two on how each fares against the list is enough, since the list is what I am after. I do not need names of authors, papers, or further reading. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a careful email.
