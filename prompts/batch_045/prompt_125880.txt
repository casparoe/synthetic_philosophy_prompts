I'm putting together a graduate seminar session on decision instability, and I want to pair the Death in Damascus case with Maugham's retelling of the fable of the appointment in Samarra: the servant who is jostled by Death in the Baghdad marketplace, borrows his master's horse, and rides for Samarra, only to learn afterwards that the gesture was one of surprise rather than threat, since the appointment with him was in Samarra that night. I'd like a reading rather than a tutorial: take causal and evidential decision theory as given and use them on the text.

Three questions. First, how exactly should the servant's situation be modelled, and how much of the standard case survives? In Death in Damascus, Death is an accurate predictor whose prediction tracks the agent's own deliberation, and that tracking is what makes the problem unstable: no deterministic policy self-ratifies, and the naive causal computation delivers a survival estimate that the agent knows to be wrong. In the fable the appointment looks as though it is already in Samarra before the servant chooses anything, so Death is right about where he will be without, apparently, tracking what he decides. If so there is no loop to break and riding to Samarra is simply the action that causally kills him. Tell me which structure the fable supports, what Death's reliability would have to consist in on the fixed-appointment reading, and what turns on the difference.

Second, on that reading, where is the servant's mistake: in his destination or in his model of Death? He rides to Samarra on the reasoning that Death will not find him there, which seems to presuppose a tracker — a Death whose location depends on his — in which case no destination helps; the fable's Death instead has an appointment at a place. So his error looks causal rather than evidential, and it looks as though both theories license the ride while neither has the machinery to call it a mistake. Is that the right moral, or does one of the two theories do better with the servant's inference from Death's presence in Baghdad?

Third, where does the text resist the reading? The fable is standardly taken as fatalist — O'Hara borrowed it as the title for a novel about a man bent on his own destruction — but on the fixed-appointment reading it is not fatalist at all: Nineveh would have served as well as Samarra. Does the decision-theoretic reading cost the fable that ambiguity, what does the fable show about acting under an accurate prediction that the formalism cannot represent, and would you in the end teach the pairing at all, or does it collapse the story into a puzzle with a moral?
