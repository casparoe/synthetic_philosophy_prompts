I write the animal welfare standard for a mid-sized food manufacturer. We revise it twice a year, and each revision allocates a fixed premium budget (currently about three per cent of ingredient spend) across the welfare changes we require of suppliers. Once we commit to a change it binds for two years, so a bad allocation is expensive and slow to undo. Our ingredient base spans, in rough order of volume: broiler chickens, laying hens, pigs, farmed salmon, farmed whiteleg shrimp, and — increasingly, because we reformulate a blended protein ingredient and can shift its composition at near-zero cost — mealworm and black soldier fly larvae. That reformulation lever is the reason I need help: I can move volume between species cheaply, so cross-species comparisons that are academic for most companies are load-bearing for us.

What I need is a decision procedure, not an essay. Specifically: an ordered set of tests that takes a proposed welfare change and yields fund it, don't fund it, or hand it back to a human; a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each test; the points where the procedure stops with a no; the points where it has to stop and let a person decide because the question is one of values rather than facts; and an honest list of the cases it cannot settle at all.

Four constraints on the procedure. It has to be runnable in a working day by an analyst with a spreadsheet and no philosophy training, using evidence a supplier can actually produce (stocking densities, mortality and cull figures, stunning and slaughter method, lighting, stocking, enrichment, breed, transport time) rather than welfare science we cannot verify from here. It has to say what to do when a needed number simply is not available, and how much that gap matters. It has to be defensible in two or three sentences to a five-person sourcing committee who will ask why this species and not that one. And it cannot rest on contested claims about which animals are sentient: our legal team will not sign a standard whose justification depends on a disputed scientific question, so the procedure has to function under unresolved uncertainty rather than wait for it to be resolved. Please don't send me to the literature or give me a reading list — we have no journal access from this office; give me the argument itself, in premises I can put in front of the committee.

Five things I don't know how to build into a checklist, and which I suspect are where the real work is.

First: I keep being told that like interests should get like weight, which plainly cannot mean treating a shrimp like a pig. I need to know which differences between species are allowed to move the number, and which are just the fact of species membership wearing a costume. Our customers care a great deal about chickens and essentially not at all about shrimp or fish; our legal team's working assumption is that nothing below farmed fish is legally protected in the markets we sell in; our comms team says a standard whose largest single spend is shrimp welfare will read as a joke. Some of those reactions I think are the thing I am trying not to smuggle in, but not all of them, and I can't separate them. Tell me which of them the procedure may treat as reasons, which it should flag as suspect, and whether there is any non-question-begging way to take the comms problem into account at all.

Second: death and a bad life are not the same complaint, and our changes trade them against each other. A slower-growing broiler breed suffers less in the last weeks but lives more days and needs more birds per tonne of meat, so the same order is filled by more animals each living longer. I don't know whether the procedure should count that as a cost, a benefit, or nothing.

Third: numbers versus intensity per animal. The shrimp and insect lines involve millions of individuals, each plausibly capable of very little experience, against tens of thousands of pigs and chickens capable of a great deal. I have been using a back-of-envelope bad-days-averted-per-euro figure; tell me whether to keep that unit, what it silently assumes, and what to use when the per-animal amount is not just small but possibly zero.

Fourth: the reply I get from every supplier I push, which is that these animals exist only because we buy them, so if tighter standards raise prices and demand falls there will be fewer of them, and a life worth living that never happens is not a benefit anyone loses. I think something is wrong with this but I can't say what, and it is deployed selectively in a way I don't trust.

Fifth: scope. Where does the procedure stop — at insects, at shrimp, at bivalves, at plants — and on what principle, given that each extension multiplies the numbers by an order of magnitude while the probability of any experience at all falls?

Please then run the finished procedure on three live proposals and show the work, including where it hands a question back to me:

1. Replace asphyxiation-in-ice with electrical stunning for our farmed shrimp supply, at roughly a quarter of a eurocent per serving.
2. Switch a broiler line to a slower-growing breed: clear welfare gains in the final weeks, a real cost premium, more bird-days per tonne of meat.
3. Move the shell-egg and egg-ingredient supply from furnished cages to multi-tier aviaries, given that our own audit data from aviary sites shows mortality and injury figures I am not comfortable publishing.

I expect at least one of these to come out somewhere I would rather not take to the committee. Say which, and why I should accept it. Also state plainly what the procedure cannot do, which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where it will systematically misfire.

Finally: for each substantive claim the procedure rests on, tell me how confident you are, and single out the one you are least sure of. Then tell me how the ranking of the three cases would change if that least-sure claim is false — I want to know which parts of the standard are load-bearing on a contested point before I sign it.
