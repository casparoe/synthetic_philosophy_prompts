I'm in the upper sixth and I run my school's philosophy society. Next week I have to lead a forty-minute session on the Principal Principle — roughly, the claim that if you know the objective chance of an outcome is x, then your credence in that outcome should be x — for about a dozen other sixth-formers. Last week's session ended with the group more or less concluding that probability is just how confident you feel, so I need to prise credence and chance apart without simply announcing that objective chances exist.

Please plan the explanation rather than give it: the order the components have to come in and what depends on what; whether the case I've been building the session around can carry the whole thing or should be swapped out, and tell me what such a case has to have and, more importantly, lack in order to do the job (I was going to use a raffle drum that a technician has secretly weighted, so that the students can be told the true 1-in-50 chance before the draw while still not knowing who will win — but I worry it invites exactly the reading I need to get rid of, that chance is just our ignorance of the weighting); the three misconceptions to expect, in the order they'll form — (1) that chance is just ignorance, (2) that the principle tells you to disregard everything else you know, (3) that there can't be a chance for a one-off event that is never repeated — and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; and what I should flatly decline to get into at this level.

Two further constraints. First, one member who plays a lot of poker will say that a known 30% chance is nothing more than the number the house would take on a bet, and that talk of a chance "out there in the world" is just that dressed up; I want this absorbed by the sequence rather than batted away. Second, I'd like, if it can be made to land in the last five minutes, to show them a "chance dial" on a lab machine whose reading was itself set by looking at yesterday's results, so that trusting the dial seems to require not fully trusting the dial — say whether this belongs in the session or is the thing I should refuse.

Finally, give me one closing question that would separate someone who has grasped the principle from someone who has merely learned to repeat that credence should equal chance. Finish with a single line in the form \answer{carrying example; stages at which misconceptions (1), (2), (3) are defused}, e.g. \answer{weighted raffle drum; 2, 3, 5}.
