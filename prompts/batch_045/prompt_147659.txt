Hey — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training beyond some half-remembered blog posts, and I got nerd-sniped by this language of thought idea (Fodor and co.?): the claim that thinking is basically an interpreter running over sentence-like data structures in your head. I don't want a reading list or citations, I want your actual take, working from the usual arguments people give: that thoughts are productive (unboundedly many of them), systematic (if you can think "the cat is on the mat" you can think "the mat is under the cat"), and compositional (built out of reusable parts).

Framing I'll use: suppose the brain ships with some bytecode / IR that thoughts are written in. Here's a list of features such an inner language might have. Please sort them into tiers by how confident you'd be that whatever is actually going on in a human brain has that feature — Tier A = I'd bet money on it, Tier B = genuinely could go either way, Tier C = probably us projecting computers onto neurons. One sentence each on why it lands where it does.

1. Discrete, re-tokenable symbols — the same "word" can appear in tons of different thoughts, roughly like several structures holding pointers to one shared object
2. Recursion / arbitrarily deep nesting
3. Variables with scope: slots that get bound and filled in
4. Logical constants — and, or, not, plus quantifier-ish stuff (some / every)
5. Static typing, in the sense that some combinations are simply unrepresentable rather than just never produced
6. Content-addressable retrieval: fetching a thought by what it says rather than by a fixed address
7. Laziness — most of what you "believe" is never actually computed until something forces it
8. A single serial instruction stream (as opposed to massively parallel with no global clock)
9. Garbage collection / forgetting as reclamation
10. An immutable, append-only belief store: updating a belief writes a new version and keeps the old one around with something like a timestamp, instead of overwriting in place

The bit I keep getting stuck on, and where I'd most like your ranking: I'm calling it the hot-swap problem. If my belief "standup is at 10" gets updated to "standup is at 11," is that like mutating shared memory — every structure that referenced the old value instantly sees the new one, with all the usual aliasing weirdness — or is it more like event sourcing with immutable values, where old versions persist and you get stale-read behaviour (you keep walking toward the 10am room after you "know" it's 11, or you keep planning around a restaurant you've already read has closed)?

So: which of those two architectures does the evidence fit better, and if it's the immutable/event-sourced one, how much of the classical language-of-thought picture actually survives? Give me a single number 0–1 for how much you'd keep.

And one more number while you're at it: of the explanatory work the language of thought does — explaining productivity, systematicity, how thought can be about stuff, how reasoning steps follow each other — what fraction is really being done by compositionality alone, with the rest of the machinery (the instruction set, the serial/parallel question, the symbol tokens) along for the ride?
