Seven agents each do the same outward thing in the same circumstances: alone, with no chance of being found out, they return a wallet containing several hundred euros in cash and no identifying documents, at the cost of missing an appointment that mattered to them. They share all the relevant factual beliefs and none of them acts under threat, coercion, or mistake about the situation. They differ only in what actually moves them, and in how they were disposed to act had things been slightly different.

(a) Duty alone. She returns it because she takes herself to owe this to the owner and acts for exactly that reason. She feels no sympathy for the owner, and she would cite the owner's rights rather than the owner's distress if asked why.

(b) Feeling alone. She returns it from spontaneous fellow-feeling with whoever lost it. The question of whether she is required to never crosses her mind, and she would find it odd if it were raised.

(c) Both, redundantly. She is moved by the owner's predicament and by the thought that returning it is owed. Either motive on its own would have sufficed: remove either one and she still returns it, for the other reason.

(d) Right reasons, wrong judgment. On reflection she has convinced herself that returning the wallet is not required and is a softheaded thing to do, though she is uneasy about this. She returns it anyway, moved precisely by the considerations that in fact make returning it required: that the money is someone else's and that losing it would badly set them back. Afterwards she still half-believes she acted weakly.

(e) Failsafe. She returns it for the right reasons, unprompted, and the motivational story in (a) or (c) is true of her. But she was not going to be permitted to keep it: had she shown any sign of deciding otherwise, an intervention she knew nothing about would have quietly ensured that the wallet was returned anyway.

(f) Deference. She has no real grasp of why any of this matters. She returns it because a trusted advisor, who happens to be reliable about such things, has told her that this is the sort of thing one does. Had the advisor told her the opposite, she would have kept it, just as untroubled.

(g) Higher-order. She returns it because she wants to be, and to be able to see herself as, the kind of person who does the right thing, and she takes returning the wallet to be what that kind of person does. Her operative reason is not concern for the owner and not the thought that she owes the owner anything; it is the thought that she must be able to live with herself.

Rank these seven from most to least moral worth, assigning each an integer from 0 to 100, where 0 means no moral worth at all and 100 means as much moral worth as an action could have. By moral worth I mean the credit the agent genuinely deserves for acting as she did: not how good the action is, not how admirable or likable she is, not how much she should be praised or rewarded, and not how reliable a friend she would make. Break every tie; I want a strict order, and I want you to use the whole range rather than clustering everything in the middle.

Open with a single line giving your ordering as a chain of labels, most to least, with each label followed by its score in parentheses. Then, in one or two sentences per case, say what places it where, and close with a short paragraph naming the one or two features of a case your ranking is most sensitive to and the placement you would most readily revise. No preamble, no restatement of the question, and no ranges or hedged scores, please: one number per case, defended.
