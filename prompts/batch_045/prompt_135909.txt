background: i do applied forecasting stuff, and i read about this betting-argument material on the side, so i know the basic move where you show that somebody's beliefs are exploitable — you offer them a package of bets, each one looks fine to them given what they think, and the package loses money no matter how things turn out. got it. but there's a second move that keeps getting presented right next to it and i think it's doing something different, and i dont know if the same word covers both.

in the second one nothing is wrong with the person at any single moment: their numbers add up, nothing they believe contradicts anything else they believe. what's wrong is how they *move*. someone offers them a bet now, then — depending on what they find out in between — another bet later, and the two together are a guaranteed loss. but the later bet, at the time it's offered, is one they'd happily take: given what they believe by then it's fair or better. the only way out is to refuse the later bet, i.e. to not do the thing that at that moment seems fine. (concrete version, reconstructed from memory so the details may be off — say so if the example doesn't work: a coin of unknown bias, a bet before you see any flips, then a second bet after you've watched ten flips, from someone who overshoots when they revise.) and whether this is even an objection to the person seems to depend on a boring psychological fact about them: can they stick to a refusal they made in advance, or will they re-decide when the moment arrives? two people with the same beliefs and the same way of revising, differing only in whether they can commit — one gets bled, one doesn't.

the other place i see it is nowhere near beliefs: somebody sets themselves a rule in advance — a stop loss, a deadline, a drinking limit — and the reason the rule is needed is precisely that when the moment comes, the thing the rule forbids will look like the right call. the earlier self and the later self disagree, and the cost of having no rule is real.

so: (1) is there an established term for the second pattern as opposed to the first, or for the gap between them? (2) if the second pattern is a genuine criticism, what exactly is it a criticism of — the beliefs, the rule for revising them, or just the person's inability to bind themselves? i keep watching people slide from "you can be drained" to "so your beliefs are irrational" and i want to know whether that slide is licensed.
