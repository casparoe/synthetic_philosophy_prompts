So I've been going back and forth with some friends about moral foundations theory for months now and I keep feeling like we're talking past each other because I don't have the vocabulary for it. My background: I do ML work that's adjacent to interpretability, and I've been reading the rationalist blogs for like a decade, so I'm genuinely fluent in things like features and feature dictionaries, sparse autoencoders, reward models and RLHF, reward hacking, Goodhart's law, in-distribution vs out-of-distribution generalization, regularization and overparameterization, mesa-optimizers, Moloch and multipolar traps. My moral psychology knowledge basically stops at having read the Righteous Mind once and a bunch of blog summaries, so I've never seen the actual psychometrics or the factor analyses.

As I understand the descriptive package: there are something like five or six candidate moral modules (care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, plus liberty/oppression bolted on later), posited as innate-ish, evolved-in-advance "taste receptors" that cultures then build different cuisines on top of; left-leaning people reportedly load mostly on care and fairness while right-leaning people use the whole set more evenly; the judgments arrive fast and affectively and the reasons get manufactured afterwards to justify them, which is why you get moral dumbfounding where people insist something is wrong and can't say why when you strip the case to the bone; and the whole thing is supposed to explain why moral argument across the political aisle is so useless, since each side is partly blind to what the other is even looking at. Fine. What I can't get any traction on is the step people then take: does any of that tell us anything whatsoever about who is right? Some people I talk to treat the even-spread-foundations profile as evidence of perceptual richness and the two-foundation profile as a kind of moral scotoma; other people treat the extra foundations as superstition that got grandfathered in; and a third group says the whole question is a category error because the theory is descriptive only.

Here's what I actually want. Translate the theory systematically into the vocabulary I already have: each foundation as a feature or detector, the innateness claim as an inductive bias built into the architecture vs. something left for the optimizer to learn, the intuition-first-plus-post-hoc-rationalization picture as a forward pass plus a story-generating head whose output doesn't reflect the computation that produced the verdict (i.e. unfaithful chain-of-thought), dumbfounding as a confident label with no retrievable rationale, the WEIRD-and-Turk sampling issue as a training distribution and an OOD generalization problem, cultural variation as different downstream fine-tunes on shared base features, and so on. Give me each piece with what survives the move and what has to get bent to fit. Then the part I actually care about, the residue report: which claims have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary, and for each of those, whether that's because my vocabulary is missing something real or because the original claim was doing less work than it sounded like it was. Specific things I want you to be explicit about: (a) the word "right" — in my world the loss function is handed to me from outside, so does the normative question just disappear, or does it come back as "which loss, and under which deployment distribution, and chosen by whom"; (b) the felt quality of moral conviction, the sense that you're not classifying but seeing, which doesn't obviously map onto anything in a classifier; (c) the claim that these things are *foundations* — irreducible modules rather than one utility function with some learned heuristics on top — since a feature dictionary has no principled line between "separate feature" and "one feature with weird geometry"; (d) the move from description to prescription, the "a functioning society needs all of them, each foundation defends against a different failure mode" thing — is that a robustness or anti-Goodharting claim I can cash out, or is it a separate value claim wearing a lab coat; and (e) whether the conservative-vs-liberal profile difference translates as overparameterization vs. under-specification, richer feature coverage vs. noisier fit, or whether that framing is already smuggling a verdict. Please flag every place where your translation is quietly importing an assumption that's doing the argumentative work — if the right comes out looking overfit or the left comes out looking under-specified, I want to know which knob you turned. Last concrete thing, since I think it's the real test: if you actually believed the theory, would you build a reward model differently, and what would you predict about a model trained on a politically skewed annotator pool — would it show the same kind of feature dropout, and what would that mean for the "who's right" question?
