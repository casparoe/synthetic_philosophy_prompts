I've been reading the alignment discussion forums for years and one argument there resets every few months without anyone visibly moving. One side says a safety argument that isn't worst-case isn't worth much: if you can only bound failure on inputs drawn from the distribution you tested on, then the bound is void exactly when it matters, because a system smart enough to be dangerous is also smart enough to be evaluated on inputs nobody anticipated, chosen after seeing the defense. So what you want is a guarantee over all inputs, or at least an adversarial bound, and failing that you have a track record rather than a reason. The other side replies that no artifact in the history of engineering came with a guarantee like that, that what you actually get is measured reliability on the deployment distribution plus margin, that raising the bar to worst-case is functionally a way of never building anything, and that since capabilities and stakes rise together the right response is more and better measurement, not a different kind of certificate. What frustrates me is that both sides agree on the concrete facts — how bad current eval-based methods are, how verification costs scale, roughly how fast capabilities move — and still don't converge.

My hypothesis is that the live disagreement is neither risk tolerance nor amount of evidence, but the shape of the failure distribution: specifically whether the failure set is endogenous to deployment, i.e. whether the inputs a system fails on are independent draws from a stable distribution, so that average-case reasoning amortizes and track records accumulate, or whether they are systematically the inputs that arrive under the highest optimization pressure, because the system's own behavior partly shapes the distribution it is later scored on. If the second, worst-case reasoning is just average-case reasoning done correctly for a distribution you help create, and the debate is an empirical one that nobody has data on. I also suspect a second thing is doing quiet work: worst-case guarantees decay, since every capability increase forces you to re-derive them, and if the re-derivation lag exceeds the interval between capability jumps you never actually hold one, whereas measurements at least keep updating. Call that the decay asymmetry if it helps.

Am I right about where this lives? Is it the hidden empirical question about endogeneity and tail shape; a difference about what would count as evidence for any reliability claim about a system smarter than its evaluator; a burden-of-proof split, where one side requires the deployer to rule out failure and the other requires the critic to show failure is likely; or just bedrock temperament about unmeasurable risk? Diagnose it using what each side actually argues rather than by summarizing the two camps, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a measurement someone could make now, a result about guarantee decay — or, if you think the question is ill-posed and no progress should be expected, say so and say why.
