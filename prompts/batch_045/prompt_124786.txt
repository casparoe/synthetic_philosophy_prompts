I've been running a graduate seminar on mental causation for two years, and the debate over the causal exclusion argument has not moved by an inch, so what I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Take the argument in its standard form: the physical domain is causally complete, mental properties are distinct from physical ones, effects are not systematically overdetermined, therefore mental properties cause nothing. The familiar replies — deny completeness, embrace systematic overdetermination, treat mental causation as inherited from or grounded in physical causation, or move to an interventionist or proportionality-sensitive account on which different levels simply carve the same counterfactual structure differently — have all been on the table for decades; everyone in the room can rehearse them, and nobody ever concedes anything.

My hypothesis is that several distinct disagreements are traveling together under one label: whether causation is a relation in nature or a feature of an explanatory model; whether the completeness of physics is an empirical discovery about this world or a methodological commitment that partly constitutes what we will count as a physical explanation at all; and what the mental cause is supposed to contribute in the first place — an additional counterfactual difference-making role, or a kind of intelligibility that a physical description was never in the business of duplicating. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually commits to, and tell me whether one of these is doing all the real work while the others are decoration.

To force the issue I have been using a case I have not found anywhere in the literature, and I want to know both whether it is coherent and which way it cuts. Call it the unstable screener-off case: suppose we possess a complete interventionist model of some decision in which every mental-level intervention has some neural-level variable that screens it off, but which neural variables do the screening off varies from trial to trial, so that no single stable neural-level generalization is available, while the mental-level generalization remains more invariant across contexts than any neural-level one. Exclusion appears vindicated by the first condition and undermined by the other two. Is that a genuine possibility, or does it collapse as soon as one side's account of causation is applied strictly?

Please answer in American English. Give me a diagnosis supported by reasons rather than a survey of positions; say whether my threefold hypothesis is correct, incomplete, or merely a restatement of the problem; assess the unstable screener-off case; and close by saying what, given your diagnosis, would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding, a change of question — or by arguing that no progress should be expected, and why.
