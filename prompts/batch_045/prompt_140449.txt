I'm putting together a thesis chapter on whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real, and I want to hang it on Ready Player One — I know its a geeky nostalgia romp rather than a thought experiment, but that's rather the point: it takes virtual stuff seriously in a way most of the literature doesnt.

The framework I've been sketching has two conditions and I'm not convinced they belong together. Call the first reachability: a virtual object counts only if some player could in principle get to it and do something with it. Call the second irreversibility: a virtual loss counts as a harm only if no one with admin rights could undo it by rolling the world-state back. The case that made me think they pull apart is the five years after Halliday dies when nobody finds a single key — the egg is fully specified in the code the whole time, and the will makes it the vehicle of a real fortune and real legal control over the OASIS, yet nobody can reach it. Then push the pair through the rest of the book: the Cataclyst wiping out every avatar and all their gear on Planet Doom, people ending up in IOI loyalty centres working off OASIS debts, the moment near the end where destroying an avatar is what gets somebody out of IOI captivity. Which condition is doing the work in each case, and is either of them anything more than my own prejudice about persistence?

I'd also like the honest version: where does Cline simply not think about any of this — especially the ending where Wade wins everything and then closes the OASIS a couple of days a week so people go outside (I always mix up which days). Does that undercut a realist reading of virtual goods, or does it actually presuppose one? And is there anything the novel notices that the philosophy papers miss — that virtual goods have a live exchange rate against real currency, or that one can be the bearer of a property right, for instance?
