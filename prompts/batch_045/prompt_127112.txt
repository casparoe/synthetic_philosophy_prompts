I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — a cosmologist, and a very stubborn one — about whether there is anything real to explain in the old question of why there is something rather than nothing. She holds a version of the deflationary view: that the alleged contrast state is not a genuine alternative, that nothing is not a possible state of affairs a universe could have been in and failed to be in, and that the question mostly gets its appearance of depth from grammar taking a quantifier for a name. I hold that even after you strip all of that away there is a residual demand for an explanation, and I think the interesting disagreements are not between theism and atheism but further upstream, about what kind of thing would even count as an answer. We gave up on convincing each other and instead agreed to build something: a decision tree that a smart non-specialist could walk through and land on a named position, so that the reading group I run (half philosophers, half physicists, all of them allergic to being told what they believe) can find out where they actually stand rather than which label they like.

Here is what I want you to write. Start from the logical space and not from a list of names: give me the diagnostic questions first, in an order, then locate the actual positions in the cells those questions carve out. The branch nodes should be distinctions that a reader can answer without knowing what they lead to, and I want these in particular — though if you think a better one belongs on the tree, put it in and tell me why. One: the thickness of the void — whether the contrast state is merely the absence of concrete objects (with laws, numbers, and modal facts somehow still in place) or the absence of absolutely everything, including the space of possibilities itself, in which case the question may be unaskable in its own terms. Two: what I have been calling the default-of-the-void claim — whether the empty state is presumptively in need of no explanation, so that the entire explanatory burden falls on the non-empty, or whether something and nothing sit symmetrically with respect to the demand for an explanation. Three: whether the question is being asked contrastively at all — why something *rather than* nothing — or as a flat question about why anything exists, and if contrastively, what supplies the contrast. Four: the leakage test, which is the bit my friend and I kept circling: whether an explanation that successfully rules out the void but leaves the rest of modal space wide open — why this rather than that, why so much rather than a single grain of dust — should count as having answered anything, or whether it is leaking. Five: the mechanism of explanation on offer, i.e. causal and temporal, non-causal grounding, or bare modal necessitation. Six: what I am calling the inheritance problem — if the whole is self-explaining or necessary, does that necessity come to it from the necessity of its parts, or is it emergent at the level of the totality. Seven: the scope of the explanatory principle one accepts, from a全局 principle covering even the big conjunctive fact of everything-that-is, down through a principle restricted to contingent facts, down to no such principle at all. Eight: tolerance for modal collapse, i.e. whether an explanation that entails that everything had to be as it is counts as a successful answer or as a reductio of the assumption that got you there.

The deliverable: eight to ten branch nodes terminating in ten to fourteen leaves, each leaf carrying the name of the position, or the note that nobody occupies it, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment and a line or two of blurb suitable for a your result are-reading-group-page. Please make sure the leaves include, somewhere, the brute-fact stopping point, a necessary-being or necessary-ground style answer, a necessitarian answer of the everything-had-to-be-so kind, a self-grounding or self-causing cosmos, an infinite regress of contingent explainers that is claimed to be vicious or benign, the denial that the void was ever possible, and the deflationary presupposition-failure view — and that my own cosmologist friend's position is not quietly made to look like a refusal to answer, since on the tree it should be a genuine leaf. Then add a short section on the unoccupied cells: for each combination of answers that no one defends, say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored, and say the same for any two leaves your tree cannot separate (I suspect the theistic necessary being and a bare non-theistic necessary state of affairs will collapse into each other, and I want that flagged rather than disguised). Finally, tell me which two leaves my friend and I would land on and which single node our whole disagreement actually turns on. Aim for something around 1200 to 1600 words, prose plus the tree, and don't give me any bibliography, reading list, or citations — I know the literature well enough to fill in names later, and I want the tree to stand on the distinctions alone.
