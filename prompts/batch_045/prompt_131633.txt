Right, something silly but conceptually load-bearing, please. Write me a parody of the annual letter to shareholders from the founder/CEO of a fintech company whose entire business model is running money pumps on people with dynamically inconsistent preferences — call it Cycles or whatever you like. Deadpan corporate-founder voice, 650–750 words, British English, with a few bullet-point "key performance indicators" and maybe three or four numbered sections.

The jokes have to come from the theory being right, not from the theory being silly. You know the setup: a customer whose preferences loop (A over B over C over A, or who just changes their mind about what they want as time passes) will pay a small fee for each swap, ends up where they started and poorer. Things I want in there:

- The three customer segments the letter can't stop talking about: the myopic ones who accept every swap and go round the loop all afternoon; the sophisticated ones who do the backwards induction, see where it ends, and refuse the very first trade — so we've had to start charging them for "planning services" instead; and the resolute ones who adopt a plan at t0 and then bloody well stick to it, who are near impossible to monetise and whom the letter obviously resents.
- The compliance problem. We're only allowed to run a pump if what looks like a change of mind isn't a genuine change of taste, so our compliance team has to document that the preference doing the work at each step is the same preference rather than a new one. Have the founder boast about having "solved" this while making it visible that the distinction can't be drawn in the cases that matter.
- The new flagship product — name it yourself, but the idea is that we've closed the precommitment loophole by selling commitment: customers pay to adopt a plan, then pay again to amend it, so the escape hatch from the pump becomes the revenue line. Resolution-as-a-Service, something equally grim.
- A real gripe for the founder to explain to investors: the more rational the customer, the less we make off them. Maybe the churn model has a name for resolutes — "the dead", or similar — and there's a slide about it.
- Anywhere the theory is genuinely silent or awkward, don't paper over it, let the letter blunder into it: e.g. that a sophisticated chooser can end up worse off than a naive one, or that nobody can really say why foreseeing the loop means you should refuse trade one rather than trade three.

Target the practice and the argument — that exploitability by a pump is what makes cyclic or diachronically inconsistent preferences irrational — not any real person or firm. Invent the founder, the regulator (something like the Office for Diachronic Fair Trading) and any academic you want to pull-quote; no real quotations from anyone. Keep it legible: someone who's never read any decision theory should be able to tell what's being taken the piss out of, and a decision theorist should definately think it's fair rather than a strawman.
