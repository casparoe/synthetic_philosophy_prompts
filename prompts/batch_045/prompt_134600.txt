THE QUESTION

Q3. Two envelopes are placed on a table. You are reliably told that one contains exactly twice the other, and that one was handed to you at random. Let S denote the smaller of the two amounts, and let g be your prior density for S.

(a) (5 marks) Before opening anything, you reason: "Let x be the amount in my envelope. The other envelope contains either 2x or x/2, each with probability 1/2, so its expected value is (1/2)(2x) + (1/2)(x/2) = 5x/4, which is more than x. So I should switch." Explain precisely what is wrong with this argument, and show what the correct calculation looks like.

(b) (7 marks) Now suppose you open your envelope and see a cheque for a dollars. (i) Give the general condition, in terms of g, under which switching has positive expected value given the observation. (ii) Now suppose specifically that your prior for the smaller amount is exponential with mean $500. For which observed amounts should you switch? What should you do if you see $80, and what if you see $600?

(c) (8 marks) Finally, suppose instead that your prior for the smaller amount is flat — constant density over the whole positive real line, which is an improper prior. Show that for every value a you might observe, your posterior probability that you hold the smaller envelope is 1/2, so that switching looks worth a/4 whatever you see. Yet before opening, the symmetry argument of part (a) says switching is worth exactly nothing. Are these two results in genuine conflict? Can you conclude that the policy "open the envelope and then switch whatever you see" is better than switching without opening? Explain carefully.

THE ANSWER (verbatim, typos included)

(a) The fallacy is an equivocation on x. Write S for the smaller amount, so the pair is (S, 2S) and the total on the table is 3S. If I hold the smaller envelope then x = S and the other contains 2S; if I hold the larger then x = 2S and the other contains S. So the "x" in the two branches of the argument denotes different quantities, and you can't hold it fixed while averaging over the branches — the branch is what fixes what x is. Doing it properly with S held fixed: E[my envelope] = (1/2)(S) + (1/2)(2S) = 3S/2, and E[other envelope] = (1/2)(2S) + (1/2)(S) = 3S/2. Equal, as symmetry already told us it had to be. Note this is conditional on S and doesn't require any assumption about how S was chosen, or that it has a finite mean.

(b) Once I've seen a there are two live hypotheses: H_s, the smaller amount is a (I hold the smaller, other contains 2a), and H_l, the smaller amount is a/2 (I hold the larger, other contains a/2). I was handed an envelope at random, so each hypothesis gets likelihood 1/2 for the observation; those factors cancel and the posterior odds are just the prior odds, g(a) : g(a/2). So P(H_s | a) = g(a)/(g(a) + g(a/2)) and P(H_l | a) = g(a/2)/(g(a) + g(a/2)).

If I switch I gain a if H_s (going from a to 2a) and lose a/2 if H_l (going from a to a/2), so

E[gain | a] = [a·g(a) − (a/2)·g(a/2)] / [g(a) + g(a/2)],

which is positive exactly when g(a) > g(a/2)/2. This is why the "2x or x/2" reasoning isn't simply dead in the open-envelope case: seeing a is informative, and whether you should switch now depends on your prior. It isn't settled by symmetry any more, because conditional on a the two hypotheses aren't symmetric.

For the exponential prior with mean 500, g(s) = (1/500)e^(−s/500), which is strictly decreasing. The practical content of the condition is that you should switch while the amount you see is still down in the lower half of the distribution of the smaller amount — i.e. below the median of g — because that's the region where a is a serious candidate for being the smaller of the pair; once a is up in the upper half of the distribution, it's far more likely to be the top of a pair than the bottom of one, since g is decreasing. The median of an exponential with mean 500 is 500·ln 2, about $347. So if I see $80 I switch, well inside the lower half; if I see $600 I keep it, since that's above the median and much more likely to be the larger amount of a $300/$600 pair than the smaller of a $600/$1200 pair.

(c) With g(s) = c constant, the posterior odds are c : c = 1 : 1 whatever a turns up, so P(H_s | a) = 1/2 for every a, and E[gain | a] = (1/2)(a) − (1/2)(a/2) = a/4 > 0 for every a. Worth saying that this prior isn't "no information about S" — it's a very strong commitment, e.g. it says the smaller amount is as likely to lie in [1000, 2000] as in [1, 2], and it can't be normalised, which is why it's improper.

I don't think there's a real conflict with (a), and I think the upshot is that opening the envelope genuinely helps. In (a) switching comes out worthless because the average runs over both hypotheses with equal weight and the +S and −S cancel. But once I've looked and seen a particular value, I'm in a situation where whatever value turned up, the conditional expectation favours switching — a/4 is positive for every a. So the policy "open, then switch whatever you see" has positive expected gain at every point at which it can be applied, whereas switching blind is worth zero by (a). The two policies in fact agree on what to do — always switch — but open-then-switch reaches the positive figure by conditioning on the observation, so it dominates switching blind: looking is worth about a/4, and the symmetry result in (a) is really just an artefact of refusing to condition. The general moral is that information is valuable, and (a) only shows that switching is worthless for someone who declines to look. For any proper prior with a finite mean the region where switching pays can't be all of (0, ∞), since integrating up positive conditional gains would contradict (a) — so in practice you get a finite cutoff beyond which you keep the envelope.
