I am a novelist, halfway through the second book of a trilogy, and I have written myself into a structural problem that I keep bumping into in Tolkien, so I would rather read him properly than piously. The premise of my own books is that a handful of rival polities are competing for a scarce, self-reinforcing asset — an ore that sharpens whoever holds it and, at the same time, quietly makes that holder more willing to do whatever is required to keep it. Every faction hates what it is becoming; each is convinced that if it alone refrains, a rival will not, and that the resulting disadvantage can never be recovered; so all of them accelerate. I want that to be a genuine race to the bottom, not a backdrop of general nastiness: the reader should feel that no one is stupid or wicked, only that the payoffs are arranged so that restraint is unilateral disarmament. So I would like a reading of The Lord of the Rings that takes that structure seriously and tells me whether the One Ring actually instantiates it. Is it a good whose acquisition by anyone forces everyone else to seek it, whose use degrades the user, and whose possession cannot be credibly renounced — that is, a real multipolar trap whose horror comes from the situation rather than from the object's magic — or is it simply a corrupting talisman, so that the temptation is a supernatural premise and nobody's reasoning has anything to teach me? I would like this argued through the scenes rather than asserted: Saruman's reasoning that the Wise must take it before Sauron does; Boromir's argument at the Council that the Free Peoples should turn the thing to their own defence; Denethor, who has concluded the contest is already lost and therefore defects out of the game altogether; Sauron himself, who is not a symmetric player at all but something closer to a hegemon, which may make this a duopoly with a dominant party rather than a Moloch; the smaller case of the palantíri, where merely looking is itself a risk that one's rivals are also running; and above all the refusals — Gandalf, Galadriel, Faramir, Sam — and what it costs each of them to walk out of the race. Which features are doing the real work in making the trap legible, and which are decoration?

The part I am most stuck on is the ending, and I suspect it is where a novelist has most to learn from Tolkien's choices. A well-built trap has no solution from inside the payoff structure; that is what makes it a trap. Tolkien resolves his at the Cracks of Doom through something no agent chose and none could have planned — the creature who is himself the trap's most damaged victim, seizing the thing and falling with it — and he underwrites the whole design with a providential order: Gandalf's insistence at the Council that Bilbo was meant to come by the Ring, that he himself was meant to guide Frodo, and that there is a purpose at work beyond the Ring-maker's; the chain of sparing that runs from Bilbo in the tunnels under the Misty Mountains, through Frodo's pity for Gollum in the Emyn Muil, to the moment that pity is repaid. My fear is that this is a cheat, and a cheat with a moral in it: that the only exit from a race to the bottom is grace, which is to say, nothing a character does and nothing a novelist can stage as an action. But I half-believe the opposite — that Tolkien is making a true point about exits, that they require a move for which the payoff structure has no slot, such as restraint that is not conditional on reciprocation, or a refusal to treat the contested thing as a good at all. I also notice that he puts one character entirely outside the trap and then declines to use him: Tom Bombadil is untouched by the Ring, and Gandalf's objection to handing it over is that he would forget it and would not understand why it had to be destroyed — immunity to the bait is not the same as reliability in disposing of it, which is exactly the sort of distinction I need.

So: is the providential frame what holds the ending together, or does it quietly dissolve the trap the book spent so long constructing? Where does the novel resist the reading outright — is the Ring's power moral and personal rather than strategic, such that refusal is always genuinely available and the trap is only ever self-deception, a story characters tell themselves to excuse what they wanted anyway? If the answer is that Tolkien is doing something subtler than either a game or a miracle, I would like that spelled out as craft advice: given that I want my own race to the bottom to end without either a massacre or a deus ex machina, what do his structural choices license me to do — distributing the refusals across several characters at different costs, making the defection sympathetic and reasonable rather than villainous, routing the resolution through the participant the trap has most deformed — and what do they license me not to do? Please write in British English, and please be willing to say where the analogy between the Ring and a multipolar trap breaks down rather than pressing the comparison past the point where it pays.
