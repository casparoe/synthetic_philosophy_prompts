And here is one student's answer, typed up pretty much verbatim (I've left their typos in):

(a) An equilateral triangle inscribed in a circle has side √3 times the radius, so the question is how likely the chord is to exceed that. The three classical methods: fix one endpoint and pick the other uniformly on the circumference — the chord is long exactly when the second endpoint lands in the 120° arc facing the first, giving 1/3. Pick a radius uniformly and a point uniformly along it, and draw the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point — the chord is long just when the point lies within R/2 of the centre, giving 1/2. Pick the chord's midpoint uniformly in the disk — again long just when the midpoint is within R/2 of the centre, which is a quarter of the area, giving 1/4. Each of these looks like a textbook application of the principle of indifference: no reason to prefer one endpoint, one radius, or one midpoint to another, so spread the probability evenly. But then the same question, asked in the absence of any relevant evidence, receives three different answers, so the principle generates contradictions and has to be abandoned. In particular there is no such thing as an objectively correct uninformative prior, which is why subjective Bayesians are happy to say that priors are just a matter of personal taste.

(b) M4 is the midpoint method in disguise. A chord is fixed by its midpoint together with its orientation, and M4 picks the point uniformly in the disk, which is exactly what the midpoint method does; choosing a direction afterwards is an extra step that cannot change anything, since by rotational symmetry the direction of a chord is independent of how long it is. So M4 gives 1/4 as well, and it adds nothing to the dialectic — it is method 3 described twice, which shows only that there are many equivalent ways of describing the same selection procedure.

(c) The claim is basically right. Jaynes's point is that "choose a chord at random" is not a well posed question, because it doesn't describe any experiment, so of course different experiments give different numbers. Once we impose the symmetries that the statement of the problem does licence, the answer is pinned down. We are entitled to assume that nothing depends on the orientation of the circle or on its size, and invariance under rotation and rescaling forces the chord's distance from the centre to be uniform, which gives 1/2. The endpoint and midpoint methods, by contrast, covertly depend on where the circle sits and on how big it is — they are defined in terms of the circle's circumference and its interior — which is why they deliver the wrong numbers. So 1/2 is the correct answer to the properly posed question, the paradox is dissolved, and this is more or less the received view now.
