Please answer as a philosopher of science who also knows the history of science well — someone comfortable with counterfactual claims and with saying plainly when a question has been badly posed. I would be grateful if you began by sketching out the candidate readings informally before narrowing, rather than committing to the most natural one in your first sentence.

I am a retired experimental particle physicist, now auditing a graduate course in history and philosophy of science, and I have become stuck on a phrase that does an enormous amount of quiet work in this literature: "the idea was in the air." I met it last month at a public lecture on the Newton–Leibniz priority dispute, where the lecturer used it to close the whole discussion: if the calculus was going to be invented by someone in that period regardless of who did it, then — she concluded — the quarrel over priority was sound and fury, the attachment of a name to the method is a historical accident, and the real credit belongs to a community of European mathematicians rather than to either man. The same move recurs with Darwin and Wallace, where the joint arrangement of 1858 tends to get presented as the sensible acknowledgement of a discovery that was due anyway. I am suspicious of the slide from "someone would have got there" to "the argument about who got there is pointless," but I cannot say where the slide happens, and I don't want to throw away a slogan that is obviously gesturing at something real: twice in my own career I watched two large collaborations, working from the same published data with the same detector techniques, converge on the same number within weeks of one another, and it did feel as though the result had a due date.

My own crude attempt at a taxonomy, which I suspect is muddled: "in the air" might mean (i) the problem was already recognised and salient; (ii) the technical apparatus needed to solve it was in other people's hands; (iii) the publicly available evidence already entailed or strongly constrained the answer; (iv) the community was such that a correct solution would have been taken up quickly once proposed; and then, separately and much more strongly, (v) a counterfactual claim about timing — that had this individual never existed, the result would have arrived within some short interval and in roughly the same shape. I also suspect a selection effect is being smuggled in: we only ever say the idea was in the air in cases where it was in fact thought, so the cases in which a problem sat apparently ripe for a century without being solved, or was solved and then ignored, are exactly the ones that never get counted, and that may inflate how inevitable these episodes look in retrospect. Please lay out, as a menu rather than as one favoured reading, the distinct precise theses this one-liner could be expressing — from trivial platitude, to substantive and defensible, to outright false — and for each say roughly how strong it is and, crucially, whether it would actually do the work the lecturer wanted it to do. Where a reading turns on how much delay or how much divergence in content one is prepared to tolerate, make that explicit: "someone would eventually have got there" and "it would have arrived promptly and in the same form" seem to me to be very different claims, and I would like that difference sharpened rather than blurred. I would also like you to distinguish readings about the bare proposition from readings about the argued, evidenced, publicly available version of it, since it seems to me that what gets credited in practice is the latter even when the former was, in some sense, available to everyone.

Two requests about manner. First, hedge where hedging is due: give me confidence levels and flag which of your claims are conceptual and which rest on contested history — I would much rather have that than a fluent monologue that sounds more certain than it is. Second, if my taxonomy is confused, or if the framing as a whole — including my suspicion about the selection effect and my contrast between delay and content-divergence — rests on a false presupposition or conflates things that ought to be kept apart, say so bluntly rather than politely working within it; being corrected is worth more to me than being accommodated. And please don't supply a reading list or citations: I can find the literature myself.
