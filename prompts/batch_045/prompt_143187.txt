I chair the serious-incident review panel at a hospital group; we investigate roughly forty incidents a year, and our findings are entered into a mandatory reporting system in which the words 'caused', 'contributed to' and 'was a background condition to' have quite different downstream effects — the first can trigger a regulatory referral and an insurance position, the second a local action plan, the third nothing at all — so the wording is contested by every department whose conduct appears in it. Increasingly the candidate we argue about is an omission: a check not performed, an escalation not made, an abnormal result not chased, a calibration skipped. We have no disciplined way of saying when a failure to act is a cause of the harm rather than a breach of protocol that merely happened to sit nearby in time.

Please write a decision procedure my panel can run in a forty-five-minute review, with no legal or philosophical training, and that a colleague could rerun on the same file and reach the same verdict. It should be grounded in the distinctions that actually matter in the literature on causation by omission — the fragility of the counterfactual, cases where the omission is causal only because an independent backup had itself been disabled, cases where the agent would have been prevented from acting had they tried, cases where the not-acting was produced by someone else's earlier omission, and the point that a non-occurrence is an omission at all only relative to a norm, role or expectation. I also want three categories my panel has started using informally made precise and then criticised: a 'live omission', where the agent could have acted and nothing would have stopped them; a 'blocked omission', where they would have been prevented; and a 'borrowed omission', where they did not act only because another party's earlier omission had already removed the means, the information or the authority to act. Tell me whether that threefold distinction cuts along the right joints, or whether 'borrowed' collapses into 'blocked' or into ordinary causation by an earlier positive act.

Give me the questions in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, an explicit stop that returns 'not a cause', and explicit hand-offs to a person where what remains is a decision about what we owe each other rather than about what happened. Then run it on three cases: a night nurse who did not escalate deteriorating vital signs while the on-call doctor's pager battery was dead; a maintenance team that skipped a scheduled calibration on a tank level sensor, where the independent high-level alarm had been muted months earlier by a different contractor; and a consultant who did not review a scan she had explicitly delegated, where the registrar who did review it also did not act, so that counterfactually nothing would have changed — a case where I expect the procedure to return a verdict my panel will not like. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: in particular, whether it can keep our desire to prevent recurrence from being smuggled in as a causal finding, and what it should say when the norm it must anchor to — for instance, what staffing level was owed — is itself the thing under dispute.
