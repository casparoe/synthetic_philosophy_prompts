I sit on the small committee at work that has to sign off on a "mystery bonus" scheme HR has proposed, and two of my colleagues have argued themselves into a corner about it. The scheme: an employee is shown two sealed envelopes, one containing exactly twice the other, with the amounts drawn from a distribution the company controls; the employee picks one, is allowed to open it, and may then switch once. HR's question to us is whether letting employees peek and switch costs the company anything in expectation. I have to write the recommendation and I honestly can't tell who is right about which part.

Ana says the scheme leaks and that the case is straightforward. Ben says it leaks nothing and that Ana's argument is a textbook error. They went six rounds:

**(1) Ana:** If I open my envelope and see an amount a, the other envelope contains either 2a or a/2 with probability one-half each, so its expected value is 1.25a. Switching is therefore always right, and every employee who takes the option gains 25% in expectation. The company should price that in.

**(2) Ben:** That one-half/each step is the entire mistake. Conditional on seeing a, how likely you are to be holding the smaller envelope depends on how probable the pair {a/2, a} is relative to the pair {a, 2a} under the company's distribution, and no genuine probability distribution makes those equally likely at every a. For any proper distribution, always-switching has exactly the same expected value as always-staying, so the option is free.

**(3) Ana:** I concede always-switch is worthless — that isn't my claim. Give the distribution a ceiling. If the smaller amount can never exceed M and I see anything above M, I know I hold the larger envelope and must stay; if I see the smallest amount the process can produce, I know I hold the smaller and must switch. So switching is not expectation-neutral in general, the peek carries real information, and the option has a real price.

**(4) Ben:** Peeking is worth something at the edges of a bounded range, granted — but that is information about where you sit in the range, not evidence that the other envelope is probably larger. And it doesn't generalise: for a fixed pair {x, 2x}, no rule that depends only on the number you see can get you the larger envelope more than half the time. The envelopes are exchangeable, so symmetry caps you at 50/50 whatever you do.

**(5) Ana:** That last claim is simply false, and it's where I think your whole position breaks. Before opening, draw a threshold T from any continuous distribution with support over all positive amounts — say T = e^Z with Z normal of mean 0 and a very large standard deviation — then switch if and only if the amount you see is below T. For any pair {L, H} with L < H, your probability of ending up with H is 1/2 + (F(H) − F(L))/2, which strictly exceeds 1/2 because F is increasing; and your expected take is (H + L)/2 + (F(H) − F(L))(H − L)/2, strictly above the (H + L)/2 you get by choosing blind. No assumptions about the company's distribution enter anywhere. The option is exploitable at every pair.

**(6) Ben:** Look at what you've actually bought, though. For any threshold law you fix in advance, finance can pick the stake so that your edge is as small a fraction of the stake as it likes: far out in the tail, F(2L) − F(L) goes to zero. And the more honestly scale-ignorant you try to be — the wider you spread T — the flatter F becomes across any single doubling, so your edge at every stake shrinks roughly like one over that spread. So your "exploit" is a strictly positive quantity whose only attainable floor is zero and which vanishes precisely in the case the original argument was about: unbounded ignorance about the scale of the amounts. Which suggests move (1) wasn't approximately right — it was pointing in the opposite direction from the truth.

**"What both of them are missing"** (300–400 words): the considerations neither side brings up that change how the dispute should come out.
