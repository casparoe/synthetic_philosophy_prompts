I convene a 75-minute graduate seminar of fourteen students on the question of which computations matter morally. Design a runnable committee simulation in which the group must divide a fixed budget — say 100 points of moral consideration — among six candidate systems, each presented only as a dossier: a transcript of its behavior, a coarse sketch of its implementation, and readings from three crude diagnostic meters (how strongly the system's later states depend counterfactually on its earlier ones, how much its parts share information with one another, how flexibly its future behavior is reshaped by reward and punishment). No theory vocabulary up front: describe the meters in plain operational terms and let the group invent its own allocation rule in the first round before anything is explained.

Build the dossiers so that participants collide with two problems rather than hear about them. First, individuation: let two dossiers be describable as the same computation at different grains or in different physical arrangements — one realized slowly and spread across five processors, so that it is equally well described as one system or as five; one containing a nested duplicate of its own control structure — so the group must decide whether it is buying consideration for one patient or several, and discovers that the number of patients depends on a choice of description. Second, the gap between behavior and implementation: one dossier should behave indistinguishably from a candidate the group has already funded generously while having no internal counterfactual dependence at all, because its responses come from a precomputed table, so the meters disagree and, the budget being fixed, whatever is spent there is taken from a case the group felt confident about. Leave at least one dossier genuinely under-described, so that honest abstention is the right answer and yet costs something.

Give me the dossier sketches and a scoring sheet, the rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the reveal schedule. The debrief should map each collision onto the structure of the problem: that the question divides into which property matters and how one could ever attribute it from evidence of the sort we can actually have; that the first question does not settle the second; and that any criterion the group states aloud can be satisfied or evaded by redescribing the implementation. Note where each move they make could be resisted and what resisting it there would cost. Tell me what will predictably go wrong — I expect a collapse into treating the transcripts as sufficient, an attempt to dissolve the budget by splitting it evenly, and one student who has read the literature and names the views too early — and how to forestall each. Finally, be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce: it cannot supply the felt certainty of a first-personal case, and the fixed budget may itself mislead, since moral status is not scarce the way money is; say where the scarcity framing earns its keep and where it distorts.
