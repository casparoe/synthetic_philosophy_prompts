I work on a team of four — two engineers, a policy lead, and a retired care coordinator — at a regional nonprofit that allocates a fixed pool of in-home care hours every week to elderly and disabled clients across four counties. We have about three applicants for every slot, and we make roughly eleven hundred allocation decisions a month. Right now a human coordinator works through the waitlist with a forty-page manual that sets out a priority ordering: clinical urgency first, then risk of institutional placement, then time on the waitlist, then caregiver burden. Everyone agrees the manual is too crude. The coordinators depart from it in about one case in five, and when these overrides get reviewed in case conferences the rest of the staff almost always agrees the override was the better call — typically because the manual can't see something like a client whose only relative is about to move out of state.

We're now building a system to do the first-pass ranking, and we've narrowed it to two designs. Option A is to encode the manual as an explicit, ordered rule, with the known cases of good noncompliance added as a fixed list of named exception categories. Option B is to train on six years of logged coordinator decisions, including the free-text notes coordinators write when they override, which would let the system pick up the patterns that make the overrides good without anyone having to state them. My policy lead says Option A is the only honest choice, because a client we turn down is owed a reason, and only a written rule can supply one; she calls Option B "illegible injustice" and says writing the rule down is what taking responsibility consists in. I partly agree, and I partly suspect I prefer Option B for a bad reason: if the system learns the pattern, no single person authored the outcome, and my name isn't on it. I should also flag two constraints. Our board requires that every denial carry a written reason a client can act on, and the appeal process turns on counterfactual questions a client's family actually asks — would I have ranked higher if my daughter didn't live twenty minutes away? And a client advocacy group is demanding that we publish the rule, which my team is resisting because a published rule can be coached against, though of course coordinators can be coached against too.

There's a further thing I can't see past. Whatever we ship will be updated. Under Option A, any change to the rule is a visible change in policy that the board has to vote on, which means it happens slowly and mostly after something goes badly wrong; under Option B, the norms shift quietly as coordinators retire and are replaced, so the standard quietly changes without anyone ever deciding it should. I've floated a hybrid — let the trained system rank, but require it to produce a justification that gets checked against the explicit rule, and send mismatches to a human — and I can't tell whether this is the honest answer or the worst of both: if the rule binds, the learner is decoration; if the learner may depart from the rule, someone has to state when departure is permitted, and that statement is just another rule, one level up. I took a single ethics course years ago and the only thing that has stuck is a diagram about runaway trolleys, which has been no help at all here.

So: which of these should we build, and what is the actual mistake in my colleague's argument, or mine? I'd especially like help with three things — whether "we wrote the rule down" really is what moral responsibility requires here, or whether that is just the version of responsibility that is easiest to audit; whether there is a principled stopping point for the regress in the hybrid design, or a way to make the override step non-fake given that eleven hundred cases a month means human review will either be a bottleneck or a rubber stamp; and which failure mode we should prefer, given that one of the two options freezes today's injustices in a form that can at least be contested in public, while the other tracks current practice but changes what it values without anyone choosing to change it.
