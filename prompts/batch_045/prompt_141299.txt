I am preparing a short talk on Bertrand's chord paradox for a reading group, and I would like an accuracy check on the source I was going to build it around: a chapter in a fairly recent book of popular mathematics, well reviewed and careful with its calculations, which may be less careful with the moral it draws. The passage I mean runs roughly like this:

"Ask a mathematician to choose a chord of a circle 'at random' and to say how probable it is that the chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Bertrand, in his 1889 textbook, produced three answers. Choose two points independently and uniformly on the circumference and join them: the probability is 1/3. Choose a radius at random and then a point at random along it, and draw the chord perpendicular to that radius through the point: the probability is 1/2. Choose the chord's midpoint uniformly from the interior of the disc: the probability is 1/4. Nor does it stop there — index the chords by their length instead, take the length to be uniform on the interval from 0 to the diameter, and one gets about 0.134; with a little ingenuity one can extract almost any number between 0 and 1. Each of these procedures is perfectly random, and each looks like an honest application of the principle of indifference, which tells us to assign equal probability to outcomes among which we have no reason to discriminate. The principle therefore returns three incompatible numbers to one and the same question: it is not merely incomplete but self-contradictory, and it has had to be abandoned. The puzzle was finally laid to rest in the 1970s by Edwin Jaynes. A problem, Jaynes argued, is well posed only if its solution is invariant under the transformations that leave the statement of the problem unchanged — rotations, translations and rescalings of the circle — and once the principle of indifference is applied correctly, by way of the maximum-entropy distribution, exactly one answer survives: 1/2. The other two numbers are artefacts of an arbitrary choice of coordinates. The lesson is that there is no fact of the matter about a 'random chord' until someone settles on a description of it, which shows that probability measures our ignorance rather than the world; and that is why the principle of indifference belongs to the prehistory of the subject, and why sensible statisticians now let data, not symmetry, choose their priors."

Two things make me suspicious, though I cannot locate the error. The section on Jaynes reads as though the matter were closed, and a statistician friend told me much the same; but the closing inference — from three numbers to a general claim about what probability is — strikes me as proving far more than the geometry can support. I have also seen the chord paradox described in passing as just the wine-and-water paradox in geometric costume, and I half-remember a footnote claiming that later writers have run Jaynes-style invariance arguments and recovered the other numbers too.

Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on the foundations of probability would accept, what they would wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong? In particular: how much of the paradox is really a problem about the principle of indifference rather than about the phrase "at random"; in what sense, if any, 1/2 is privileged; how much of Jaynes's argument turns on invariance requirements as opposed to maximum entropy; whether the comparison with the wine-and-water paradox is apt or misleading; whether the chapter's final moral survives once its inaccuracies are corrected; and what a sympathetic defender of the principle of indifference would say in reply to the charge that it is self-contradictory.
