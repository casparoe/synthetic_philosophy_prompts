i've been going back and forth in a comment thread about the pasadena game (fair coin, flip till heads, if that's on toss n you get (-1)^(n+1) * 2^n / n dollars) for about a week now and i want to get my actual position down on paper and have someone audit it before i defend any of it out loud. here's where i've landed, more or less in the order i'd argue it:

1. value is unbounded and additive. no best outcome, no cap, and if one good life is worth something then ten trillion of them is ten trillion times as good. i've never felt the pull of a bounded or saturating value function.

2. when an expectation isn't absolutely convergent it's just undefined, and then there's nothing for expected-value maximization to say. that's the right diagnosis of st petersburg and pasadena, and there's no fact of the matter about what either is worth. i also think those two are basically the same phenomenon — heavy tails plus payoffs with no ceiling — and deserve the same treatment.

3. i'd still pay to play pasadena. couple of bucks. you win something two times in three and the median outcome is plus two dollars, so the ticket feels clearly better than nothing.

4. when expectations go undefined, state-wise dominance is the one thing left standing and i trust it completely: pasadena-plus-a-dollar beats pasadena, full stop, and that verdict doesn't care that neither option has an expectation.

5. i'm an anti-fanatic. pascal's mugger gets nothing from me. i won't trade a certain modest good for a one-in-a-bajillion shot at an astronomical one, and my instinct is that the right response to very small probabilities is to shrink them further or ignore them below some threshold rather than to multiply them out.

6. but i also think bundling shouldn't matter: if i'd accept a big package of independent gambles, i shouldn't turn each of them down as it comes up one at a time.

7. and i think a lot of the mugger cases are really about probabilities i can't estimate at all rather than about small ones as such — the mugger's story might be true and might be nonsense and i have no grip on which, which feels like a different problem from a fair lottery with a known one-in-10^30 chance.

8. my pet move on pasadena: make the coin biased, P(heads) = q > 1/2, and the expectation is suddenly perfectly well behaved. i worked it out on a napkin as (q/(1-q)) * ln(1 + 2(1-q)), which goes to ln 2 as q goes to 1/2. i like this because it says pasadena has a value after all, and because it makes it continuous with the ordinary cases instead of a freak.

so: which of these are actually pulling against each other, and which only look like they are? i have a vague worry about 2 and 8 but i genuinely can't tell where the real problem is and i don't have a favourite to drop. please rank the tensions worst-first, for each one say exactly what would have to give and what giving it up costs me, and then commit to one overall recommendation — the single revision that buys the most coherence most cheaply. don't hedge into "it depends on your framework," don't give me the literature on who holds which view (i don't care), and do tell me if the arithmetic in 8 is wrong or if 3 and 8 already contradict each other.
