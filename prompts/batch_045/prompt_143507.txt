I co-teach a joint upper-level seminar on the question of whether the brain is a computer; half the room is philosophy of mind students who care about representation, content, and what follows for the mind, and half is computational neuroscience students who care whether the computational description of a circuit actually earns its keep in the lab. I want a handout that works as a positioning instrument: roughly eight to ten diagnostic questions, each a concrete case or claim, each answerable by someone with only introductory background and without any of them saying outright whether the brain is a computer or naming a position, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto ten to thirteen leaves. My own suspicion, which I would like the instrument to test rather than presuppose, is that the debate keeps collapsing three separable questions: whether neural tissue literally computes in a sense that is not merely imposed by a describer, whether computational models do explanatory work that a purely biophysical account would not, and whether cognition is constitutively computational rather than merely computationally modellable. So I would like three sub-scores, not one, and the key should say which combinations of the three are actually occupied by positions people defend and which are empty. Please build from the logical space first: fix the discriminating questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the real views in that space, and for any unoccupied combination give one sentence on why nobody is there — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

The questions should bite on cases rather than on slogans, and I have a list I would like used: a stomach, or a stretch of gut, described by a textbook as processing information (does digestion compute?); a valley whose drainage pattern hydrologists exploit to read off solutions to an equation they would otherwise have to integrate numerically; a circuit that is equally well described by two formally incompatible programs; a model fitted to recorded neural data after the fact that could never have been run forward in real time; neurons replaced one at a time by devices matching their input-output timing and their metabolic exchange with the surrounding tissue; the same preparation described computationally at the level of spikes, at the level of individual dendritic compartments, and at the level of the whole animal coupled to its environment; a central pattern generator whose relevant state variables are continuous and whose timing is essential, so that any digital program is a discretization artifact; a circuit explained entirely in biophysical and energetic terms with no computational vocabulary at all; and two candidate programs that agree on every prediction anyone knows how to test. Among the leaves I want at least these: representational computationalism about cognition; mechanistic computationalism that dispenses with representations; analogue or hybrid computationalism; computationalism extended across brain, body, and world; the deflationary view that everything computes under some mapping and the claim therefore says almost nothing; the view that the brain computes but that nothing about the mind follows; the instrumentalist or fictionalist view that computational ascriptions are useful without being literally true; anti-computationalism about neural mechanisms; and the dynamical or embodied alternative. Among the branch questions I want, at minimum, one on what would have to be true for a physical system to implement a computation rather than merely be described by one, one on the grain or vehicle of the putative computation, one on whether discrete states and rule-governed transitions are required, one on whether the computation must be about anything, and one on whether the description is falsifiable by neural data. Please also flag any pair of leaves your instrument cannot separate — I would rather have the overlap marked than papered over with a verbal distinction — and say which of my required cases fail to discriminate anything and could be dropped.

I do not want citations, further reading, or bibliographies; describe every position by its content so that nothing depends on who said it first or in which year. The handout should be usable in a fifty-minute class, so keep the questions crisp enough to answer quickly. At the end, add a short instructor's note that separates what is genuinely settled in this literature from what is contested among people who work on it: in particular, say plainly which of the trunk questions has no agreed answer, mark each leaf as either a position that is actually defended or one you had to reconstruct for completeness, and note where my own three-way framing of the debate embeds a contested choice rather than a neutral one.
