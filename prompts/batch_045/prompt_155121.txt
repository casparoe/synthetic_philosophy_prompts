**Q3.** In this course we have separated three claims that often get run together:

**(a)** *(the "mathematical" or informal-to-formal thesis)* Every function that is effectively computable — i.e. computable by carrying out a finite, explicitly stated, purely mechanical procedure — is computable by a Turing machine.

**(b)** *(the "physical" thesis)* Every function computable by any physical system that could actually be built and run is computable by a Turing machine; equivalently, any finitely specifiable physical process can be simulated by a Turing machine to any desired degree of accuracy.

**(c)** *(the "feasibility" or "extended" thesis)* Any physically realizable computing device can be simulated by a Turing machine with at most polynomial overhead in time, so that Turing machines are the right model not merely of what is computable but of what is *feasibly* computable.

**(i)** For each of (a), (b), (c): what kind of claim is it, and what would count as evidence for or against it? Is any of them the kind of thing that could be *proved*? Where one of them cannot be proved, explain precisely why not, and explain what it would even mean for it to be *false*.

**(ii)** Suppose someone constructs a "Zeno machine": a device that performs its first step in 1/2 second, its second in 1/4 second, its third in 1/8 second, and so on, so that after one second it has completed infinitely many steps, and which then lights a lamp if and only if the Turing machine whose description it was given never halts. A classmate says: this decides the halting problem, therefore the Church–Turing thesis is false. Assess that argument separately with respect to (a), (b) and (c). Which, if any, does the machine refute? Does your answer to (i) make a difference here, and if so why?

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

> The first thing to say is that there are really three separate claims here and most of the confusion comes from running them together. (a) connects an intuitive notion, effective computability, with a precise formal one, Turing-machine computability. (b) is a claim about the world, about what physical systems can do. (c) is a claim about efficiency rather than computability, so it belongs to complexity theory really.
>
> (a) cannot be proved in the strict mathematical sense. The reason is that the left-hand side is informal: "effectively computable" is an intuitive notion, and a proof needs precise definitions on both sides, so no proof can bridge an informal notion to a formal class. What we have instead is very strong evidence: every independent attempt to formalize the intuitive notion — Turing machines, general recursive functions, lambda-definability, register machines, and many others — turned out to be extensionally equivalent, defining exactly the same class of functions. That convergence is about as good as evidence gets for a claim of this kind, and there is also the fact that nobody has ever produced a procedure that is clearly effective but not Turing-computable. If (a) were false, that would mean there is some genuinely effective procedure — finite, mechanical, explicitly specifiable — that no Turing machine can carry out.
>
> (b) is different: it is an empirical hypothesis about physics, and it could in principle be overturned by building something. This is also the claim that quantum computing threatens, since quantum computers do things faster, and there are proposals involving exotic spacetimes or supertasks that would go beyond merely simulating a Turing machine. (c) is empirical too, and it is the one most directly challenged by quantum computing, since a quantum computer appears to give an exponential speedup for factoring and simulating one on a Turing machine is believed to take exponential time.
>
> For part (ii) I think the classmate is basically right, though the argument needs care. A Zeno machine would decide the halting problem, and so it would refute the Church–Turing thesis, because the thesis implies that the halting problem is not decidable by anything at all. To be careful about which one: what it refutes is (a), since (a) says the computable functions are exactly the Turing-computable ones, and a machine that computes a function no Turing machine computes — the characteristic function of the non-halting set — is a direct counterexample to (a). It refutes (b) as well, since the machine is a physical device. (c) is refuted too, or rather (c) isn't really tested by this example since (c) is about overhead and there is no overhead here to speak of.
>
> One thing that makes me doubt the whole worry is this. The halting problem is undecidable, and that is a mathematical theorem about any formalism whatsoever — you can always diagonalize. So even if you strengthen your model, you get a new halting problem for the strengthened model, and you never get a model that decides everything. That shows the thesis can't be refuted by any possible machine: whatever machine you build, there will still be functions it cannot compute, so the situation is always exactly the same as it is for Turing machines.
>
> So my conclusion is that (a) is an unprovable but extremely well-supported bridge claim between an intuitive notion and a formal one, and its epistemic status is close to that of a well-confirmed scientific hypothesis: contingent, testable, and falsifiable — which is precisely what a Zeno machine would show if one existed.
