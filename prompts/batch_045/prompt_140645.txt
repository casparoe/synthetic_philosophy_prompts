My office mate is a PhD student in philosophy of physics and we've been having the same circling argument about whether the wavefunction is real since a conference trip last fall: I keep thinking half of it is an identifiability question in disguise, she keeps thinking I'm bulldozing the interesting part. Here's my crude three-way taxonomy, which I'd like you to use as the target: (1) the world state — whatever is really there on a given run of an experiment; (2) the encoding state — the thing my model carries around and updates, which may or may not be recoverable from the world state; (3) the readout state — the function of the encoding that actually spits out predictions for a given measurement setting. My claim is that the whole psi-ontic / psi-epistemic business is just the question of whether the encoding state (the wavefunction) is a deterministic function of the world state, or whether two distinct wavefunctions can be compatible with one and the same world state with nonzero probability — i.e., whether the latent is identifiable from the world or has genuinely overlapping posteriors. Restate both sides in those terms as faithfully as you can, and also restate two variants: the one where the wavefunction isn't a per-datapoint latent at all but something more like a global parameter or a law, and the toy-theory-style one where the allowed beliefs over the world state are structurally restricted (not just "whatever posterior you happen to have"), which I suspect doesn't fit anywhere in my taxonomy. Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. In my terms that second assumption is just that the latents of two independently drawn datapoints factorize, p(lambda_1, lambda_2) = p(lambda_1) p(lambda_2), which doesn't feel like a metaphysical assumption to me at all — it's baked into every generative model I've ever written.
