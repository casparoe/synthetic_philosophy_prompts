I've been meeting with a small reading group — two philosophers, a psychologist, a physician who works in palliative care, and me — and over the past two months I have drifted into holding a version of suffering-focused ethics that I now cannot get anyone in the group to attack in a way I find damaging. I would like you to try. The view, as precisely as I can state it: (1) suffering is the only thing that is intrinsically bad, and it is bad in virtue of how it feels rather than in virtue of anyone's desires about it; (2) happiness is not intrinsically good — at most its presence is a permission and its absence is not a misfortune — so no quantity of happiness compensates for any quantity of suffering, which I hold lexically rather than as a mere weighting; (3) correspondingly, suffering generates strong agent-neutral reasons to prevent or relieve it, while happiness generates at most weak reasons to promote it and no reason whatsoever to bring new subjects into existence, which is the familiar four-cell asymmetry about existence applied instead to experiences; (4) because our situation is one of deep uncertainty about long-run consequences, the view's practical output is a priority on the worst, most concentrated, most tractable and most neglected suffering, which lands close enough to ordinary humanitarian common sense that my friends mostly nod along.

I also have two patches on the view that I suspect are doing more work than I admit. The first is what I call the already-in-the-room case: once a particular subject already exists, with a settled psychology and a foreseeable future, my intuitions about adding happiness to her life strike me as nearly as strong as my intuitions about adding suffering, and the asymmetry that drives everything else in the view increasingly looks to me like an asymmetry about whether to create a subject rather than about how to treat one who is already there. My psychologist friend presses exactly this, and I do not have a reply I trust. The second is authorized suffering — the ordeal the climber chose and would choose again, the labour the parent would not trade away, the pain that partly constitutes an achievement — which I have been handling by saying that the badness is diminished or cancelled by the subject's stance toward it, and not merely outweighed by something else. I worry that this patch quietly converts the view into a preference-based view wearing suffering-shaped vocabulary, and I want to know whether that worry is justified.

So: what would count against the view as a whole? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and separated into those a committed suffering-focused ethicist should concede as counting against it and those such a theorist would dismiss as changing the subject. Include the ones I have not mentioned. Tell me what each of my two patches specifically risks: if I grant the already-in-the-room case, do I retain anything distinguishable from a person-affecting or existence-asymmetry view, and if I grant the authorized-suffering patch, do I retain anything distinguishable from a preference view? Say what the view predicts that its rivals do not — about concrete cases, about the actual distribution of suffering in the world, and about what I should be willing to trade — since a view that predicts nothing distinctive cannot be tested at all. And give me an honest verdict on whether clause (4) has insulated the whole thing: is the practical layer doing the real work while the axiological core risks nothing, is that a problem for a view of this kind, and does the answer differ for the core claim and the practical claim? The consideration I find most persuasive in the view's favour is phenomenological — that pain presents itself as to-be-ended in a way that pleasure never presents itself as to-be-produced — so I would most like to know what would break that particular support, including anything empirical about pain, its measurement, or its role in a life that I ought to go and check. Skip citations and reading suggestions; I want the arguments. Please open with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
