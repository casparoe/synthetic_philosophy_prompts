I work at a demographic research institute that has been asked to draft a short internal briefing on whether the state should subsidise births. Two of my colleagues have spent most of three lunch breaks stuck on what they agree is the prior question: whether a larger population would be better at all, setting aside distributional and environmental complications and holding the quality of life of each person fixed. I was there for most of it and genuinely cannot tell who is winning. Their exchange, compressed but not tidied up:

(1) Marcus: Take a country of ten million people, all with lives well worth living, and compare it with an otherwise identical country of twenty million at the same level of well-being. The larger country contains twice as much of the only thing that matters — lives going well. If you want to deny that it is the better of the two, you owe an account of what else could be doing the work, and I do not think there is a candidate.

(2) Nadia: More of a good thing is better only when there is someone it is better for. In your twenty-million country no one is better off than in the ten-million country. The extra ten million are not better off than they would otherwise have been, since the alternative is not a condition they are ever in; the original ten million are exactly as well off as before. You have described a world with more well-being in it. You have not described a better world. Better needs a subject; you are quietly using it to mean contains a larger quantity of what matters.

(3) Marcus: Then consider extinction. Compare humanity continuing for another ten thousand years with humanity ending in fifty years, everyone alive now living well to the end. Almost everyone says the first is better, and on your test it cannot be, because there is nobody in the continuation world who is better off than in the extinction world. Whatever is wrong with my principle, it is not that it mishandles that case.

(4) Nadia: I do not accept that. Continuation is better because of what it means to the people who exist now and in the near future: their projects, their children, the communities they belong to not coming to an end. That is a relation to actual people, not an entry in a cosmic ledger. But notice what it buys. It is a reason for there being enough births to carry a society forward, and it runs out at the point where additional people make no one's life go better. That is my answer to your case, and it is not indifference to the size of the future; it is a refusal to treat size itself as the good.

(5) Marcus: Two worries. First, your version is less stable than it looks. If what matters is people's stake in a flourishing, continuing community, that stake does not switch off at replacement: a larger and more resilient society serves those interests better than a smaller one, and you will end up counting very nearly what I count, only by a longer route. Second, on your own terms you have no complaint against a deliberate policy of shrinking the population to a handful of contented people, provided nobody's welfare falls — a conclusion I find incredible, and one I do not think you have faced up to.

(6) Nadia: And your currency fails at the other end. For any population of excellent lives there is a vastly larger population of lives barely worth living with more total well-being, so you are committed to calling the second better; and you can run that argument again after every improvement, which pushes you towards a population of enormous size and minimal quality. If that is where more of what matters leads, so much the worse for more of what matters.

(7) Marcus: That is an artefact of counting raw totals. Put a neutral level on a life: a life adds to the value of a world only to the extent that it goes well above the level at which existence is neither a benefit nor a burden. Then adding clearly good lives is an improvement, so larger is better across the ordinary range, but the slide you describe stops, because lives that are barely worth living add almost nothing and cannot be traded in bulk against quality. And the level is not the invented quantity you will want to call it: we already treat the arrival of a good life as a gain and the arrival of a miserable one as a loss, which is just the shape of a threshold.

(8) Nadia: The level is doing all the work and nothing anchors it. Any positive level makes a life just above it worth a great deal and a life just below it worth nothing, a discontinuity that has to be located somewhere and can be located anywhere. Set it low and your slide returns in slow motion, since you can always add a very large number of lives just barely above the line. And you have misdescribed my position in (5): I do not measure people's stake in a continuing community by headcount, so nothing creeps towards your view.

(9) Marcus: Then set the value question down entirely. There is an argument for a larger population that needs no view about totals, averages or thresholds. A bigger population is insurance. How likely we are to get through a pandemic we cannot treat, or to recover a capability we have lost, depends partly on how many people are working on the problem and how many independent attempts get made. Everyone has a stake in the future not collapsing, and even on your reckoning the people who exist and their immediate successors have an interest in there being enough society left to carry their projects and to catch them if things go wrong. So larger is better in the only sense the briefing needs: it is the safer size, and it is better for the people who are actually there to be better for.

(10) Nadia: A larger population also carries more hosts for a pathogen, more researchers racing towards the same dangerous capability, more people with the means to do damage. You have identified a consideration whose direction is an empirical matter and whose size nobody knows; you have not shown that it points to larger. And it is instrumental anyway. If it worked it would tell us to subsidise births for the security payoff, not because a larger population is better. That is a different briefing — and an awkward one, because then the gains accrue to one group's security and the costs to another group's consumption, and we are back in distribution, which is exactly what we agreed to set aside.

I have to write the briefing with them. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each of the ten moves, say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and why — including the cases where a move succeeds against the point it was aimed at but does not establish what its author takes it to establish. Then tell me what both of them are missing: whether there is a distinction, a third position, or an empirical question that would dissolve part of the dispute rather than decide it. Finally, say whether the briefing can be written without settling the value question: could a pro-natalist policy be defended on the insurance argument alone whilst remaining agnostic between Marcus and Nadia, what would have to be true for that to work, and what would be the strongest objection to going about it that way?
