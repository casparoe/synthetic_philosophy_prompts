I staff our hospital system's clinical algorithm review committee, and I have to draft the memo for next week's decision, so I want the reasoning laid out rather than just a bottom line.

Our track record so far:

1. Care-management enrollment model, two patient groups with essentially identical event rates (14% and 15%). One threshold, the score calibrated within groups, false-positive and false-negative rates equal across groups within noise. Approved without conditions.
2. A vendor model that used prior-year cost as a feature. On audit, a patient in group B assigned a given score had materially higher true risk than a group A patient with the same score, and group B's miss rate was nearly double group A's. Rejected; the written reason was that the same number has to carry the same risk for every patient.
3. A model that equalized flag rates across groups even though event rates differed (20% vs 14%). Rejected; the opinion said the committee was not imposing rate parity, and objected that both the miss rates and the share of flags that turned out to be true cases diverged sharply between groups.
4. One review with unequal event rates where the committee deadlocked, then rejected on an unrelated data-provenance problem while explicitly declining to say anything about error rates.

The pattern I want checked: in every decided case the two demands we might be running — that a given score mean the same thing in every group, and that false-positive and false-negative rates be equalized across groups — have pointed the same way. Everything we approved satisfied both; everything we rejected violated both. We have never seen them come apart.

The new submission does that. It allocates 500 post-discharge nursing slots. Event rates are 22% in group A and 9% in group B; the committee has already accepted those registry rates as accurate rather than as an artifact of who gets diagnosed. As submitted, the score is calibrated within groups and one threshold is used for everyone, giving: group A flagged 26% of the time, 15% false-positive rate, 35% miss rate, 55% of flags are true cases; group B flagged 9.5% of the time, 6% false-positive rate, 55% miss rate, 43% of flags are true cases. The vendor also offers a second variant with group-specific thresholds that equalizes both error rates (12% false positives, 40% misses in each group); there, group B is flagged 16% of the time and group A 23%, and the share of flags that are true cases becomes roughly 58% for A and 33% for B.

One complication that none of our precedents had: this intervention is not merely predictive. Enrollment itself causally lowers the event rate, and our pilot data suggest the reduction is about 2 percentage points for A patients and about 5 for B patients, so a flag that turns out not to be a true case is not simply a wasted slot in the way our earlier framing assumed. Slots are capped, so more for B is fewer for A.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which rule have we been implicitly running: equalized error rates, equal predictive meaning of the score, benefit maximization subject to a floor, or something else I have not named? Which precedent is the closest analog, and if our precedents genuinely underdetermine this, what principle would we be adopting with each option — including any variant I should ask the vendor for that neither of these two is? Finally, name the single further number, audit, or hypothetical variant that would best force us to reveal which rule we have really been applying. Say how confident you are in the recommendation, and single out the point you are least sure of.
