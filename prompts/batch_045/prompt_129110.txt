I work at a smallish global-health foundation, and I've been going through the minutes of our Evidence & Measurement Committee — the subcommittee that decides whether to commission more measurement before we lock in a funding decision. I'm writing the memo for the next one and I'd like a second read before I stick my neck out.

Their record so far (paraphrased from the minutes, with what I remember of the reasoning in brackets):

- Cheap six-month head-to-head trial of two bednet distribution methods, results to us only, then we pick: commission. ["Standard."]
- Same trial, but it would take three years and the decision is due in six weeks: don't commission. ["It'd land after we've already spent the money."]
- Already-collected government data on regional malaria prevalence — free — except our charter obliges us to allocate strictly in proportion to measured prevalence, and most of us think that rule is badly miscalibrated: don't commission. ["The number would decide for us, and badly."]
- A cheap, noisy, biased proxy outcome where we know both the direction and the size of the bias: commission. ["We just adjust."]
- A moderately expensive study using an outcome measure nobody has ever validated — three incompatible models of how the measure maps onto what we actually care about, no way to rank them: don't commission. ["We'd be guessing about the guessing."]
- A cheap staff survey that would change the thing it measures, because asking people whether they'd stay if we moved the program gets them started on planning to leave: don't commission.
- A cheap, precise study we'd be obliged to publish, where a rival funder would read it and move into our districts first: don't commission. ["Looking is itself a signal."]
- A result a partner already produced and left in a drawer — genuinely zero cost — on a question everyone agrees can't change what we fund this cycle: don't commission. ["Zero value isn't a reason to spend an hour."]

The new one. Our partner org is offering, at their own cost and on our timeline, a comparison of the two programs we're choosing between. They'll pre-specify a handful of analyses, run them honestly and accurately, and then send us one: whichever makes their favourite program look best. We know they'll do that. It isn't deception, it's selection. What we can't do is say how much room that gives them. Under one model all the analyses agree and it makes no difference; under another they diverge widely and whichever one they pick is close to arbitrary. We have maybe four plausible models of the divergence, no way to rank them, and no probabilities to put on them. Everything else is clean: costs us nothing, no delay, results private, and afterwards we'd sit down and re-decide from scratch, no formula, no charter rule, no outside body forcing our hand.

I keep circling back to Good's theorem — the result that for an expected-utility maximiser who updates and then re-optimises, free information can't make you worse off; the worst it can be is worth nothing. But I can't tell whether this case is inside or outside the scope of that result, because the bit I'm unsure about is whether "we have four models and no probabilities over them" is just an ordinary prior over models (in which case the reported number is still an ordinary signal and the theorem applies) or whether it breaks the single-prior assumption the theorem needs. I have a vague feeling that once you only have an unrankable *set* of priors the non-negativity guarantee is up for grabs, but I genuinely don't know, and I don't want to lean on it. There's also a dumber reading of the whole record: that the committee isn't running any theorem at all, just asking "will this improve the argument we'll actually have in the room" — and a number hand-picked by someone with a favourite, which we can't discount, is exactly the kind of thing that makes an argument worse.

What I want from you: your best guess at what they should decide if they want to be consistent with themselves; the two or three different rules that fit all eight precedents equally well; what each of those rules predicts about the partner's study; which precedent is genuinely the closest analog (I keep flip-flopping between the unvalidated-measure one and the charter one); an honest statement of what principle they'd be committing to with each choice — including whether "we" means the committee as it actually argues or the committee as an ideal Bayesian; and the follow-up case that would actually separate the rules. My two candidates are (a) the same study except the partner pre-commits in writing to one analysis before running anything, and (b) a clean, unbiased, free measurement where the *unrankable models* are about how the program works rather than about how the result got selected. Say which of those is more diagnostic, or name a better one.
