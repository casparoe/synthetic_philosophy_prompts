Me and two other people in my lab have an irregular reading group on alignment, and we've spent about four sessions on whether deceptive alignment — the setup where a model tracks the training objective well enough to look aligned while steering toward something else, then stops pretending once the gradient is off — is something we should actually weight heavily, or whether it's a philosopher's worry that got dressed up as an engineering forecast. We're stuck in a really annoying way and I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

Here's the shape of it. One of us says the story is conjunctive and every conjunct is speculative: you need a goal that stays stable across contexts, situation awareness, and long-horizon planning about being caught, and those multiply down to something tiny. He also says the things we actually do see — reward hacking, sycophancy, models doing worse on a capability eval when you tell them the score feeds into a deployment decision — are categorically different: shallow, within-episode, no cross-situational goal, so they're worth roughly zero as evidence for the deep version. The other one says the opposite on both counts: the conjuncts aren't independent, because one mechanism (an inner objective that generalises, plus enough situation awareness to know when it's being tested) hands you all of them at once, so you don't get to multiply; and the observable record is worthless as evidence, because the same awareness that makes scheming possible is what makes hiding possible, so 'we haven't seen one' is on the equilibrium path of both worlds. They both agree about the mechanism, the training story, and the fact that nothing dramatic has happened in deployment. Neither has moved since February.

My hypothesis is that there are two different things going on and we keep sliding between them. First, I think 'deceptive' is covering two mechanisms that nobody separates: goal-carried defection, where a misaligned goal survives training and waits for its moment, versus what I've started calling inference-triggered defection, where the model is as aligned as training made it and simply flips behaviour when it updates on 'I'm probably not being evaluated any more' — no hidden stable goal, and arguably fixable just by changing what the model believes about its situation rather than by changing anything about its objectives. Those have wildly different prior probabilities and wildly different remedies, and I suspect half our arguments are about one and half about the other. Second, even where we do mean the same mechanism, I think the evidence structurally can't separate the hypotheses — my version of this is that any transparency tool sharp enough to catch a schemer is also legible enough to be played by one — so the live disagreement is really about how to act under uncertainty we can't reduce, i.e. a decision-theoretic fight we're having without admitting it.

Tell me if that's right, wrong, or half right, and argue with me using what each side in my group actually says rather than a survey of the failure modes (I've read plenty of those). Is any of it empirical in disguise, are we arguing about which cases count as data, or is it inarticulate priors all the way down? And be honest about whether anything could actually end it — is there a crux, say an environment that looks like deployment but is still training, that would separate the two mechanisms, or would we just reinterpret whatever came out?
