I had a three-hour argument about Boltzmann brains last night with a friend (several beers, no resolution) and I want to turn it into something usable for the little reading group I run, so help me build it.

What I want is a decision tree — a "which position on the Boltzmann brain problem do you actually hold?" flow chart. The starting commitment everyone in the group already shares is: a brain, or a brain plus a room's worth of apparent surroundings, assembled by a thermal fluctuation and full of memories of a past that never happened, is physically possible in the relevant sense, and it matters how many observers of that kind there are compared to ordinary evolved ones. Take that as given and go from there to a named leaf.

The rules I want it to follow, because most versions of this I've seen are useless:

1. Every branch node has to be a question someone who has never heard the labels can answer — a concrete case or a claim they can just accept or reject. No "do you accept the self-sampling assumption?", no "which anthropic principle are you?". If a node is really about whether you count observers or observer-moments, find the case that forces the choice: a fluctuation that exists for one second versus one that lasts a century, or a duplicated observer, or whatever actually does the work.

2. Build the branches out of the logical space first and only attach names afterwards. My guesses at the load-bearing questions, but use better ones if you have them: is the fact that our evidence looks orderly, law-governed and mutually consistent itself strong evidence against our being a fluctuation; does a cosmology that implies you are probably a fluctuation thereby discredit itself, or does it just tell you something depressing while remaining the best theory available; does the problem survive if the far future of the universe is finite rather than eternal; should the counting be over observers, observer-moments, or something else; and — the branch I most want — if you land on "yes, I am probably a Boltzmann brain", does anything whatever follow, and if nothing does, has the problem quietly been defused rather than solved?

3. Each leaf gets its name where the position has one and "unnamed" where nobody defends it, plus one sentence on the most awkward thing that leaf is committed to. Include at least a leaf for rejecting the whole framework — the view that there is no well-defined measure over observers, or no fact about what a typical observer is like, so the counts were never meaningful in the first place — since that's where my friend retreated to when cornered.

4. There's one position I couldn't crack and want the tree to locate. My friend's version of it, which I'll call the Boltzmann bargain: a fluctuation that lasts a moment, has no future, can't act and can't be disappointed isn't the kind of thing that can be wronged or that has plans, so even if the counting says we are almost certainly such things, there is no practical or epistemic upshot — the whole thing is a puzzle about how to count, not a puzzle about us. I genuinely don't know whether this is a real position, a confused version of one, or something that looks stable only because it's hiding a commitment. If it's confused, I want the tree to show which branch it collides with.

5. Flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate rather than papering over it. I suspect "there's no fact about typicality" and some versions of "the measure exists but we can't ever be in a position to use it" will be hard to tell apart by cases, and I want that said out loud. Also list any combination of answers the tree permits that nobody actually holds, and tell me why: incoherent, unatractive, or merely unexplored.

Audience is six people, mixed levels: two have read some anthropic material, the rest have popular-book cosmology. Six to eight questions, around ten leaves. And I'm writing this from a place with no library access, so please don't point me at papers or hand me a reading list — I want the conceptual structure and I'll go find the literature myself later.
