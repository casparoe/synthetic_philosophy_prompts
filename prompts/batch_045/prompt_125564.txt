I sit on the Corrections Research Review Board, the joint university–Department of Corrections body that has to sign off on any study involving people in our state's custody. The case in front of us next month is not a routine protocol. A private foundation has offered to pay for converting one 120-bed housing unit at Fairhaven, the state's aging maximum-security prison, into something like the Norwegian model — private rooms with doors the prisoners can close, a kitchen they cook in, normalized visiting space, correctional officers without riot gear whose job description includes eating meals with the men. Nine hundred people at Fairhaven would be eligible for those 120 beds, so the unit cannot house everyone who wants it. The donor's two conditions are what make this hard: assignment must be by public random lottery, and the unit must be evaluated as a five-year recidivism trial, with the foundation's money reverting and the wing reverting to its current design if the measured effect on reoffending is not statistically distinguishable from the rest of the prison. The warden is for it, the officers' union is suspicious (their members would be asked to do a different, more skilled and more emotionally demanding job, and would effectively be research subjects along with the prisoners), and a victims' advocacy group has invoked an old nineteenth-century poor-law doctrine — that the condition of the convicted must never be made more eligible than that of the poorest law-abiding citizen — while also objecting that lottery winners get a better prison by luck.

What has divided us, though, is a cluster of arguments I raised myself and am no longer sure I believe. First, there is a selection worry: I teach a seminar on punishment theory, and I have always used Norway as my example, but Norway imprisons a different population for much shorter stretches and releases them into a welfare state, so the comparison may tell us about who goes to prison in Oslo and for how long rather than about architecture, and we may be buying an expensive and uninformative trial. Second — and this is the thing I can't shake — there is what I have started calling comparative degradation: the eight hundred men left in the old blocks will now know, in a way they can see and touch, that the state is capable of housing them decently and has chosen not to, and it strikes me that the reform may actively worsen the lived experience of the majority it does not touch, even if it improves the experience of the few. Third, if the trial succeeds, decency will have been made contingent on producing a measurable recidivism dividend, and if it "fails," men who have spent five years with a key to their own door will be moved back into a cage; whichever way it goes, humane conditions will have been distributed by an experimental apparatus rather than as a standing feature of what the state owes. I should add that our own staff analyst has floated alternative allocation rules that avoid the lottery — assign by risk score, assign by disciplinary record, assign by first-come-first-served seniority — each of which seems to me to encode its own objectionable message about what a decent cell is for.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote: a recommendation to approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with reasons that people on the other side of the split could actually answer rather than merely being outvoted. If you recommend conditions, specify which allocation mechanism we should require instead of the lottery and why, say what should happen to the unit and its residents at the end of the five years, and explain how we should handle informed consent from men who would be agreeing to a chance at the better unit while still in custody. The part I most want done honestly: I know which objection I think is strongest against the position I am inclined to take — that if we keep refusing to let reform be tested, we will go on spending decades choosing between punishment and rehabilitation on the strength of anecdote and national mood, and the eight hundred men in the old blocks will be no better off for our scruples. I want that argument given its full weight in the memo even if you end up rejecting the proposal, and I want the recommendation to be concrete enough that the Foundation's lawyer and the warden could act on it the day after we vote.
