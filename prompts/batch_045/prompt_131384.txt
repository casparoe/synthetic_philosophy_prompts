I sit on the review panel for a small foundation's thematic grant program: roughly six postdoctoral fellowships for three years, plus a modest travel and workshop budget, all earmarked for what the call calls contradiction-tolerant reasoning. The program was motivated less by the semantic paradoxes than by engineering. Teams building retrieval systems over large heterogeneous corpora, agent frameworks that aggregate the outputs of several models, and verification pipelines working from specifications written by different people all report the same complaint: their inputs are straightforwardly inconsistent, classical consequence is therefore useless to them, and yet nothing in the paraconsistent literature seems usable off the shelf. One engineer on the panel put the complaint in a form I keep coming back to. The blocking problem, he said, is not explosion but choice. There are many non-explosive consequence relations; they disagree about disjunctive syllogism, about how negation behaves, about whether and how classical reasoning can be recovered in regions where no inconsistency has been found, and no one can tell his team which of these they should implement or on what grounds one would be better than another. He added that his group has informally been using a stopgap — a hand-tuned rule about how far the effects of a detected contradiction are allowed to spread before the pipeline quarantines the affected claims — and that nobody, including him, can say what the principled version of that rule would be.

A second disagreement on the panel is the one I'd most like help with. One member argues that the entire program rests on a category mistake: paraconsistent logics tell you what follows from inconsistent premises, not how to reason rationally in the face of inconsistency, and what the engineers actually want is an account of belief, thresholds for acceptance, and revision under inconsistency — epistemology and perhaps formal epistemology, not new consequence relations. Another replies that this is backwards, that a non-classical consequence relation is precisely the missing ingredient, since any revision policy presupposes some notion of what is and isn't entailed by an inconsistent corpus, and that the epistemological work will be unconstrained until that is settled. I don't think either has fully answered the other, and the answer seems to determine whether we should be funding logicians at all.

So here is what I want from you: a defended research agenda for this money, not a survey and not a reading list. Give me three or four lines of work you would actually fund, in priority order, drawn from things like the choice problem among rival non-explosive consequence relations; the question of classical recapture in apparently consistent regions; a principled account of the locality or spread of a contradiction's effects, along the lines of the engineers' ad hoc quarantine rule; the revenge and strengthened-paradox phenomena that seem to dog non-classical treatments of truth; the logic-versus-epistemology dispute above; and anything I haven't listed that you think matters more. For each, say what makes it worth the fellowship years, what is upstream of what, whether it is tractable in three years by people trained in logic and philosophy as opposed to needing ML collaborators, and how neglected it already is. Be explicit about what you would refuse to fund and why — in particular, whether the foundational work on truth and the paradoxes should be in the portfolio at all given that the money came with an engineering motivation attached. Finally, tell me what evidence over the next two years — a theorem, a benchmark, a negative result, a change in what practitioners report needing — would move items up or down your ranking.
