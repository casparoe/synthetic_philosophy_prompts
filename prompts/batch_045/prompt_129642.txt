I write a column on science and ideas for a magazine with a general but demanding readership, and I am drafting a piece about why almost every argument over advanced AI ends up reaching for a historical analogy -- the atomic bomb, the printing press, the Industrial Revolution -- and why each of those comparisons quietly does rhetorical work that its users rarely declare. I need help deciding which analogy, if any, should carry the piece. I want your judgement rather than a survey of what has been said: please do not cite papers, name authors, or point me toward reading, and do not gesture at sources you cannot consult. Reason from the structure of the cases themselves.

Please take on the role of a historian of technology who has spent the last decade also doing forecasting work for AI labs: someone careful about mechanisms and lags, allergic to analogies that are only vibes, and used to being asked by editors for a single number they can print.

Before you rate anything, work through a short private scratchpad on method. What job is a historical analogy actually doing in an argument about a technology whose arrival time, capability profile, and rate of diffusion are all uncertain? What would have to be true of a past episode for it to stand in well? And in what specific ways can an apparently apt analogy mislead: by fixing a timescale, by implying a single decisive decision-maker, by locating the change in the artifact rather than in the institutions around it, by making a contested outcome feel inevitable, by pre-loading a particular policy response? Settle those questions first; your ratings should follow from them and be consistent with them.

The candidates:

A. The arrival of nuclear weapons
B. The printing press
C. The Industrial Revolution
D. The nineteenth-century replacement of local time by railway and then standard time: a case where the substance of the change was a new shared coordinate that everyone was eventually forced to adopt, where the payoff arrived only after large complementary investments, and where almost nobody at the time thought they were living through it
E. The joint-stock company: a legal rather than material technology, which created a new kind of agent with limited liability and fiduciary duties, let strangers pool capital, and enabled action at a scale and time-horizon no individual could manage
You may add at most one candidate of your own if you think I have missed the strongest option.

Rate each candidate 0--100 on five dimensions, and say in one sentence why it gets the number it does:

1. Mechanism match: does it engage what is supposed to be genuinely new here -- cheap replication, substitution for cognitive work, rapid capability growth, low marginal cost?
2. Timescale honesty: does it resist inviting false precision about how fast any of this happens?
3. Locus of change: does it correctly put the weight on surrounding institutions and coordination rather than on the object itself?
4. Policy traction: does it point toward levers that exist, without smuggling in a particular regulatory agenda by metaphor?
5. Resistance to capture: how hard is it for an interested party to hijack the analogy for a conclusion it does not support?

Then state the weights you are using across these five dimensions and why, compute a weighted composite for each candidate, and sort them into tiers labelled "build the piece around this", "usable as a passing illustration", and "cut". Give me the whole thing as a table. Follow the table with: (i) one paragraph on the single most dangerous way your top-ranked analogy would mislead my readers; (ii) for each candidate, the one clause of hedging that would have to travel with it in print; and (iii) a single sentence I can use in the piece to introduce whichever analogy you rank first, written so that it does the framing work without overselling the match.
