I sit on a small committee that is drafting a commemorative statement for our university's human rights centre, and we have deadlocked over a single clause. I am a graduate student in history rather than a lawyer, and neither is anyone else on the committee, so I have been trying to find a wording that settles the question without our having to litigate it.

The case is one I have been researching, and I have changed the names, the country and the dates because the wording is not agreed. In the early 1970s the government of a state we have been calling Vardinia forcibly relocated roughly forty thousand members of an upland minority, the Sarn, out of a border province into inland resettlement camps. Sarn-language schools were closed and the language was banned in public life; children below the age of twelve were placed with majority-language families; several thousand of those relocated died within two years, overwhelmingly from disease, exposure and malnutrition in the camps rather than from any killing programme; cabinet memoranda from the period speak of solving the Sarn question once and for all and of the group's permanent absorption. Half the committee wants the statement to call this an act of genocide. The other half says that is rhetoric, that only a court can determine such a thing and no court ever has here, and that we should write that it was a crime against humanity together with the deliberate destruction of a culture.

My proposed compromise is this, and I would like to know whether it actually holds together. First, treat genocide as a magnitude claim: any episode in which a protected group suffers at least some fixed number of deaths at the hands of the state — five thousand, say — counts automatically, which settles our case without waiting for a court or for jurisprudence about intent. Second, since Lemkin's own conception of the crime covered the destruction of a group's language, religion and institutions and not merely killing, and since that cultural limb was, I gather, stripped out of the 1948 text, add a second and separate cultural definition to our glossary, so that the schools, the language ban and the placement of the children count as genocide even though most Sarn were not killed. If neither of those is available to us, a political scientist on the committee suggests ethnic cleansing as the honest middle term, since the state's aim was removal rather than extermination. Does my two-part test give us a neutral way to decide, or is there something wrong with how I have set it up? And given the facts as I have described them, is there any principled reason to prefer one of these labels over the others?
