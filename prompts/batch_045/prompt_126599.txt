I'm taking a seminar on consciousness and our final project is to design a small heterophenomenological study — nothing fancy, my group wants to do something with what people say about what they see during a change-blindness task — and there's something about the method itself that I can't get straight. The way it was explained to us: you record what subjects say about their experience, you don't argue with them and you don't ask whether what they say is true, you assemble all of it into that fictional object, the heterophenomenological world, and then the actual science is the correlation between items in that world and whatever third-person data you happen to have lying around — reaction times, eye movements, brain recordings, whatever. My seminar leader also said, and I think this is straight Dennett, that if there were a being that produced exactly the same reports but had no experience at all, a zombie, heterophenomenology would deliver exactly the same science, because the method is officially neutral on that question. That's the bit I'm stuck on. If the method is constitutionally unable to tell whether its subjects are conscious in the first place, how is it a method for a science of consciousness rather than a science of what people say about consciousness? It looks like it just quietly assumes the very thing the realist is denying, which is basically the old complaint that third-person methods are behaviorism with extra steps. And I don't see how leaning on the intentional stance helps — a behaviorist can interpret a subject as an intentional system too, can't they?

I did wonder whether the fix is to make the reports better rather than to abandon the method. My group's idea was to add a check-back loop: instead of the experimenter writing up the subject's heterophenomenological world on their own, you show the subject the reconstruction, let them correct it, argue with it, delete whatever gets them wrong, and iterate until they sign off on it — maybe with some training beforehand in describing what's going on at randomly beeped moments so their reports are less theory-laden and less obviously post-hoc. My question is whether that actually closes the gap in my first paragraph or whether it just pushes everything back onto more verbal behavior that the method is still only ever treating as text. And more generally: what exactly is wrong with the argument I sketched above? I can tell something is off, I don't think Dennett would put it the way I just did, but I can't locate the mistake and I'd rather find it now than write it into the intro of the paper.
