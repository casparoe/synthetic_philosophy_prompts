hey — I'm on the comittee that hands out my uni's small emergency grant fund, maybe 60 applicants a term, and every meeting goes in circles because people are quietly arguing to equalise different things. I want to hand round a flowchart before the next meeting that sorts people by what they actually think about who should get what, not by which label they've heard of.

Could you build me a decision tree that starts from "this applicant should get more money than that one" and ends on a named position about what it is we should be equalising?

Constraints:

- 8 to 10 branch questions, roughly 12 leaves.
- Every node has to be a question a volunteer with no philosophy background could answer, phrased about an applicant or a pair of applicants, never about a theory. No "is welfare the right currency?" questions.
- Build it from the logical space rather than from the list of names: give the splitting questions first, note which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and for each empty combination say briefly whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
- Leaves I want included: strict egalitarianism, prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, leximin, luck-egalitarian equality of fortune, Rawls-style primary goods, Sen/Nussbaum-style capabilities, Arneson-style equality of opportunity for welfare, Cohen-style access to advantage.
- Plus one leaf for a currency I've been toying with and can't place: call it "equality of slack" — the idea that what should be equalised isn't how well off someone is or what they can do, but how much margin for error they have, i.e. how many things can go wrong in their life before they're in real trouble. Give it a fair run: the tree should contain the questions that would actually seperate it from capabilities and from plain resources, and if it collapses into one of them, say so and say exactly where.
- The cases used at the nodes have to include: an applicant with expensive tastes (only a costly gym setup and a specific diet make their life go well), an applicant who's perfectly cheerful on very little, a wheelchair user who needs more cash to get the same mobility, someone who recieved a grant last term and blew it immediately, and a PhD student whose income is fine but whose supervisor can pull their funding at will.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate instead of pretending it can, and finish with a one-line scoring key: which pattern of answers lands on which leaf.

Don't give me a reading list or citations, and don't spend words on definitions or on summarising the views — I roughly know them, I want the instrument.
