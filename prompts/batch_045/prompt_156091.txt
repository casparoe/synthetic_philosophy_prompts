Hi — I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about why two perfectly competent people can look at the same pile of leaked documents, the same timeline, the same denials, and one comes away convinced there was a cover-up while the other comes away convinced there's nothing there. I don't want another he-said-she-said piece. I want to try running an adversarial collaboration on paper between the two camps and publish the joint document, so I need you to draft the statement both sides could actually sign.

The two parties (names I can use in the piece, keep them if they work):

- The Particularists: a conspiracy theory is just a causal claim about some people acting together in secret, to be judged on its evidence like any other historical claim. Real conspiracies happen, official accounts are sometimes false, and the phrase is mostly a slur used to end inquiry rather than start it.
- The Structural Skeptics: conspiracy theorizing has failure modes that attach to the activity rather than to any particular claim — runaway distrust of institutional sources, explanatory ambition that grows to fit whatever the evidence does, a habit of treating the absence of proof as proof of concealment — and the base rate of large, successful, long-lived secrets is low enough that a default discount is warranted before anyone opens the file.

Two things I definitely want in the weeds, because they're the parts I think the usual arguments miss:

First, the archive glut. This isn't 1975. Someone making a case now often has terabytes of genuine primary material — court filings, FOIA dumps, flight logs, depositions, metadata, old news clips — with no editor and no gatekeeper, selected by a distributed community rather than a single author. So the old picture of the conspiracy theorist as evidence-starved, spinning theory out of thin air, doesn't fit. Please have the collaboration address what breaks when the problem is too much raw evidence instead of too little: selection from huge corpora, the fact that any large document set contains something suspicious, disagreement-within-a-movement about which documents matter, and whether abundant primary sources help or hurt a case that no institution has vouched for.

Second, leakage. Both sides should write down actual numbers: for a secret involving roughly N participants maintained for about T years, what's the probability it stays secret for that long, and how does that change as N and T go up? Give me both camps' estimates, not just their reasoning. I think the two sides are much further apart on this than they realize and it's where a lot of the real disagreement lives.

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. Things I'd expect to see in it: what both sides mean by a conspiracy; that real ones occur and that some official statements are false; that unfalsifiability is a genuine defect and not just a debating trick; that being mocked or ignored doesn't make a theory true; something about what a valid inference from a leaked or declassified document looks like versus an invalid one; and something about the asymmetry in costs between chasing a conspiracy that isn't there and missing one that is. Don't pad it — I want claims that actually do work later in the document, not filler both sides could sign in their sleep.

2. A disagreements table, with exactly these columns:
   - crux_id — a short label
   - question — the sub-question, one sentence, phrased neutrally
   - skeptic_wording — the Skeptic's position, in wording a Skeptic would sign
   - particularist_wording — the Particularist's position, in wording a Particularist would sign
   - agreed_probe — a concrete test, case, or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on the crux, and that could realistically happen or be checked within a few years (a specific kind of document release, a specific kind of insider admission, a specific pattern in the record). Real classes of evidence, not fantasies
   - skeptic_prediction — what the Skeptic predicts the probe will show, written down in advance
   - particularist_prediction — same for the Particularist
   - update_condition — what each would concede if the probe came out the other way; if a side genuinely wouldn't move, say so and say why

3. A short section on which parts of their fight are merely verbal. I suspect one is the definition — whether "conspiracy theory" already contains a verdict of being unwarranted, or just names a kind of explanation — and that this quietly settles (or dissolves) disputes that look substantive. Tell me which disagreements survive once the words are sorted out.

4. Both camps' leakage numbers, with the assumptions each is making explicit.

Hard rules: no strawmen, and nothing vacuous — if a disagreement is just "we weigh the evidence differently," that's a sign you haven't found the crux yet. Each disagreement should be sharp enough that if I read it to a member of that camp they'd say yes, that's my position.

After the document, please give me a JSON cover sheet I can use as a sidebar, with exactly these fields:
- n_agreed_claims (integer)
- n_genuine_cruxes (integer)
- n_verbal_disputes (integer)
- verbal_disputes (array of short strings, one per merely-verbal disagreement)
- most_load_bearing_crux (a crux_id)
- leakage_estimate_skeptic (string: their figures for N participants and T years, plus the shape of the curve)
- leakage_estimate_particularist (same)
- one_sentence_both_signed (a single sentence both camps could put their names under)
- what_the_document_cant_settle (1–2 sentences: the residual question the collaboration leaves open)
- confidence_collaboration_would_hold (one of "high", "medium", "low")
