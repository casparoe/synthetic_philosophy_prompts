ok so context: I'm in a political theory reading group, and last night after the session I got into a two-hour argument with my friend (she's more of a social choice/econ person) about Sen's liberal paradox — the prude and the lewd and the copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, three options (nobody reads it, prude reads it, lewd reads it), each of them decisive over whether *they* read it, Pareto says prude-reads beats lewd-reads because both of them prefer that, and you get a cycle. She kept saying "just restrict the domain and move on," I kept saying that misses the point, and we went in circles because we just restated ourselves louder each time. So I want to write down what I actually think and get it audited, and I honestly don't know where the weak joint is — I'd rather find out now than in front of the group.

Here's the set, more or less in the order I'd defend them:
(1) Background welfarism: outside rights questions, social evaluation should just track people's preferences over social states, and I accept the Pareto condition with zero hesitation — if everyone strictly prefers x to y then x is socially better, full stop.
(2) Minimal liberalism, but I read it as a claim about rights rather than about preferences: each person has at least one pair of alternatives in their own personal sphere over which they're decisive, and their being decisive settles it. Rights are side-constraints, not entries in a sum — how strongly other people feel about the matter isn't something you weigh against them.
(3) I think the result is deep, not a formal curiosity. It isn't "oh, like Arrow, you just need a domain condition." I think it shows something real about the limits of any liberalism that wants to stay welfarist, and I don't think there's a cheap fix available by tinkering inside the preference-aggregation framework.
(4) But I do want a meddlesomeness filter, and I'll state it precisely because she kept misrepresenting it: call a pair of alternatives clean for a person if the two differ only in that person's own conduct. A person's say over a clean pair is a genuine exercise of a right; and preferences that are essentially about what someone else does inside *their* sphere — the lewd's enjoyment at the thought of the prude being made to read — shouldn't be counted by the social ranking at all, not even for Pareto purposes. I think excluding those is principled rather than ad hoc.
(5) I'm also anti-perfectionist here: I don't think the mechanism should sort people's reasons into the noble ones (the prude wanting to spare the lewd corruption) and the base ones (envy, malice, busybodying). That's exactly the kind of judgement a liberal order is supposed to abstain from.
(6) And I think preferences are holistic: what someone prefers is a whole social state, you can't carve their ranking into a self-regarding component and an other-regarding component and feed only the first one into the machine, and their reasons for a preference don't change what the preference is. This is the bit she claimed kills everything and I genuinely couldn't see why.
(7) Case verdict: in Sen's own example I think the right social outcome is that nobody reads the book — and I want to say that without saying the prude's right counts for more than the lewd's, because as far as I'm concerned they're symmetric.
(8) Where I think the real answer eventually lies: rights shouldn't be modeled as a condition on a social ordering at all, but as giving someone control over a strategy set in a game form. And I want to keep (3) alongside that.
