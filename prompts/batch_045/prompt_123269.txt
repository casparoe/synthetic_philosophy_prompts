I sit on a small working group that is drafting guidance on algorithmic accountability, and one member — a mathematician by training, among lawyers and policy people — keeps closing discussion with the same move. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, he says, is just the liar paradox in symbols: you construct a sentence which asserts of itself that it cannot be derived from the axioms, and you find that it is true but unprovable. The moral he extracts is that no system can certify itself, because every system needs a judge standing outside it to vouch for its soundness. From there he moves to his conclusion: that it is a mathematical necessity, and not merely prudent, for a regulator to refuse a firm's own self-assessment of its models, and that the point generalises — no audit regime, no professional code, no constitution can be self-sufficient. He adds that the correspondence with the halting problem is exact, since in both cases the general question cannot be settled from inside the machinery in which it is posed.

I would like to audit this rather than simply disagree with it, and I am not confident of the details. Could you lay the mapping out explicitly: what, in the setting of the first and second incompleteness theorems, plays the role of the liar sentence, of truth, of proof, and of the external judge; where the analogy with the liar breaks down; and which features of the liar it quietly imports that Gödel's argument does not have? Two points in particular puzzle me. One is whether the apparent self-reference of a Gödel sentence is anything like the vicious circularity of a liar sentence, or whether it is something tamer which simply has a determinate truth value in the intended model. The other is whether the external standpoint is genuinely external, or whether the theory that certifies a given system is only another theory with the same limitation one level up.

Then the part that matters for our draft: does his conclusion survive the audit? He wants to read a substantive institutional prescription — mandatory outside audit — off a formal result. My suspicion is that the theorem does less work than he thinks: it concerns consistent, effectively axiomatised theories strong enough to encode a certain amount of arithmetic, and I cannot tell whether an audit regime or a professional code is enough like such a theory for the result to transfer at all, or whether what we actually find in codes is vagueness and conflict rather than incompleteness. It would help me most to have the disanalogies separated into those that are load-bearing for his conclusion and those that are harmless, and to hear whether he should have reached instead for something like Carroll's regress about rules for applying rules, or for a better analogy altogether.
