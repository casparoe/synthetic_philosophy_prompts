I'm a novelist, maybe two hundred pages into a book whose main character is a retired taxidermist, Odile, who has quietly turned her dead stock into a homemade census of local bird species, and because of her I've ended up absurdly deep in the old raven puzzle — the one where noticing a white shoe in the hall is supposed to be evidence that all ravens are black. The move I keep running into is the arithmetic one: sure, the shoe counts, but it counts by a vastly tinier amount than an actual black raven does, because there are so many more non-black things in the world than there are ravens, and that size difference is supposed to explain why our guts insist the shoe is irrelevant. That more or less satisfied me for about a week.

Then I started running Odile's counting exercises with actual numbers, for a scene where she does the same thing on her own shelves, and I think I broke it. Her collection is nothing like the world: she has a hundred mounted birds and somewhere around ninety of them are ravens, and when I redo the sums with those numbers the ranking flips — pulling a non-black non-raven out of her collection does more for the generalization than pulling a black raven does. Which might be fine, except it seems to mean that which observation is the better evidence isn't a fact about the hypothesis and the observation at all, it's a fact about whatever pool of objects you happen to be drawing from. A second version of the same worry: if Odile deliberately sorts the shelf by colour first and only then checks whether the pale things are ravens, the whole thing feels different from drawing blindfolded, even though what she ends up holding in her hand is the same stuffed bird, which I would of thought shouldn't matter. Have I got the arithmetic wrong, or does the fix genuinely predict the flip?

What I keep noticing, and don't have a word for, is that the puzzle doesn't so much get solved as get shoved one step back: everwhere I look, the real work is being done by something about the setup — how big the pool is, what's in it, whether she picked the object or bumped into it — and once you see that, the original weirdness is still sitting there, just relocated into the sampling arrangement. People keep telling me this is the same shape as the grue business, or that it's just the reference-class thing, or plain sampling bias, but none of those sit right with me: there's nothing gerrymandered about 'raven' or 'black', Odile knows perfectly well which class each bird belongs to, and she isn't being biased, she's drawing blind. I'm not after a reading list, and a name by itself won't do me much good either — I want to know what the thing is, what it isn't, and where its edges are. Is there an established concept for the way the paradox relocates into the sampling setup, how does it differ from those three neighbours, and if there isn't one, what would you call it, how would you define it, and what would count as a borderline case? And underneath all of it: does any of this vindicate the gut feeling that the shoe is irrelevant, or does it only explain the feeling away?
