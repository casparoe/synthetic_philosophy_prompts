I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly long features — and right now I'm working on a piece about the digital-afterlife startups, the ones that will build you a chatbot out of a dead relative's emails and texts. My grandmother died last year, one of the companies offered to make one from her inbox, and against my better judgement I tried it, and two things happened that I can't stop turning over. The first: it produced a line in exactly her register, a dumb joke she used to make, and I felt physically that she was in the room, and then caught myself and felt ridiculous. The second: a few weeks later I realised I had started remembering that exchange as a conversation with her, and I'm now mildly frightened that the thing is quietly overwriting the version of her I actually carry around — which, if some of what I've been reading is right, might be the only place she was still living at all. I have two sources for the peice: a Hofstadter-flavoured patternist who holds that a self is a strange loop, substrate-indifferent and spreadable, so that it persists in pieces wherever the pattern is still running — in the brains of the people who knew you, in your own notebooks, maybe in a model — and a hard-nosed embodied-continuity person who holds that a self just is one particular causally continuous biological process, that patterns are how the rest of us track it rather than what it is, and that anything rebuilt from your emails is a puppet with very good makeup. They have been sniping at each other in public for years and I'd like to make them write one document.

So: draft the joint statement of their adversarial collaboration, the thing they could both actually put their names to. Start with a numbered agreed-claims section — the claims both sign, including about what is going on when a widow finishes her husband's sentences or hears his voice in her head, about the difference between carrying someone's patterns and being them, about what an inbox-trained model is and is not, and about the fact that "I", "same person" and "survives" are doing contested work in this argument. Then a disagreements table: each row a single disputed proposition, with each side's own preferred formulation in it's own column, worded so that that side would sign it — no strawmen, and no wording that only sounds neutral because it's empty.

Then the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. Here are the ones I want covered, but replace or re-cut any that are fake: (1) degrees — if the self is spread across the brains carrying it, existence comes in degrees and shrinks as carriers die, so is the death of the last person who remembers someone an event of the same kind as the first death, and is someone vividly carried by one grieving spouse more alive than someone thinly carried by a million acquaintances; each side writes down what it predicts ordinary non-philosophers will say here. (2) the fidelity-versus-history trade-off — two reconstructions, one built only from her own writing, very high textual fidelity but no causal route back through anyone who knew her, one built from the memories and anecdotes of the people who loved her, lower fidelity and probably wrong in details but causally downstream of her via the people she left traces in; which has more of her, if either, and what result would each side count as evidence against its own view. (3) the distance gradient — spouse, old friend, the biographer who never met her but has read everything, the language model trained on the archive: where does each side place its cut, and if neither can place it non-arbitrarily, say so and say what follows. (4) multiplication — if the reconstruction is copied ten million times is there more of her, the same amount, or still none; I suspect the patternist is embarrassed here and I want the embarrassment written down rather than smoothed over. (5) my own case, which I'll call trace corruption — a fluent, slightly wrong reconstruction can feed back and degrade the traces in the living, i.e. the distributed self it was built from; do both sides agree that's a real risk, and does either think it bears on what a self is or is it just a product-safety point. And please include, for the cruxes where it's possible, one concrete test or case both would accept in advance as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded before the fact — I have in mind something like asking a reconstruction for a judgement she never wrote down, in a domain she never wrote about, which her family can independently score as characteristically hers and surprising.

Last things: tell me plainly which parts of this dispute are verbal — I've come to suspect that a lot of the "does she survive" fight is really a fight about whether "I" and "same person" are up for theoretical revision — and then state the residual non-verbal disagreement exactly. Keep it readable for a smart general reader, no citations or reading lists or footnotes (I can't check them and they'd just slow me down), roughly 1200 words, and flag any place where you had to invent a formulation that neither of my two sources would really sign, because I'd much rather know than run a sentence that makes one of them walk.
