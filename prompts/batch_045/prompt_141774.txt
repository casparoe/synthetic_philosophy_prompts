I'm a high school senior writing an independent research paper on whether individuals can have obligations with respect to collective harms, and I have been going in circles with a friend about the case of buying factory-farmed chicken. I would like your credence on one precisely specified proposition, with the reasoning that produces it broken open.

Stipulate the following, so that no data is needed and we are not arguing about empirical guesses. A regional grocery chain orders chickens in fixed lots of 5,000 birds per week, from a forecast that rounds to the nearest lot; weekly demand would have to fall by roughly 2,500 birds' worth for the order to drop by a single lot. There are about 40,000 regular customers, my friend and I among them, and our purchasing decisions are not statistically independent, since people of the same social circle tend to influence each other's habits. Wholesale prices respond to demand, so that when one customer stops buying, the price falls slightly and other customers buy somewhat more, recovering somewhere between 0 and 40 percent of the reduction. Finally, any bird not ordered is never hatched, so no existing bird is spared a bad life; there is simply a bird who never exists. Stipulate also that the life of such a bird is, on balance, worse for the bird than never existing.

The proposition, call it P: given all of that, a regular customer who keeps buying the chicken does something she has a moral reason not to do, that is, the inefficacy objection in its strongest form (my own act makes no difference to any outcome, therefore I have no individual moral reason to abstain) fails in this case. If you think the truth of P depends on some further empirical fact I have not stipulated, name it and treat it as one of the nodes below rather than stalling.

My own credence in P is about 0.65. My friend's is about 0.25.

What I want from you:

1. Your number as a probability, and then the decomposition: the sub-questions your number depends on. Please include at least these, each with a number and one line of justification:

(a) whether the mechanism as stipulated really yields a nonzero expected reduction at all, once the lot-rounding, the threshold, and the price pass-through are combined;
(b) whether a life that is prevented from being lived can count as a benefit to anyone, and so whether the reduction in that node is a moral gain or merely a change in population;
(c) whether making a difference in expectation, however tiny, is the right moral currency here at all, as opposed to participation in a harmful practice, the expression of a attitude, or what would happen if everyone relevant acted the same way;
(d) whether there is some threshold of causal significance below which moral reasons simply do not attach, or whether small chances of large effects scale down without vanishing;
(e) whether the fact that abstainers cluster socially, rather than being independent, raises the effective expected difference enough to matter.

Add any further node you think belongs, and delete nothing without saying why.

2. How the numbers combine into your headline figure, stated explicitly. If they do not multiply cleanly, say so and say why.

3. Which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and which one argument or piece of evidence would move that term the most, in which direction.

4. Which node the gap between my 0.65 and my friend's 0.25 most likely lives in, and why you think that is where the disagreement is rather than somewhere else.

5. One or two sentences on whether your answer carries over to voting in a large election and to an individual's own greenhouse gas emissions, or whether something in the stipulations above is doing special work.

Please do not give me citations, a bibliography, or claims about what the literature says; I have no way to check sources, and I do not want arguments from authority. Reason from the structure of the case as I have set it out. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
