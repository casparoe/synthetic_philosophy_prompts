Hi — Im a novelist, not a philosopher, and next month Im giving the closing talk at a two day workshop on personal identity. The room is mostly analytic philosophers who treat a broadly Parfitian reductionism as close to orthodoxy, plus one Pali scholar who has already emailed me that anatta is a claim about which things are not self (the formula applied to each of the five aggregates) rather than a denial that there is anyone there, a memory researcher who works on episodic memory and the narrative self, and a scattering of meditation teachers in the back who will want to know what any of this does for them.

My thesis: novelists have been running the no-self argument as a laboratory experiment for a couple of centuries, and what the novel shows is that identity-tracking is a practical achievement, not a fact we discover. Readers will happily follow a protagonist whose name, body, and memories change mid-book — in my current draft the point of view character goes by three different names, one section is in second person, and not one early reader has been confused — as long as enough anchors stay in place: a recurring worry, a scar, a walk, a causal slot in the plot. You can strip any one anchor and readers keep tracking; you cant strip them all at once; and how few you can get away with is set by genre, not by metaphysics. So on my telling anatta is not the claim that there is no one here, it is the claim that there is no anchor that could not in principle be stripped, and no anchor that was doing the work of an owner. Parfit's target is a different animal: a further fact of identity over and above the anchors, which is not the sort of thing anybody was ever tracking in a novel anyway.
