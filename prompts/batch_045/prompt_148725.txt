I'm a PhD student and next week I'm running a single 75-minute session on Bertrand's paradox for a mixed undergraduate module called something like "Probability and Rational Choice". Most of the room is philosophy majors who stopped maths at sixteen and have forgotten most of it, plus the one joint-honours maths student every year who will tune out unless there's something in it for them. I've been told to "do the paradox and say something about the principle of indifference". I was arguing about it with a friend in the stats department who said the whole thing is a non-problem and that any physicist would just ask how the chord was generated — which is exactly the reaction I expect from the room and exactly the one I'm unsure how to handle. So please: don't explain Bertrand's paradox to me, plan how I build it for them.

The materials I'm assuming are the usual ones: a circle, an inscribed equilateral triangle, the question "what's the probability that a randomly drawn chord is longer than the side of the triangle", and the three classical answers — 1/3 if you pick two points uniformly on the circumference, 1/2 if you pick a radius and then a point uniformly along it, 1/4 if you pick a midpoint uniformly in the disc. What I want is the order the pieces have to go in and why. What has to be in place before the three numbers can even look like a contradiction rather than three different questions? My guess is that "same event, same question, three procedures" has to be nailed down before anything else, but maybe the set-up of the chord itself is doing more work than I think. And where in that order should the principle of indifference actually be stated — up front, as the thing under test, or extracted from the three methods at the end?

Here are the misconceptions I'm expecting, in the order I think they'll arrive, and I want to know where in the sequence each one gets headed off — and tell me if I've got the order wrong or missed the big one. (1) "There's no paradox, the question was just under-specified; tell me the physical set-up and I'll tell you the answer." I don't want this simply defused, because I think it's half right and I want them to see what it leaves standing. (2) The bijection thought: "every chord has exactly one midpoint, so the midpoint method is the natural one" — quietly assuming that a one-to-one reparametrisation carries uniformity across. (3) Something about "every chord equally likely" coming apart from uniformity on a continuum. (4) "Surely rotation invariance settles it", and then the stronger invariance reply, that requiring the distribution to survive translation and rescaling is supposed to single out one of the three — I honestly can't remember which one it's meant to favour, and I'd rather they see why the constraint bites than memorise a number.

Two more things. First, the carrying example. I don't love the bare circle. I've been toying with building the whole session around two machines instead — one that spins a diameter and then slides a marker uniformly along it, versus two independent spinners that set the two endpoints — so that "same physical parts, assembled differently, different answer" is visible, and then a third machine that picks one of the first two at random, to show that the answer becomes a mixture depending on a distribution over procedures. Is that better or worse than the circle for this audience? What does the example have to have, and lack, to carry the point? Where exactly should the metamachine appear, if at all? Relatedly, my real ambition is that they leave with the thought that specifying a mechanism only defers indifference to the initial conditions of that mechanism rather than eliminating it. Is that a good final beat for 75 minutes, or will it read as a bait-and-switch? Second, I need a check-for-understanding question for the last five minutes that separates someone who got the point from someone who has just learnt three numbers, and I need an explicit list of what I should openly refuse to go into at this level — I'm assuming proper measure theory, the maximum-entropy formalism, and anything about Bertrand's own text, but tell me if I should cut more, or honestly if I should cut less.
