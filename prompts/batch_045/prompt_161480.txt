I hold a fairly specific version of the Russellian account of definite descriptions, and I want to know how much it actually risks.

The version I hold: a definite description is not a referring expression but a quantificational device. An utterance of a sentence containing the F asserts that there is exactly one F in the contextually supplied domain and that it satisfies the predicate; the descriptive condition may be enriched, and the domain of quantification restricted, by whatever the context makes salient, which is how incomplete descriptions get their completers. The classic puzzles are handled by scope: failure of existential import under negation and modal operators, failures of substitutivity, and the informativeness of true identities are all consequences of where the description takes scope relative to other operators, not signs that descriptions have a second kind of meaning. Referential uses, in Donnellan's sense, are cases of speaker's reference rather than semantic reference, and Strawson-style cases where a description lacks a satisfier are cases where the presuppositions of an assertion fail, not cases where a false proposition has been expressed.

I know the standard objections and do not want them rehearsed at length. What I want is a map of the view's exposure: which observations, arguments, intuitions, and results would count against it, and how decisively. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender of the view would concede it as counting against them or would dismiss it as a pragmatic or metasemantic side issue.

Some cases I want addressed specifically, because I think they are harder than the ones usually discussed:

1. Covarying and functional readings without a bound variable inside the description. Consider a sentence of the form every singer who auditioned was accompanied by the pianist, uttered in a setting where the singers were accompanied by different pianists and no single person accompanied all of them. Speakers can hear this as true, and nothing inside the description borrows a variable from the quantifier to make uniqueness relative to each singer. A Russellian seems forced to posit a hidden relational argument or a Skolem function. Does that count against the view, or is it an extension it can absorb, and does the extension generalize in a way that changes the theory's character?

2. Descriptions whose completing property is not recoverable from context: the best candidate was rejected, the right thing to do was obvious, the usual precautions were taken. Say what is supposed to supply the missing condition here, and at what point the claim that the context supplies it becomes the claim that there is no semantic uniqueness condition at all.

3. A hypothetical cross-linguistic finding. Suppose fieldwork on a language with a definite determiner showed that the determiner behaves exactly as ours does in anaphora, in modal contexts, and in so-called referential uses, but is demonstrably compatible with multiple satisfiers: speakers unhesitatingly accept, as straightforwardly true, direct translations of descriptions whose uniqueness condition fails. Would that refute the account, show that uniqueness is merely one parametrized component of definiteness, or be dismissed as evidence about a different construction?

4. A hypothetical processing result. Suppose comprehension studies showed that hearers do not check uniqueness obligatorily, treating a description as satisfied whenever a contextually salient satisfier exists among several. How much should a theory of this kind care about that kind of evidence, and would a defender be right to treat it as irrelevant to semantics proper?

I also want an honest assessment of insulation. The reply to nearly every apparent counterexample is that the domain was restricted, the condition enriched, or a hidden variable introduced. Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? If so, say whether that is a defect in a theory of compositional content or merely an acceptable division of labour with pragmatics, and say plainly if you think the view is well insulated and that this is or is not a problem for it.

Finally, tell me what the view predicts that its rivals do not — that no definite description is ever semantically referential, that every description admits scope readings, that a description without a satisfier yields falsity rather than a truth-value gap — and which of those predictions are genuinely risky rather than guaranteed by how the framework is set up. What I would personally find most persuasive is a truth-conditional difference between referential and attributive uses that survives every scope and enrichment manoeuvre, and secondarily the cross-linguistic kind of evidence rather than intuition about isolated sentences. Please argue from the cases; I am not asking for citations.
