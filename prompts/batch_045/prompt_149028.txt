I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy or decision-theory training, and I need help with a concrete call my team has to make by Thursday.

We're scheduled to cut our payments service over to a new datastore on the 30th. I have the option of a one-week shadow test: replay a copy of live traffic against the new store alongside the old one and compare error rates and tail latency. The costs are real — a week of delay, two engineers' time, and the replay rig putting extra load on production. A senior engineer argues that the delay is the only genuine cost, on the grounds that you can always ignore what you learn, so extra information is never worth less than nothing. He says he has seen this stated as a theorem, something he called Good's theorem, which I haven't been able to track down a source for, and that it settles the question: the information itself is pure upside, and all we are really pricing is the week.

Three things make me doubt that it applies here:

- We can't actually ignore the result. Our change-management process auto-aborts any cutover whose pre-flight metric crosses a fixed threshold. I think the shadow metric is noisy enough that an ugly number is reasonably likely even if the new store is healthy, and an abort would push us past a contractual date. So the measurement would hand the decision to a rule rather than to us.
- The measurement perturbs the thing measured. The replay rig shares a connection pool and a network path with production, so the tail-latency figures we would get describe a system that won't exist at cutover. (The week's delay also means we'd be sampling a traffic mix that may not be the one we ship into.)
- The information changes people rather than just beliefs. The engineer who would run the cutover is the kind who, on a clean shadow report, will quietly drop the rehearsed rollback plan to save time, and who, on a mediocre one, will lose confidence and stall for another month. Both of those look worse to me than deciding now on what we already know.

Is my colleague's principle correct, and does it cover this case? Please: identify the structure of the argument you're applying instead of giving me generic team-management advice; tell me which of my three worries actually defeats the principle and which is merely a cost to be weighed against the delay; gloss any term of art in a single clause; and give me a recommendation plus the one or two things that would most change it.

On how committed to be: I want you to separate what is settled — the result itself and the assumptions it rests on — from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this area: whether real agents or organizations can satisfy the assumption that you can simply ignore what you learn, whether information can rationally be refused for reasons other than its cash price, and whether the right response is a pre-commitment, a delegation, or something else. Mark clearly where you are extrapolating beyond views anyone has actually defended, and don't present a contested position as the consensus. Equally, don't hedge to the point of uselessness: I have a decision to make this week.

End with a single final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: SHADOW-RUN | NO-SHADOW-RUN | SHADOW-RUN-ONLY-IF-PRECOMMITTED

with one of the three labels selected, optionally followed by a parenthetical of at most ten words.
