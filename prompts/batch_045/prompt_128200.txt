Hey — I need something for the little reading group I run with two friends (one econ PhD, one ML person), and also, honestly, for an argument I keep losing.

Last month I signed up for one of those commitment apps: you put up $200 and it goes to a charity you actively dislike if you don't log four gym sessions a week. My friend says this is just an admission that future-me will be irrational, and that a rational agent has no use for such things — you do what you have most reason to do at each moment and that's it. I said, what about Parfit's hitchhiker: the driver who takes you to town only if he predicts you'll pay him once you're there, at which point paying costs you and does nothing. Then we got completely stuck, because we couldn't agree on what the object of evaluation even is — the act of paying, the disposition to pay, or the earlier choice to acquire the disposition. And my ML friend kept insisting this has the same shape as an agent that removes its own off-switch or hides its reward function from itself, which I think is maybe right, but he thinks that settles it and I definately don't.

So before we read anything else I want a spec. Write me the desiderata that any adequate account of this territory — the rationality of being rationally irrational, or of self-binding, call it what you like — has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each. Here are the cases I want it to say something about; don't feel bound by them and tell me if any is a red herring: (1) the hitchhiker in its pure prudential form, with no gratitude or owing sneaked in — the account shouldn't be able to cheat by importing moral reasons; (2) the inverse case — the person known to pay ransoms gets kidnapped, so your dispositions partly determine which situations you end up in at all, and the account has to handle that selection effect and not just within-situation payoffs; (3) an imperfect predictor (the driver is right 70% of the time) plus a real cost to maintaining the disposition, since being the paying sort apparently requires being the sort who feels guilty; (4) the fact that you usually can't choose a disposition directly, only acts that produce it — a public vow, money in escrow, deleting the app, or deliberately not reading the safety statistics about the route — so does the rationality of the means transmit, and what if the only available means is making yourself believe something false?; (5) the trait you never chose, because you were raised to be a promise-keeper, where it's unclear whether rationality can even evaluate something you didn't acquire and whether the answer should differ from the acquired case; (6) self-effacing dispositions, where the only disposition that would get the driver to stop is one you couldn't retain while fully knowing you'd adopted it as a device — what is the recommendation supposed to be there?; (7) a scope constraint, so the account doesn't generalize into making every temptation a case for self-binding, or let you count the value of having the disposition twice, once when you adopt it and again when you act on it; and (8) the level question — acts, dispositions, belief-forming policies, plans over time — whether one account has to deliver a single verdict across all of them, whether the verdicts are allowed to come apart, and if they do, which level is the one you're supposed to actually follow.
