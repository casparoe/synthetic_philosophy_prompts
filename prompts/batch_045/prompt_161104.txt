I am a junior at my high school and I run our philosophy club, which meets on Thursday afternoons and spends most of its time arguing about questions none of us are equipped to settle. The question I have been stuck on for months is whether hurting characters inside video games can be morally wrong, and rather than read about it I have been collecting verdicts from my friend Julian, a senior who is applying to study computer science and philosophy and who is the most internally consistent person I know. His verdicts so far: crushing hundreds of identical enemies in a platformer is not wrong in any way, and he says the number makes no difference; killing a hand-written quest-giver in an RPG who has a name, a backstory, and a scripted widow who mourns him is not wrong, though he adds that he would not advertise doing it, because enjoying it tells you something unflattering about the person who enjoys it; starving a virtual pet of the old handheld kind that beeps when neglected is mildly wrong; burning the shelter of a creature in a game where the creatures evolve and learn by trial and error, so that afterward they flinch and avoid the object that hurt them, he called much closer to wrong than the RPG case, but he refused to commit either way; bullying a stranger's avatar in a social VR world is clearly wrong, but he insists that is entirely because of the person behind the avatar and not the avatar; and being cruel to a vividly described character in a text-only adventure whose behavior never changes in response to anything is not wrong at all.

The case I want to put to him next is one I designed myself, and I have already described it to him in detail without asking for his verdict. It is a single-player sandbox game played offline, in which each villager is a small reinforcement-learning agent rather than a scripted character: nothing the villager says or does was written by a designer, it carries a persistent memory of the last several hundred interactions with the player, and its learned policy was shaped by a reward function that penalizes being struck and losing its house, so it comes to anticipate the player, hide, and behave in ways that look like distress when its house is destroyed. The wrinkle I care about is that its distress behaviour was trained partly on recordings of how real human beings acted when threatened with the loss of their homes, so the flinching is not invented by a writer but patterned on actual human behaviour. I also insisted on three stipulations, because I want to isolate the question: no one ever watches the play session or sees the save file, no real person is affected in any way by what happens in the game, and the player's conduct toward actual people afterward is completely unchanged. Would Julian say that systematically tormenting such a villager is morally wrong, and how wrong relative to the scripted quest-giver and the handheld pet?

What I would like from you is not a verdict of your own so much as a reconstruction. Please identify the two or three rules that fit all of Julian's earlier verdicts about equally well, state exactly what each of them predicts about my new case and why, give me your best guess at what he will say with the reasons for preferring that guess, and then name one further case I could put to him that would separate the rules that survive this round — ideally a case where those rules disagree rather than one where they merely differ in degree. Two half-formed ideas of my own, which you should feel free to discard: I have been playing with a distinction between an entity's having a stake in what happens to it and its merely depicting or representing a stake, and with the thought that distress borrowed from real human behaviour might matter more, or possibly less, than distress authored by a designer who made it up. If either of those does real work in reconstructing his view, show me where; if they are confused, say which part is confused.
