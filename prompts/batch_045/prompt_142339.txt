I am writing a handout for an advanced seminar on Frege's puzzle about identity statements. The handout has to get past the point where everyone can recite the standard package — co-referential names, difference of sense, sense as a mode of presentation, failure of substitution in attitude contexts — and instead compare two readings of what a sense would have to *be* for that package to do any work. Please write the comparison as the core of the handout.

Set up the issue with the following test. Call a pair of co-referential expressions *trivialized for a subject S at a time t* when both of the following hold for S at t: the statement "a is b" carries no more information than "a is a"; and substituting "a" for "b" inside ascriptions of S's own attitudes never turns a true ascription into a false one. Ordinary speakers and the names "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" fail this test. But consider a working astronomer who internalized the identity years ago, computes from a single ephemeris for Venus, would call any report about what she believes of Hesperus correct exactly when the corresponding report about Phosphorus is correct, and for whom the identity statement is now as flat as "Hesperus is Hesperus." She passes the test. Nothing about the reference of either name changed between the novice and the astronomer.

Compare these two readings of "sense" against that contrast.

The first reading takes sense to be objective: the sense of an expression is the publicly available mode of presentation of its reference, graspable by any number of speakers, independent of what any one of them happens to know, believe, or have done with it. Frege's insistence that a sense is not a private mental item belongs to this reading.

The second reading takes sense to be cognitive: a sense is whatever fixes, for a given subject at a given time, which co-referential expressions are interchangeable in that subject's thought and talk. On this reading two expressions share a sense for S exactly when S's information-handling treats them as one, and a sense can fuse or split as S learns or forgets.

Please do the following in your answer.

Explain what, on each reading, changed when the novice became the astronomer, and what each reading must say stayed the same. If the answer on one reading is "nothing about sense changed; only something psychological did," say whether that reading can still ground the datum the notion of sense was introduced to explain — the difference in cognitive value between "a is a" and "a is b" — or whether that datum has been handed over to something the theory does not model.

Apply both readings to a further case: a single name that a subject has acquired twice from unrelated sources without realizing it is the same name, so that the subject is disposed to affirm and to deny what is in effect one and the same thing. Say which reading handles this case more naturally and why the other one struggles with it, since on that reading there is only one name and hence, apparently, only one sense.

Then say which of the two readings is doing the real work in the Fregean solution, and whether it can be held together with the other commitments that give the solution its point: that senses are what thoughts are composed of, that a thought can be shared by different speakers and grasped by one speaker at different times, and that senses are not private mental items. If the two readings pull apart here, say which commitment a defender should give up, and what is lost by giving it up.

Finally, give a verdict: state which reading you would defend, give the strongest objection to it, and say in one sentence whether Frege's puzzle is one puzzle or two.

Constraints for the answer: argue the point directly, in about seven hundred words, without citing, quoting, or recommending any literature — I want the reasoning, not references. Assume the reader knows the Hesperus/Phosphorus setup but nothing beyond it, so define any further term you need. Keep the register of a seminar handout: compressed, explicit about where a step is contested, and honest about which moves are stipulations rather than arguments.
