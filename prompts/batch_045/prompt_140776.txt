I'm teaching a third-year course on time, chance and prediction and I'm writing the final. I need one question on chaos and determinism, and I'm sick of the answer everyone gives: sensitive dependence, therefore unpredictable, but unpredictable isn't indeterministic, butterfly effect, Lorenz, done. I want something that seperates people who actually get the structure of the issue from people whove memorised the slogans. Write me the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a 20-mark scheme built around five must-hit moves, each with a one-line note on how people blow it.

Things I want the question to force:

- Students have to hold determinism apart from predictability. Determinism is a claim about the state-transition map — any two worlds that agree at one time agree at all times — while predictability is epistemic. A top answer should say what you'd have to build into "determinism" for chaos to even look like a threat to it, and why that added ingredient is doing all the work.
- There's a gadget I've been using in lectures that I want them to apply. Call her the Archivist: she has unlimited computing power and exact knowledge of the laws, but the state is only ever given to her to finite precision — she knows the true initial state lies somewhere in a ball of radius ε and nothing more. Her job is to announce a state at horizon T that is within δ of the actual one for every initial condition in that ball. They should be able to say why she fails for large T in a system with a positive Lyapunov exponent (nearby states separate exponentially), and — this is the bit people get wrong — why her failure is not a failure of determinism, since determinism tells you what the image of the whole ball is, not that the image is small.
- There's a trap I want it to spring. A bright student will reach for the shadowing lemma — informally, that every approximate trajectory is closely followed by some exact one — and say that rescues her. It doesn't. The question should make them explain why, and the scheme should reward anyone who spots that shadowing is existential (some true trajectory stays close) while the Archivist needs a claim about the actual trajectory.
- Final kicker: suppose the dynamics really are deterministic but the actual initial condition is a real number no algorithm can compute. Ask whether determinism fails, or whether something weaker-but-still-interesting does. Make them formulate a stronger thesis of their own — my name for it in lectures is "simulable determinism": the laws plus a finite specification of the state fix a computable trajectory — and take a position on whether physics should care about it. Anyone who notices that the computable reals are countable, so the non-computable ones are the measure-one case rather than some freak exception, should get a bonus mark; the question shouldn't hand them that, but it shouldn't punish them for going there either.

Constraints: it's one of four questions in a two-hour exam, so about 30 minutes of work, and it's an essay, not multiple choice. The question text has to be self-contained — define every term you use, don't assume they've seen the shadowing lemma, and dont ask for citations, reading or anything they'd need a library for.

Start with the question text itself, first line, no preamble and no "here's a question that tests whether students can...". Then the model answer, which should lead with its bottom line in the first sentence and then argue for it. Then the scheme.
