Two decision rules are on the table for a subject facing gambles over monetary outcomes. List the possible final wealth levels of a gamble A from worst to best as x₁ through xₙ, let A yield xᵢ with probability pᵢ, let u be the subject's utility function over final wealth, and let r be an increasing risk function with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. Expected utility ranks A by

EU(A) = Σᵢ pᵢ u(xᵢ).

Risk-weighted expected utility ranks it by

REU(A) = u(x₁) + Σᵢ₌₂ⁿ r(pᵢ + … + pₙ) · (u(xᵢ) − u(xᵢ₋₁)),

so that each successive improvement above the worst outcome is weighted by the risk-transformed probability of receiving at least that much. Setting r(t) = t recovers expected utility. Where r(t) < t throughout, the upper increments receive less weight than their objective probabilities, which shows up as caution over and above anything implied by the shape of u.

Now consider this experiment. A subject must accept two independent, calibrated fair coins, each paying £100 or nothing, and consumes only once both have been tossed; she makes no decision in between. Three arrangements are on offer. Clumped: both coins are tossed and both results revealed together at the end. Spread: the first coin is tossed and revealed, then the second is tossed and revealed. Sequestered: both coins are tossed at the outset, both results are sealed, and both are revealed at the end. All three induce the same distribution over final wealth — £0, £100 and £200 with probabilities ¼, ½ and ¼ — and an expected-utility agent can always ignore what she is told, so expected utility predicts indifference among all three. Risk-weighted utility does not, provided one extends it to staged prospects in the natural recursive way: reapply the rule at the second stage, treat the resulting continuation value as the utility of that stage's outcome, then reapply the rule at the first stage. Writing Δ₁ for u(w + 100) − u(w) and Δ₂ for u(w + 200) − u(w + 100), the clumped and sequestered value is u(w) + r(¾)Δ₁ + r(¼)Δ₂, whereas the spread value is u(w) + r(½)(2 − r(½))Δ₁ + r(½)²Δ₂; the two coincide when r is the identity and need not coincide otherwise.

Suppose the experiment is run and yields the following findings, and assume for the purposes of the discussion that they are robust. Subjects strictly prefer Clumped to Spread and are indifferent between Clumped and Sequestered. They will pay up to roughly four per cent of the stake to have the intermediate result withheld, and the premium tracks the caution they display in static choices measured independently of it. When the design is explained to them and they are invited to reconsider, most still decline the intermediate result and volunteer that they would rather take the two risks in one go. And the entire effect vanishes when the two coins are perfectly correlated, so that the first toss already settles the second.

Write a dialogue of about 1,200 to 1,600 words between two philosophers of decision theory, Miriam and Douglas, who have spent years on this material and know each other well.

Miriam holds that the findings vindicate risk-weighting: r is a genuine, separately identifiable and rationally permissible attitude, and expected utility is not merely silent here but positively wrong, since it forbids any strict preference among the three arrangements. Douglas holds that the findings, however striking, do not vindicate risk-weighting: either they can be absorbed by expected utility once outcomes are individuated finely enough to include how the uncertainty was resolved, in which case the risk function is bookkeeping rather than a further attitude, or they document a psychological response to the phenomenology of suspense that no theory of instrumental rationality should accommodate. The live question between them is whether the second parameter carries any explanatory surplus or is merely a re-parametrisation.

Both should argue well and neither should be a straw man. Each should answer the other's actual objection rather than a nearby weaker one, and if either makes an inference the other can show to be invalid, the correction should be made and should stick. Douglas should be permitted to press the re-individuation move hard, including the challenge that the recursive extension is an extra axiom doing all the explanatory work. Miriam should be permitted to press the correlation result against both the re-individuation and the suspense readings, and to argue that r cannot be a belief-like distortion of probability, since nothing in the subject's evidence about the coins differs between Clumped, Spread and Sequestered. Let them also work out what each rule implies about a subject who, once a decision is inserted between the two tosses, pays to bind her later self. Have them do the relevant algebra explicitly wherever it carries argumentative weight. Close the dialogue with their agreement about which single further experiment would most sharply separate the two readings of the data, together with a clear statement of what they still disagree about and why.

Do not cite, name or allude to any published author, paper or book, and make no appeal to what the literature is thought to say: argue everything from the definitions above, the experiment as described, and first-order reasoning. Write in British English.
