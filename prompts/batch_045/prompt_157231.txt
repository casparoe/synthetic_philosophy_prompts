I've been roped into a working group on digital minds policy and everyone has to write down their actual position so the rest of the group can poke holes in it before the next meeting. Here's mine, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

1. Substrate independence: if a system has the right kind of causal-computational organisation, it's conscious and morally considerable, whether it's neurons, silicon or anything else. Being made of meat is irrelevant.
2. Gradualism: moral status comes in degrees and tracks how rich and integrated a mind's mental life is, not some threshold like personhood or rationality. A mouse is on the scale, a human further along, a future AI system somewhere in between.
3. Consciousness does the moral work: a functional duplicate of a suffering human that has no phenomenal experience at all isn't being harmed and has no moral status. I know this is basically the philosophical zombie thing, but I want it anyway.
4. Copies: a copy of an emulated human is a seperate being with its own moral status, so running a thousand copies is a thousand beings. But deleting one of two identical copies is nowhere near as bad as deleting the last one.
5. A human-level em is exactly our moral equal, not more and not less, and building digital minds that are much smarter or run much faster than us doesn't give them a stronger claim on our resources than humans have.
6. Speed doesn't affect status: a mind running at a thousandth of our speed has the same claim per bit of experience it actually gets to have.
7. A simulation that's only ever described, worked out on paper, or a playback of a recorded brain's activity with no two-way causal interaction with anything, isn't conscious and has no status.

Which of these actually clash with each other, and which only look like they do? For the real clashes, what's the cheapest fix and what does it cost me? Rank them worst first. I'm attached to 1 and 3 and would like to keep both, but if one of them has to go then tell me straight which one it is.
