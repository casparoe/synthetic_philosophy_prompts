A friend of mine is in a clinical psychology PhD program, and she had two people from her cohort over for dinner on Saturday — Marta, who's on the more clinical side and does some diagnostic interviewing, and Devin, who did his MA in philosophy before switching tracks and now argues about literally everything — and the two of them spent the whole evening stuck on a case that's (allegedly, I'm repeating what I heard) in front of the working group for the next edition of the diagnostic manual. The proposed category is mostly seen in teenagers and young adults: exhaustion, depersonalization-ish episodes, catastrophic interpretations of ordinary bodily signals. The strange part is that nearly none of these patients arrive naive. They come in already using the vocabulary, they describe their symptoms in roughly the same order as the draft criteria, and a decent share of them get measurably better once they're given a name and a treatment pathway. Let's call it Autonomic Depletion Syndrome, ADS, since I'd rather not use their real label.

The argument went roughly like this. (1) Devin: this is Hacking's looping effect manufacturing the appearance of validity rather than a valid category. Inter-rater reliability is high because the patients have read the criteria; the symptom cluster hangs together because the description taught them how to have it; the treatment response is real but it's the response you'd get from any coherent explanatory story. A category whose evidence base is generated by the act of classifying shouldn't be in the manual. (2) Marta: if looping disqualified a category it would disqualify most of the manual — depression, PTSD and anorexia all changed shape as their concepts spread through the culture, and nobody wants to hand those back. Hacking's point about interactive versus indifferent kinds isn't a verdict on which kinds are real, it's about which ones stay still. So looping isn't an objection by itself. (3) Devin: fine, but there's a difference between a kind that moves once you classify it and a kind with no pre-classification existence at all — he called these recruited versus bootstrapped. The test should be whether there's a pre-label substrate: were kids showing up with this distress before the words existed? If not, we're not describing, we're authoring. (4) Marta: that test can't be run. Anything recorded before the label is recorded in the vocabulary of whatever categories existed at the time, so you'll never find the substrate even when it's there — no pre-history is exactly what you'd expect from a category that captured something previously invisible. And the metaphysics of origins has no bearing on who she's supposed to treat: the person in front of her is suffering now, and "you wouldn't be suffering this particular way if the label hadn't circulated" isn't something a clinician can say to a patient. (5) Devin: but "the suffering is real once the loop is running" proves too much — moral panics and demonic-possession frameworks produced genuine suffering too, and that didn't make them diseases; there has to be some gatekeeping beyond people currently suffering under this description. Also she's quietly switched standards, from is it true to is it handy, which is fine for a treatment protocol but the manual claims to say what people have, and patients are told it. (6) Marta: for a clinical manual "true" doesn't have more content than predictive and therapeutic utility anyway, and here's what Devin's missing: adding the label doesn't just respond to the loop, it steers it. It gives clinicians something to do with a kid who's already halfway into the identity, and it makes a recovery story available — you have a condition, it's treatable, it's not your character. Refusing the category doesn't stop the looping, it just leaves the loop to the internet, where the version of ADS in circulation has no clinician attached to it and no exit. (7) Devin: if she concedes the label steers the loop, she's conceded that the case for ADS is a bet about social effects, which is something the working group has neither expertise nor evidence about; and once ADS is official the criteria leak, so the next cohort presents the official symptoms even faster, and the evidence base gets stronger for reasons that have nothing to do with discovery. He called it the reliability trap: the more a category loops, the better its numbers look, so the numbers can never count against it. (8) Marta: that's just blanket skepticism about diagnostic feedback, and if it were right the response wouldn't be to freeze the manual, it'd be to admit that interactive kinds can only be judged prospectively — introduce, watch, revise — instead of pretending you can validate them in advance. Then she muttered something about a separate category for people whose distress is basically organized around their diagnostic identity, Devin said that's just looping all the way down, and we ran out of wine.

Please be committed rather than balanced: give me your verdict on each point with reasons, and don't just lay out the considerations and leave me to pick. Where you're genuinely unsure, say so in a sentence and then give your best guess anyway.
