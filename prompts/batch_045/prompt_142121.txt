Two people in my policy-and-philosophy reading group spent most of an hour going at each other last week over a hypothetical bill, and since I'm chairing the next session I'd quite like to know who actually won rather than just nodding along.

Setup: a legislature is openly considering banning, explicitly on grounds of public morality and not harm, the commercial production of photorealistic synthetic sexual material featuring only fictional adults — no real person depicted, consumed alone, so no victim, and if nobody ever finds out, no offence either. Call the pro-ban side Mara and the anti-ban side Theo.

(1) Mara: the "no victim, no crime" move can't be the whole story, because criminal law already punishes plenty of things with no identifiable victim — cruelty to animals, mistreatment of corpses, consensual adult incest, some environmental offences. So the real question isn't whether victimless wrongdoing can be criminalised but which victimless wrongs count, and a society is entitled to protect the shared moral environment it depends on.
(2) Theo: fine, but Hart doesn't need a pure harm principle, he needs a distinction between rules that hold a society's cooperative framework together and rules that just register majority disgust. Concretely — a coordination audit: criminalise only where my compliance sensibly depends on your compliance, like driving on one side, keeping contracts, not perjuring yourself. Self-regarding sexual conduct flunks that audit; animal cruelty and pollution can be recast as harm to sentient creatures or to identifiable present people.
(3) Mara: the audit sounds principled but it's useless as a stopping rule, because moral norms are held up by mutual expectations too. Nobody thinks their private indulgence is being observed, yet the norm decays once enough people quietly stop treating it as binding, and you only find out afterwards — it's like antibiotic resistance, the evidence arrives once the thing is already gone. Demanding proof of decay before acting is demanding the one kind of proof you can only get by waiting till it's too late, so the right posture is precaution, not evidence of harm.
(4) Theo: precautionary reasoning here is perfectly symmetric and therefore empty. Every liberal reform we now take as progress — decriminalising homosexuality, loosening divorce law — was opposed with exactly this "the moral fabric will fray" claim, and it was false then. You owe us a way to tell real fraying from moral panic, and "we'll know retrospectively" isn't one.
(5) Mara: okay, then take irreversibility as the test instead of harm or evidence — enforce moral norms whose loss can't be undone. You can re-legalise a drug; you can't restore a norm once enough people have abandoned it. And underneath all this, you're assuming law is purely instrumental. It isn't: law also expresses what a society stands for, and you already accept expressive criminalisation for hate crime, so your objection isn't to moralism as such, it's to moralism you happen to disagree with.
(6) Theo: irreversibility is cheap to allege and moral progress is often irreversible too, so the test cuts both ways and picks out nothing. On expressivism, I'll grant the law expresses things — but an expressive argument has to be won on the content of what's expressed, and a law can express "we protect the vulnerable" without expressing "we despise you," so expressivism doesn't get you to Devlin.

Can you adjudicate this move by move — which of the six land, which are weak or question-begging, and where each of them is definately right about something the other sidesteps — and then say who's more right overall, plus what both of them are missing?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question. British English please.
