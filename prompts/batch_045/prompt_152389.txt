I help run a small, very unofficial reading group that meets above a pub in Leeds every other Monday — no one's an academic, it's me, a retired GP, two software people and a librarian — and for the last few months we've been crawling through Wittgenstein's last notebooks, the ones published after he died, where he's arguing with Moore about holding up your hands and about what you're doing when you say you know something. We argue a lot, and the argument we've been having for weeks is basically: which of the things we feel certain about are the load-bearing ones (the hinges, the ones that have to stay put for anything else to be checked), which are just extremely well-confirmed ordinary facts, and which are true only because of how the words work? People keep bringing examples like: I have two hands; the earth has been around for a very long time; nobody has travelled into the future and come back; that person over there is in pain; my memory is mostly reliable; water is H2O; two and two make four; I am not a brain in a vat being fed all of this; the world didn't start five minutes ago with my memories already in place. And we genuinely cannot agree whether any two of those are the same kind of thing, or why.

What I'd like is a decision tree we could actually work through at a meeting, eight branch questions at most, where each branch is a question someone with no philosophy training could answer without knowing where it leads — ideally questions about what you'd do rather than what you'd call it: if somebody denied this, would you go and look for evidence, would you try to explain what the words mean, or would you conclude that arguing with them is pointless in some deeper way? Nothing that just asks 'is this a hinge?' or 'is this empirical?'. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of famous views: set out the distinctions and the combinations of answers they allow first, then put the named positions on the leaves afterwards, six to nine leaves, named where a name exists and 'unnamed' where the cell is empty with one sentence on why nobody sits there (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Two of the distinctions matter more to us than the rest, so please make them load-bearing: whether a hinge can be local to some practice or institution rather than something no human life could do without, and whether the very same sentence can be a hinge for me and an ordinary checkable claim for somebody else at the same time — say for a neuroscientist, or for someone raised in a very different community. There's a third thing I'd like the tree to have a place for if it can: that image of a riverbed where the bed itself slowly shifts, so a proposition that was rock-solid framework for my grandmother's generation is now a live empirical question — tell us where the tree puts that, or if it breaks the tree, say so plainly. Then run all nine of our examples through it and tell us which leaf each one lands on, flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really seperate rather than pretending it can, and mark the branches where an answer is a judgement call rather than something the distinctions force. No reading lists or citations, please — we're not going to look any of it up, and half the fun is arguing it out ourselves.
