Give one example — chosen and defended, not selected from a menu — designed to show that the problem of logical omniscience is not a single problem. In it, an agent believes each premise of a valid argument; the argument is short and the inference is one she could carry out without difficulty; and no limitation of computation, memory, or attention is doing any work. Still, you will argue, her failure to believe the conclusion is no fault of hers.

Build the case around a conclusion whose content is partly about the agent's own epistemic situation, so that what blocks her is not an inference left unperformed but the fact that performing it would undo the very situation one of the premises reports. Then explain why the standard relaxations — bounding the depth or length of proof search, restricting closure under known implication to a tractable fragment, replacing possible-worlds models with impossible-worlds or otherwise finer-grained representations, distinguishing implicit from explicit belief — would each either miss this case or get it right only by accident, and say what the case shows about how the problem should be reconceived. Deliver one example, one diagnosis, and one reformulation, and mark the weakest point in your own argument.
