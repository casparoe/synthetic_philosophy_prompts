I work on distributed systems, and that is the vocabulary I think in most fluently: replicated state machines, quorum-backed commits under a single leader epoch, leases and failure detectors, at-least-once delivery with idempotent handlers, two-phase commit, safety versus liveness, Byzantine versus crash faults, and the standard result that no protocol can manufacture common knowledge among nodes on lossy asynchronous links (the two-generals problem), so real systems settle for weaker things — quorums, leases, timeouts, and best-effort views that are known to be possibly stale.

I have been reading about shared intention and joint action, and I want that debate reconstructed in my terms. The three positions I keep meeting are: the individualist analysis, on which two of us share an intention just in case our individual intentions interlock (each intends that *we* act, each intends to do it by way of the other's sub-plans), we are mutually responsive in execution, we are each committed to supporting the other, and all of this is common knowledge between us; the irreducibility view, on which a we-intention is a primitive, biologically basic mode of intentionality that sits in each individual head and is not analysable into I-intentions plus beliefs about others, even when those are common knowledge; and the joint-commitment or plural-subject view, on which the relevant move is not a special kind of mental state at all but a normative one — a commitment entered into together, which generates obligations and gives each participant standing to demand or rebuke. There is also the background consensus that individual intentions plus common knowledge are not enough, since you and I can each intend to go to the same place, know all of that about each other, and still not be going together.

Please translate each of these into distributed-systems vocabulary as faithfully as you can, using as your running parallel the case of two people manoeuvring a sofa up a narrow stairwell and the case of two nodes coordinating a schema migration across shards under a lease. To force the translation to be precise, use a construct my team actually reasons with: a joint lease, meaning a lease held not by a node but by an ephemeral group principal, created by a quorum-anchored commit in one epoch, expiring unless renewed, whose violation triggers a compensating action rather than a crash. Tell me which of the three positions the joint lease implements, which it fails to reach, and why.

Then give me the residue report: what could not be expressed at all in my vocabulary, where you had to smuggle in something my framework does not contain (normativity, accountability, acting for the sake of the joint activity rather than for one's own reasons), and where the translation distorted rather than carried the claim. Finish with a verdict on two questions. First, is the residue evidence that my engineering framework is missing something real, or evidence that the philosophers are disagreeing less than they appear to — concretely, if I built one system on the individualist specification and one on the irreducibility specification, what observable difference would there be between their runs, and if the answer is none, what would have to be added to either system to make the difference observable? Second, what does my framework predict about joint action that the philosophical literature arguably under-discusses: the role of shared clocks and shared perceptual environments in making common knowledge attainable at all, the safety/liveness distinction applied to joint action, participants who accept a commitment and then defect, and whether any of these accounts still describe anything recognisable when the number of participants grows from two to a few hundred or a few million. Push the translation to the point where it breaks rather than resting on the observation that people are not nodes.
