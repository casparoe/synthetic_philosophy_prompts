Ok so I've been half convinced by the Solomonoff/Kolmogorov story that Occam's razor is just Bayesian inference done with a universal prior — shortest program dominates, the invariance theorem says your choice of reference machine costs you only an additive constant, and that constant washes out asymptotically, so simplicity looks like objectivity in disguise rather than a taste. I want to know what would actually count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I especially want the bits a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against them, not just what critics say.

Here's the thing I keep chewing on. The invariance theorem only says the constant is finite; nothing stops it from being astronomical. So picture a reference machine that's universal but "grue-coded": its primitive instructions include something like grue(x), so that "emeralds are green until 2050 and blue after" gets a much shorter encoding than plain "emeralds are green". On any dataset we will ever actually have, the razor then cuts whichever way my choice of machine dictates, and the objectivist story looks like a preference for one encoding smuggled in and then amortised away by a limit theorem that never arrives. Is that a real defeater, or just the familiar short-run sensitivity complaint that everyone already knows about and discounts?

Also: where does incomputability sit on that scale — given that real agents have to use computable approximations like speed priors, and thereby seem to give up the very optimality bound that was supposed to justify the razor in the first place? And please flag clearly what's settled math versus contested interpretation; I dont want to walk away thinking the philosophical reading is consensus when it isn't.
