I'm a senior in my school's philosophy club, and last week we read the dialogue from Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* in which a colony of ants — the characters call her Aunt Hillary — is spoken of as a single individual who notices things, wants things, and can be told about things. Two members of the club, Mira and Devin, ended up in a long argument about whether that way of talking is anything more than a literary device. They went several rounds, neither of them would concede, and I honestly cannot tell who was getting the better of it, which is why I'm writing to you. I've numbered their moves and reproduced them as faithfully as I can. Please don't point me toward further reading or ask me to go look things up — I'm interested in the arguments as they were actually made, not in sources.

1. MIRA: The colony remembers things that no ant inside it has ever learned. Its foraging routes, the architecture of the nest, which entrances are worth guarding, where the reliable aphid herds are — all of that persists for many years, while individual workers live a year or two. So when the colony sends foragers back to a food source that was first exploited four years ago, there is not a single ant in it who was alive when that route was established. The memory is in the colony and in nothing else. But memory is a mental property, so at least one mental property is genuinely realized at the colony level, and you can't make it go away by redescribing the ants.

2. DEVIN: A riverbed remembers the flood, and a worn path through the woods remembers where people used to walk, but nobody thinks a riverbed has a mind. What you've found is colony-level *information* that shapes behaviour, and that is a different thing from a memory. A memory is something the rememberer can retrieve, redeploy for purposes other than the one that laid it down, and be wrong about. A pheromone trail is a read-only structure that ants run along. Call it a scar, not a memory.

3. MIRA: Being wrong is exactly the case I want. If you paint an artificial trail with the colony's own recruitment pheromone leading to a feeder you have emptied overnight, the colony throws a large part of its workforce at an empty dish for a few hours and then stops sending them. It acted on a falsehood and then revised. That is a state with correctness conditions that gets updated on the basis of what it finds, which is about the least you can ask of a belief — and again, the ants that stop reinforcing the trail are mostly not the ants that started it, so the revising isn't happening inside any one ant.

4. DEVIN: None of that needs a colony-level state to do any work. Every step is accounted for by rules about individual ants: follow the strongest gradient; if you arrive and there is no food, do not lay trail on the way back. The colony-level story is a convenient compression of a great many ant-level events, and that is all it is. And notice that compression is not mentality. Temperature compresses the motion of trillions of molecules and is a perfectly real pattern, but a box of gas doesn't believe anything. You keep showing that our descriptions can be economical; I keep telling you that economy is a fact about *us*.

5. MIRA: Two replies. First, if describing the colony in intentional terms lets you predict behaviour you could not otherwise predict in practice — and it does; nobody forecasts a colony's response to a new barrier by simulating ants — then the pattern isn't merely in the eye of the beholder. Second, I know you're about to say that the colony has no central controller, and that objection cuts both ways, because a brain doesn't have one either. There is no foreman in the skull reading off the activity of the neurons; there are only layers of the same kind of mindless local processing you're objecting to in the colony.

6. DEVIN: I wasn't going to say "no central controller," I was going to say no unified subject — and the timescale is what shows it. In a brain, whatever becomes globally available does so in fractions of a second and can then interact with anything else; in a colony, information moves at the speed of an ant walking and is available only to whoever physically bumps into it. There is no moment at which the colony is in one total state and no place where the information is collected, so there is no one whose point of view it is. And when you ask where the colony stops, the answer is nowhere in particular: some ants form vast super-colonies spread over continents, with no sharp line between one colony and the next. A colony isn't even a well-defined object to hand a mind to.

7. MIRA: On the boundary: it is a matter of degree, and so mentality is a matter of degree, and I'm happy with that. It arrives gradually as the parts get more integrated — which is, on the usual story, roughly what happened when our own brains appeared from something that began as a colony of cells. On the subject: what you're really asking is whether there is something it is like to be a colony, and that is a different question from whether the colony has beliefs. I can be wrong about what the colony believes without knowing anything about how it feels, and I don't see why the second question has to be settled before we're allowed to answer the first.
