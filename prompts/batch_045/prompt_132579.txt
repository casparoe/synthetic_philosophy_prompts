Hey. I run root cause analysis for the patient safety office at a mid-sized hospital — about forty incident reports a year, three days per report, written by me and one nurse investigator, and every one of them goes to a review committee, to our liability insurer, and, in a redacted form, to the patient or the family. So whatever I write has to survive a medical director signing off and a lawyer reading it, and it has to be something two non-philosophers can run in an afternoon.

Here's what I've been chewing on. I got interested in al-Ghazālī's occasionalism — roughly, the view he pushes in his attack on the philosophers, that there's no necessary connection between what we call a cause and what we call its effect: the fire doesn't burn the cotton, God creates the burning on the occasion of the contact, and the regularity we observe is a habit rather than something the fire does by its own nature. The part I keep coming back to is that he doesn't deny the regularity and he doesn't tell us to stop acting on it; he tells us the necessity isn't there.

We obviously can't put any of the theology in a report — our team is religiously mixed, the reports are public-ish documents, and "God willed it" is not a sentence that survives legal review. But I think the philosophy does survive the translation, and I think it's aimed straight at the three things we're required to write every time: a box labelled "cause," a box labelled "root cause," and a mandatory counterfactual sentence — "but for X, the harm would not have occurred." My worry is that all three smuggle in exactly the productive necessity that occasionalism denies, and that we're ranking conditions as if one of them generated the outcome when really we've just picked the one we'd like to fix. If every condition present at the moment is equally an occasion, I don't see how any of them is *the* cause — but I still have to put something in the box.

So: write me a procedure my team can actually run on an incident, that takes that worry seriously but still produces a usable report in three days, without any theology in it and without collapsing into "anything could happen so nothing follows." Specifically I want:

- the questions in order, and what counts as a good enough answer to each when we don't have the data
- what we may call a cause, what we have to rename as a condition or occasion, and the exact wording of the report when we can't name one at all
- what becomes of "root cause" and of the "but for" sentence — keep, rewrite, or drop, and what the replacement sentence is, because legal will not let us just leave them blank
- where the procedure stops and hands the question to a person because it's a values call and not a factual one
- what it costs us: where this procedure will misfire, what it makes us say that we'd rather not say, and what kind of incident it's simply the wrong tool for

Two guardrails I care about: the procedure can't license refusing to recommend an intervention (we have to act, and acting on a habit that has held is fine), but it also can't let us dress up a bet on the habit continuing as a deduction from a mechanism — especially when we're extrapolating to a ward or a workflow we've never seen the sequence in before.

Then run it on three cases, and I want at least one of them to come out in a way I will not like:

1. A patient was given the wrong strength of an anticoagulant because two brand names look almost identical in the ordering dropdown. No harm resulted, and we later found the same pair had been ordered together something like two hundred times before with no error at all.
2. A post-op patient died of sepsis. The team noticed the fever and charted it four hours late; the review says earlier antibiotics "probably" would have saved him, and I want to know what "probably" is allowed to mean here and what it is allowed to license.
3. Same as 2, except that night the ward was short three nurses and the bedside monitor alarm had been muted by a nurse who was covering two floors at once.

If your procedure's answer in case 3 is "list all of them and refuse to rank them," tell me plainly what I'm supposed to do with that when the committee wants a name and the family wants an apology that says why this happened — and whether there's an honest version of that sentence we can still write.
