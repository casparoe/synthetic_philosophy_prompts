I direct the evaluation and contracting team at a state workforce agency that purchases employment and reentry services from roughly forty nonprofit providers. Over the next two years we are moving from paying for services delivered to paying for measured results: an increasing share of each provider's funding will be tied to a small set of reported numbers. My constraints are these. I have three analysts and no in-house econometrician. Our legal and procurement office requires every payment rule to be written into the contract in language a provider can read and a judge could enforce. Contracts run three years. The providers are sophisticated repeat players who will reallocate staff, change intake practice, and revise how they record dates within a quarter of a rule taking effect. I can fund an in-depth audit of each provider about once a year and no more often. Each candidate indicator has to survive review by our deputy director in about a day of analyst time, and whatever I adopt must also be reported upward to a legislature that has already been told to expect certain numbers.

I know the folk version of Goodhart's law — that a measure tends to stop tracking what it was meant to track once people are rewarded for it — and I will not be helped by being told to use a dashboard of several indicators, to add random audits, or to be careful. What I need is a decision procedure my team can actually run on a candidate indicator before it goes into a contract, ending in one of four verdicts: pay on it as written; pay on it only with the following guardrails; monitor it but attach no money; or drop it. The procedure should be about a dozen ordered steps, and each step should say what kind of evidence counts as a pass, what counts as a fail, and where a fail sends you.

Specifically, please include:

A first step that forces the goal to be written separately from the measure, and that produces an explicit written statement of the part of the goal the measure structurally cannot see — not a caveat, a named artifact my analysts can draft in an afternoon from files we already hold.

A short sequence of named stress tests, in the order I should run them, each with a specified failure action. The four I have in mind are: a no-real-improvement test, which asks for the cheapest route by which a provider could hit the target while the participant is no better off, and estimates that route's cost to them against the payment; a who-goes-missing test, which asks which participants a provider would prefer never to enroll, and whether our data could detect their absence; a moving-the-line test, which asks where in the causal chain a provider can reset a clock, redefine a denominator, or reclassify an exit; and a what-gets-sacrificed test, which asks which unmeasured goods a provider would have to trade away in order to move the number, and what signal would tell me that trade had happened.

The points where the procedure stops with a no, and what a no means operationally — how a dropped indicator becomes a monitored-only line item, and how a capped weight is expressed in contract language.

The points where the procedure hands the decision to a person because the question is about values rather than facts, along with a one-page format for that sign-off, since I have to defend these calls after the fact.

The guardrails that can survive being written into a contract: smoothing payment so that marginal effort yields marginal money rather than a cliff; holding back at least one measure that is collected but never paid on; and a way of stating a target before the baseline year has closed. Tell me which of these procurement or legal is likely to strike down, and what to substitute.

A note on which parts of the procedure must stay out of the contract. If a step in my own anti-gaming machinery is one a provider could read and plan around, I need it flagged as such, and I need to know what I lose by keeping it secret.

A re-review trigger: what pattern in year-one data forces us to reopen an indicator, and how we tell a genuine improvement from adaptation once money is attached, given how rarely we can audit.

Then run the full procedure on three candidates, in order, showing where each one stops: the share of participants employed at ninety days after exit; median days from referral to first paycheck; and a documented completion of our soft-skills workshop. The first is the number the legislature already expects, and I suspect it is our most gameable measure — I want that case worked through to the answer I will not like, including what I should do instead of paying on it and what I say to the legislature when I do it.

Close with an honest section on what the procedure cannot do: the residual risk it leaves on the table, the kind of provider adaptation it will never catch, and the point at which I should stop trusting indicators at all and fall back on the judgment of the staff who visit these programs. Do not cite literature or recommend reading; I want the procedure and its applications, not sources.
