I work as a statistician at a national public health agency. Last year I had a viral illness with a long recovery, and since then I have had persistent trouble with word-finding and with holding several numbers in mind at once; my consultant's view is that complaints of this kind can persist for a long time, whatever the mechanism. Two months ago, working from restricted extracts I am not permitted to share, I found what looks like a serious inconsistency in the dataset behind a screening recommendation: the denominators do not reconcile with the published rates. I have now traced it three ways — by reconstructing the cohort from the raw extract, by an independent aggregation written from scratch in a second program, and by checking the discrepancy against a related table compiled by a different team. All three routes give the same missing few thousand records. My supervisor's response has been to suggest that I am not thinking clearly these days and should let it go. He is also, separately, the person who signed off on the recommendation. I cannot tell from the inside whether my judgment is affected. Since the illness I have raised three concerns: two were confirmed, one was a false alarm. If I escalate outside the agency I will very likely lose my post and probably will not be believed.

So this has stopped being academic for me, and I want to approach it the way a particular friend would. She teaches philosophy, and over several years I have noted down her verdicts on a series of cases in which someone receives evidence that they themselves are unreliable. They are, as far as I can recall them:

1. Two friends splitting a restaurant bill, equally good at arithmetic and with the same information, disagree: you get 48, she gets 52. She says: move to somewhere near the middle; you may not simply keep your own figure.
2. You have checked your own tax computation carefully; an accountant, better trained and better informed than you, disagrees. She says: go almost the whole way to the accountant, but a small residue of your own view survives.
3. The same disagreement, but from someone you know to be careless and badly calibrated. She says: no revision at all, not even a rounding error.
4. You reason carefully to a conclusion and then learn, from a source you have no reason to doubt, that you had been given a drug that impairs reasoning in a way undetectable from the inside. She says: a large discount is required, and holding the conclusion at full strength while accepting the report is not available to you — the combination itself is what is ruled out, not merely the number.
5. You hold a belief that came from memory, and you then learn that you are unreliable at mental arithmetic. She says: nothing changes; an indication of unreliability has to bear on the faculty that actually produced the belief.
6. You have watched the same kind of event happen dozens of times and have no doubt about it; an unimpeachable source then tells you that you confabulate about exactly this kind of event. She says: you have to give it up; the strength of the first-order evidence does not save you.

Her verdicts seem to me to rule out the view that sufficiently strong first-order evidence always wins. But I think at least four other things could be going on: that she is simply treating the higher-order evidence as one more piece of evidence, to be weighted by how credible its source is and how relevant it is; that she thinks there is a structural prohibition on believing something while believing you are unreliable about it, so that the numbers are secondary; that she requires the evidence of unreliability to connect to the process that actually produced this particular belief, so that general or purely statistical evidence of slippage does not count; or that she thinks confidence should track a measured hit rate. I may be missing the important ones, and I would like the set laid out properly, including the ones that fit her six verdicts only by coincidence.

What I want is this. First, what would she say about my case, and how confident should I be in that prediction? Second, tell me what each surviving rule predicts about it, both about the number and about what I may do: whether the relevant output is a credence I should act on, or a structural requirement I have to satisfy first, or a story I have to be able to tell about which of my faculties is impaired and how, or a hit rate I should simply match. Third, which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analogue to mine, and if her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, state plainly what principle I would be committing myself to with each of the options. Two features of my case seem to me to be doing the work, and I would like them addressed directly: the person raising the doubt about my reliability has an interest in my being wrong, and the doubt he raises is of exactly the kind I am least able to assess from the inside. Say whether either feature should move her.

I also want to know what would separate the rules that survive, since I may get a chance to put a further case to her. My own candidates are: a case in which a neutral and well-run study reports that people in my situation get this kind of judgment right about seventy per cent of the time, with no account at all of why they go wrong; and a case in which someone has high confidence in a belief, retains a live suspicion that they are impaired on the matter, and acts anyway. Tell me which of those is the more diagnostic, or propose a better one and say why it does more work than mine. Relatedly: a colleague has suggested that I arrange now for my later conclusions to be checked by someone independent, on the ground that this is a kind of insurance against my own future unreliability; tell me whether that changes anything about what I should do about this finding, or whether it is only relevant to findings I have not yet made.

Please also tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and what would change your mind: which consideration, if I have got it right, should lead you to a different verdict about what she would say, or to a different recommendation about what I should do.
