I write literary fiction — three published novels, and I teach one term a year on an MA in prose fiction, where I run a workshop on voice, point of view and unreliable narration. My most recent book is narrated in the first person by a man who is not exactly lying and not exactly telling the truth, and writing him forced on me a working distinction I have never seen named: between a character who is *inhabited* — rendered from inside, so that what he notices, omits and misremembers follows from the kind of mind he has — and a character who is merely *ventriloquised* — rendered as if from inside, by imitating the surface habits of a voice. I am not confident the distinction is real, but I can feel it when a draft goes wrong.

I have agreed, perhaps rashly, to sit on a panel next month with a philosopher of mind and a machine-learning researcher, on the question of what a large language model actually is: a tool, an agent, or something else — the view I keep encountering is that it is not a person-like thing at all but a kind of simulator, which takes a prompt as an invitation and produces a stretch of text as whoever or whatever the prompt seems to be asking for. The labels strike me as doing less work than their users think, and I would like to see whether my own vocabulary does better. So: translate the candidate positions — tool, agent, simulator, and anything else you think belongs on the list — into the vocabulary I actually work in. Give me the agent claim in terms of character, agency and intention; the tool claim in terms of instrument, craft and the intentions that live in the maker rather than the made; the simulator claim in terms of mimesis, impersonation, free indirect style, dramatic irony, and the gap between author, narrator and character. Be strict about where the fit is exact and where you had to bend something: if a position only translates once you quietly assume that a voice must have a single unified psychology behind it, or that narration must have someone who means it, say so, because that assumption is the very thing in dispute.

Then I want the residue report, and it is the part I care most about. Which claims in the dispute have no expression at all in craft terms — perhaps because my vocabulary has no word for the difference between a process that models a speaker and a speaker, or because it cannot distinguish competence from uptake? Which parts translate only by distorting something — for instance, does 'the model is not an agent' come out as 'the narrator is not a character', and is that the same thought or merely a comforting echo of it? And where the residue lands, tell me honestly whether it indicts my vocabulary or the debate: I would rather hear that the disagreement between the agent theorist and the simulator theorist is thinner than it sounds than be told my craft terms are adequate to everything. Two further things. First, apply the translation to a concrete pair: the same system asked to continue a paragraph in the voice of my unreliable narrator, and then asked to be a helpful assistant. In my terms, is that one act or two, and does the answer tell me anything about whether the 'character' in the first case is inhabited or ventriloquised? Second, tell me what I could type at a keyboard — something I can do from the desk I am sitting at, with no library database and no references to chase, and please give me none — that would discriminate between the surviving positions. About nine hundred to eleven hundred words, and I would be grateful if you wrote it as a translator's report rather than a survey of what people have said.
