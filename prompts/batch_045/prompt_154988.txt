I direct data engineering for a regional health system, and my team owns the master patient index. Several hundred times a week an analyst faces the same decision: two records arrive in the merge queue, and someone has to rule that they denote the same patient or two different patients. The stakes are real and lopsided. A false merge can attach the wrong allergy list or the wrong medication order to a patient; a false split buries half a history and, since we joined the state health information exchange last year, can silently break a record link across hospitals. The analysts are not philosophers, they have roughly ten minutes per queue item, their decisions have to be auditable after the fact, and anything touching a merge that has already been used in a clinical order must be signed off by a nurse informaticist. So whatever I hand them has to be short, defensible line by line, and honest about where it gives up.

I would like the metaphysics of identity turned into that instrument. My rough sense of the relevant material, which I would like you to correct where I have it wrong before you build anything on it: Leibniz's Law has two directions that pull apart badly here. The indiscernibility of the identical gives me a one-way test only — a hard property conflict (incompatible sex chromosomes, incompatible blood types, a date of birth inconsistent with the age on the record) decisively refutes identity, while no amount of field agreement can establish it. The identity of indiscernibles, which is what a probabilistic matcher implicitly assumes whenever it merges on agreement, looks to be the weak direction: Black's example of a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, each equidistant from the other, is supposed to show that total agreement across all qualitative and relational properties is still compatible with their being two, from which I take it that no similarity threshold can be constitutive of identity and that the residual move available to a defender of the principle — appealing to something like a primitive thisness, a property of being this very sphere — names something no matcher can store as a field. There is also, I think, a distinction I am currently conflating: the question whether two records are about the same patient is an identity question, while the question my queue actually asks is an evidential one, namely whether we have warrant to treat them as the same given our error costs.

Please write the decision procedure my analysts can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a hard no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of stipulation or values rather than evidence — in particular the moment, if there is one, at which an enterprise identifier has to be assigned by convention at an enrollment event rather than discovered by comparison. Tell me which distinctions from the metaphysics have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped without loss, and say plainly where a ten-minute procedure will misfire. I would also value your view on one thing I am unsure of: whether identity claims in the index need a sortal supplied — the same patient, the same account, the same person under a family policy — and whether merging under one of these licenses merging under another.

Then run it on three cases. First, two records with the same name, one carrying a generational suffix the other dropped, the same date of birth, addresses about two miles apart, one flagged as a newborn male and one as a thirty-four-year-old male. Second, two records agreeing on every stored field, created four hundred milliseconds apart by the same registration clerk during a downtime-recovery replay. Third, adult monozygotic twins who share a date of birth, an address, and a family-level member identifier on the exchange, so that no anchor identifier distinguishes them. My team currently merges the second on score and has no settled policy on the third; I expect the procedure to tell me something I do not want to hear about at least one of these, and I would rather hear it now than after an adverse event. Finish by stating what the procedure cannot do at all.
