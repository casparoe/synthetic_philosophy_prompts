I work in interpretability and I hold a realist view about features: when a direction in a network's activation space can be isolated by sparse dictionary learning, a linear probe, or a steering vector, and interventions along that direction produce the systematic downstream and behavioural effects characteristic of some feature, then the network genuinely represents that feature, and features are the right units for auditing and controlling systems. Because I know the choice of basis is conventional, I attach a qualified version: what is real is the causal structure, not the coordinates, so my proposed test is basis-free transport — align two networks up to an invertible linear map and ask whether the causal role of a feature survives when matched that way rather than by nearest-neighbour matching of dictionary elements.

Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I want the cases and results that should move me, including ones that have arguably already happened: probes that recover features the network never uses, steering that changes behaviour without tracking anything the network itself computes, near-zero correspondence between the dictionaries of networks trained identically apart from seed, and the hypothetical I call encoding degeneracy, where for any feature set an unsupervised method finds, one can train a behaviourally equivalent network whose sparse directions are disjunctions and conjunctions of the originals, equally sparse and equally causally efficacious. For each, say whether a committed realist should concede it as counting against the view or is entitled to dismiss it, and whether my basis-free qualification insulates the position into unfalsifiability — and if it does, whether that is a problem for a view I want to lean on in a safety case. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
