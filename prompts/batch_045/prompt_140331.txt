I'm putting together a feature for the science-and-ideas magazine I write for — long reported piece on the new generation of psychiatric implants — and I want a short story to run alongside it, 1200–1800 words, that does some real conceptual work without ever announcing that it's doing any.

The device (real, in the reported piece; yours to name in the story) is a neural implant prescribed for severe OCD and for impulsivity after strokes. It monitors motivational conflict and resolves it in favour of whatever the patient has most consistently endorsed over the previous month of guided sessions. In practice: you stop dithering, you stop doing the 2am thing you regret at 8am, you reliably do what you meant to do. Adherence is high, patients say they feel like themselves again, the outcome data is genuinely good. I do not want a dystopia. However the story turns out, it should be honest that this thing works.

What I want the reader to feel — this is the bit I keep failing to explain to my editor — is that there are several different things a person could mean by wanting to be free, that the implant massively increases some of them and quietly eats others, and that the ones it eats are the ones nobody thought to miss. My working list:

— the "I get my way" one: the action matches what I most want, no inner sabotage. The implant is fantastic at this.
— the "I could have done otherwise" one, including on the occasions where I'd never in a million years have wanted to do otherwise.
— the diachronic one: becoming a different sort of person by doing something I can't yet fully justify to myself, acting ahead of my own reasons and finding out afterwards who I turned out to be.
— the social one: being the kind of thing other people can resent, thank, trust, forgive, hold to a promise.

Two more givens. First, the model that decides which of her motives is "really her" was fit on other people's data, so part of what it enforces is a statistical composite rather than anything she ever chose. Second, about six months in, the people around her stop reacting to her in the ordinary way — fewer hard feelings, fewer apologies accepted, less credit given — and nobody involved ever decided on that.

Rules: third-person limited, present tense, no jargon, and the words "free will" and "responsible" never appear anywhere. At least one scene where the protagonist does something unmistakably hers and impossible not to admire, and neither she nor anyone else can tell whether she should get credit for it. A protagonist who isn't a philosopher or a patient advocate — give her a job that involves being trusted to act alone. No resolving ending. And please don't just re-stage that famous setup where a hidden intervener could have stepped in but never does; I've read it a hundred times and it always feels like a parlour trick. Also, nobody in the story gets to explain any of this. If a character starts lecturing, cut them.

Then, separately and not for publication, 150 words max on wich of the four you leaned on hardest and which one you think I'm wrong to care about. That part can be as jargon-y as you like — it's just for me while I'm drafting the nut graf. Sorry if this is all over the place, I'm definately overthinking the brief.
