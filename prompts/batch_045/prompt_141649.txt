You are a professional philosopher who also designs classroom simulations, and you are unusually good at making an abstract argument show up in what participants actually do rather than in what the facilitator says. Please think this through carefully: before committing to a design, brainstorm at least three different mechanics for the activity and say in a sentence or two why you are discarding the other two.

I run the philosophy club at my school. I have one 50-minute lunch session, about 14 students aged 15 to 18, with very mixed confidence about numbers, no homework and no prep, and only index cards, pens and maybe a die as materials. I want them to run into the regress problem on their own: I would rather they spend most of the session actually building chains of reasons and noticing that a chain has to stop somewhere, or loop back on itself, or go on without end, than hear any of that described. They should not hear the labels foundationalism, coherentism and infinitism until the last ten minutes, if at all.

Here is the twist I want built into the rules. Give every link in a chain a small, explicit cost: each time one claim is supported by another, the support that arrives is a bit weaker than the support that was already there. Then vary the transmission rule between rounds. In one round the loss compounds down the chain, so a long chain is much weaker than a short one and an endless chain leaves you with nothing. In another round each new link adds a fixed increment of support up to a ceiling, so length still helps and an endless chain is fine. Do not tell them the rules differ in this way; let them discover it. Circular chains should be legal but should not be a free lunch. The point I want them to feel is that how crazy it is to answer the regress by just keeping going forever depends on a substantive assumption about how justification is transmitted that nobody had noticed they were making.

Give me: the rules and a payoff scheme that needs no money and no real stakes; a minute-by-minute timeline; a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the three positions and onto the transmission assumption; an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover; and a frank note on what the game cannot show, in particular that it hands the group their starting points for free, whereas a real foundationalist owes an account of why some beliefs need no support from others. Keep all arithmetic to simple fractions or percentages.
