Last night I had a long argument with a friend about Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge, and I would like a case built to order to settle it.

The argument, in brief. Take the theory in its familiar four-condition form: S knows that p iff p is true, S believes that p, if p were false S would not believe that p, and if p were true S would believe that p — with the counterfactuals relativized to the method by which S actually arrives at the belief, so that a subject can know p by one route even though some other route available to her would have produced the same belief regardless of the facts. Closure, in the form we were arguing about, says: if S knows that p, knows that p entails q, and comes to believe q by competently drawing that entailment, then S knows that q. Tracking does not deliver closure, and everyone agrees about the headline case: the theory is built to let you know an ordinary proposition while denying that you know the denial of the corresponding remote skeptical hypothesis. My friend says this is a straight reductio, and that outside skeptical hypotheses the only closure failures you can build are fake-barn cases in disguise or lottery cases in disguise, both of which are already contested grounds. I said the failure is structural and shows up in perfectly ordinary life, with no skeptical hypothesis anywhere nearby, and that it is a feature rather than a bug: it is the theory correctly registering that a consequence can be harder to know than a premise, when the consequence's falsifying possibilities are remote but systematic. We went in circles, because neither of us could produce a case the other would accept.

So: build me one. One paragraph, ordinary setting — an office, a kitchen, a clinic, a station platform; no demons, no brains in vats, no simulations, no fake barns, no disguised animals, no duplicates or twins standing in for the real thing if that is doing the work, no lotteries and no reasoning from tiny probabilities. The case has to satisfy all of the following at once, with the verdicts falling out of the theory rather than being written into the story:

1. S knows that p by tracking's own lights: p is true, S believes it, and the method she actually used satisfies both subjunctive conditions.
2. S knows the entailment p → q by tracking's own lights, by some method, and that method can be named.
3. S actually performs the deduction, believes q on that basis, and q is true. Nothing S believes in the case is false, and the failure must not come from the deduction being shaky or from the entailment being beyond her grasp.
4. No method available to her tracks q — not any direct method, and not the derived method either, where the derived method is the one Nozick-style maneuver postulates: believe q because you believe p, believe p entails q, and infer. The failure must show up in the sensitivity condition, not in the adherence condition, and not in S's failing to believe q or failing to make the inference.

Here is my hunch about what the load-bearing feature will be, which you should use only if it is right and correct me on if it is not. Because p entails q, the worlds where q is false form a subset of the worlds where p is false, so the nearest q-false worlds can never be closer than the nearest p-false worlds — entailment prunes the falsifying region from the inside. That means the nearest q-false worlds can sit farther out, in a corner of the p-false region where the subject's method, reliable near at hand, fails in a systematic way and still delivers the belief. Sensitivity is verified only at the nearest falsifying worlds, so it can hold for p and fail for q even though q is entailed by p. If that is the mechanism your case exploits, I want it stated in a form general enough that I could recognize the same feature in a case on unrelated subject matter.

What I want back, after the paragraph:

(a) The check. State p, q, the entailment, and each candidate method; state, for each, the nearest relevant worlds and what S believes in them; run all four conditions for the direct method for q and for the derived method; and show separately that each premise-belief tracks on its own method, so that the failure is not quietly inherited from an insensitive premise.
(b) The single feature doing the work, named and generalized.
(c) An ablation: the smallest change to the scenario that removes that feature, and confirmation that with it removed the theory returns the closure-friendly verdict, so that the case is not overdetermined by some second defect.
(d) The defense. I expect two moves, and I want both taken seriously: first, re-individuating the derived method so that it includes the background conditions under which the premise-belief was formed, so that the method does not fire in the offending worlds; second, restricting which worlds count as relevantly near. For each: does it rescue closure in your case, does it generalize, and what does it cost? In particular, does either move make method descriptions gerrymanderable in the way critics complain about, so that any true belief can be made to track by narrowing the description of the method until it fits? And does either move cost the theory the one result my friend and I agree it gets right, namely the skeptical pairing in which S knows the ordinary proposition while failing to know the denial of the remote hypothesis? If a defense costs that, say so plainly.
(e) One more thing I noticed while arguing and could not settle: if your entailment is a necessary or conceptual truth, tracking certifies knowledge of it vacuously, and my friend will say the case is rigged by that vacuity. So tell me which kind of entailment you have used, and whether the failure survives if you swap in a contingent entailment whose falsifier the subject would detect. If the swap cannot be made to work, say what that shows about how general the closure failure really is.
(f) Where untutored intuition actually lands on your case, honestly. If everyone's immediate reaction is that of course she does not know q, the case begs the question against my friend; if it is that of course she does, it begs the question against me. I would rather have a case with a contested intuition and a note saying so than a clean verdict manufactured by the setup.

No bibliography, no references, no citations — just the case and the reasoning.
