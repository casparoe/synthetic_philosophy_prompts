Hey — I'm TAing a crossover seminar on game theory and philosophy of action this term, and after a talk last week I got into an argument with a friend that I haven't been able to shake, partly because I can't tell whether I'm defending a real claim or just defending my own stipulations. The position I've ended up holding is: the so-called paradox of backward induction isn't a genuine problem for the epistemic program. Roughly, if by "rational" you mean the forward-looking, counterfactual thing — an agent is rational iff at every information set, including ones that are only reached if she herself plays irrationally, she'd take an action maximizing expected utility given her beliefs there — then common knowledge of rationality (plus common knowledge of the game) does deliver the backward induction outcome in perfect-information games, and the intuitive resistance ("once someone has visibly deviated you should stop assuming they're rational, so the reasoning eats itself") only gets going by sliding over to a different, diachronic notion where an observed past mistake is evidence about future play. On my view the whole literature is a fight about modeling conventions for beliefs at counterfactual nodes, not about rationality or common knowledge. I also want the corollaries: that experimental deviations in centipede-type games are evidence about subjects' beliefs about each other, not against the rationality assumptions, and that the other "paradoxes" (finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma, chain-store, that three-player case where the last mover has to act after two earlier players have already gone off the backward induction path) get dissolved the same way.

So: tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and — the part I care most about — separate what a defender of the view should concede as a real defeater from what critics call fatal and we'd legitimately wave off. Some things I want you to work through rather than just list. First, the self-refutation worry in its sharpest form: if rationality at never-reached nodes is constrained by nothing observable, isn't "common knowledge of rationality implies backward induction" a theorem about a stipulation — any agent I define as satisfying the constraint behaves as the constraint says? Is there any refutable content left, or does the position quietly reduce to the claim that the counterfactual notion is *the right* notion of rationality, and if so what could possibly settle that? Second, cases: the driver at two indistinguishable exits who can't remember which one he's at (if I remember right, here the "plan of action" reading and the "sequential rationality with conditional beliefs" reading come apart, and I'm not sure my view survives that); and a variant I made up where each player can, at a tiny cost, publicly install a known small probability of playing "cooperate" no matter what before a finitely repeated PD starts — my guess is that under common knowledge of rationality installing it is rational, which would mean the backward induction conclusion isn't robust to tiny changes in what counts as "the game." Third, the empirical and continuity stuff: the old reputation result where a sliver of doubt that your opponent is a mechanical cooperator makes cooperation rational for nearly the whole horizon, and the lab results on centipede — are these decisive, or only evidence that the idealization is discontinuous? And fourth, a structural worry: does the epistemic framework smuggle in a causal independence of the players' choices that fails when the two players are literally the same program, or the same person twice, so that their moves are correlated in a way the strategy-space doesn't represent?

Wrap up with which single defeater you think is strongest overall, an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability (and whether that's a problem for a claim of this kind or just what you should expect from a claim about the content of a modeling assumption), and what you'd personally find most persuasive — I think I'd be most moved by an argument about whether the counterfactual notion of rationality makes contact with anything we care about independently of getting the theorem to come out. Please don't give me citations, paper titles, or author names — no web access needed and I'll look those up myself — but do tell me if I've misremembered how any of the classic results actually go.
