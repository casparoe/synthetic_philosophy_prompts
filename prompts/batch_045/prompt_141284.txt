I'm in a final-year seminar on the science and philosophy of consciousness, and for the last assignment we each have to post a short statement of where we stand so the rest of the group can attack it in writing before we defend it out loud in class. I've written up my position on integrated information theory and I'd like an audit of it before I post, because I genuinely can't tell which of the things I believe is doing the work. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(a) I accept IIT's overall architecture — start from what is essential to experience, read physical postulates off the essential properties, and identify a system's consciousness with its irreducible intrinsic cause-effect power, so that how conscious a system is comes to how much integrated information it has, what its experience is like comes to the shape of that cause-effect structure, and the subject is the single maximally irreducible complex, with the exclusion postulate saying that no nested or overlapping complex inside it is conscious too. (b) I regard that last feature as a strength rather than an embarrassment: it is what makes IIT a theory of the subject rather than a theory of information processing, and I am happy to pay the price of saying that a human being contains no other conscious entity besides the one maximal complex. (c) I also take IIT to be the most empirically serious of the current theories, because it is the one that puts a number on things; the evidence I have in mind is the familiar kind — the contrast between cortex and cerebellum, where an enormous number of neurons sit in a modular, largely feed-forward architecture and there is apparently nothing it is like, and the way consciousness fades when cortical integration breaks down in deep sleep or under anaesthesia. (d) I bite the bullet on the consequences other people treat as reductions, including that a grid of simple units with no behaviour and no useful input-output role would be more conscious than I am if its integrated information were higher, and including the weak panpsychist consequence, since the values for ordinary objects are so small as to be negligible. (e) The part I would defend hardest is the unfolding story, on which particular qualities of experience, such as the felt extendedness of space, fall out of particular structural features of the complex, for instance the grid-like connectivity of posterior cortex; I don't think any current theory dissolves the hard problem, but accounting for the structure of experience looks to me like most of the work that is actually available. (f) Finally, I think the charge that IIT is unfalsifiable or close to pseudoscience is overstated, while conceding two things to the people who make it: that the quantity at the centre of the theory can only be computed for toy systems, so what gets tested in real nervous systems is always a proxy such as perturbational complexity rather than the quantity itself, and that the value obtained depends on the grain at which you describe the system, which is why the theory instructs you to describe it at the grain that maximizes the value.

What I want is a consistency check. Work through the list and tell me which pairs of these commitments are really in tension with each other, how severe each tension is, and precisely what I would have to give up to resolve it. I suspect at least one of the conflicts that jumps out is not a genuine one, and I'd like you to say which and why it dissolves rather than flagging it alongside the others. Rank the real tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally bad, and then tell me which single revision to the set would clean up the most. Please don't just tell me that IIT is contested or that the empirical situation is unsettled — I know that; I want the internal structure of my own commitments, and which of them is covertly supporting which. It would also help to know if the cheapest fix pushes me toward one of the views my seminar-mates hold, since several of them are global workspace people and one is a biological naturalist about consciousness.
