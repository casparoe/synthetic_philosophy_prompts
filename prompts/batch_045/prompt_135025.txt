I'm TAing an undergrad rational choice module this term (way more philosophy than econ) and the seminar on money pumps always goes the same way: everyone agrees that cyclic preferences let somebody walk you around in a circle and take your money, and then they immediately start talking past each other about whether that shows the preferences are *irrational*, or just that the world happens to contain people who will take your money, or whether the real problem is that you can't bind your future self. So instead of a readings discussion I want to hand out a sorting quiz: eight or so questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that someone who has never heard any of the labels can answer it. Then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto six to nine named leaves — I want the tree built from the questions first, out of the logical space, and only then have the actual positions dropped into it, with any combination of answers that nobody seems to actually hold flagged and given one line on whether it's incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unoccupied. Where two leaves can't be separated by anything on the quiz, say so rather than inventing a question that pretends to separate them. If a leaf is standardly known by a name I'd half-recognize from the syllabus (I think "resolute choice" and "sophisticated chooser" are two of them, but don't trust me on that), give me the label in a word or two — but please don't turn this into a literature review or a history of who argued what.

The cases I want used, because these are the ones my students actually get stuck on: the ordinary three-option cycle where each pairwise trade costs a dollar; the same pump run by someone who burns the money afterwards so nobody profits (does your verdict change if no one gains?); the pump you can see coming in advance and can pre-commit against for free; a group or committee with majority-rule cycles being pumped in exactly the same way (is the group irrational, or does it just lack a single will, and does your answer there carry over to a person over time?); a time-inconsistency case where you'd prefer the larger later reward now but switch when the earlier date arrives, so I can see whether that's the same defect as the cycle or a different one; a case where every step is imperceptibly small and each one is worth taking, so you end somewhere horrible (I half-remember this as Quinn's self-torturer, but I may be mangling it, so just use the structure); and my own pet case, where the reason you can't plan is that your future preferences aren't just different but currently unimaginable to you — you'll care about something you can't now evaluate at all, so resolute planning looks impossible rather than merely costly. If you think I'm missing a case that would do real diagnostic work, swap one in, but tell me which one you dropped and why.

Epistemically: I'd like it clear which bits of the taxonomy are boringly orthodox among people who work on this and which are live disputes, and flag anywhere you're reconstructing rather than reporting — my students will treat the handout as settled truth otherwise, and I'd rather it said "people disagree about this" than pick a side silently. Also tell me which of the leaves you think are actually defensible, and which you'd abandon, since I'll have to defend the thing in seminar.
