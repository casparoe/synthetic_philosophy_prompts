I'm writing the final for my upper-level epistemology seminar — 90 minutes, in class, closed book, no notes — and I want one question on luminosity and the KK principle that can't be answered by writing out the warming-room / margin-for-error argument from memory and then concluding "so much for KK". That is exactly the move half the class made on the midterm, and I can't tell from it whether they understood anything. So the question has to force them to say what the anti-luminosity argument actually delivers, whether it reaches KK at all, and what extra premise you would need to get from one to the other.

Here's the mess I keep going back and forth on with my flatmate (he's writing on Williamson, so he's biased, but he's also the only person who'll talk to me about this). Luminosity says, roughly: necessarily, if condition C obtains in a case, then in that case the subject is in a position to know that C obtains. KK says: necessarily, if you know that p, you know that you know that p. Those aren't the same shape — one talks about being in a position to know, the other about actually knowing, and one is about a condition obtaining while the other is about a piece of knowledge obtaining — and I suspect all the interesting work is in that gap. I'd also like to bolt on a wrinkle that isn't in the readings, so they can't have memorised a response to it. Call a condition C hyper-luminous when: necessarily, if C obtains in a case, then in that case the subject both knows that C obtains and knows that they know that C obtains. Then hand them a two-level version of the usual series case: not just a series in which the subject's first-order position with respect to p degrades one grain at a time, but one in which their grip on their own knowledge state degrades alongside it — in each successive case they have slightly worse first-order evidence and slightly worse introspective access to whether they know — and ask them whether running the margin-for-error reasoning at the second order gives you an argument against KK that is genuinely new, or just the first-order argument with extra steps. Whether the two collapse into each other is the thing I most want them to have a view about.

So: write me the question itself (self-contained — define every term, since they won't have the readings in front of them), a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five or six must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on the way students typically mess each one up. The distinction I most want the scheme to reward is the one between a student who simply asserts that luminosity failure entails KK failure, and one who can say what the bridge premise would have to be — something about being in a position to know entailing knowing, or about the margin/safety constraint applying to knowledge itself — and whether that premise is even available to someone who already accepts the anti-luminosity argument. No bibliography or reading list at the end, I've got the syllabus; just the question, the model answer, and the scheme.
