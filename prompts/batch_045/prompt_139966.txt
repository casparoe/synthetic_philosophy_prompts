I help out a friend by cleaning up the machine-translated subtitles on her climbing videos (Spanish into English, mostly, and I speak just enough Spanish to notice when something has gone wrong), and I've got a running argument going with her brother, who writes software and insists the program doesn't understand a single word of what it translates — it just squishes patterns. There's an essay people keep sending me about how shallow machine translation is (Hofstadter? I think that's the name) which I only half follow. The thing is, when I go case by case my gut doesn't line up with either slogan, "it understands" or "it's just doing statistics." Here's roughly where I land:

- My friend uses a very common idiom, then in the next clause makes a joke by taking it literally — the spinning-your-wheels kind of phrase, followed by "and the snow was flying everywhere." The program translated it as the idiom. My verdict: no understanding, it didn't get the joke.
- She coins a fresh metaphor for a route that shreds your hands, and the program renders the metaphor as the literal object and produces word salad. Obviously no understanding. But when she uses a really common metaphor for the same thing, the program nails it, and my gut says "that's just memorized" — I feel nothing at all.
- A sentence like "the rope wouldn't fit in the bag because it was too long," where swapping "long" for "short" flips what "it" refers to. The program tracks the flip correctly. My verdict: okay, that's genuinely getting what's being said.
- A word with two unrelated senses (the kind like river bank / bank where you keep money) disambiguated correctly from the other words in the sentence. My verdict: that's just word association, I'm not impressed.
- Choosing the right register — casual vs. formal "you" — across a whole paragraph because of one slang word three sentences earlier. Somehow less impressive to me than the rope case, and I can't say why.
- Assembly instructions for a rack: it reversed a spatial relation ("the bracket goes on the far side of the post, facing you") and produced the opposite. My verdict: this is the one that really convinces me, because it never has to picture anything, so of course it gets this wrong.
- Her brother proposed a test: swap every noun for another of the same kind and see whether the translation changes only where the meaning changed. I tried it, and the program passes a lot of the time — and I still don't feel convinced. Which makes me think whatever I'm actually responding to, it isn't that.
- When I ask the same tool, in plain language, what a sentence means or why the joke is funny, it often explains it well in both languages. I go back and forth on whether that should change my verdict on everything above. Her brother says that only shows it's read a lot of people talking about sentences.

The pair that bugs me most is the rope case and the bank case: they look like the exact same trick, picking the right reading from the surrounding words, and I feel completely differently about them. So here's the principle I've been trying out: it understands only if it tracks meaning rather than surface — change the surface and keep the meaning, and the translation should stay put; change the meaning and keep the surface, and the translation should move. But the bank case seems to pass that test too, so it can't be what's driving my reaction, and I need a second clause and can't figure out what it is.

Can you do the reflective-equilibrium thing for me: state the best principle you can (or two or three candidates) precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save and which one I should probably give up. Then tell me what it predicts about cases I haven't thought about — definately these: a system trained only on instruction manuals and weather reports that's perfect on those and hopeless on everything else; a system that answers "this sentence is ambiguous, here are both readings" instead of picking one; a human translator who's brilliant but can't explain a single one of her choices; and someone who learned the language entirely from books and has never been to the country (my gut says they understand a little less, which pushes toward needing contact with the world rather than with more text, but then the rope case came out of text alone, so I'm wierdly stuck).
