You are a philosopher of cognitive science and education retained as an external advisor to the Instructional Materials and Technology Committee of a large public school district. Take as your working framework the familiar four-fold taxonomy of minds: Darwinian creatures, whose responses are wired in by selection over generations; Skinnerian creatures, whose repertoire is shaped within a lifetime by reinforcement; Popperian creatures, which let candidate actions fail in imagination or in surrogate rather than in the body; and Gregorian creatures, which internalize culturally supplied representational tools — words, number, diagrams, procedures — that reorganize what they can so much as think. Assume, as that taxonomy suggests, that what distinguishes the later grades of mindedness is not more raw processing power but a new way of getting information about the world before acting on it.

The committee votes in nine days on a three-year district-wide license for an adaptive mastery-learning platform for grades 6–10 mathematics. The platform models each student continuously and adjusts item difficulty and hint timing in real time; it is marketed on the claim that it holds every student inside a narrow band of success by supplying a hint or an easier item before the student can commit an error. The district's own pilot shows solid gains on standardized items, largest in the lowest quartile, and a marked drop in self-reported math anxiety; teachers report that students who use it volunteer answers far more readily.

A minority of the committee has raised an objection the district has no vocabulary for: that the platform produces competence that lives in the student-plus-software loop rather than in the student. The learner is shaped by a feedback schedule he neither sees nor models; errors are prevented rather than made and then corrected; and little of what the system teaches is a tool the student can carry off-screen and deploy on paper. In the taxonomy's terms, the charge is that this is an excellent Skinnerian environment and a poor Gregorian one, and that it starves the Popperian learner of precisely the consequential, publicly visible failures that make hypothesis-testing worth doing.

Before you draft, think carefully and work through, in a few paragraphs you do not turn in: what exactly is the cognitively and morally relevant difference between competence located in a loop and competence located in a learner; whether preventing an error can ever teach what correcting one teaches; whether the district's actual aim is item-level performance or the production of Popperian and Gregorian minds; and whether pre-emptive scaffolding is defensible for some content, such as rote fluency, while corrosive for other content, such as multi-step reasoning.

Then write the memo I will circulate, at most about 800 words, addressed to a committee of teachers, two administrators and one school board member who have no philosophy background — translate the framework into criteria they can apply without lecturing them. It must (1) give a clear bottom line — approve, reject, or approve under stated conditions — with a timeline; (2) state the operational tests any approval would be conditional on, which should include some way of measuring what students can do with the adaptive scaffolding switched off, and some requirement that each unit leave the student holding a representational tool rather than only a reinforced disposition; and (3) treat the strongest consideration on the losing side at full strength: the equity case for the platform, given that the students who gain most from it are those whose current alternative is not Popperian struggle but silence and no feedback at all. Close by naming the empirical finding that should trigger a reversal of the recommendation.
