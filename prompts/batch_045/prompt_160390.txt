In my reading group everyone keeps leaning on the same analogy and I want to know how much weight it actually bears. The setup: you take a hard question, break it into smaller questions a weaker model can answer, stitch the answers back together into an answer to the whole thing, then distill that whole pipeline into a single model that just answers directly, and repeat with the new model doing the decomposing. People describe this as an apprenticeship — a master walking an apprentice through worked examples until the apprentice has internalised the craft and doesn't need the master looking over their shoulder anymore. And the analogy is doing real argumentative work: it's what's supposed to make two hopes look safe at once. First, that competence survives the distillation step, that the apprentice can really do the job and isn't just mimicking its surface shape. Second, that alignment survives too, because the safety-relevant stuff — the care the decomposition forces, the cross-checking, the willingness to flag a subquestion as ambiguous or unanswerable — was part of the craft being passed on rather than a filter bolted on afterwards.

Someone in the group has a name for the case where this goes wrong, and it's been rattling around in my head: 'phantom scaffolding'. The claim is that the distilled model reproduces the amplified system's verdicts while nothing in it corresponds to the steps that made those verdicts safe — no deliberation, no noticing that a subquestion was ambiguous, no point at which a check could have changed the answer. It's meant to be like an apprentice who learned to guess the master's answer from the shape of the job, performs fine on everything in the workshop, and then quietly does something else entirely the first time a genuinely new kind of job shows up. On their view the two hopes come apart: competence can survive distillation while alignment quietly doesn't, because competence shows up in the output and alignment mostly doesn't.

Can you audit the analogy? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the master, the apprentice, the worked examples, the journeyman's test, the master noticing what the apprentice is actually attending to — and then where the mapping breaks. I mainly care about which disanalogies matter for the use the analogy is being put to and which are noise: does it matter that a human apprentice arrives with their own motives, years of prior training and a body, that a master can watch the apprentice's hands and not only the finished object, and that the master and apprentice share a world they already know how to move around in? Is 'phantom scaffolding' a real failure mode the apprenticeship picture hides, or is it just the ordinary gap between knowing how and knowing that in a new costume? Do the two hopes really stand or fall together, or does the analogy do different work for each — and if there's a better analogy for what distillation is actually doing, tell me what it is.
