I work in programming languages — mostly partial evaluation, staging and metaprogramming — so my working vocabulary is Futamura projections, metacircular interpreters, self-hosting compilers, quines, reify and reflect, binding-time analysis, and the recursion theorem used as a programmer's tool rather than as a theorem. I have just been reading Hofstadter's chapter on Typogenetics in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* and I want his central claim restated in that vocabulary, together with an honest account of what does not survive the translation.

The set-up, as I take it: a strand is a string over four letters; a fixed table maps each three-letter group either to one of a small stock of named 'amino acids' or to a punctuation mark that ends the enzyme currently being assembled; running the table along a strand therefore yields enzymes, and an enzyme is a sequence of instructions for editing strands — cut here, move one letter left, search rightward for a pyrimidine, switch copying on, and so on. Those enzymes are then set loose on strands, including in principle the strand they were translated from. Hofstadter's claim, as I read him, is that this is a toy model of the relation between DNA and proteins, and that that relation is the same phenomenon as Gödel numbering: in both cases a realm of apparently inert strings turns out also to be a realm of operations on strings, and this double life is what makes self-reference possible in the one case and self-reproduction in the other.

I would like three things.

First, restate the whole apparatus in my terms, but more carefully than the obvious mapping. I assume strand corresponds to data, enzyme to program, translation to compilation, and the fixed table plus the editing rules to the machine; I want you to press on that. In particular: what staging level does each piece occupy, and is translation a compile-time or a run-time operation here? It looks to me like it is somehow both at once, and I suspect that is where the interesting content is.

Second, translate the isomorphism claim itself rather than granting it. In my world, code-as-data is an isomorphism in a demanding sense: quote and unquote, or reification and reflection, are inverses up to the equivalence I care about, and that invertibility is precisely what the second and third Futamura projections and the recursion theorem lean on. Hofstadter's translation is many-to-one, and as far as I can see nothing in the system reconstitutes an enzyme into the strand that produced it — and Crick's central dogma, as I understand it, forbids the biological counterpart of that reverse step. So tell me what the structure actually is. What would have to be paired with translation to yield something I would call an isomorphism, what implements that pairing in the toy and in the cell, and is the result an isomorphism, a surjection with a chosen section, an adjunction, or something weaker? If the honest answer is that the word is doing rhetorical work, say so plainly and say what would be needed to earn it.

Third, the residue report. My suspicion is that three distinct things are being run together under one heading. Gödel's self-reference looks semantic to me: a numbered formula is about proofs, and the diagonal sentence comes out true or false. Quines and Futamura self-application are syntactic but total. Von Neumann-style self-reproduction looks constructive: the description has to be used twice, once as instructions to execute and once as a template to copy, and the machinery has to build something. I cannot tell whether these are genuinely different phenomena or whether my vocabulary collapses them all into one fixed-point construction, with the apparent differences coming from what is bolted on around it. So: translate each into my vocabulary; tell me which of them Typogenetics actually achieves; give me boundary cases in my own terms — systems with two of the three properties and not the third — so I can see whether the distinctions hold; and tell me what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. My own hunch is that nothing in Typogenetics corresponds to an interpreter *inside* the system, since the translation table belongs to the rules rather than to anything a strand can encode, and if that is right I want to know whether it is a limitation of my vocabulary, a fault in the toy, or a sign that the isomorphism claim was never meant to bear the weight it appears to bear.

Please also make this concrete: what artefact in my world would a self-reproducing strand correspond to — a quine, a staged and specialised residual program, or the stage-0 binary of a self-hosting compiler — and which of those is genuinely attainable in Hofstadter's system? I do not want a summary of the chapter, and I do not want to be told that it is all very much like DNA; I want the translation and the wreckage.
