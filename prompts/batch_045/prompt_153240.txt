I'm helping draft the design doc for a city-level citizens' assembly on housing and transit — couple hundred people picked by lot, meet over weekends for about a year, hand down recommendations on zoning and bus routes. I've been asked to write down my own position on the design choices, and I want to know whether the set of things I believe actually hangs together before I put my name on it. Roughly where I've landed:

(1) The core case for sortition is procedural, not consequentialist: nobody has a claim to rule over anyone else, and a lottery gives every citizen an equal shot at holding office, which elections never do.
(2) The draw should still be stratified — quotas on income, education, housing tenure, immigration status, neighborhood — because a pure lottery will in practice throw up a room full of people who have the time and money to show up.
(3) The real payoff is epistemic: give a group like that decent information and time to talk and it beats both a ballot measure and a city council on messy, long-horizon stuff.
(4) If the body gets handed its question by elected officials, the whole thing is theater — it should set its own agenda.
(5) For the same reason, the expert witnesses and the briefing packets should be drawn by lot too, from a pool of people with the relevant credentials. Whoever curates the evidence decides the outcome, so randomize the inputs, not just the participants.
(6) Service should be compulsory, like jury duty, with the usual hardship exemptions.
(7) The body should mirror the city — but I also want members to deliberate properly and change their minds, including toward conclusions most residents wouldn't currently endorse. I don't think delegates should be bound by what their neighbors happen to think.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? My own worry is that (6) sits badly with (1), since making people serve looks like exactly the kind of coercion the anti-domination story is supposed to rule out, and that (2) and (7) are in trouble, since if you're engineering the room to mirror the city you can't also want it to end up somewhere the city isn't. But I might defintely be looking in the wrong place, and I don't want a list where everything is in tension with everything.

Rank the tensions by how bad they are, tell me for each what would have to give and what that costs me, and then say which single revision buys the most consistency. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning — no preamble and no restating my list back at me. American English, and no need to cite anybody or name theorists.
