I chair the small committee that allocates our annual discretionary bonus at the forty-person energy-policy institute where I have worked for eleven years. The pool is modest, the decision has to be unanimous, and it has to be announced with a short written justification. This year I have three cases that pull in different directions, and I would like help with the reasoning rather than with the decision itself.

Priya inherited, in her third month, a donor relationship that a departing colleague had built over six years. She made one call and the foundation doubled its gift, which is roughly sixty per cent of our new money this year. She is a solid performer but, by general agreement, no better at this work than two colleagues who spent the year cold-emailing and brought in very little.

Tom ran our campaign to get a state utility commission to adopt a rate design we had been advocating for years. He worked punishing hours, and on every internal measure of effort, judgment, and persistence he was our strongest person this year. The commission voted three to two against us, partly because a sympathetic commissioner resigned in August and her replacement was hostile. The campaign produced nothing I can point to, and there is a real chance it made the next attempt harder.

Ada found an error in the model we use to estimate avoided emissions. Correcting it cut our headline figure by about a third, cost us two funders, and has largely been experienced internally as an embarrassment. It is also the thing I would point to if someone asked what actually made our work better this year.

Two things I have already settled, so please do not spend your answer on them. First, I am unpersuaded by the sweeping thought that nobody has really earned anything, since natural talents and even the capacity for hard work are themselves matters of luck. I suspect that is true, and it does not discriminate between my cases, every one of which is shot through with luck: an inherited relationship, a commissioner's resignation, a stale spreadsheet. What I need is a way of thinking about how much the luck matters in each case, and where, if anywhere, it stops mattering. Second, I have been experimenting with a rule — reward the difference a person made that would not have happened anyway, that is, discount the result by how easily someone else could have produced it — and it keeps returning answers I do not trust. It gives Priya almost nothing, since almost anyone could have made that call. It gives Tom almost nothing, since the campaign failed. It gives Ada a great deal. I find the Ada answer right and the Tom answer wrong, and I cannot tell whether that means the rule is defective or my feeling about Tom is sentimentality about effort.

There are two further complications. We disagree internally about what our outputs even are — money raised, policy changed, or research made more credible — and the three cases seem to sort differently depending on which of those you take as the point of the exercise. And Ada leaves in January for a lower-paid job, which I keep being told is irrelevant and which I suspect is not, though I cannot say why.

So: what is the question I should actually be asking, is the replaceability rule salvageable in some modified form, and how should I think about Tom? Please write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though you were writing me a careful email.
