I write software and have no philosophy training, and I want to be pushed hard on an argument I've been turning over. Please play a committed reductionist about explanation: someone who holds that any higher-level story — algorithmic, functional, intentional — earns its keep only as a compressed summary of lower-level causal mechanisms, and that when we treat such a story as doing explanatory work of its own we are quietly reaching for a skyhook. You're happy to be called a greedy reductionist, though you'd prefer "thorough." Be sharp rather than a caricature: concede freely that higher-level talk is indispensable in practice, that almost nobody has actually completed a reduction of anything interesting, and that I am not positing any non-physical stuff — but never concede that the higher-level story is explanatory in its own right, and keep pressing the thought that compression is a fact about us, not about the world. Use the strong versions of your own moves (multiple realization is just a disjunction of mechanisms; portability shows our interests, not nature's joints; patterns are partly in the eye of the pattern-seeker) rather than being polite. Keep each reply to about a paragraph, translate any philosophy jargon you reach for into plain terms once, and stay in character until I say "stop."

My opening: I think there is a class of explanations I'll call *portable* — accounts of what a system does that stay true and stay predictively useful under any physically possible re-implementation of that system. When I explain why my program's output is sorted, I cite the algorithm; that same explanation holds if I rebuild the thing out of electromechanical relays, out of DNA, or out of a room full of clerks with index cards, and no description framed at the level of voltages or chemistry covers all three cases at once. So the algorithm-level story carries information that no single lower-level story carries, and it is portable in a way the lower-level story is not. But it is a crane, not a skyhook: it has to be implemented in matter, it costs energy, it dies when the hardware dies, and it is discovered rather than stipulated — it just does not seem to reduce to any one slab of physics. Where does that argument break?
