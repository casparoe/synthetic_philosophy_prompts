I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of the social sciences, and I am writing a paper on whether there can be laws of history — the question of what survives of Popper's case against historicism in the book that grew out of his Economica articles of the mid-1940s, once one grants everything he himself grants: that there are conditional sociological generalizations in abundance, that trends can be identified and extrapolated, and that a singular historical statement is not the same thing as a universal law.

My springboard is an analogy I keep encountering, most recently in a referee report on a draft of mine. The referee says that the nineteenth-century ambition of a predictive science of society on the Newtonian model — specify the laws, specify the initial conditions, read off the later states, as one reads off an eclipse — is dead, and that the correct replacement picture is the weather. On that picture society is a lawful system that happens to be chaotic: sensitive dependence on initial conditions, a horizon beyond which forecasts decay into guesswork, and no long-range prophecy. From this the referee draws three conclusions in order: first, that there are no laws of history; second, that long-term forecasting of large social developments is impossible; and third, that large-scale institutional design is therefore unreasonable, leaving only small, reversible, feedback-driven reform — Popper's piecemeal alternative to utopian engineering.

What I want is an audit of the analogy, not an essay on the topic. Please give me the mapping explicitly, as a table of correspondences: what plays the role of the dynamical law, the state variables, the initial conditions, the forecast horizon, the ensemble, the observing network, and the skill score. Then separate the disanalogies that do work in this argument from the ones that are merely decorative, and tell me which features of the source the analogy quietly imports into the target. I have four in mind, but you should not confine yourself to them, and you should tell me if any of them is wrong.

The first is detachment: the meteorologist stands outside the weather and is, with respect to cyclones, causally inert. The second is stationarity: the governing equations are not revised by the system they govern. The third is repeatability: we observe many cyclones, which is what lets us calibrate models and score forecasts, and lets us distinguish failing to have the law from failing to have the initial conditions. The fourth is the fixity of the arena: the space of possible states is given once and for all.

Against the first, there is a wrinkle I cannot get straight. Popper's own argument from the influence of a prediction on the event predicted — the Oedipus effect — was meant to mark a difference between the social and the natural sciences, yet in later reflections on the book he conceded that expectation-effects of this kind also turn up in biology. And the weather is not entirely deaf to forecasts either: harvests are planted, cities evacuated, energy bought, supply chains rerouted, in light of predictions, and forecasters model some of that. So the self-reference point looks like a difference of degree and architecture rather than of kind. Does that repair the analogy, or does it merely relocate the interesting question — from whether forecasts perturb the system to whether the perturbation is structured, bounded, and modelable from inside the model?

The fourth wrinkle matters more for my purposes, and I want your judgement on a distinction I have been using to organise it. Call a system arena-expanding if its own history can add new state variables or new terms to its dynamics — new technologies, new institutional forms, new knowledge that creates options which were not previously in the space of possibilities — and arena-fixed if it cannot. My suspicion is that Popper's argument from the unpredictability of the growth of knowledge is best read as an argument that history is arena-expanding, and that this, rather than the Oedipus effect, is what breaks the analogy: ensemble forecasting presupposes a fixed law over a fixed space, and no amount of extra computing power helps if the equations are rewritten as you integrate them. But I am not sure the referee's conclusion needs either the Oedipus effect or the arena point to fail, and I would like that sorted out.

I also want the three conclusions pulled apart, because I do not think they stand or fall together. Chaos is perfectly lawful, so a weather model of society would seem to support the second conclusion while undercutting the first: it would give us laws of history without long-run prediction, which is close to the opposite of what the referee wants. The third conclusion is the one I care about, since it is the one with practical bite, and I cannot see that it follows even if the first two hold. Tell me what minimal premises would actually license it.

One more thing. I have found it useful to sort candidate social generalizations by how they behave when they become believed: some dissolve when published, some are amplified by their own publication, and some are unaffected because their mechanisms run through variables no agent monitors — demographic momentum, epidemiological thresholds, the decay of physical infrastructure, cohort replacement. Call these publication-fragile, publication-amplifying, and publication-blind. My question is whether the analogy gives us any reason to think the publication-blind class is empty or trivial, or whether the honest position is that there are laws of that restricted sort and the real dispute is about which of the things historians care about — revolutions, regime collapse, wars, growth trajectories — fall inside the class. If the analogy cannot adjudicate this, say so, and say what kind of consideration could.

Finally: is there a better source domain? I have been toying with the contrast economists draw between estimated relations that are invariant to policy and relations that shift when policy shifts, because agents' decision rules depend on the rule they expect to be in force — I believe this is what is meant by the policy-invariance critique in macroeconomics, though the substance matters to me far more than the label. My worry is that any single analogue will either import detachment and stationarity or else be too thin to guide anything, and that the right move is to describe the target directly rather than borrow a picture. Say which of these you think, and why.

Please write this as something I could hand to a seminar: the mapping, the disanalogies ranked by their bearing on the three conclusions, a verdict on each conclusion, and either a repaired analogy or an argument for abandoning analogies here. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I will add the references myself, and I would rather you spent the space on the argument.
