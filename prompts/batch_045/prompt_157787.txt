I'm 71, retired now — thirty-odd years as a field entomologist, mostly systematics and taxonomy of beetles — and about a year ago I signed up for a philosophy of science evening class at the local university and now sit in on a reading group in philosophy of medicine too, which is where all this comes from. I got into an argument at a birding weekend with an old colleague who happens to be a psychiatrist, and realised I couldn't say clearly what I actually think about psychiatric classification, so I've written my position down as a list and I want to know whether it hangs together. I have a feeling there's at least one place where two things on the list are each perfectly respectable on their own but their usual motivations can't both be mine — treat that as a hint about the shape of the problem and nothing more, I genuinely can't locate it.

(1) I don't think the DSM categories are natural kinds. No underlying essence as far as I can tell, no single mechanism, the boundaries are drawn by committee and move at every revision, and patients given the same label are heterogeneous in exactly the way that would make me give up on a taxon.
(2) But I think the categories can genuinely explain things — that she couldn't hold the job because of her bipolar disorder can be a real explanation and not just a relabelling of the behaviour. My chemist friend says you can't have it both ways, explanation needs something doing causal work behind the label. I think he's wrong, and here's my reason: species were like that too. No essence, just clusters of properties that hang together well enough, and we still got perfectly good explanations out of species taxa. I don't see why psychiatrists can't.
(3) I take Hacking's looping point seriously: the people classified learn the classification, it changes how they experience themselves and what they do, so the kind is a moving target and the picture of the scientist calmly discovering a kind that was there all along is wrong here. And I think for the mood and personality disorders this isn't a marginal effect, it goes quite deep.
(4) At the same time I don't want to slide in to saying it's all constructed. The suffering and the impairment are real and they'd be there if we'd never named them, and I'd bet on at least rough cross-cultural recurrence of something schizophrenia-shaped and something severe-depression-shaped even under quite different local idioms of distress.
(5) Methodologically I think the right move is the one NIMH started pushing a decade or so back — drop the boxes, go dimensional, build the classification up from circuits and mechanisms rather than symptom checklists, and expect today's categories to split or dissolve.
(6) I think the chemical imbalance critique of psychiatry is overdone. Most diseases had a wrong mechanism story for a while; that the serotonin story got oversold to patients doesn't tell us much about whether depression is a disorder.
(7) But I am sure that severe opioid addiction is a brain disease, and I think saying it in that idiom is clinically and politically useful — it gets people treatment instead of blame.
(8) And I want to keep the existing categories for clinics, insurance forms and court reports even though I think they should be replaced, because you need a line somewhere and I'd rather a committee drew it than a judge.

You're a professional philosopher of science who works on classification and philosophy of psychiatry — take it properly seriously, brainstorm the candidate tensions before you settle on anything, and then audit the set. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
