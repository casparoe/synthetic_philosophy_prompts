I run a small games-and-philosophy reading group — about a dozen of us, half philosophy postgrads, half games design students — and we keep going round in circles on whether video-game characters can matter morally. Everyone just announces a slogan ("it's only code", "if it can suffer it counts") and then we're stuck, so for the next session I want to hand out something that sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by which badge they'd pin on themselves. Could you build me a decision tree, with roughly eight branch questions ending in ten to fourteen leaves?

Everyone starts from the same place — that some character's suffering or death is a genuine event in the fiction of the game — and then each node should be one concrete case with a yes/no or three-way answer, phrased so that nobody can guess which leaf it's steering them to (nothing as bare as "do you think NPCs are persons?"). The cases I really want in there: a companion in a story game who dies in a scripted battle and whom you could bring back by reloading an earlier save (does failing to reload wrong her, and does reloading un-wrong her?); a villager in a farming sim you can let starve by simply never buying the greenhouse upgrade she needs; a mercenary hired in a roguelike who was generated fresh by the game's algorithm on that run and who would never have existed at all if you'd walked a different route (so the Parfit-style non-identity wrinkle is doing the work, not just "you killed someone"); a character whose entire arc was cut before release and who survives only as unused data in the files; a modder-created companion installed on a few thousand machines; and a companion whose dialogue and mood are generated at runtime by a language model rather than written by any author. Each leaf should carry the name of the position if it has one (sentience or simulated-minds views, artefact and authorial-intention views, relation-to-actual-players views, person-affecting asymmetry views, type-versus-token views, and so on) or "unnamed" if nobody defends it, plus one line on its most awkward commitment — I especially want to see what the type-versus-token leaf has to say about aggregating a character's suffering across thousands of simultaneous playthroughs, and whether a character who dies in ninety per cent of runs is thereby worse off than one who dies in five.

Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: give me the questions first, then note which combinations of answers nothing actually occupies and say whether that's because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't seperate instead of inventing a question to force them apart. The distinctions that matter most for my purposes are: whether moral status comes apart from the question of which individual this even is; whether the player's reasons for killing (a speedrunner dispatching a companion to save forty seconds versus doing it out of cruelty) can change the verdict; and whether duties owed by the player come apart from duties owed by whoever designed or generated the character. Stick it on one side of A4 if you can, and add a short scoring key at the end so I can also run the same thing as a flat quiz next term.
