I have been working through Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument and I have assembled an argument I no longer trust, because it ends somewhere I did not set out to reach and cannot accept.

Set-up: start from a distribution D1 that satisfies whatever patterned principle you like. Chamberlain signs a contract allowing a million fans to pay him a small admission price each to watch him play. Every payment is voluntary; every fan prefers paying and watching to not paying and not watching. The season ends at D2, in which Chamberlain holds vastly more than anyone else and the pattern is broken. Call the general point the Liberty Objection: any pattern can be upset by consensual transfers, so any patterned principle can be upheld only by continuously forbidding consenting adults to do as they please with what they hold, and that is decisive against it.

My argument:

(1) The Liberty Objection is sound, and it is the main reason to reject patterned principles of justice.
(2) On the entitlement theory, a holding is just iff it traces back, through voluntary transfers, to an acquisition that satisfied the Lockean proviso — roughly, that appropriation is permissible only if it leaves others not worse off than they would have been had the resource been left unappropriated, or leaves them enough and as good in common.
(3) In the Chamberlain story, each payment is a voluntary transfer, and each leaves both parties better off than the alternative actually available to them at that moment. So every step of the sequence improves on what would otherwise have happened at that step.
(4) If the proviso is applied act by act, with the comparison fixed to what would otherwise have happened at each step, then the Chamberlain sequence satisfies the proviso — and so does any sequence of voluntary transfers, however concentrated the outcome, since the same is true of every step of every market exchange.
(5) So on that reading the proviso forbids nothing at all. It certifies whatever the market produces, and does none of the work Nozick needs it to do at the point of initial acquisition.
(6) The only way to make it bite is to assess the sequence as a whole, or the rule under which it takes place, against a baseline that is not simply the parties' step-by-step alternatives — for instance, how well off the worst off would be under a regime in which such payments were forbidden or capped. But then the proviso condemns the Chamberlain sequence, and by the same reasoning any regime of free transfer, since for any market there is some alternative regime under which the badly off do better.
(7) So the entitlement theory has no usable criterion of just acquisition: its proviso is either vacuous or incompatible with markets. And if (6) is right, the theory is after all assessing outcomes against a standard imposed on the system of rules — which is what a patterned principle does — so the Liberty Objection in (1) turns on it too, and Nozick's argument refutes his own theory.

Where does this go wrong? I want to keep (1), which I think has genuine force, and I also want to keep something like the thought behind (6), that the proviso cannot be assessed one transaction at a time without becoming self-validating. But together those seem to hand the case to the patterned theorist, and I do not want to conclude that. Which step do you reject, what is the flaw in it if there is one, and what does rejecting it cost me — in particular, is it possible to keep the Liberty Objection while letting the proviso be assessed at the level of the whole system of rules?
