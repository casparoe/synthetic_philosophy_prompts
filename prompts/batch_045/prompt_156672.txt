I spent forty years in solid-state physics and have only begun reading philosophy of mathematics since retiring, and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems are the first result I have met that I cannot tell whether to be worried about. The formal part I can follow. What I cannot extract from what I have read is a statement of what would count as having *dealt with* the relativity of "uncountable". So I want the specification, not a survey of positions: the requirements a resolution would have to meet to be adequate for a working scientist who would like to go on using the word "uncountable" without embarrassment — each requirement together with the case or consideration behind it, and each marked as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp.

My own provisional list, which I want you to prune, correct, and extend:

- it must concede the mathematics — that ZFC, if consistent, has a countable model is a theorem, not a datum to be explained away;
- it must say whether "the reals are uncountable" has a determinate answer at all, and whether cardinality is absolute or only relative to a model;
- it must handle the informant case: a community of set theorists working inside a countable model M of ZFC is handed, by someone outside it, an explicit bijection from ω onto M — a map their own theory proves cannot exist. Must a resolution say whether they have thereby learned something about their reals, whether their earlier use of "uncountable" was mistaken or merely relative, and what, if anything, they should conclude?
- it must apply to us and not only to hypothetical denizens of models — it must say what it would take for us to be in that position, or why the question is empty;
- it must leave actual set-theoretic practice intact: forcing, large cardinals, and the standing of "uncountable" as settled within the discipline;
- it must generalize, or explain its refusal to generalize, to the parallel relativity of "finite" in nonstandard models of arithmetic; if the two cases are to be treated differently, the disanalogy has to be stated rather than assumed;
- it should declare what kind of thesis it is — about reference and quantifier domains, about what there is, or about what we can know — since I suspect some arguments here are only apparently about cardinality.

Two things matter most to me. First: flag any pair of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, since if the list is itself unsatisfiable that is the interesting result. Second: say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the familiar moves — "the quantifiers simply range over what is in the model", "it is an artifact of first-order logic", "move to second-order logic", "the universe really is uncountable and the countable model is a curiosity of the metatheory". A brief scorecard of those moves against your list is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria.

Finally, distinguish clearly between what is settled — the metamathematics I take to be agreed — and what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. I would rather be told that the experts disagree than be handed a party line presented as the consensus.
