I spent a working life in experimental physical chemistry, much of it on hydrogen bonding in proteins and in hydration shells, and in retirement I have taken up metaphysics. I keep recognising in these pages an argument from my own discipline. For years we argued about whether the hydrogen bond is real. The argument was never settled; it simply stopped mattering once we noticed that each side could restate the other side's claims in its own vocabulary without either side seeming to have made a mistake. I would now like to know, in a disciplined way, how much of that old dispute was a disagreement about the world and how much was a disagreement about what we were quantifying over.

What I would like from you is a document of a specific kind: the joint statement that would come out of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between two philosophers of quite different temperaments.

The first is a neo-Carnapian deflationist about ontology: someone who holds that disputes over what exists are, when properly conducted, questions internal to a framework and are answered by uncontentious inference from empirical premises; that where two frameworks return different answers the choice between them is a practical or pragmatic matter, or an exercise in conceptual negotiation, rather than a discovery; and that the words exists and object can be used with systematically different but equally admissible quantifier meanings, so that the parties to an ontological dispute are frequently talking past one another without either being at fault. Please make this the strongest available version: one that concedes many disputes are substantive, but locates the substance entirely in the empirical or pragmatic question and denies that a further ontological question remains.

The second is a realist of the structuralist sort: someone who holds that there is a privileged way the world is carved up, that naturalness or joint-carving is a feature of reality rather than of our language or interests, that the natural sciences are in the business of tracking it, and that a dispute over whether a candidate entity exists can therefore be fully substantive even when no possible evidence would settle it. Please also make this the strongest available version: one that concedes some disputes really are verbal, and that has an account of how one could tell the difference.

I want the document, not a verdict on who wins. Concretely, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims that both could sign. They should be neither vacuous nor slanted: I would rather you listed a candidate agreed claim and explained why one side must reject it than that you smuggled in a thesis only one of them holds. Among the things I would expect the section to cover: what quantification and singular terms commit a theory to; what the pattern of mutual restatement without mistake does and does not establish, given that both sides agree it sometimes occurs; whether a dispute that is verbal can nonetheless be worth settling, and on what grounds (fruitfulness, simplicity, fit with the rest of science, practical or moral upshot); whether the fact that ordinary object-talk is settled by trivial inference from empirical premises settles anything about the contested cases; and what each side owes the other by way of an explicit criterion for sorting verbal from substantive disputes.

2. A disagreements table, with one row per contested proposition, columns for the realist's own preferred formulation, the deflationist's own preferred formulation, and a note on what makes the disagreement genuine rather than a collision over words. Each formulation should be one that its own side would actually sign, not a paraphrase imposed by you.

3. A cruxes table: for each crux, the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the one argument, case, or piece of evidence that both sides accept as bearing on it; and each side's prediction, written down in advance, of what that argument will show. I suspect at least three cruxes: whether naturalness is mind-independent; whether a dispute can be substantive while intractable; and whether the internal-question picture can accommodate the fact that working scientists shift between frameworks that are strictly incompatible and yet lose nothing by doing so. Add whichever further cruxes the collaboration would in fact generate.

4. An application section, treating as the worked case a dispute from my own field. In one framework a hydrogen bond is a directional interaction between a donor group and an acceptor group; in another it is a bond critical point in the electron density between two nuclei; in a third it is not an additional constituent of the world at all but a region of a certain topology in the potential energy surface; and there is an older idiom in which the hydrogen bond is a genuine object with a length, an energy and a lifetime. My colleagues moved among all four without explanatory or predictive loss. I have been using a rough test of my own, and I would like both parties to rule on it: if the reinterpretation that one side offers for the other side's existence claim can be substituted everywhere at no cost in simplicity, in predictive power, or in the generalisations the discipline wants to state, then the dispute is verbal; if it incurs such a cost, then it is not. Please have each party say whether it accepts the test, what it counts as a cost, what it predicts the test returns for the hydrogen bond, and whether the residual disagreement about what counts as a cost is itself verbal or substantive. If the crude test is defective in a way both parties can see, say what it should be replaced by.

Finally, the document should state explicitly which parts of the dispute, if any, both parties would now classify as verbal, and by what route they reached that classification; and it should close with a short section addressed to me, saying what I should have concluded about the hydrogen bond when the argument finally petered out.
