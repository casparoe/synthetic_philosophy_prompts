**3. Two jobs, one metaphor**

Talk of carving nature at its joints runs together two questions that I want to pry apart. Call them *anchoring* and *licensing*. Anchoring: in virtue of what is this thing a member of that kind? Licensing: what entitles us to move from examined members to unexamined ones? The standard hope is that one story does both — the microstructure, or on the usual homeostatic-property-cluster (HPC) story the mechanism, both makes the thing what it is and underwrites the induction. My claim is that these can come apart, and that in the special sciences they usually do.

Say a kind K has *borrowed projectibility* when K's membership condition is, at K's own scale, a bit of a mess — no essence, no single homeostatic mechanism, maybe not even a cluster with clean edges — but K is nevertheless reliably projectible because there is a mapping, not itself lawful or even perfectly specifiable, from K's members onto a genuinely homeostatic cluster at some other scale, and the mapping is stable enough around here that induction over K piggybacks on induction over that cluster. The license is real. It just isn't earned at the level where the kind lives. It's borrowed, from below or from above.

Take the gene. Ask for the anchoring story and you get a mess: open reading frames, alternatively spliced transcripts, regulatory elements sitting inside what used to be somebody else's gene, mobile elements, the same stretch of DNA doing different things in different contexts. There is no microstructure shared by and only by genes, and the candidate mechanisms don't cluster the way an HPC account wants. So: not a natural kind. But population genetics runs on genes and gets stunningly projectible results, and it isn't cheating. On my account that's because 'gene' as used there is a compressed way of pointing at molecular machinery that really is homeostatic, via a mapping that is loose, context-relative, and hopeless if you insist on it being one-to-one. Anchoring: no. Licensing, by borrowing: yes. The long arguement about whether the gene is a natural kind was, I want to say, two people answering two different questions.

Second case, and it's the one I'm least sure about. Joints presuppose a single articulation: nature has a skeletal structure everywhere and a good taxonomy finds it. But some of the cuts we care about look less like joints than like what I'll call *seams*. A joint is where two rigid pieces meet — the boundary is there because of what is going on *inside* each piece. A seam, in the sense I mean, is a line along which causal traffic is unusually sparse even though neither side is unified by an essence or held together by a homeostatic mechanism. The boundary is there because of what *isn't* going on across it. Dialect and language boundaries look like this to me: nothing informative is shared by all and only speakers of a given language, there is no mechanism keeping the cluster together in the HPC sense, and yet the cut tracks something, because contact and transmission thin out across it. If that's right, a taxonomy can be objectively better or worse without tracking any essence at all, and 'carving at the joints' was the wrong picture from the start.

So the question to ask of a taxonomy is not whether it finds joints but how many articulations nature has and how well our cuts survive translation between them. Call the virtue *interscalar stability*: a taxonomy is in good shape to the extent that its boundaries survive the mappings between scales — molecular to cellular to organismic to population. Natural-kind talk then survives as a claim about the stability of those mappings, not about the nature of the things mapped.

The obvious objection: if borrowed license is everywhere, 'natural kind' does no work, since every special-science term can borrow from something. My answer is that not every candidate borrows *successfully*, and the failures are the informative part. Phlogiston, biological race, the 'gene for X' of popular genetics: in each case the mapping down to a homeostatic cluster is unstable or lands on nothing, and the inductions fail in a characteristic way.
