I have been working on the epistemic regress problem and have built an argument whose conclusion I will not accept, and I cannot locate the mistake. Please diagnose the argument itself rather than telling me which of the three standard views is correct.

Call it the Audit Argument:

(1) Nothing is epistemically free: S's belief that p is justified only if there is something available to S — a ground — in virtue of which it is justified.
(2) No borrowing from a bankrupt: a ground confers justification only if it is itself in good epistemic standing; a ground that is unjustified, or that is in no epistemic standing at all, confers nothing.
(3) Good standing is never primitive: an item is in good standing only if it is itself justified or belongs to a kind that is exempt from needing justification.
(4) No kind is exempt. For every candidate source — perception, memory, introspection, a priori seeming, testimony — there are cases in which what it delivers is defeated by the subject's other beliefs, and in those cases it stands or falls by support drawn from outside the source. Conditional exemption, though, is not exemption; it is justification by other means.
(5) So every ground needs a ground: justification requires an endless chain of distinct grounds, each available to S. Foundationalism dies at (2)–(4). Coherentism dies because mutual support violates the no-double-duty condition, that no item may both give and receive support within a single justificatory structure. Infinitism is what survives.
(6) Availability is a production condition: a ground is available to S only if S could produce it, under suitable prompting, in some finite time.
(7) A finite subject cannot produce them all. Producing each link is compatible with finitude; producing all of them is not; and (5) requires all of them.
(8) So no belief of any finite subject is justified — whereupon the argument consumes itself, since its conclusion is itself a belief and, if it is true, nothing recommends accepting (1)–(7) either.

That last step is what makes me suspicious: an argument that finishes by removing the ground beneath its own premises looks like one that proved too much. But every premise looked fine when I wrote it, and I cannot tell which one is carrying the weight.

Two constraints on your answer. I would rather keep (1) and (2) than escape the conclusion: that justification is an earned status, and that a reason in bad standing confers nothing, are to me what asking for reasons amounts to. And I would rather keep the observation behind (4): every proposed class of basic beliefs I have examined sometimes needs support from outside itself. I will not accept (8).

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I have three candidates and want to know which is the real fault line. (i) Does (2) equivocate between beliefs and non-doxastic items — experiences, seemings — which may not be the sort of thing that can be justified at all, so that a foundationalist can end the regress at something that is neither exempt nor justified? (ii) Is there a quantifier shift between (4) and (5), from every source sometimes needs outside support to no source ever suffices on its own — in which case the argument also proves that perception and testimony justify nothing even in undefeated cases? (iii) Is (7) too strong for (6): does infinitism require all the links to be producible or only each, and is the each-on-demand-never-all-at-once position stable, or does the production condition regenerate a second-order regress about S's reasons for taking each link to be a reason?

Finally, price the three escapes: what must the foundationalist say about defeated perceptual beliefs, the coherentist about no-double-duty, and the infinitist about production? Tell me which price is lowest and why.
