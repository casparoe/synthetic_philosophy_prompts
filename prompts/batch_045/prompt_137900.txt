Putnam's Twin Earth argument is standardly taken to show that what a speaker refers to with a natural-kind term isn't fixed by anything intrinsic to her: Oscar and his molecule-for-molecule duplicate mean different things by 'water' because one is embedded in an environment of H2O and the other in one of XYZ. I've been trying to work out what the view implies about a less tidy case. Suppose Mara is raised in a sealed habitat whose taps deliver a stable mixture — say 60% H2O and 40% XYZ — that no test available to her can separate, is taught a single word, 'water', for what comes out of them, and grows up to hold an elaborate but mistaken theory of its microstructure. She then meets Oscar, and they argue about whether water boils at 100 °C at sea level. Intuitively there is a genuine disagreement, and intuitively Oscar can correct her. But if externalism is right, her term refers to the mixture (or to some disjunction, or to nothing determinate) while his refers to H2O; they therefore affirm and deny different propositions, so there is no disagreement and nothing for Oscar to correct. And since real samples are never chemically uniform anyway — ordinary tap water is already a mixture — doesn't this show that externalism yields verdicts only inside artificially purified thought experiments, and that it falls apart once we ask what an actual speaker's word refers to? If the reply is that the two share a concept or a 'conception' of water even though their words differ in reference, doesn't that concede that what disagreement and correction really track is something internal?
