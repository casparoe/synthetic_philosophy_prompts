I'm commissioning a piece of institutional satire and I want it built to a fairly exacting specification, so please read the whole brief before drafting.

Subject: the determination of death in a hospital that also procures organs. The object of the satire is the conceptual apparatus — the criteria, the protocols, the certificates, the language of irreversibility — and the self-satisfaction with which institutions administer it. It is not the patients, not the families, and not the clinicians as people; if a joke lands by making someone in a bed or at a bedside look foolish, it is the wrong joke.

Form and venue: a public-facing document, roughly 900 words, published by the Office of Vital Status Integrity of an invented regional hospital network as part of that network's "Transparency in Vital Status" initiative, and written in the register such documents actually use: second person, warm, procedural, fond of the passive voice, and fluent at recasting anything at all as a service to families. Sections: a short welcome from the network's Chief of Vital Status Integrity; a "two pathways" explainer with a minute-by-minute numbered timeline; six to eight frequently-asked questions; a glossary of four or five defined terms; a sidebar headed "Our Commitments to You"; and a closing line of institutional pride.

The apparatus — all of it invented, and each piece described concretely enough that no prior knowledge of transplant practice is needed:

- The network maintains a single Vital Status Ledger and records one official minute of death per patient. A patient may satisfy neurological criteria at one time and circulatory criteria at another, in an order that varies, so the protocol must contain a rule for choosing among candidate minutes. Give it one, and let the rule quietly track what the organs need while being described as though it tracked the facts.
- A "preparation window": while the patient is still living and no determination has been made, the team may place lines and administer a drug that keeps blood from clotting, so that the organs remain usable. The document should state, without noticing the strain, that nothing done during this window may cause or hasten death.
- The interval that must elapse after the heart stops before a determination may be made is fixed by protocol rather than by physiology, and it differs between the network's own campuses. Present the variation as a benefit — more time at the bedside.
- A reperfusion phase: after death is declared on circulatory grounds, circulation is restored to the organs while the vessels supplying the brain are clamped off. The document must explain in one confident sentence why this does not undo the determination, and must state the abort condition — a monitor dedicated to watching for any return of flow to the brain — in a way that lets the reader see what the condition is really there for.
- Continued somatic support after a neurological determination: some patients declared dead on brain criteria are maintained on a ventilator for days or weeks, whether to carry a pregnancy or to let a family travel. The document should address, with an eye to the chart, the billing, and the consent forms, who the patient is during that period.
- Two limits on patient and family authority, handled with regret: members who have registered an advance instruction declaring that the permanent loss of consciousness would be their death, and families who reject neurological criteria outright. The network can accommodate some of this and not other parts; let the accommodation's boundary be visible.

What the reader should see without being told: that "irreversible" slides between cannot be reversed and will not be reversed because no one is going to try; that death as the hospital handles it is determinable only inside a protocol, and the protocol's content is partly shaped by what it licenses downstream; that the same body is dead under one criterion and not under another, and that the official minute is a choice with a certificate behind it. The closing line of institutional pride should land as the reductio.

Constraints: stay deadpan to the end and never tip the wink — no editorial aside, no final paragraph that explains what we were laughing at. Include at least one unacknowledged self-contradiction that an attentive reader catches and the document does not (for instance, the official time of death being adjusted retroactively for insurance purposes while the organs stay allocated). Name no real hospital, statute, professional body, jurisdiction, or person; the network, its office, its accreditor, and any official you quote are invented, and invented figures in place of real statistics are welcome. Do not quote from any real text. And keep the exaggeration honest: every joke should come from a real feature of the apparatus pushed one step, not from a feature invented for the sake of the joke — a philosopher of medicine should recognize the practice and find the satire fair.
