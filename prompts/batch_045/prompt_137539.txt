I am in the third year of a philosophy PhD, working mainly in normative ethics and decision theory, and for roughly a year now I have been trying to get clear on the problem of consequentialist cluelessness: the worry that, because the causal consequences of nearly any action ramify indefinitely and our evidence about the remote effects is close to nil, we typically have no idea which of the options before us has the best consequences, even in apparently mundane choices about careers, spending, or whom to help.

My problem is that I can now reconstruct every position in the debate and every standard reply to it, and I have lost the ability to tell whether anyone is disagreeing with anyone. As I see it there are four families of response. First, the reductio response: the epistemology cannot be repaired, so the fault lies in the normative theory, and we should abandon any standard of rightness that requires estimating remote effects in favour of something evidence-relative, contractualist, or virtue-theoretic. Second, the deflationary response: we are not nearly as clueless as advertised, because long-run effects either wash out symmetrically across the options or are dominated by a small set of tractable considerations, so that measurable near-term effects are a defensible proxy. Third, the absorptive response: cluelessness is just the condition of having imprecise or mushy credences, decision theory already has means of handling that, and the upshot is permissivism rather than paralysis. Fourth, the redirection response: if first-order action is clueless, then the rational thing is to redirect effort away from doing and towards learning — a value-of-information move.

Each has an obvious rejoinder, and each rejoinder has been made many times: that the first changes the subject while leaving the original question standing; that the second rests on an empirical claim about wash-out for which nobody offers evidence, and is anyway hostage to small probabilities of enormous stakes; that the third relabels the problem rather than solving it when the imprecision is near-total; and that the fourth quietly exempts one particular action — investigating — from the very cluelessness it diagnoses, since choosing to gather information is itself an action with unknowable long-run effects.

Two things I have noticed make me think the surface debate is not where the real disagreement lives. The first is that people seem to conflate uncertainty about *what* the long-run effects of an action will be with uncertainty about *how to value* the long-run states that might result; the second half looks to me both harder and logically independent, since perfect causal foresight would leave it entirely untouched. The second is that the four responses each look compelling under a different reading of what it is to be clueless — unable to say which option is better, unable to say by how much, or unable to assign any comparative probability at all — and I suspect much of the apparent dispute is slippage between those readings.

My hypothesis, then, is that this is less a first-order ethical disagreement than a disagreement about how to model the agent's epistemic situation, with the ethical conclusions downstream of it. But I am not confident, and I would rather have a diagnosis than a survey.

So: what kind of impasse is this? Is it verbal or scope-relative in the way I suspect; is it a substantive disagreement about the normative target (rightness versus rationality versus what there is reason to do) that my hypothesis papers over; is there a hidden empirical question about the predictability of long-run social and technological effects doing the real work; or are the sides simply starting from different intuitions about how much an agent may be asked to know? I would also like your view on whether my two distinctions actually carve at the joints. And finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result about decision rules under severe imprecision, a distinction, an empirical finding about forecasting, a change of question — or is there a principled reason to expect the debate to keep circling?
