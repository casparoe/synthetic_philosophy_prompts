I keep noticing an asymmetry in arguments about the very long-run future, and I want to know whether it has been picked out and named, or whether I am confusing two things that merely look alike.

The first place I see it is in discussions of risk. Take an intervention that moves a very small amount of probability — a thousandth of a percent, say — from a future containing an enormous amount of suffering to an otherwise similar future containing an enormous amount of well-being. Describe it as lowering the chance of an astronomically bad outcome, and the sheer size of the stakes is treated as settling the question: any reduction, however small, matters a great deal. Describe the very same intervention as raising the chance of an astronomically good outcome, and the same people reach for the familiar complaints — that this is fanatical, that it is a Pascalian style of argument, that once you allow sufficiently large numbers to do that kind of work you can justify anything. Nothing about the affected population, the numbers involved, or the size of the stakes differs between the two descriptions; only the sign does.

The second place is in population ethics, where bringing into existence enormous numbers of flourishing beings tends to be treated as, at best, weakly valuable or as nobody's loss if it doesn't happen, while preventing a comparable quantity of suffering is treated as urgent. I don't know whether this is the same phenomenon wearing different clothes or a different one that I have run together with it, and part of what I want is to know which.

I am not mainly after a label, and a bare name would not settle anything for me. What I want is the carving: whether anything established names this; how it differs from the nearest candidates on each side — the psychological ones about losses and bad news counting more than gains, and the normative ones about suffering having a distinctive weight, or about creating well-being being different in kind from preventing harm; which of them, if any, actually fits; and where the edges lie. In particular I would like to know whether the asymmetry is really about valence or about direction of change: does it survive if the good outcome is recast as averting the loss of happiness that would otherwise have existed? Does it depend on the stakes being astronomical, or does it persist at ordinary scales? Does it apply to a handful of beings in agony as much as to vast numbers in mild discomfort? And is what I have described best read as a claim about how people in fact respond to large numbers, a claim about what there is reason to do, or something that has to be pulled apart into both? If nothing established fits, please say so plainly rather than implying a literature exists, and then name and define it yourself, with the boundary cases marked.

I have no way to chase down references, so please argue from the distinctions themselves rather than pointing me at things to read. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question; and tell me how confident you are in what you claim, singling out the point you are least sure of.
