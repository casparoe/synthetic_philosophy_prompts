I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and I host a small interview show on decision theory for that audience. Next month's guest is an invented stand-in for a very real position: a decision theorist who defends the hard line on money pump arguments — that cyclic or otherwise diachronically incoherent preferences are simply irrational, because there exists a sequence of trades each of which the agent prefers to make that returns her to where she started and poorer, and because being exploitable in that way is not merely evidence of irrationality but close to what irrationality consists in for an agent extended through time.

Draft eight to ten questions, each with the reply she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that reply. At least one question each must press on:

1. Whether exploitability is constitutive of irrationality or merely diagnostic of it — and what the argument is even supposed to show about an agent who knows she is cyclic and declines the first trade (the standard foresight objection), so that the pump never runs and no money changes hands.
2. My own variant: an agent who ranks histories rather than terminal states and discounts the future, so that the journey A to B to C and back to A, minus three small fees, is by her lights a good one — she spends the intervals in states she prefers while she is in them, and the return to A is a discounted cost she barely feels. Can such an agent be pumped at all, or do she and the exploiter both come out ahead in present-value terms?
3. A second variant: an exploiter who hides the cycle from her — different counterparties, randomized delays, a number of rounds drawn from a distribution she cannot see — so that she cannot tell which step of which cycle she is in. Does foresight still protect her, and if the only safe policy becomes refusing every unsolicited offer, is that a cost the argument can honestly claim as irrationality rather than as bad luck about her environment?
4. The one-shot version, where the whole cycle is offered as a single irrevocable package: does this block the foresight reply, and does the argument then quietly become a synchronic requirement on preferences over plans rather than a diachronic one?
5. The belief-side analogue, diachronic Dutch book arguments against departing from conditionalization, and whether the same replies transfer.

Close with one question about what evidence or argument could count against her view.

Give me three titled sections, in this order: Questions, roughly 600 words, as a numbered sequence with anticipated reply and follow-up for each; Where she will deflect, roughly 150 words, on the moves she is likely to use to avoid answering; and Confidence flags, roughly 150 words, where you state how confident you are in each anticipated reply. In that last section, also tell me plainly if any of my questions rests on a false premise about how the argument is supposed to work — especially my two variants — or is otherwise unanswerable as posed, and what I should ask instead; I would rather hear that than receive a polite question she can swat away in one sentence.
