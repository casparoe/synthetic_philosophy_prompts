I'm writing a novel and I've got a consistency problem I can't think my way out of, so I'm handing it to you.

Setup: my protagonist, Ines, is the harbor officer on a small island. When something goes wrong at sea she's the one who decides where the single rescue launch goes. There's never time for two runs. She's not a philosopher, she's a woman with a radio and about ninety seconds, and she has this half-articulated code that she never explains properly, which is the whole point of her. A friend who read a draft said she sounds like she's cribbing from some old paper arguing that the numbers shouldn't count when you're deciding who to save — Taurek? I think that was the name. I haven't read it and I don't want to, and I definitely don't want her turned into a mouthpiece for a position I can't defend. I just want her to be the same woman in every chapter.

Here's what's already written and I can't change it now:

- Ch. 3: a ferry with five aboard going down, or a single man on a reef. All strangers to her, roughly the same distance, everyone dies without her. She sends the launch to the one. Her line is something like "I'm not adding them up."
- Ch. 5: two people in the water, one to port one to starboard, both adults, both strangers, no difference she can see. She flips a coin. Says the coin is how she stops herself picking favourites.
- Ch. 6: her own coxswain, someone she's worked beside for years, versus a stranger. Doesn't hesitate, doesn't flip, goes to her coxswain.
- Ch. 8: one versus one, a girl of about seventeen against a man of about eighty in bad health. She goes to the seventeen-year-old.
- Ch. 10: a boat with two people on it, a woman and her six-year-old, against a boat with one adult man. She goes to the two. Her coxswain calls her on it later and she says "I wasn't counting them. I was looking at the child."
- Ch. 12, short scene: two swimmers, one of them yells at her to go to the other one, and she goes to the other one.

The scene I have to write this week, Ch. 16: she can reach either a raft with three people on it — strangers to each other, all adults in their forties, one badly hurt but all three would live if she got there in time — or a single figure clinging to an upturned hull, a girl of about nine, also a stranger, no relation to anyone on the raft, too far off to shout to. Both will drown without her. One run.

What I need from you: what does she do? More usefully — what rule has she actually been running across those six scenes, and what other rule fits them all just as well but says something different here? Which of the earlier scenes is the closest match to this one, and why is it or isn't it a real precedent? And if her track record genuinely doesn't decide it: what principle am I committing her to with each option, including the coin — because if she flips, is it a plain fifty-fifty, or the kind where each person gets an equal shot and the raft gets three chances in four, and can someone who won't add people up even flip that second kind without contradicting herself?

Also: tell me the smallest thing that would settle it. A line she says in the scene, or a case I could put her in two chapters later, that would make it obvious which rule she was on the whole time.

Please don't answer by telling me what the actual literature says or who I should go read, and don't tell me to do whatever serves the plot. I need it definately to be her.
