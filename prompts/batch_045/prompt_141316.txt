You are a philosopher of social science who has sat through both history seminars and quantitative political economy workshops, and who has refereed for journals on each side. The setting: next month I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary social science history seminar. The room is roughly two historical political economists who live in panel data, difference-in-differences and event-study designs and who think in terms of identification strategies and credible research designs; a cliometrician working on long-run growth and institutional persistence; an interpretive cultural historian of early modern Europe who thinks quantification flattens meaning and distrusts "structure" as a reification; a historical geographer with GIS layers and proxy-based climate reconstructions who knows how noisy pre-1800 environmental data is; and a few graduate students who will follow whoever sounds most confident.

My thesis, in brief: the longue durée is not just a slower timescale but a different causal structure, and the standard toolkit of event-based causal inference is not neutral toward it. Specifically, two-way fixed effects designs — unit plus period — absorb exactly the variance that structural explanations concern, so the designs this literature counts as most credible are the ones structurally incapable of registering the causes I care about; I call this structural absorption. My case is European state formation between 1500 and 1800, where I argue that fiscal capacity and administrative reach track slow variables (agricultural suitability, access to navigable water, antecedent land-tenure institutions) more closely than they track wars, reigns, ministries and reforms, and that some of the apparent effect of events is an artifact of the design's absorption of the slow variables. I close with a modest recommendation: report estimates with and without unit fixed effects and treat the gap between them as a diagnostic.
