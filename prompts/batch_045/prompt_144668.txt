I'm a backend engineer at a company that makes scheduling and check-in software for infusion clinics, and I've never studied philosophy, so I don't know whether there's a name for the disagreement I'm about to describe.

We can ship one of two things next quarter. The first is unglamorous: better prediction of chair availability, which would cut the average visit's wait — on a hard plastic chair, often in pain — by about forty minutes. It touches a few thousand people a week, and the relief is real but small; nobody's life is transformed. The second touches maybe twenty patients a year: a private room with adjustable lighting and music for the three hours of their infusion. Those patients describe it as the difference between dreading treatment and having one genuinely good day.

My product colleague says this is just arithmetic: add up quality-adjusted minutes and take the larger number. That comes out in favour of the first option, but only because of the headcount, not because the relief runs deeper. My own view is different in kind, not just in amount — I would still pick the relief even if the arithmetic went the other way, because taking away something bad seems to me a different sort of thing from adding something good. He replies that if I really believed that, I would have to say a clinic with no patients at all is doing better than one that treats thousands successfully, which sounds absurd to me.

Then our patient advisory panel complicated it. Several long-term patients said they would rather have the second feature: a genuinely good day is what makes the bad ones bearable, and minimising their pain scores isn't the same as treating them well.

Is there a coherent version of what I believe? And what does holding it commit me to — including conclusions I might not like, and including whether the panel's objection is even about the same question?
