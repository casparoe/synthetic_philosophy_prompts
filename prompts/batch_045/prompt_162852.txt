I joined the board of my family's small foundation last winter — we give away roughly sixty thousand dollars a year, in grants between about two and twenty-five thousand — and I have been going back through our minutes, because a friend who reads decision theory pointed out that the very first request we ever turned down was, structurally, a Pascal's mugging: an unsolicited email offering, for three thousand dollars, to use a method the writer declined to describe on beings he numbered at ten to the thirtieth power. We said no in about five minutes. Since then our record is this: we gave twenty-five thousand to a school-based parasite treatment program with randomized evaluations behind it, where we could state with real confidence that the money would avert on the order of a handful of deaths; we gave twelve thousand to a proposal with a fully specified engineering mechanism, where an independent engineer put a five percent chance on its working and, if it worked, on its sparing roughly a thousand cases of severe illness; we gave four thousand for a one-month scoping review of a speculative policy proposal and then declined the full grant when the review came back lukewarm; and we declined a proposal whose mechanism was spelled out in detail but which rested on a long chain of speculative steps, where the applicants' own materials put the chance of success at one in a hundred million and the payoff at the equivalent of ten to the twenty-second lives. Nobody on the board has ever articulated a principle, and it now looks to me as though everything we have done is equally compatible with several: capping how much value we allow a very large number of lives to carry; discounting claimed leverage, so that the larger the asserted payoff the stronger the evidence we require before the number moves us at all; refusing to act on probabilities below some floor whatever the stake; or treating the purchase of information as our default response whenever an estimate is shaky.

The new proposal resembles none of these. A small physics group asks for nine thousand dollars and says that if a particular speculative cosmological hypothesis is true, an apparatus they can build now would bring into existence something like ten to the twenty-fourth lives that are, on balance, worth living. Two things separate it from our earlier refusals: the probability estimate comes from three independent physicists we consulted rather than from the applicants, and all three put the hypothesis at about one in ten thousand while confirming that the causal chain from hypothesis to apparatus is sound; and no one is claiming any privileged access or special power — the mechanism is public, physical, and in principle directly testable, though a decisive test would cost forty thousand dollars. One of the three reviewers adds, in a minority note the group disputes, that a different variant of the same hypothesis — she puts it at maybe one in ten million — would make the apparatus instead produce an astronomical quantity of suffering.

What should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which of our own precedents is genuinely the closest analog here? Please set out the two or three rules that fit our track record about equally well, say what each implies for this proposal, tell me whether the minority note should move us and why, then give me your best recommendation with the reason for preferring that reading of our record — and, separately, what you would expect us actually to decide in the room next month. My fellow trustees will probably propose deferring and spending a thousand dollars on a further feasibility study; I would like to know whether that is a genuine fourth rule or just one of the others in procedural disguise. Finally, name the further case that would really tell these rules apart, since I still have room in the budget to commission one more review if it would settle anything.
