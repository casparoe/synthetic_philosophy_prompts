I'm applying for a one-term research fellowship and need a short proposal — about two pages, 1,000 words or so — for a project on Leibniz's claim that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. I need the proposal document itself, not the project: the question stated precisely; why the usual dialectic, on both sides, leaves it open; the method, phase by phase, across the twelve weeks; what would count as a result and what would count as failure; and a realistic statement of scope. Please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I have no reliable library access during the fellowship and will add references afterwards.

Here is where I have got to. The familiar objections — that the world contains appalling suffering, and that for any candidate world there is a better one, since one can add a further happy creature or make an existing creature slightly happier — seem to me to rely on a picture of betterness as dimension-wise improvement: a rival world beats the actual one because it contains everything the actual one contains plus something good, or contains the same things in better condition. On that picture the ordering of possible worlds has no maximal element, and Leibniz's claim is not merely false but unsatisfiable.

But Leibniz's own machinery is not obviously committed to that picture. Possible things are compossible or not; a world is a maximally interconnected whole; goods trade off against one another. So he can hold what I'll call the saturation thesis: no possible world is an improvement on the actual one along some dimension without a compensating loss elsewhere. If saturation holds, the regress never gets started, and best need not mean *beats every rival on every dimension*; it can mean what an optimum means in a space with trade-offs.

My worry — and what I want the proposal to organise — is that saturation looks considerably stronger than anything that machinery delivers, and that pressing on it turns optimism into a different thesis from the one either side argues about. Three sub-questions I keep circling. First: does the interconnectedness of a world support saturation, or does it make things worse, by making whole worlds the only objects of comparison and hence making betterness a holistic relation, possibly incomplete or intransitive, to which *best* has no clear application? Second: is there a stable distinction between additive improvements — more creatures, more variety — and modificational ones — the same creatures better off — and does Leibniz have a reply to the first kind that does no work against the second? Third: if there is genuinely no maximum, does the damage stop at the claim that this world is the best, or does it spread to the argument from the principle of sufficient reason — given no best world, is there then no sufficient reason for creating this one rather than that, and can a non-maximising reading of the divine choice survive, on which God selects a world that is unsurpassed but not unsurpassable, for a reason that is sufficient without being optimising? If it can, what does it cost elsewhere in the system?
