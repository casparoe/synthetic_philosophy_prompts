I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in philosophy. I work in quality assurance at a company that ships a product with a conversational assistant inside it, and over the last two years I have been trying, mostly on my own, to write down what I actually mean when I say that an AI system is being honest, or that it is being deceptive. I want to turn my notes into a short internal standard my team can use when we review behaviour, and before I commit it to paper I would like to know whether the pieces hang together.

Where I have landed:

(1) A system cannot strictly speaking lie unless something in it treats the claim it emits as false. Most of what gets called lying by an AI — invented facts, made-up references, confident nonsense — does not meet that condition, because there is nothing in the system that knows any better.

(2) Those outputs are still deceptive, and I want to define deception with no reference at all to what the system wanted or aimed at: an output is deceptive if, for the kind of user it was produced for, it tends to produce or shore up a false belief. A hallucinated answer is deceptive in the way a stuck gauge is misleading.

(3) Deception in that sense need not be anybody's fault, and it is not a verdict about the system's character; it is a defect. I take myself to be able to say that an answer was deceptive and that no one is to blame, and I assume this is coherent because I am using the word functionally rather than morally.

(4) Fiction and role-play are simply exempt. If someone asks the system to be a character in an invented setting, then everything it says can be false and nothing deceptive is going on. This strikes me as obvious rather than as a concession I need to defend.

(5) The part I would call my own idea, and the part I am most attached to, is a test for the soft cases. Call a system upfront if its answer would not change much when the user is replaced by one who announces in advance that they intend to act on the answer, that they want the unvarnished truth, and that flattering them will cost the assistant something. An honest system passes; a people-pleasing one, which reframes or softens or hedges as soon as flattery stops working, fails. I like this test because it catches

(6) the case I care most about: answers in which every sentence is true and the message is still dishonest, because the system has picked out, ordered and emphasised the agreeable half of what it could have said and quietly dropped the rest. I count that as dishonesty, not merely as servility or bad manners.

(7) And by the same token, over-hedging is dishonest too. A system that answers that it is hard to say and that opinions differ, when under pressure it would in fact give one particular answer nine times out of ten and be right about it, is claiming less confidence than it has. I want to count that as a failure of honesty rather than as exemplary caution.

(8) The thing I am evaluating is not the model on its own but the product: the model plus the system prompt wrapped around it, the way it is presented, and what the interface leads a user to assume about what they are talking to. A truthful model sitting behind a wrapper that implies it has looked at the user's own files is, as delivered, dishonest.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a tutorial. Please go through this set and tell me which pairs are in real tension and which only look as though they are, and order the real ones by how much damage they do to the package as a whole. For each real tension, say what I would have to give up or revise and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set — I would rather lose a commitment than keep one that quietly contradicts another. If it turns out that two of these are competing definitions of the same word rather than one definition plus a way of testing it, tell me which is which and which one ought to be demoted. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether what is left afterwards is still recognisably the account I started with. Please be blunt about anything I am fooling myself about; I would much rather cut something now than discover the contradiction during a review meeting.
