Something I keep chewing on for my political theory seminar: in Norway and Finland the tax authority publishes what everyone earned and paid, and anyone can look up a neighbour, a colleague or a footballer and see the number. In the US we treat pay as almost pruriently private — plenty of firms tell employees not to discuss salaries, and a manager who posts the team's numbers has committed a social crime. Except that we also publish public employees' salaries, and public companies are legally required to disclose what they pay their top executives. So the transparency climbs the income scale instead of levelling it.

Help me get my students past "the Nordic list is just egalitarian theatre" (or just envy with a database). What does publishing it actually presuppose about success, desert and legibility? Is it a way of cutting the tall poppy down to size, or is it rather the precondition for an earned success being honoured instead of resented, since it's the only way the claim that someone earnt it can be checked — in which case the American Dream's celebration of success sits oddly with our secrecy?

Two things I want kept apart: what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits with (could you run the list with no levelling instinct at all, or no meritocratic one?), and whether it's underdetermined — several theories of envy, resentment, status and accountability licensing the same institution. And if we swapped the implicit theory for one on which envy is never a reason for anything, what would have to change: the list, who can see it, or only the story we tell about it?
