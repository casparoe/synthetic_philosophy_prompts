I'm in a loose reading group on classical Indian epistemology — we did the Nyāya material a couple weeks ago and it's been rattling around my head since. The thing I've come around to is this: that śabda (word-knowledge, what we'd lump under testimony) is a genuinely distinct pramāṇa, not inference in disguise, and that the Nyāya package around it beats the reductionism/anti-reductionism shouting match in contemporary analytic epistemology. The package as I got it: a pramāṇa is a source that produces pramā (true cognition), and there are four — perception, inference, upamāna (knowledge from similarity, like being told "a gavaya is an animal like a cow" and then later recognising one in the field), and śabda; a cognition from testimony counts when it comes from an āpta, a speaker who is competent and truthful about that thing; and the whole account is externalist and causal, so you don't need to know your source is reliable for it to give you knowledge, and validity is extrinsic (as I understood it, vs. the Mīmāṃsā-style intrinsic view). Two things make me like it. First, the reductionist — testimony is just inference from evidence that the speaker is reliable — seems to me to predict that small children can't learn much from what anyone says, since they have no track record on anyone; the Nyāya story doesn't predict that. Second, treating the pramāṇas as causal kinds makes questions about defeaters look like questions about the source misfiring rather than about the subject's reasons, which feels like the right shape to me. But I can't tell whether I've just picked up a view, or a view that's insulated against everything.

So: play it straight and tell me what would actually count against it. I'd rather you work with cases than with history, and I've cooked up a few that I think bite hardest. (a) The speaking archive: there is no speaker. Some mechanism — an oracle, a machine that emits sentences, whatever — produces well-formed claims in a language you understand, is extremely reliable about a domain, and has no understanding of its own output and no intention to convey anything. You hear one of its claims and come to believe it truly. If that's knowledge, is it śabda? If Nyāya says no, because there's no āpta who grasps the meaning, then what is it — inference from a mark, perception, nothing? And if they say yes, then śabda's individuation conditions don't include speaker-competence, which starts to sound like generic reliabilism plus an inference from "this sentence appeared," which is the reductionist's picture under another name. (b) The borrowed-credit case: someone is a genuine āpta in one domain and, unbeknownst to anyone, reliably right in another domain for a reason nobody could have tracked; you have independent evidence suggesting they're unreliable in that second domain, and they tell you something true. Does the hearer's positive defeater block śabda-knowledge? If yes, the view has quietly become internalist in a way its externalist self-description doesn't advertise; if no, it's committed to knowledge where everything you could go on says don't trust it. (c) The individuation worry: why stop at four? If upamāna can be carved off as its own source, why not a source for memory, or for knowledge from a single unmatched footprint, or for "I just know this street"? If the list is gerrymandered, then "śabda is irreducible" is a claim about a category someone made up.
