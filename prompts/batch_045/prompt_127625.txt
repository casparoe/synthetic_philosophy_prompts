Context: I run a monthly philosophy-and-film night for a mixed crowd (some philosophy undergrads, some just people who like movies) and next month's pick is Inception. I want to do the philosophy of mind angle rather than the usual one. Specifically, I want it read against the old fight about whether dreams are experiences at all: Malcolm's Wittgensteinian line (roughly, that "I dreamt that p" isn't a report of something that went on while you were asleep, because the criteria for ascribing experiences across sleep just aren't there — whatever content a dream report has, it gets from waking language-games and waking ways of checking), and Dennett's cassette-style view (roughly, that nothing experiential happens during sleep; the dream is composed at arousal out of memory fragments, so there may be no fact of the matter about what happened, in what order, or how long it lasted). Both views, in different ways, deny the naive premise that the film runs on: there you are in there, walking around, deciding things.

Here's what I'd like you to work through.

1. The obvious move is to say Inception isn't about dreaming at all — it's about a fictional technology (shared hallucination, linked REM, whatever you want to call it) and so it's no counterexample to anything. Is that dismissal right? And if it is, does the film stop being philosophically interesting, or does the question just move: what would the shared-screen version of the technology show about the ordinary case? I've been calling this the shared-screen objection and I want to know if it defuses the film or relocates it.

2. Time. The film's central trick is dilation — minutes stretching into hours, and then limbo, where Saito is supposed to have lived decades while hours pass upstairs. On a report-constituted view there's arguably no fact of the matter about dream duration. But the film needs there to be: it's why Saito comes out aged and why Cobb owes him anything. Where exactly does the plot require a fact that a Dennett-style view says isn't there — and, flipping it, does the film's own vagueness about how long limbo lasts (nobody can say) actually dramatize the anti-experience side better than the characters know?

3. Dream-Mal. She's a projection: built out of Cobb's memory and expectation, not someone he meets. But that's basically a construction story about what's in a dream, which is closer to the anti-experience camp than to the film's own premise. Look at the scenes where dream content visibly gets generated — the architect learning to build and fold a city, the way the target's subconscious shows up as armed security — and tell me whether the film's mechanics quietly undercut the thing the drama depends on, i.e. that Cobb is really spending time with his dead wife.

4. Mal, in the world, concludes that this world isn't real and acts on it. The film treats that as pathology. Does it thereby take a normative stance on the metaphysics — that refusing to grant reality to dream-life is a kind of madness? And does the film notice that if Malcolm or Dennett are right, Mal's confusion is less crazy than the movie thinks it is?

Be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration, and don't bend the philosophical positions to make them fit.
