I am trying to work out what I actually believe about which animals are conscious, and I would rather start from my reactions to particular cases than from a theory I would only be reciting. Here, then, are the verdicts I find myself giving, with the confidence I actually feel: a healthy, awake adult human, conscious; the same human in slow-wave sleep from which they report no dream, not conscious; the same human midway through a vivid, coherent REM dream, conscious — though this one already bothers me, since the dreamer is cut off from the environment, integrates poorly, and initiates nothing; a surgical patient at an anaesthetic depth at which they do not move to incision and later report nothing, not conscious; a patient who looks unresponsive at the bedside but who can steer their own brain activity in a scanner so that it tracks spoken instructions, conscious; a patient indistinguishable from that one at the bedside for whom no such steering can be elicited, where I withhold judgment entirely, since I do not think the evidence licenses probably not; chimpanzee, dog and crow, conscious; octopus, conscious but with noticeably less confidence than the dog; zebrafish, conscious, probably; a decapod crustacean that gives up a preferred dark shelter in order to avoid a place where it was shocked, but only when it is already stressed, which I lean toward calling not conscious, and where I suspect I am being inconsistent with the zebrafish, since the behavioural evidence has the same shape in both; honeybee, with flexible learning and with sleep-like states that show homeostatic rebound, not conscious; a nematode with a few hundred neurons, not conscious; and a stroke patient who withdraws from a pinprick, grimaces and shows the full autonomic response, but reports the sharpness of the prick with none of its unpleasantness, who is of course conscious but about whom I cannot say whether the prick hurts, and about whom I do not know what my own view implies.

There is also a case I keep circling: a bird raised from hatching with its two hemispheres surgically isolated from each other, so that each controls behaviour on alternate days with no transfer of information between them. My verdict slides: sometimes one animal living one discontinuous life, sometimes two subjects sharing a body and a history.

My own attempted principle is this. A system is conscious at a given time just in case, at that time, it constructs a single integrated, cross-modal, temporally extended model of its own body and surroundings that is simultaneously available to many distinct downstream systems at once — to memory, to action selection, to motivation — and, further, states of that model are for the system loaded with valence, so that some are to be kept and others avoided, and flexible trade-offs among them become possible rather than mere reflex. The first clause is meant to handle anaesthesia and dreamless sleep; the second to keep out thermostats and spinal preparations.

What I want from you is the reflective-equilibrium work. State one or two candidate principles precisely enough that I could apply them myself, and say which of my cases motivates each clause. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate and which you would overturn, giving the case that forces each concession; tell me what it says about the split-brained bird; and give me its verdicts on two cases I have not listed, namely the colony of a social insect species whose individual workers I have already judged not to be conscious, and an animal neurally organised exactly like my zebrafish but whose neurons have been replaced one at a time with functionally identical artificial units. Please write your reply as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
