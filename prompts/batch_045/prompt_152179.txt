"Nāgārjuna was an ancient Indian Buddhist philosopher and his big idea is emptiness. His main argument is the tetralemma: for any claim about anything there are four possibilities — it's true, it's false, it's both, or it's neither. Greek logic only allows the first two, but Nāgārjuna says reality is messier than that, and in the end all four of them fail, because everything is empty. Emptiness means that nothing has an existence of its own. Take a university: it's nothing but its students, its buildings and its paperwork, and if you take those away there's no university left, so it has no existence in its own right. And it's the same all the way down, with the students and the bricks as well. Which means that what he's really saying is that how things are depends on us — reality is mind-dependent, a bit like a dream or a hallucination, since nothing can be pinned down independently of how we carve it up. The upshot for practice is that the wise person gives up all beliefs and stops making claims about anything, and that's why Nāgārjuna says he has no thesis of his own: his words are medicine, not description, so they aren't the sort of thing that can be true or false. The standard objection — that 'everything is empty' is itself a claim, so the view refutes itself — misses the point, because he never asserted anything in the first place."
