I'm putting together the handout for the second session of a graduate seminar on confirmation theory, and I want the centerpiece to be a decision tree rather than another survey of schools. The session is on the problem of old evidence: a piece of evidence E is already known with certainty when a new hypothesis H is proposed, so conditionalizing on E cannot move the probability of H, yet everyone including the Bayesians wants to say that E supports H — the stock example being the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion and general relativity. I want students to be able to walk themselves from the initial commitment ("there is something here that needs explaining, and conditionalization on a certainty is not going to deliver it") to a named position, so please build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of labels: first the yes/no questions on which the responses genuinely diverge, then the positions located in the cells those questions open up. Each branch node should be a single claim a student can accept or reject without already knowing where it leads, phrased in the vocabulary of the problem itself — what the agent's credence function is defined over; whether confirmation is a two-place relation between sentences or something that depends on how the agent came to have the evidence; whether confirmation requires an actual change in credence or can consist in support that is simply already priced in; whether the agent is credited with knowledge of the logical and mathematical relations between her hypotheses and her evidence; whether the right comparison is with the credence she would have had without E, with a prior assigned at some earlier "ur" time, or with a prior that is treated as a counterfactual device rather than as a record of what she once believed.

The reason I don't want a canned survey is that I want the tree to actually sort the cases that come up in the seminar, so please build the branch questions around concrete variants rather than around slogans. Include at least these: the case where the agent knew E but did not know that H entails E and only later found the derivation; the case where the agent once knew E and has straightforwardly forgotten it; the case where the old evidence is a rough numerical coincidence and the new hypothesis retrodicts a much more precise value, so that what is new may be a sharpened proposition rather than a new one; the case where E cannot be formulated in the language available before H was introduced, because H supplies the vocabulary in which E is first statable; and the case where H was constructed partly by looking at E, so that the question of whether novel prediction matters more than accommodation is forced. That last one matters to me because I teach the predictivism material in the following session and I would like each leaf of this tree to carry a one-line verdict on whether, on that view, a theory's having been built to fit the evidence makes a difference to how much the evidence supports it.

Format-wise: seven branch nodes, eight to ten leaves, each leaf carrying a doctrinal name (say "unnamed" if you think nobody actually holds it), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and — only where you are confident — the name of someone who has defended it; I would rather have a hedge than a wrong attribution, and please don't pad it with references. Mark explicitly any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate instead of forcing a distinction between them, and for the combinations of answers that come out empty say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unoccupied so far. Close with a short note on which leaves survive the worry that a view has made the relation too easy to get: if the account lets an agent reach back to a hypothetical earlier prior, or add new sentences to the space, or redescribe old evidence as new whenever it is convenient, can she manufacture support for an arbitrary hypothesis, and which leaves can block that without collapsing back into the original problem?
