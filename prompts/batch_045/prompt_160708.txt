I'm in a grad reading group on Kant and his descendents (we're on the bit about transcendental arguments — the move from "our experience has this feature" to "so the world has to"). One guy in the group, Marek, has a line he trots out whenever things get hard: "the conditions of experience are the conditions of its objects." Sometimes he says it as "no object without a possible experience of it." Nobody ever says which version is the real one; it's just currency. Last week he used it to kill a point I was making about shade.

Shade is my example, so let me set it up. Imagine a finished physics that needs two kinds of basic thing for the equations to come out: call them grit and shade. Everything observable — every measurement, every instrument reading, anything any creature inside that physics could ever build or do — comes out exactly the same whether shade exists or not. But shade does real work in the theory: delete it and the predictions break. So: no possible experience, even in principle, tells you whether there's shade. I said that means the shade question is one we can never settle, but it's still a question, there's still something to be right or wrong about. Marek said no: experience is our only route to objects, so if something can't show up in experience at all, there's nothing for the claim to be about — the posit isn't unknowable, it's empty. And he said the slogan is what shows that.

I'm sure he's sliding between a few different claims and I can't get them apart cleanly, or tell which one (if any) actually gets him from "unknowable" to "empty." So: lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing, as a numbered menu I can bring to the next session. For each one, tell me (a) what sort of thesis it is — about what we can know, about what we can think or mean, or about what there is; (b) whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false / self-undermining; (c) exactly what it verdicts on shade — meaningless, false, or merely unknowable; and (d) how strong a reading of "experience" it needs: experience with our actual senses, experience available to any possible knower, or experience under any conceptual scheme at all.

Two more things I want dealt with. First, someone in the group half-remembered an objection along the lines of: either these arguments only tell you what you're committed to thinking, or they need an extra premise about what experience requires that you can't get for free. Is that just one of your readings in different words, or a separate problem that bites several of them? Second: whichever reading Marek picks, does it apply to itself — does the slogan's own claim that the conditions of experience are conditions of its objects have to be experienceable to be true, and does anything bad happen if it does?

End by telling me which reading Marek is most likely actually reaching for, and which one I should concede to him if I want to keep my "unknowable but not empty" line about shade.
