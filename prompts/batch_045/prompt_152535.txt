I sit on a small commission on electoral law reform, and I have been asked to write the short normative appendix to our report. Before I commit it to paper I would like my own position audited, because my worry is not that any one of these claims is indefensible but that I have been quietly using different principles in different chapters. I do not want a survey of the literature or a map of the debate, and I do not want to be told that reasonable people disagree; I want the argumentative structure of my own set. Please write in British English.

Here is where I have landed.

(1) The boundary of the franchise should be fixed by affected interests rather than by citizenship or by membership in a national community: roughly, anyone whose significant interests are foreseeably and non-trivially affected by a decision is entitled to a say in making it. I have argued against both the coercion test and the national self-determination test as criteria of inclusion.

(2) Consequently, lawful non-citizen residents should have the vote in national as well as local elections after a short period of residence — residence being only a proxy for affectedness, not a test of belonging — while citizens who have lived abroad for years, pay no tax here and have no intention of returning should lose it.

(3) The voting age should come down to sixteen, but no further. I oppose proxy votes cast by parents on behalf of younger children, I oppose any competence or civics test as a condition of adult voting, and I would leave eighteen as the age for jury service and for standing for election.

(4) Voting should be legally compulsory: a duty to attend and return a ballot, with a formal none-of-the-above option and a modest fine for non-attendance. The secret ballot together with that option is, I take it, what answers the objection that compulsion coerces political expression.

(5) Two grounds for (4). First, that abstention is a collective-action problem — no individual vote makes a difference, but low and socially skewed turnout biases policy against the worse-off — so compulsion is a legal remedy for a coordination failure and improves the representativeness of outcomes. Second, more broadly, that those who live under and benefit from a legal order owe it their participation.

(6) The franchise is a right that individuals hold against the state, which they are free to exercise or refrain from exercising; that is part of why disenfranchisement — of serving prisoners, for instance — strikes me as a grave wrong.

What I want from you: which of these genuinely pull against which, ordered by how severe the strain is, and for each tension what would have to give, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the set. Please also say explicitly which of the apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why, rather than flagging everything as a problem. If the package survives intact, say so — and then tell me where it is weakest regardless.
