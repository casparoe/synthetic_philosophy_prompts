I work as an independent pest-management adviser. I walk about forty blocks of cropping a season, and perhaps two dozen times a year I am the person who puts a recommendation in writing — intervene, delay, or hold — usually within a day, for a grower who needs an answer before the weekend and, on the certified blocks, for an auditor who has to be able to defend the decision on paper months later.

Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether a single aphid spray is a mass atrocity against animals that may well feel pain or a moral rounding error, and I came away less confident of the answer than of one thing: my own verdict changes depending on which number I happen to look at first — the death toll implied by the treatment, or the fact that in most of these populations nearly every individual that will ever exist dies slowly, of crowding, disease or predation, whether I act or not. So I don't want a verdict on spraying. I want a procedure I can run identically each time, that reduces how much rides on which number I saw first.

What I need is an ordered set of questions, each with what would count as a good enough answer to move to the next, where the procedure returns a hard no, and — most importantly — where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to be explicit about the baseline, and it must never let the number of animals the treatment kills stand in for the difference the treatment makes to what would otherwise have happened, since those come apart in both directions: killed individuals are often replaced by others who would never otherwise have existed, and a treatment that spares the pest now can leave a larger population to die later, or remove the predators and parasitoids that were holding it down and so produce a secondary outbreak and a second, heavier intervention. It also has to say what to do with the quantities nobody can measure — how much a day of an insect's distress counts for, whether a typical life of the pest in question is on balance worth living, whether replaced individuals count at all, and whether the relevant baseline is everything that will happen if I do nothing or only what I bring about. My inclination is to test whether the ranking of the options survives the whole plausible range of each and to escalate rather than choose whenever the ranking flips, but I'd like that made precise, and I'd like to be told plainly which steps are judgement calls in the costume of procedure.

Then run it on three real-shaped cases: greenhouse aphids, where the options are a fast knockdown, a parasitoid release that kills slowly over days, and waiting for the natural enemies and the overshoot-and-crash that follows; codling moth in an orchard already under mating disruption, where the intervention mostly prevents animals from being conceived rather than killing any; and a low-value cosmetic pest sitting below its economic threshold on a block near harvest. Don't spare me on that last one: if the procedure's honest output is that I should recommend a spray with no economic justification, or stand aside while a population starves, I want to see that rather than a version of the procedure rigged to agree with the thresholds I've spent my career defending. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot tell me, and what I should do on the days it goes quiet.
