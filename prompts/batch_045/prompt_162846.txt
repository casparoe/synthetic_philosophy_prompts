Bit of a favour. I'm rewriting the dispositions section of my third-year metaphysics option over the summer — the bit where I do finks and masks and then watch everyone shrug — and it's dawned on me that I never actually say what a theory of dispositions is supposed to *do* before I start knocking holes in it. So I want the spec first this time.

Purpose matters, so let me pin it down: I want an account of what it is for a thing to have a disposition that would be usable outside modal metaphysics. Something a philosopher of chemistry or biology, or someone asking what an engineered system can do, could pick up and use to say what a system can do, how strongly, and in virtue of what — without first having to settle whether the fundamental properties are all powers. It doesn't need to be a reduction that would satisfy a Humean. It does need to earn its keep in the special sciences.

So: give me the desiderata. For each one, the case or consideration behind it; whether it's non-negotiable given that purpose, merely negotiable, or just a prejudice of a particular camp (say whose); and which candidate it rules out.

Please don't just march the usual fink, mask and antidote trio past me again. Work through the cases I've been collecting, most of which nobody seems to discuss:

- the one-shot fuse: a component disposed to blow under overload, whose blowing destroys the very disposition to blow. Does manifestation have to preserve the disposition?
- the probabilistic fink: the interfering mechanism only kicks in most of the time, so the thing manifests on some occasions and not others under identical stimulus. Does that make the disposition partial, indeterminate, or graded?
- the mimicking device: a rig that shatters a pane whenever it's struck, by a mechanism entirely unlike fragility. Behaviour that looks exactly like a manifestation, no disposition there at all.
- the unstimulated shatterer: a thing that breaks with no stimulus whatsoever. Must every disposition have a characteristic stimulus?
- the disjunctive stimulus: a sample that melts if heated *or* if compressed, with nothing interestingly common to the two routes.
- solubility: a solute that is soluble only relative to a solvent that isn't part of it. Are dispositions intrinsic, and can pluralities have them?

Adjudicate these for me rather than assuming: is intrinsicness a genuine requirement or a prejudice of people who like their properties portable? Can we insist both on a non-circular account (nothing dispositional in the analysans) *and* on handling finks and masks, or does the standard normality rider smuggle dispositionality back in and make that pair unsatisfiable? Do we need degrees, and can a theory that demands a complete fundamental basis deliver them? Which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates?

If the honest answer is that the full list can't be met by anything, say so — that's a result I can teach.

Format: I'll be reading this on a train, so keep it tight. Main deliverable is a table with exactly these columns: Requirement | Motivating case (one sentence) | Status (non-negotiable / negotiable / camp prejudice — and whose) | What it rules out | Notes. Then a second short table: Requirement A | Requirement B | Why they can't both hold | Which one I should give up. After that, no more than three short paragraphs scoring the three families I usually teach — the conditional analysis with normality riders, the view that a disposition is (or is grounded in) its causal basis, and the everything-is-powers view — and don't let the scoring take over, the list is the point. Finish with a JSON object with these fields: most_excluding_requirement, most_expensive_prejudice, list_is_jointly_satisfiable (true or false), one_thing_id_drop.
