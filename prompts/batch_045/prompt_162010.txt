I am a high-school senior doing an independent-study paper on moral responsibility, and I have managed to get stuck inside an argument of my own construction. I began from a summary of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument — the one that starts from the thought that you do what you do because of the way you are, and ends by denying that anyone is ever truly morally responsible — and I tried to restate it with each principle written out separately, so I could see which one is carrying the weight. What came out proves considerably more than I take Strawson himself to claim, and I cannot locate the place to cut. Here it is.

(1) Dependence. Every action is explained, at the moment it happens, by the way the agent then is: their desires, values, habits of attention, temperament, and beliefs about what they have reason to do. Had the agent been relevantly different in those respects, the action would have been different.

(2) Transfer. You are responsible for an action only if you are responsible for the way you are that explains it. The thought behind it is that responsibility travels along explanation: if you are on the hook for what came out of you, you are on the hook for what produced it.

(3) The only stopping points. You can be responsible for the way you are only by having deliberately made yourself that way at some earlier time, working from the way you then were. And that earlier self-making has to satisfy (2) as well, so you must also be responsible for the way you were when you made yourself, and so on.

(4) No self-origination. Any deliberate self-making is done by someone who already has some character, and the character you start from is not one you chose. A chain with no first member cannot be completed by a being with a finite history, which is what we are. So the requirement stated in (3) is never met.

(5) Therefore no one is ever responsible for being the way they are when they act.

(6) By (1), (2), and (5), no one is ever responsible for anything they do.

(7) Desert rides on responsibility: if you are not responsible for something, you do not deserve credit or blame for it. You might still be trained, deterred, or restrained, but praise and censure in the sense that implies desert are out.

(8) So no one ever deserves anything for anything.

Step (8) is where I stop believing myself. The problem is not that it removes blame but that it removes credit too, including credit for the care with which someone reasons. That seems to make the whole discussion eat itself: if the argument is sound, then no one deserves credit for having thought it through correctly, and no one deserves blame for having botched it.

There is a second thing that bothers me, pointing the other way. Transfer looks as if it should inflate responsibility as well as destroy it. If being responsible for what I do requires being responsible for the character behind it, then anyone who is responsible for anything at all is responsible for a great deal: for the dispositions behind the action, for the habits that shaped those dispositions, for the upbringing that shaped the habits, and eventually for facts — being born at all, other people's decisions, the weather — that nobody thinks are up to anybody. I have never seen anyone accept that.

The only escape I can see is what I have been calling acquisition by endorsement: even if you never originated a trait, you might come to be responsible for it later, by noticing it, judging it, and then keeping it. Think of someone who knows they are quick to anger, decides they do not like that about themselves, does nothing about it for years, and goes on being quick to anger. My teacher says this only restarts the regress, because the noticing, the deciding, and the not-acting all flow from the character you already had. I am not convinced that settles it. If each endorsement is genuine and the trait is sustained over time by a long chain of them, why should the chain count for nothing? And if the reply is that the chain has no first member, then (4) is doing all the work — and (4) is only an argument about finite beings, so a being with no beginning, who had always been revising itself, would come out responsible after all. That is the wrong result in the opposite direction, which makes me think the mistake is earlier, in (2) or (3).

So: which step do you reject? I would rather keep (1) and (4) than accept (8). Please tell me why the step you reject looked fine when I wrote it down, what giving it up costs, and — if you think the trouble is in Transfer — how Transfer would have to be stated so that it neither cancels all responsibility nor inflates it into responsibility for the weather.
