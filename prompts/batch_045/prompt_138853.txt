im in a early christian / medieval ethics seminar and we just did a week on Augustine on lying, and for my response paper i want to run a real case through him instead of one of his own examples, so im doing the Tuskegee syphilis study (1932-72, US Public Health Service and then CDC, ~600 Black sharecroppers in Macon County Alabama, about 400 with latent syphilis and ~200 controls, recruited with promisses of free medical care and burial money, told they were being treated for "bad blood", lumbar punctures and placebos passed off as treatment, penicillin standard care by the late 40s and deliberately withheld, ended only when it leaked to the press in 1972, and the thing people cite as the reason we now have the Belmont report and IRBs). I know that history so dont spend the paper on it — what I need is the analysis through Augustine.

Roughly what I take his view to be, correct me where im off: a lie is a false signification by words (or by signs generally), and the thing that makes it a lie is the will to deceive rather than the falsity of the sentence on its own; every lie is a sin, no exceptions, but sins come in grades — he's got that eightfold taxonomy that runs from lies in religious teaching at the top down to lies that hurt nobody and save a life or protect someone's body at the bottom, and he himself thinks you may never lie even to keep someone from being murdered. He also (or Aquinas after him) leans on the etymology of mendacium as speaking against your own mind.

what I want:

1. The mapping, done carefully. Who is the liar here in Augustine's sense? What exactly is the false signification, and whose will to deceive does it have to be — the surgeon general's office, the individual doctor on the day, the nurse who did the recruiting and follow-up (I think her name was Rivers), the men who signed up? Where does "we are treating your bad blood" land in the eight kinds, and what grade of guilt does that give it, given that the same deception also kept people from treatment and got wives and children infected? Does it stay a lie if some of the people uttering it half-believed the framing themselves, or if some of the men suspected something?

2. What the theory genuinely explains about the case. My sense is that Augustine gives you something the usual utilitarian write-up doesn't: the idea that speech is a shared good given for making minds known, so that the wrong is done through the abuse of a trusting practice rather than through force or through the outcome. Say whether that actually does work here or whether im reading it in.

3. Where it breaks, with two variants I made up. (a) The silent version: nobody ever says a false sentence — they just never tell the men the name of their disease, never mention penicillin, and rely on the men having no other access to a doctor. Does Augustine's machinery call either of these a lie? 4. The scale problem. This ran 40 years through an institution, with staff rotating through and paperwork filed in journals. An account built around one speaker uttering one false signification with one will to deceive — how does it even get a grip on that, and is the grip it gets the right one?

Does the episode end up confirming Augustine (e.g. because the damage it did to trust in medicine is exactly the damage an abuse of speech does) or is it a case his framework kind of never anticipated (because the wrong-making feature looks like it's about consent, autonomy, or using people as means, and not about falsity at all)?
