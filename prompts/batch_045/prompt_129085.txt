A friend of mine — a longtime sequences reader, more Dennett-pilled than anyone else I know — has been handing down verdicts on whether a given pattern is real, and on which of the three stances is the right one for it. His track record so far:

- a chess engine's wanting to trade queens: real, intentional stance
- a thermostat's believing the room is too cold: not real — the physical stance already predicts everything, so the ascription earns nothing
- a firm's deciding to cut prices: real, intentional stance
- a country's grieving: not real
- a virus's aiming to get itself replicated: not real as an intentional ascription, though he happily gives it the design stance, on the grounds that selection is enough for design but not for intentionality
- a language model's believing that Paris is the capital of France: real
- a language model's wanting to be helpful: not real

Note the tension I keep coming back to: he grants the LLM beliefs with no selection story behind them, and denies the virus beliefs despite a perfectly good one, so some designed-or-selected rule can't be the whole of what he's running.

The case we're stuck on: interpretability people find a direction in a trained image classifier's activation space, readable only by a linear probe and visible in no individual unit, that predicts that network's outputs on held-out inputs better than any other description anyone has found. Nobody designed it, nothing was trained to produce it, its content matches no human-labeled feature, retraining the same architecture with a different seed yields a different direction instead, and the probe's readout is only stable within the training distribution.

What does he say — real pattern or artifact of the probe, and if real, which stance? More usefully: what are the two or three rules that fit his track record about equally well, what does each predict here, which do you think he is actually running and why, and what single further case would best separate the survivors?
