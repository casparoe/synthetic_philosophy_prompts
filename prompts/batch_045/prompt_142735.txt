My film club did Manchester by the Sea a couple weeks ago and I haven't been able to stop picking at it. We'd had a few glasses of wine, someone said "it's not really his fault, it was an accident," someone else said "yeah but he was wasted and he left the fire going," and a third person said "that's literally moral luck." I'd never heard the term, so I went home and read a few encyclopaedia-type writeups, and now I'm more confused than when I started. I get the basic shape: one philosopher uses the example of a truck driver who hits a child he couldn't possibly have seen, and the point is supposed to be that we can't keep moral judgement completely insulated from luck the way we'd like to. But I don't want a reading that just slaps a label on Lee and stops.

Here's what actually bugs me. Every example in that literature seems to be about someone who did nothing wrong at all — a blameless driver, a guy who gets handed circumstances he didn't choose. Lee isn't that. He did something careless that on a hundred other nights would have cost him nothing, and then something went wrong in a way nobody could have predicted and it cost him everything. So is the film even a moral luck movie, or is it a different animal — small everyday negligence plus catastrophic outcome — where the real puzzle is that his guilt scales with the outcome but nobody else's blame does? Because that's the thing I keep coming back to: the town doesn't blame him. They're unfailingly kind to him, and the kindness seems to be its own kind of torture. And then there's the scene on the street with his ex-wife, where she stops him and more or less begs him to let her take back the horrible things she said to him, and he can't accept it. She hands him something and it doesn't reach him. I don't think the truck-driver framework has a word for that failure, and it feels like the real subject of the film.

So read the movie through this stuff for me, seriously in both directions: where does the moral luck idea actually illuminate Lee's choices and the scenes around him, and where does the film resist it — is the movie maybe saying the operative luck isn't the fire at all, but whether you end up surrounded by people who can carry it with you (his brother could, and then his brother died too, which feels like yet another kind of luck that the standard list of types doesn't have a slot for — I've started calling it "audience luck," tell me if that's a real thing with a proper name or if I'm just reinventing the wheel). And please don't send me to papers or drop citations: I can't look anything up and I've got no way of telling whether a title you mention is real, so just argue it out from the film and the ideas themselves. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — is this a moral luck movie or isn't it — then give me the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
