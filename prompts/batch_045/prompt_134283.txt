I have been turning over the question of when we actually have to do what the law says, and I have built a case that I think isolates the part I am stuck on.

Imagine a country that is, by ordinary standards, a decent and nearly just democracy: regular free elections, independent courts, no systematic rights violations. It also has one unusual institution, a public exception registry. Any adult may file a written, signed exception to any single statute, free of charge, renewed once a year. Filing removes the criminal penalty you would otherwise face under that statute, but it does not shield you from civil liability to anyone you harm, and it does not touch any other law. About 4 percent of adults have an active exception on file, and surveys suggest only around half the population knows the registry exists.

Now Dara. She thinks Statute X is unjust: it makes it a crime to repair a neighbour's household appliance for free without a licence, and in practice it mostly burdens people who cannot afford a licensed repairer. Dara knows about the registry and has never filed. One afternoon she repairs her neighbour's stove for free, in full view, after telling the local paper; she does not resist arrest and pays the fine. She says she broke the law instead of filing because filing would have made the protest invisible, and making the injustice visible was the whole point.

My credence that Dara had a moral obligation to comply with Statute X, that is, that she acted wrongly in deliberately breaking it, is about 0.3. A friend I described the case to puts it at 0.7.

Give me your own number, but mainly the decomposition: whether there is any general duty to obey the law in a near-just society in the first place; whether a free and easy legal exit makes that duty stronger or weaker, and specifically whether not filing amounts to consent, and whether it matters that half the population has never heard of the exit; whether considerations about fairness or free-riding do any work when the benefits of the scheme come to you whether or not you comply; whether the conditions people usually list for justified civil disobedience are met here, such as just cause, last resort, acting openly, and accepting the punishment, and whether having a legal alternative available defeats the last-resort condition; and anything else you think the number turns on. Attach a rough probability and a line of justification to each term, show how they combine into your headline number, say which term the number is most sensitive to and what change in the case or what argument would move it most, and then tell me which of these terms my friend and I are most likely disagreeing about.
