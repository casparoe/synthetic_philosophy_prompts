My partner and I have gone city → suburbs → back to the city in about three years, each move felt completely right when we made it, and I finally added up the removals, the overlap rent, the repainting and it is definately painful money. She says we were irrational in a way we could have avoided by adopting a rule and sticking to it. I say each move was the right call given how we felt at the time, and that no such rule would have survived contact with us anyway. I want a thought experiment to settle who is closer to right, but I want it built to spec rather than the standard textbook thing.

What I want is a case of diachronic exploitation that does not secretly rest on intransitivity. Specs:

- Realistic and small: one household-level decision (moving, a renovation, a car, a custody schedule, whatever), one paragraph of setup max. No demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic psychology. You may help yourself to exactly two ingredients: preferences that shift depending on what you currently have or have recently had, and people who can partly foresee that shift. Nothing else weird.
- At every single moment, the agent's preferences over the options must be complete and transitive. If your case works only because at some time A beats B beats C beats A, it fails the brief — I've seen that pump and it's not the one I need. Everything bad has to come from change over time.
- Every individual transaction must be one the agent, at the moment of choosing, genuinely takes to be an improvement, and would still take knowing everything: the whole future sequence of offers, their own future preference changes, and the fact that the other party profits. No threats, no deception, no hidden costs. The other party can be entirely benign — an agent, a contractor, a landlord who simply offers things that are good for them and, at that moment, good for you.
- The agent should be fully sophisticated: they foresee the path correctly. And still end up strictly worse than if the other party had never turned up — same or worse final situation, minus money a normal household would notice. There can be no precommitment device, no binding contract, no resolute chooser who just sticks to a plan, and I want the reason those are unavailable to be an ordinary realistic one, not a stipulation in the setup.
- Bonus, and this is the bit I'm most curious about: I'd like foresight to be part of the problem rather than the cure. Ideally the naive version of the same agent — same dynamics, no foresight — comes out no better than the sophisticated one, maybe even worse. If you think that direction can't be made to work, build the best case you can where foresight merely buys nothing, and tell me exactly what blocks the stronger version.
- At most three transactions, and state the total loss in actual numbers.

After the scenario I want the demonstration, not just the story:

1. Lay out the preference profile at each time and show it is complete and transitive, and say where the change comes from.
2. Walk the path of choices, node by node, and compute the loss.
3. Tell me which structural feature is doing the work — the adaptation, the status-quo dependence, the inability to commit, the fact that offers arrive one at a time rather than as a package — and show what happens when you delete it. Specifically: what if all the offers were presented up front as one take-it-or-leave-it package; what if a binding agreement were available; what if preferences were stable.
4. Say which diachronic principle, if any, the case is really evidence for — something about the shape your preference changes are allowed to have, or about being able to commit, or about evaluating whole plans rather than moments — and be honest about whether the case generates that principle or just restates it.
5. Give me the strongest version of the "nothing irrational happened here, each trade was a genuine improvement, you just changed your mind and it cost you" reply, and say what it turns on.
6. And then bring it back to us: given the case, would a rule like hers actually have helped, and does anything in the verdict depend on treating a two-person household as one persisting agent rather than two people whose weight in the decision shifts over time? That last question is the one I actually care about, so don't skip it.
