Hi — I could use a hand drafting something I can put in front of my committee on Thursday.

Some background on me: I spent thirty years as a groundwater geochemist, retired now, and for the past couple of years I've been sitting in on an evening philosophy seminar at the local university (auditing, though they let me talk too much). Which is how I became the person on our society's public statements committee who gets asked what "know" means. I'd rather not name the society — a water-quality professional association, about four thousand members.

The case, short version. Six years ago we put out a one-page fact sheet for county fairs and high-school teachers. It was about a town whose wells had turned up contaminated, and one line in it said that we know the contamination came from the chemical plant's wastewater lagoon upstream. As far as anyone cared at the time it was fine: two independent lines of evidence — a tracer test and the isotopic fingerprint of the solvent — point the same way, one downgradient monitoring well is ambiguous, and the practical upshot was just "test your well and don't give infants the tap water." Nobody was suing anybody.

Now that one sentence has been quoted verbatim in a state enforcement proceeding. The state wants the plant to pay for a fourteen-million-dollar treatment system; the company says the plume is really from an old landfill across the valley and that three hundred jobs ride on the order. Their lawyers have already told the press that our own society doesn't claim to know that anymore, and we've recieved letters from both sides asking us to either say the sentence again or take it back.

The committee is split, and I think the split is philosophical even though only one member would put it that way. Roughly:

- Some say the sentence was fine then and would be dishonest now, because (as one of them put it) how good your evidence has to be before it counts as knowledge depends on how much is riding on being right. I've seen this called pragmatic encroachment, I think.

- Others say nothing changed in the world or in the evidence; only the conversation changed. The word sets the bar higher or lower depending on the context it's used in, so the old sentence was true when it was written and saying it again now would be saying something stronger, and maybe false. I gather that's the contextualist idea, but I may be mixing the two up.

- And a third group says this is word games: either the evidence supports the claim or it doesn't, stakes only bear on what anyone is entitled to do about it, and we should just reaffirm the sentence and add a separate paragraph about what we think ought to happen.

There's one more wrinkle nobody has an answer to, and it's the one I most want you to deal with. Whatever we say now will be read at the same time by (a) the hearing officer deciding a fourteen-million-dollar order and three hundred jobs, and (b) about nine hundred households deciding whether to spend two hundred dollars on a point-of-use filter or keep buying bottled water. One sentence, two audiences, wildly different stakes. Which of them gets to set the standard for the word? Our bylaws say the society speaks only where it has knowledge, which is no help, since they never say knowledge of what standard.

So here's what I need. Draft the decision memo I'll circulate — somewhere around eight hundred words, addressed to the committee. It should recommend one concrete course of action, with a timeline for getting it done before the hearing in three weeks: reaffirm the sentence, withdraw it, replace it with a hedged one, adopt a standing rule about how we use knowledge language from now on, whatever you actually think is right. It should be something we could vote on, with reasons our own members could argue against rather than bare assertions. Please give the losing side its best shot — I want the paragraph that the people who disagree with you would point to as the strongest thing on their side, stated as strongly as you can honestly state it.

Two smaller things. First, our chair wants every future statement to carry an explicit numeric probability instead of any knowledge word; a couple of us think that's honest and the rest think it will sound evasive and will definately be used against us the moment it gets quoted. Tell us which way to go on that. Second, if I've got those two philosophical positions muddled, or I'm describing a distinction that doesn't really exist, say so briefly in the memo instead of politely going along with my version of it.

Last thing, and it's the bit my seminar friends would insist on: end with a short "what would change our mind" section — the argument or consideration that would lead the committee to the opposite recommendation, and where our reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
