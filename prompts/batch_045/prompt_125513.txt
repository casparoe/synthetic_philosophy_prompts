I recently sat in on a reading group on Solomonoff induction in which two participants argued for the better part of an hour without either of them conceding anything. I was the only non-specialist in the room and genuinely cannot tell who had the better of it, so I am passing the exchange on — names changed, notation tidied, nothing of substance altered — and asking you to adjudicate it.

Both parties accept the following. Fix a universal prefix machine U; let ℓ(p) be the length of program p; and let the Solomonoff prior be M(x) = Σ 2^(−ℓ(p)), the sum over the self-delimiting programs p that output x, so that a shortest such program contributes on the order of 2^(−K(x)) with K Kolmogorov complexity. They also accept the invariance theorem (replacing U by another universal machine changes K(x) by at most an additive constant that does not depend on x) and Solomonoff's convergence result (for any computable data-generating measure μ, prediction by M converges to prediction by μ, with cumulative error exceeding that of the best computable predictor only by an amount bounded in terms of K(μ)).

(1) Rowan: Solomonoff induction is the closest thing we have to a proof of Occam's razor. The prior charges each description a fixed multiplicative penalty per bit, so simpler hypotheses are more probable; and the convergence result upgrades that from a methodological maxim to a theorem — betting in proportion to M does, in the limit, at least as well as betting on any computable hypothesis you like, up to a fixed penalty depending only on how complex that hypothesis is. The razor is no longer a prejudice; it is optimal inference.

(2) Tobias: Two problems. First, K is defined only up to an additive constant, and hence M only up to a multiplicative one; and that constant is not small in any practical sense, since switching machines can shift K(x) by an amount on the order of the complexity of one machine relative to the other. So for any finite body of evidence and any hypothesis you favour, there is a universal machine with respect to which that hypothesis is the shortest description of the data: call this razor-shopping. The razor is then either empty before the limit, which is where people actually want it, or idle in the limit, where every universal machine converges to the same predictions and simplicity is doing no work. Second, and worse, M does not cut anything. It is a weighted average over every computable hypothesis, infinitely many of them, most of them grotesquely complicated. Occam's razor tells you which hypothesis to adopt and which to discard; a mixture over all of them, with weights that merely decay, is a hedge, not a razor.

(3) Rowan: On the constant: this worry is entirely general. Any two Bayesian priors that are mutually absolutely continuous differ by a density ratio, and every prior is sensitive to some choice at finite sample size; what is machine-invariant about M is the structural claim that each additional bit of description costs a fixed factor. Razor-shopping is prior-gerrymandering, and we have independent reasons not to gerrymander; that a tool can be abused does not make it vacuous, or every statistical method is vacuous. On the hedging point: the hedge is not neutral — the shortest compatible descriptions carry the most weight, and if you insist on a single hypothesis, the natural one to read off is the minimal-length description of the data, which is the minimum-description-length principle, now with a theorem behind it. And the convergence result is not merely predictive: the posterior concentrates on programs that agree with the true source, and that is convergence on the truth.

(4) Tobias: That last step conflates two things. The posterior concentrates on programs observationally indistinguishable from the source, not on the true mechanism; there are always infinitely many such programs, differing in every respect you might call ontological. So even granting convergence, what is vindicated is a predictive Occam — bet as if the world were simple — not the Occam scientists actually invoke when they reject a theory for positing too many unobservable entities. And the guarantee is close to circular anyway: it says that betting on simplicity works provided the world is cheaply describable. If the source has large K, the bound is enormous and the theorem tells you nothing, so the content boils down to: if simplicity pays, simplicity pays. Meanwhile, your MDL readout quietly abandons the framework. Solomonoff's theorem is a theorem about the mixture; acting as if the single shortest program were true is a different and notably brittle policy, and no comparable dominance result holds for it.

(5) Rowan: The circularity charge is unfair. What is non-trivial is that you need not know in advance which simple program, or how simple: one prior, specified once, dominates every computable source, each with its own penalty. That is a real dominance result and not a restatement of its own assumption. And as for the ontological razor: where two hypotheses are empirically equivalent without limit, it is hard to see what fact of the matter is left for a razor to track, so a simplicity ordering is exactly the tie-breaker one wants — and M supplies a privileged one, machine-independent up to a constant and provably optimal.

(6) Tobias: Here is a case where description length and simplicity come apart, and I think it is fatal. Take two programs that fit the same data. The first is two thousand bits: an explicit generative model, a few equations, a table of measured constants, clear structure. The second is sixty bits: given the data so far, search a space of astronomically many candidate extrapolators and output the prediction of whichever one has fitted the past best. M gives the second exponentially more weight per program, because it is shorter. But the second is not a simple hypothesis in any sense the razor cares about: it is a machine for searching an enormous, flexible hypothesis class, which is precisely the vice, excess flexibility, that the razor exists to punish. Short description, wildly non-simple hypothesis. And the counting does not rescue you: there are 2^ℓ programs of length ℓ, which exactly cancels the 2^(−ℓ) penalty, so prior mass per description-length level is roughly flat. The preference for simplicity holds only program against program, never length level against length level.

(7) Rowan: Your counting is wrong in a way that matters. Of the 2^ℓ programs of length ℓ, only about 2^(ℓ−K(x)) output a given x at all; the remainder contribute nothing to M(x). So the contribution of level ℓ is on the order of 2^(−K(x)), with a tail that decays, and M(x) comes out on the order of 2^(−K(x)) after all — the simplicity preference survives in the aggregate, which is all the vindication claim needs. And the search program is not a counterexample to anything I claimed. That the mixture contains flexible search strategies is a feature, not a bug: the dominance result concerns a weighted ensemble, not a single hypothesis you are supposed to believe, and a short program that searches well may well deserve its weight. What your example shows is that Solomonoff's prior is not a ranking of hypotheses by intuitiveness, which I never denied.
