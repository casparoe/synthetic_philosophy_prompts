Write a satirical document: the full terms and conditions of a loyalty programme for moral agents, in which members earn status tiers by performing acts that are morally good but not morally required. Call the scheme whatever a rewards consultancy would call it; give it a short piece of marketing copy at the top, a numbered set of clauses in the flat, exhaustive register of airline or credit-card fine print, a small points table of sample acts with the credit awarded in each case, and a definitions section at the bottom. About 800 words.

The comedy has to come from the philosophy being right rather than from invented absurdity. The category the programme administers is a real one in moral theory — conduct that it is praiseworthy to perform, not blameworthy to omit, and in some sense beyond what duty demands — and every clause should be a recognisable rendering of a problem that the category genuinely generates. Work in at least the following, each as a clause or sub-clause, and never as a named doctrine:

1. The asymmetry at the core of the category: qualifying acts are those it would be praiseworthy to perform and not blameworthy to omit. Make the asymmetry do administrative work — separate ledgers for praise and blame, no conversion rate between them, no penalty attaching to a zero balance in either.
2. The paradox that the scheme's own marketing cannot avoid: the highest tier must be described, in the programme's words, as the morally best available conduct for members in good standing, and must at the same time remain strictly voluntary, with no member ever to be blamed, downgraded, or reproached for declining it. Resolve the tension, if at all, the way corporate boilerplate does: assert both, cross-refer, move on.
3. The dependence of the whole scheme on where duty stops. The schedule that fixes which acts earn nothing at all should visibly be the load-bearing object in the document, and the programme should have a stated procedure for revising it. Build in the ratchet: once a large share of the membership performs some act, it is reclassified as baseline duty and ceases to earn credit, with the consequence allowed to appear, unremarked, in the small print that a growing and improving membership steadily converts today's sainthood into tomorrow's minimum.
4. Net-of-cost accounting: credit is calculated after deducting the cost or risk to the member, so that the same act earns less from a member to whom it is cheap. Let the table produce the consequence that the wealthiest members reach the upper tiers at negligible real cost while the poorest cannot, and let the programme defend the arrangement in its own voice, as though fairness were obviously served.
5. A forfeiture rule for acts performed from self-interested or status-seeking motives, together with the programme's candid admission that it has no reliable means of auditing motives, and a sub-clause for the mixed case in which the member's reasons were partly creditable and partly not.
6. The awkward counterparts of the category: a tier of conduct that is permissible yet merits a negative response and earns negative credit, which nevertheless cannot be recorded as blame or set against a praise debt; and rules for the cases where a member performed the act in ignorance of its being required, or where someone in authority commanded it and thereby converted it into a duty.
7. Aggregation: where a harm would have been averted only by many members acting together, and each was free not to act, the scheme must still credit each member in full — and must say, deadpan, what it then makes of the harm that occurred.

Constraints on the voice. Deadpan and single-minded throughout: the programme never notices that it is trading in moral categories, and handles saints, duties, praise and blame as administrative items like any other, with the same bland confidence it brings to baggage allowances. Do not wink at the reader; do not name any philosopher, theory, or text; do not include a single line that explains the joke; do not resolve any of the difficulties above by argument, only by clause. Close on a final reservation of rights under which the programme may reclassify any act at any time, including retroactively, without notice.

The target is the category itself and the comfort it offers agents, not any person who holds that there is such a category. A reader with no prior acquaintance with the term should enjoy the document simply as corporate satire; a reader who works on the topic should be able to reconstruct from the clauses alone which commitment each one is squeezing, and should finish with the sense that the joke was fair.
