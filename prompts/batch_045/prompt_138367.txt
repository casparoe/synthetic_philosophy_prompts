I'm a political theory PhD student working on a chapter about how defenses of commercial society end up moralizing self-interest, and I keep circling back to Rand, mostly because I can't tell how much of what people say about her is actually her. I picked up one of those big popular intellectual-history books at a used sale and read the chapter on her over coffee, and here's roughly what it says about her critique of altruism (I'm typing this from memory so it's probably a bit smoothed out):

Rand borrowed the word from Comte and defined altruism as the doctrine that the individual exists for the sake of others and that self-sacrifice is the highest virtue. Her central argument is that the doctrine self-destructs: if the moral purpose of every life is to serve other lives, then no one's life is an end in itself, so there's nothing left for morality to be *for* — everybody is simultaneously a sacrificial animal and a sacrificer, and the whole thing collapses into a free-for-all where each person's needs cancel out everyone else's. From this she concluded that rational egoism is the only coherent ethics, which on the chapter's telling means you should never act for the sake of another person, only for your own rational self-interest; helping people is at best long-term self-investment, something you do because their goodwill or their company is worth something to you, never because they need it. Charity is accordingly never a duty, just a luxury you can indulge when it pays. The chapter then connects this to her politics: altruism and capitalism are incompatible, because capitalism runs on everyone pursuing their own interests and trading, so any altruistic act is a species of parasitism — the giver parasites off the recipient's need, the recipient parasites off the giver's guilt. And it closes by saying her target was really any ethics that asks you to be impartial, so the critique lands just as hard on Kant and the utilitarians as on Christianity, and that in this she was basically finishing Nietzsche's attack on slave morality, a thinker she admired her whole life.

I'm fairly confident the Nietzsche bit is off and that "never act for the sake of another person" can't be right — I've been reading around in The Virtue of Selfishness and there's a lot in there about friendship and love being real personal values, but I can't reconstruct the distinction she actually draws well enough to say what's wrong with the sentence. I'm much less sure about the parasitism claim and about the scope claim (that her critique lands on Kant and the utilitarians too).

One thing I've been playing with, and I'd like your take on it: it seems to me her real target is better reconstructed as a thesis about who the beneficiary of an action has to be for the action to be moral — a claim about the structure of moral justification, roughly that justification has to terminate in the agent's own life as the ultimate end and can't terminate in someone else's need — rather than as a thesis about how selfishly anyone should behave. On that reading the "never act for others" line is not a side error but the hinge, and once you fix it the critique looks a lot less like a cartoon.
