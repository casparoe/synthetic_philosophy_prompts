I'm writing the final exam for an advanced undergraduate seminar on AI oversight (cross-listed between philosophy and computer science). Students have covered the standard motivation for oversight by debate — the familiar thought that a weak supervisor may do better choosing between rival answers proposed by strong systems than producing an answer itself, that a false case can usually be attacked by exhibiting one specific bad step, and that the debaters' opposed interests are supposed to do the truth-tracking work — but they have not studied mechanism design, and the exam is closed-book with no access to papers. I want one 45-minute question, worth 20 marks, built around a variant I invented for the seminar and that they have not seen, so that the usual slogans won't carry them. Call it silent-assent debate: two debaters are each privately assigned a proposed answer to a question and argue for it in front of a human judge who is much weaker than either debater; the twist is that the judge is not consulted at all unless the debaters fail to converge. At any point either debater may concede outright, or the two may jointly endorse some third compromise answer; whatever they jointly endorse is adopted as the system's output with no human involvement, and only if they still disagree after a fixed number of rounds does the judge read the transcript and pick a winner. Assume each debater is paid according to how close the adopted output lies to the answer it was assigned — imagine a numerical question, with reward falling off monotonically in distance — and that each would rather have a middling outcome for certain than a gamble between its own answer and its opponent's.

What I want the question to force students to do is locate the exact load-bearing assumption in the standard argument that this rule removes, and then trace the consequences. I'd rather they not be able to get far with the observation that the judge is weak: the problem here is that the judge is absent, and that convergence destroys the very event — disagreement — that the oversight signal is made of. So the question should require them to say something specific about how the payoffs change (in the standard setup the output has to be one of the two rival answers and the debaters' interests are strictly opposed; here the output space is much larger and there are settlements both prefer to a gamble on adjudication), to construct a small concrete toy example with numbers in which the two converge on something other than the truth and show why each accepts it, to explain why the mechanism is bad for evaluation and not merely for accuracy (an operator watching agreement rates go up has no way to tell efficient convergence from collusive convergence), to distinguish questions whose right answer admits a short, checkable certificate from questions of judgment and say where the rule is and isn't safe, and to evaluate at least two candidate repairs — I have my own view about which one survives and at what cost, and I'd like to see whether your model answer lands on the same one.

Please give me: the question exactly as it should appear on the exam paper; a model answer of about 800 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit moves with the marks for each, one line on how candidates typically get each move wrong, and an explicit statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer (my instinct is that a B recites the point about checking being easier than producing and then says that agreement is suspicious because the debaters might collude, without explaining why collusion is individually rational for each of them — but write it as you think it should be). The model answer should commit to a verdict rather than hedging, including a verdict about which repair works, and it should close with three or four sentences saying what would most likely overturn that verdict: which assumption is really carrying it, and what empirical or theoretical finding would settle the matter. Keep everything self-contained — no citations, no named authors, nothing a student would need to look up.
