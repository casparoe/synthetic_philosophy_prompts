yo, id love a short story (ballpark 1000–1500 words, actual prose with a setting and characters, not a script or a Socratic dialogue) built around a distinction I keep turning over about the meta-problem of consciousness.

Setup first, in case the phrase isnt familiar. The hard problem is why all the physical goings-on in a brain are accompanied by experience at all rather than by nothing. The meta-problem is the second-order one: explaining why we say and believe the things we do about experience — why we call some of it ineffable, why so many of us insist theres a further fact beyond the functions and the report-behaviour, why we find philosophical zombies conceivable, why we end up arguing about it at 2am. Some people think a full solution to the meta-problem would more or less dissolve the hard problem: if you can completely explain why we produce all these reports, maybe theres nothing left over to explain. Others think you could nail the meta-problem perfectly and the hard problem would still be sitting there, untouched.

Heres the wrinkle I want the story to actually be about, and i dont think ive seen it drawn quite this way. Distinguish a weak from a strong solution to the meta-problem. A weak solution is any system or theory that predicts, to whatever accuracy you like, what a given person will say about their experience in any situation — their verdicts, their hesitations, the moment they give up and fall back on there are no words, its just itself, you know what I mean. A strong solution is one that doesnt merely predict those reports but arrives at them in the way we do: the reports come out of it for the same kind of reason they come out of us, tracking whatever it is they track in us, rather than being pattern-matched from the outside. My suspicion, which I want the story to take seriously enough that a reader feels it, is that the gap between these two is the hard problem wearing a disguise. The weak solution is exactly what someone who thinks the reports are all there is should be content with; the strong solution is exactly the extra thing they have to insist nobody needs.

The scenario Im imagining: a near-future, slightly shabby research group has a system trained only on the discursive record of what humans say about what its like — interview transcripts, experience-sampling diaries, decades of forum fights about inverted spectra and rooms and bats, no neural data, no cameras, no embodiment, just text about experience. Call it whatever you want. It gets unsettlingly good at predicting reports, good enough that people start using it as a referee: it can tell you what a subject will say about their experience under some manipulation, so why bother running the study.

Then the turn. Somebody notices it predicting reports in cases that carry weight — a systematic shift nobody designed for (people stop reaching for ineffability under some condition, or start), or confident predictions about a case no human has actually been in: a subject who has never seen red, a brain quietly rewired overnight, a person told a lie about their own past answer. The team splits. One of them argues that a complete weak solution settles it: the meta-problem was the whole problem, and the hard problem was never a separate question. The other argues that the system is a perfect weak solution by construction — nobody thinks theres something it is like to be it, and it emits all the reports anyway — so a perfect weak solution is precisely the demonstration that weak solutions arent enough.

Ending I want earned rather than announced: the narrator, one of the team, realizes the distinction cuts at themselves. From the inside they cannot tell whether they are a strong solution to their own meta-problem or merely a weak one — whether their own certainty that there is something it is like to be them is a report produced for the right kind of reason, or just the next thing the system would have predicted. Id like that to land as a chill, not as a conclusion.

A few constraints: nobody gives a tidy explanation of the hard problem to a naive colleague or into a microphone, and if anyone explains anything it should be mid-argument with something to lose. No character named after their view, no cave riffs, no final paragraph that quietly turns into an encyclopedia entry. Give me concrete mundane detail so it reads like a story and not a thought experiment with names stuck on — the bad coffee, the grant deadline, the group chat going off. And obv no citations or further reading, just write the thing.
