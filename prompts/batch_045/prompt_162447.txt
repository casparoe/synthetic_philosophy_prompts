hey — I'm writing a short piece for a general-reader maths column about the St Petersburg game and I keep going round in circles on it, so I want a proper explanation I can steal a structure from. British English please, and prose a smart non-specialist could follow. Also: no citations or reading lists — I've got no library access and dont want a bibliography, just the argument.

Setup as I'm using it: keep flipping a coin until it comes up heads; if that takes n flips you get £2^n. So £2 with probability 1/2, £4 with 1/4, £2^k with 2^-k, and every single outcome contributes exactly £1 to the expectation, which is therefore infinite — while your median payoff is £2 and you've got roughly a 94% chance of walking away with £16 or less.

Three things I want explained properly, at a level that's actually in the weeds rather than the usual two-paragraph version:

1. The structural point about where the expectation lives. In an ordinary game the expectation is carried by outcomes you could plausibly see; here no outcome carries more than £1 of it and each extra doubling of the prize adds the same £1, and it's spread over outcomes of steadily vanishing probability. Explain why that stops "expected value" from behaving like an average of anything anyone could ever observe, and in particular why the "play it lots of times and it averages out" reflex fails here: the running average after n plays doesn't settle down to anything, it creeps upward like log₂ n, so each doubling of the number of plays only adds about £1 to your average payoff, and the handful of freak payouts in the sample are carrying a large share of the total (a share that shrinks only very slowly). Give me the back-of-envelope for why log₂ n is the right shape, and then say what that does to anyone who claims "fine, pay £50 a go, you'll make it back eventually" — how many plays before that's true?

2. Now the bit I haven't seen discussed and that I want to be the spine of the piece. Suppose the coin is very slightly biased toward heads: it comes up tails 49.95% of the time instead of 50%. Now the expectation isn't infinite, it's about £1001. Make the bias a hundred times smaller and it's about £100,000. Make it exactly zero and it's infinite again — the value goes like roughly 1/(2δ) for a bias of δ, and blows up as δ goes to zero. But the experience of playing is essentially unchanged: still about 50/50 on the £2, still that ~94% chance of £16 or less, still the occasional freak payout. And telling a 0.05% bias from a fair coin takes on the order of a million flips, which is way more than you'd rack up even if you played the thing a thousand times. So: what should we take from the fact that the value is discontinuous in the coin's bias? Does this show the infinite expectation was always an artefact of an idealisation nobody could verify, or does it show something more damaging — that expected value is just a bad summary statistic for a distribution this skewed, infinity or not?

3. And the reason I think the discontinuity doesn't actually rescue the expected-value theorist: if I'm genuinely unsure about the coin — say 0.9 on exactly fair, 0.1 on some small bias — then the expectation under my uncertainty is still infinite, because 0.9 times infinity is still infinity. So uncertainty about the bias doesn't dilute the paradox, it seems to make it stickier. Is that right, or am I messing something up? And if it is right, what does it tell us about whether the standard fix — valuing money logarithmically, or just bounding utility — is addressing the real problem or merely deleting the infinity?

What I'd like at the end is an honest verdict on what the paradox is a paradox about. Is it a problem about unbounded utility, about expectation as a guide to one-shot choices under extreme skew, or about taking idealisations (a perfectly fair coin, a casino with no limit) too literally? If the answer is "all three, and they're separable", then use the biased-coin version to show me how they come apart — which of them survives when the expectation is finite but still absurd.
