I read philosophy on my own, with no formal training, and for some months now I have been trying to work out whether panpsychism can answer what is usually called the combination problem. I find the view attractive: it is the only one I have met that does not ask me to believe consciousness simply switches on at some level of organisation. But I have built an argument which, if it works, does not merely embarrass panpsychism — it seems to show that no view which counts minds by how integrated a system is can say how many minds there are anywhere, including in an ordinary person falling asleep. An argument that strong is usually an argument with a faulty step. I would like you to find the step, tell me why it looked sound, and tell me what giving it up costs me.

The case the argument runs on is one I made up, because I could not find it anywhere. Suppose two people's brains are joined by an interface whose bandwidth can be turned up continuously, like a dimmer: at zero nothing passes; at the top the two brains share something close to a single perceptual and motor economy. Call it the coupling dial. At zero everyone agrees there are two subjects. I will assume, for the sake of the puzzle, that at the top a panpsychist who explains a macro-subject by the integration of its parts should say there is one. If you think that assumption is where I go wrong, say so — but then I want to know how the view explains why my two hemispheres make one subject while two fully coupled brains make two.

(1) Panpsychism earns its keep only if the relation between my experience and the experiences of my parts is constitutive rather than causal: the experience of the whole is made of the experiences of the parts, not produced by them.

(2) What makes a collection of micro-experiences constitute one macro-experience rather than several is the causal and structural integration of the system they belong to.

(3) Integration is a matter of degree, and in the coupling case it can be varied continuously, in arbitrarily small steps.

(4) So somewhere between the bottom and the top of the dial, the number of subjects changes from two to one.

(5) But nothing in the physical situation marks such a point. Any setting I nominate as the threshold differs from its neighbours by as little as I like, and any principle of the form "this much integration and no less" looks arbitrary in exactly the way sorites thresholds do.

(6) So one of two things holds: either there is no fact of the matter how many subjects exist across a wide stretch of the dial, or the number never really changes and panpsychism never combines anything.

(7) The first I cannot accept. "There is no fact of the matter how many conscious subjects there are" is not a confession of ignorance; it says reality itself is unsettled about whether there is something it is like to be this system once or twice. But consciousness is not the sort of thing that can be indeterminate: either there is something it is like to be a given system or there is not, and how many subjects there are is part of the answer to that question.

(8) The second is worse. If panpsychism never combines, then either every integrated clump of matter houses its own subject and I am a crowd, or the one genuine subject is the whole universe and what I call my mind is an aspect of it — and either way (1) and (2) have been abandoned rather than satisfied.

(9) So panpsychism cannot answer the combination problem. It owes me a threshold it cannot locate, or an indeterminacy about consciousness, or a crowd of minds, or a single cosmic mind.

The trouble is that (9) is far more than I set out to show, and I take that as the sign of a bad step. Run the same reasoning on a single brain with integration dialled down — falling asleep, a slowly growing lesion, alcohol. Steps (3) to (7) would then show that an ordinary human being is a borderline subject, or flickers between being one subject and two, for a good part of every day. I will not believe that. And run it on something with no mind in it at all: two droplets of water merging through a slowly widened channel, or two conversations in a room becoming one as people drift between them. There the same steps yield only that the count is vague or depends on what we care about, and no one takes that for a metaphysical breakdown. So my argument either reduces the number of minds to a matter of convention, which is absurd, or it is an argument form so powerful that it wrecks everything it touches. Both make me think I have gone wrong somewhere.

Which step do you reject? My guess is that the fault lies in (5) or (7), but I cannot say which, and I may be wrong about both. If there is a distinction I am missing — about how vagueness in a base bears on vagueness in what it grounds, or about the difference between being unable to find a boundary and there being none — name it, and say what it does to the rest of the argument. If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than keep (7); so if (7) is the bill, I want it itemised: what exactly am I accepting about consciousness if I allow that the number of subjects can be indeterminate? And if the honest answer is that the argument survives, tell me which of the four options in (9) is least expensive and why. Please argue it out rather than point me to sources: I have no library access and no one to ask.
