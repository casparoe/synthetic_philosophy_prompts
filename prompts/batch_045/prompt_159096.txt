I am writing a short paper on Hume's essay on the standard of taste, and I need a case built to order rather than an illustration of a point I have already made. My thesis is that the joint verdict of ideal critics does not settle whether a work is beautiful; it relocates the disagreement, turning a dispute about an object into a dispute about which body of comparison the critics are entitled to draw on. To make that relocation visible I need a scenario in which Hume's own machinery — the very conditions meant to secure the verdict — generates the instability.

Design me a scenario in which the same critics judge the same work twice and reverse their verdict.

Requirements for the scenario:

1. One paragraph, realistic, set in a recognizable contemporary setting (a museum acquisition, a conservatoire's repertoire, a literary prize, a building competition). The object is a single work, judged under the same conditions at both times; the panel is the same panel throughout.
2. Hume's five marks of a true judge — good sense, delicacy of sentiment, practice, comparison, and freedom from prejudice — are genuinely satisfied at both times. Show this rather than assert it.
3. The reversal may not be produced by any physical change in the work, by any change in the critics' native capacities, by any newly discovered fact about the work's authorship, origin, or making, by any shift in their moral or political convictions, or by any change in the panel's membership or size.
4. The only lever you may use is what the critics have met in the interval: sustained, serious experience of a body of work they had not previously encountered, which reorganizes what they compare the object to. Call this a comparison-base reversal. The critics must be able to report the change as an improvement in discernment — pointing to what they now notice that they previously passed over — not as a change of taste, mood, fashion, or appetite.
5. Both verdicts are unanimous, and the second is reached with full knowledge of the first.

After the scenario, deliver the following:

(a) The verdict at each time, and why each is unanimous.
(b) Whether, by Hume's own account, the standard has failed to return a verdict at all, or has returned two verdicts — and what that difference amounts to for my thesis.
(c) The feature of the case doing the work, and what happens if it is removed: show that deleting it collapses the case back into an ordinary story about critics who simply changed their minds.
(d) The strongest reply available to someone who wants the standard to yield a single verdict for a single work, together with one sentence on whether that reply presupposes the point at issue.

What counts as cheating: you may not stipulate that the critics converge, or stipulate that the reversal is a genuine gain in discernment. Both must fall out of the Humean conditions plus ordinary facts about how extended familiarity with a range of works reshapes judgment. If your case only works for a kind of object whose appreciation is essentially moral or political, say so explicitly and rebuild it for a kind of object where that is not true.
