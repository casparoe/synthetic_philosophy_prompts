I spent a working life in experimental physics — the last couple of decades of it running a small group doing precision photometry, where most of our time went not into taking data but into arguing about error bars, and in particular about whether a run had been spoiled by an instrument whose zero point had drifted. Since retiring I have been sitting in on a formal epistemology reading group in the philosophy department here, and one of our recent sessions nearly derailed itself over Jeffrey conditionalization, or probability kinematics: the rule that when your credence in some cell of a partition moves from one value to another without becoming one or zero, you should redistribute within cells, holding fixed the ratios of credence inside each cell, and take the new marginal as your new credence. I found the discussion oddly unsatisfying. The literature, as far as I can tell from the reading group, spends its energy on whether the rigidity condition can be motivated, on information-theoretic characterizations of the rule as a minimal departure from the old distribution, and on the worry that the result depends on which partition you happen to update on. What it does not seem to spend energy on is a distinction that dominated my professional life: the difference between noise that is independent from reading to reading and error that has a common cause, such as a single mis-set calibration constant afflicting an entire run. These strike me as utterly different evidential situations, and I cannot see that the standard rule treats them differently.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal. Suppose a sequence of imperfect readings arrives, each of which nudges your credence in some partition of hypotheses without settling it. If the errors in the readings are independent, then intuitively nothing is learned from the pattern of disagreement itself; each reading speaks only about the quantity. If the errors share a common cause, the pattern of disagreement is itself evidence — evidence about the instrument, which then reflects back onto the quantity — and I would expect that feeding the readings in one at a time, redistributing within cells at each step, gives you something different from, and I suspect worse than, treating the whole run as one body of evidence. In some toy finite models I have fiddled with, sequential applications of the rule are not even order-independent in the second kind of case, whereas they look nearly so in the first. I want to know whether that is a real distinction or an artifact of the particular numbers I chose, and if it is real, what it tells us about what kind of rule this is supposed to be: is it a rule for incorporating evidence, in which case the answer ought to depend only on the total evidence and not on the temporal order in which it happened to arrive, or is it a rule for being acted upon by experience, in which case path-dependence is no more objectionable in the update than it is in the physics? My thought is to use, as a yardstick, ordinary conditioning inside an explicit model that contains a variable for the calibration state as well as one for the quantity: wherever such a model can be written down, global conditioning on the total evidence is the standard of correctness, and the question becomes when the partition-based rule reproduces it and when and why it comes apart.

I am applying for a three-month visiting fellowship and need a two-page proposal for a paper of perhaps eight thousand words on this. Please write it as a proposal rather than as the paper: state the question so that it has a determinate answer; say why the existing ways of defending or criticizing the rule leave it open; give me the approach, including what I would actually do month by month, and why that approach is better than the obvious alternatives (simply proving a small algebra result about commutation, or simply arguing the interpretive question about experience versus evidence in the abstract); say what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them; and be honest about what would count as the project failing or as it being too big for the time available. Two constraints. First, I know the case for radical probabilism is precisely that you may not be able to articulate the evidence proposition, so a proposal that silently assumes a fully specified generative model may be begging the central question — I would rather the proposal confront that objection than dodge it, and tell me what the project is worth if the objection is sound. Second, I am comfortable with graduate-level discrete probability but not with measure theory, so please keep the formalization finite or explicitly note where it cannot be. Do not give me a literature review or any references; I will fill in the citations myself, and I would rather the proposal not be built around them.
