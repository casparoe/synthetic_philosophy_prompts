I need help writing something and I'm hoping you can do it, because I've been going in circles on it for about a week.

I ran undergraduate admissions at a small liberal arts college in the Midwest from 1994 to 2003, and the thing I'm known for there is a race-conscious admissions policy I built in 1998 — pretty aggressive for its time, extra weight for applicants from a list of specific zip codes plus a whole second review track. It was ruled unconstitutional a few years ago and the college has been retrofitting ever since. Next month the class that entered in 1999 is having its 25th reunion and they've asked me to give the welcome remarks at the Friday dinner, roughly six minutes. Families are invited, so there will be grown kids in the room.

Here's where I'm stuck. A lot of the people in that class wouldn't have been at that college at all without the policy — they'd have gone somewhere else and, as far as anyone can tell, been fine, and that part I sort of know how to think about. The part that's actually doing my head in is their children. A good number of couples in that class met during orientation week in 1999, and there's no version of events where they meet anywhere else. (They tell me this cheerfully, all the time, because to them it's a lovely story.) So the two sentences a speech like this is normally built out of both feel fake to me. I can't say "I'm sorry for what was done to you" to a room that partly wouldn't exist if I'd acted otherwise — and I don't just mean it would be awkward, I mean the complaint seems to need a comparison, someone being worse off than they'd otherwise have been, and for those kids there's nothing on the other side to compare to, just no one. But the other move is just as bad: "look how well it turned out," "you owe your existence to it, so be grateful." As far as I can tell you can't really be grateful for having been caused to exist, because there's no state of yourself you got rescued from. Gratitude needs a before, and there isn't one. So: no apology, no gratitude, six minutes to fill.

The room makes it worse in the good way. There's a group of eight or ten alums from that class who want the college to issue a formal apology for the policy and who'd like me to endorse that from the podium; there's a group about the same size who think the policy is the only reason the place is worth anything now and who will hear hedging as betrayal; and a decent number of people are in both groups and also have their kids at the table. The current dean, who was hired to clean up after me and who I'd genuinely like to make look good, will be there. And I should be honest: I myself am not sure it was right. I thought it was, hard, for about ten years, and now I don't know, and I don't want to fake certainty in either direction just to have something to say.

One more thing, which is the part I most want you to get right. The complaints from alums that I take most seriously aren't about how their lives went at all. They're about what it was like to be admitted under a rule that basically announced "you're here because of a category," and about the fact that everyone else on campus assumed that was why they were there and treated them accordingly. That feels like a real objection to me, and importantly not one that gets defused by pointing out that they'd have done worse somewhere else — it's the wrong shape for that reply. But I have no idea how to say that in six minutes at a banquet without turning it into a lecture, and definately no idea how to say it without it sounding like I'm explaining their own experience back to them.

Can you write the remarks? About six minutes, meant to be spoken out loud, to that room. Continuous prose only, please — no headings, no bullets, no bold, nothing in brackets, just what I'd actually say standing up there. Then, still in plain prose, a paragraph or two on what you deliberately left out and why.
