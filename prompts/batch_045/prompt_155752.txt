I'm writing a paper on how thought experiments in science can yield knowledge, and the problem I keep hitting is structural rather than scholarly: every account of the phenomenon is normally illustrated with the same handful of canonical cases — inclined planes, buckets, elevators, demons, cats — and those cases have been polished by so much commentary that each account has effectively been reverse-engineered to fit them. The disagreement between accounts then looks verbal, because everyone has already adjusted to the same examples. A friend who works in ecosystem ecology recently described a dispute in her field that was settled by an armchair argument rather than a new dataset, which convinced me there is usable material outside the usual playgrounds. I also want to try out a distinction I have been toying with: that "yield" should be split along two axes — acquisition (a new justified true belief) versus calibration (an improvement in the reliability of one's capacity to model the domain, without any new proposition being justified), and first-personal yield (had by the person who runs the thought experiment) versus dialectical yield (had by a field that can now use the result in argument). My suspicion is that the standard accounts are silent about the second axis, and that this is where they actually diverge.

So I need a case built to specification rather than found. Take three families of account: the deflationary view, on which a thought experiment is an argument in rhetorical costume and its yield is exactly the yield of the explicit argument it unpacks into; the model-simulation view, on which it is a simulation run on a mental model, so that its yield depends on the causal fidelity of that model and is at least partly a matter of having a calibrated modelling capacity rather than holding a new belief; and the a priori intuition view, on which the special cases give non-empirical access to nomic or modal truth, while the rest degenerate into ordinary argument. I want a scientific thought experiment — in a real science, and I would prefer a special science such as ecology, epidemiology, earth system science or materials, though if you think the specification can only be met in fundamental physics, say so and explain why — that makes all three give different verdicts. The deflationary view should say that no knowledge is yielded, because the inference rests on a premise that was not antecedently available to the field. The simulation view should say that knowledge is yielded, but only to the person who runs the case, and that what that person acquires is not fully transferable to a colleague who reads a complete explicit reconstruction of the argument. The intuition view should say that knowledge is yielded a priori and is available to anyone at all who genuinely performs the imaginative exercise, expert or not. Constraints on the scenario itself: one paragraph; nothing science-fictional, no demons or perfect predictors, no infinite populations or frictionless idealisations that a working scientist in that field would dismiss; the setup should be something a practitioner could seriously offer as a reason for belief; and the conclusion should be one that most practitioners now accept. The feature I expect to be doing the work is that the conclusion is not entailed by the theory the field antecedently accepted — the thought experiment reaches past the available premises — but you should tell me if it is a different feature.

The deliverable I need is the scenario plus a demonstration that it meets each part of the specification, with the verdicts read off the accounts rather than stipulated: I should be able to see which feature each account is responding to and why it responds that way. Then: state which single feature of the case is doing the discriminating work, and what happens to the verdicts if it is removed — ideally, show that with the feature gone the three accounts collapse back into agreement, so the case is really isolating that variable. Give me the deflationary theorist's best attack on the case (I expect something like: the tacit premise was in fact available or independently supported, so the yield is ordinary and the appearance of surplus is rhetorical), and the intuition theorist's best reply, and tell me honestly which of the two you think wins for this case. For each of the three verdicts, place the yield on my two axes: acquisition or calibration, first-personal or dialectical. Finally, if the specification as stated cannot be jointly satisfied, tell me which conjunct has to be relaxed and what a near-miss case would look like. Please don't include references or a literature review — I will supply the citations myself, and I want the argumentative structure rather than the bibliography.
