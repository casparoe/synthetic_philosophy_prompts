**Carving, grain, and the mat (draft)**

**1.** Here's the dilemma as I see it. Either there's one correct way to sort the furniture of the world into kinds and the mature sciences are converging on it, or our sortings answer to our interests and the success of science tells us more about what we care about than about how the world is put together. I don't like either horn. The first looks straightforwardly false: chemistry, genetics and ecology sort the same organisms differently, all three work, and none of the three is just a shorthand for another. The second throws away too much: if classification only answered to us, it'd be a miracle that our classifications let us predict things we never designed them to predict.

**2.** The standard realist fix is to say that the scientifically good classifications are the ones that support induction and turn up in laws. But that's too cheap. Any classification supports induction about itself — you can always project 'all Ks are K'. So the realist needs something stronger: a good kind supports inductions about properties that weren't used to decide what belongs to the kind. Call that surplus. Problem: surplus isn't enough either. Sorting library books by decade of publication gives you a pile of surplus — paper stock, binding glue, typefaces — and nobody thinks decades are natural kinds.

**3.** So here's my thought experiment, which is the bit I actually care about. Suppose we one day finish a science of a tide-pool mat ecosystem, and it converges on two classifications of the very same organisms. Scheme A sorts them by 'channel', which of eight chemical routes they use to dump electrons. Scheme B sorts them by 'cohort', which cycle of the tidal clock they reproduce on. Both support exceptionless generalizations. Both have enormous surplus. Neither is a refinement of the other: every organism has exactly one channel and exactly one cohort, and knowing the channel tells you nothing about the cohort. And crucially, no third scheme built out of the same measurements does better than just using both, and there's no hidden variable that unifies them. They're orthogonal. Now: what does the friend of natural kinds say about the mat? Either both are real (and then what does 'joints' even mean — a skeleton doesn't have cross-cutting joints), or neither is real (and then natural kinds are doing no work in explaining why this science succeeds), or one is real and the finished science can't tell us which. All three options seem bad.

**4.** My way out. Maybe what's mind-independent isn't a partition at all but a texture: directions in the space of possible classifications along which causal influence runs easily, and directions across which it doesn't. I want to call this the grain, and reserve 'joint' for the special case where the grain is genuinely discontinuous. Lots of different partitions can follow the same grain, which explains pluralism; but which grain there is isn't up to us, which explains realism. The mat isn't a counter-example, it's a case where the grain runs two ways at once.

**5.** The wrinkles I can't shake. First, any domain that contains two causally independent systems will show this two-way grain, so my test might just be detecting 'there is more than one causal system here' rather than anything special about these partitions — in which case it's trivial and says nothing. Second, I keep assuming there's some geometry on the space of possible classifications, so that it makes sense to perturb one slightly and see how fast its surplus decays, and I have no idea where that geometry is supposed to come from or who put it there.

**6.** And a smaller thing: in section 2 I gesture at the essentialist picture people associate with Kripke and Putnam, and I have a feeling I'm caricaturing it. If the version I'm attacking is a strawman, say so and tell me what the real version commits them to.
