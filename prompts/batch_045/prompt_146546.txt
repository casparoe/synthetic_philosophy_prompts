So I've went back and forth with a friend about whether the money pump argument actually shows anything, and I realized I cant cleanly say what it's even supposed to be showing. Help me get the shape of it straight.

Setup: suppose my preferences over three options run in a cycle. I strictly prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, each strongly enough that I'll pay a penny to make the swap. So I start holding A, pay a penny for C, a penny for B, a penny for A, and I end up with A again and three cents poorer. Run that a thousand times and I'm out thirty bucks holding the thing I started with.

Here are two readings I keep sliding between, and I want them pulled apart properly:

1. The being-fleeced reading: incoherence is bad because it makes you exploitable. Someone else can run a profit off you, and that's an ordinary external cost, like leaving your wallet on a park bench.
2. The paying-for-nothing reading: a single cycle already leaves you with A minus three cents, a situation you yourself rank below the one you were in five minutes ago, and every step along the way was one you freely took. So the badness seems internal to your own standards and doesn't depend on anyone profiting.

What I want from you is: which one is doing the real argumentative work, and what happens to each when you press on it. My three sticking points:

- The planner who sees it coming. If I can foresee the whole cycle, I'll refuse the first trade, because I know I'd refuse the later ones too, so nobody can actually pump me and no money ever moves. Does that defuse reading 1 completely while leaving reading 2 untouched, or does reading 2 have the same structural problem? Is the step from each step is one you accept to the whole path is one you endorse doing any work, or is it a composition fallacy I should stop leaning on?
- A variant I made up, which is the bit I care about most: suppose my cycle is only strong enough that I'd pay a tenth of a penny for each swap, and the person offering the trades has to pay a one-penny fee to post each offer, so pumping me is never profitable and nobody ever bothers. Also suppose the cycle is over something I care about a lot, so the amounts are trivial next to the stakes. Is my incoherence now costless and therefore fine? Put sharply: is the argument supposed to be sensitive to whether there is actually a profit motive out there, so that the wrongness comes in degrees with the amount pumpable and goes to zero when the pump doesn't pay? If it is sensitive that way, that seems like a weird result, and I want to know whether that's a bullet the argument has to bite or a sign I've described the argument wrong.
- Scope. Even granting the argument, what does it get you? I can't see how to run a pump on someone whose preferences are incomplete and who therefore just declines to trade when two options are incomparable, and I can't see how to run one on someone who violates independence in an Allais-ish way while staying perfectly transitive. So is the pump argument only ever an argument for acyclicity, with completeness and independence needing separate arguments of a different shape? If so, sketch what those arguments are and whether they have the same you-would-pay-to-undo-it structure, or whether they have to appeal to something else entirely.

Please don't just list the axioms of expected utility theory at me or give me a representation-theorem proof sketch, I've read those and they don't answer this. I want the conceptual move: what exactly the pump is an argument for, what it silently assumes about the agent (that she'll pay for improvements, that she can't plan, that she treats a sequence of choices as a package), and which of the two readings is left standing when all this is poked at. A few paragraphs is fine, and please say plainly where this is genuinely unsettled rather than papering over it.
