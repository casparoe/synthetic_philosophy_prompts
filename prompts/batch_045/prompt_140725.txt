hey — I'm putting together the take-home final for my advanced population ethics seminar (mixed upper-level undergrads and first-year PhDs) and I need one hard question. Its a 90-minute question, closed-book, and the thing I'm trying to kill is the essay I've now read forty times: "total utilitarianism gives the repugnant conclusion, average utilitarianism avoids it but has the absurd conclusion, critical-level views try to split the difference, the end." If a student can pass by writing that, I've failed.

So I want a question where they have to actually manipulate a formal principle and notice something that isn't in the survey-article version. Here's the raw material — rework the numbers if my choices make the arithmetic ugly, but keep the structure:

Setup they all have: a population is a list of welfare levels; 0 is the neutral level (a life at 0 is neither good nor bad for the person living it); positive numbers are lives worth living; welfare is interpersonally comparable on a ratio scale. Write n for headcount, T for the sum, A = T/n for the mean. The repugnant conclusion (RC): for a baseline population A* of ten billion people all at welfare 100, and for any ε > 0 however tiny, there's some N such that N people each at ε is better than A*.

The four things I want them to work through:

1. The one-parameter family V_α = A · n^α, for fixed α between 0 and 1. α = 1 is totalism, α = 0 is averagism, α = 1/2 is the "geometric mean of total and average" compromise view. Make them solve for the N needed to beat A* as a function of α and ε (I was going to use ε = 0.01), and then say whether turning α down is a solution to the RC or just a way of pushing the repugnant population out past the number of atoms in the observable universe — and whether "it would take more people than could physically exist" is a real answer or a dodge.

2. The α = 0 endpoint, which really does avoid the RC. Make them price it: two people at 101 beat ten billion at 100, and adding a person whose life is well worth living but below the mean makes the outcome worse.

3. The part that should seperate the A's from the B's: a lexical threshold view. Fix τ > 0; P is better than Q if P has fewer people below τ; ties broken by greater total welfare. This thing avoids the RC, doesn't have the "two people at 101" problem, and is complete and transitive — so anyone who's absorbed the line that avoiding the RC forces you into intransitivity (Parfit-style mere-addition reasoning) has to give that up here. So make them find what actually breaks. I think there are two things: the RC doesn't go away, it relocates (for every finite τ there's a repugnant conclusion at τ + δ, and if τ is a modest sufficiency level the N involved is more attainable, not less); and there's a cliff at τ where nudging a couple hundred billion lives from τ − η to τ + η flips the comparison from "much worse than A*" to "much better than A*". Have them judge whether that cliff is worse than the original RC.

4. The continuous cousin: value each life at (w − c) for a fixed positive critical level c, then sum. No cliff, and for ε < c the RC is genuinely gone. Make them say where the repugnant conclusion went (it's now at ε > c, and the N needed is a function of how close ε sits to c), and what the view pays for it — lives worth living but below c count as net harms to create.

Then the synthesis I actually care about: what has to be given up to escape the RC — completeness, unbounded aggregation, continuity in the quality of lives, or the idea that a barely-worth-living life is a benefit at all — and whether going person-affecting and declaring A* and the huge population incomparable really helps when somebody has to actually choose.

What I need from you:

- The question as I'd hand it to students. Self-contained: every principle defined in the question itself with its formula, no reliance on remembered names, and nothing that asks for citations or a reading list — its closed-book.
- A model answer of about 1000 words, in American English, doing the arithmetic explicitly rather than saying "for sufficiently large N", and not just asserting "that's repugnant" at each step — I want the trade-off named each time.
- A marking scheme: roughly 30 marks, split into must-hit points with the marks for each, plus a one-line note per point on how people usually get it wrong. My predictions: a lot of them will say "α < 1 avoids the RC because the numbers are damped" without checking whether damping is actually enough; and a lot will test the threshold view only with lives below τ and conclude it works.
