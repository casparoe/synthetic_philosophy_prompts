I'm in a tiny reading group on AI alignment (four people, one of whom mostly just eats the snacks), and after we read something on inverse reinforcement learning two of them — Sam, a theory PhD student, and Ines, who does robot learning — got into an argument that I've been turning over in my head ever since. Rough reconstruction of how it went:

(1) Sam: IRL can't recover preferences even in principle. For any behavior you actually observe, there are tons of reward functions that make it optimal — shift everything by a constant, scale by a positive number, or add a potential-based term of the form γΦ(s′) − Φ(s), which leaves the ranking of policies completely untouched. So what the algorithm "recovers" is just whatever its features, its regularizer and its paramaterization happened to select. You're not discovering what someone wants; you're inventing a tidy story in whatever language you handed the algorithm.

(2) Ines: that's gauge freedom, not underdetermination. Every reward in that family predicts exactly the same behavior, and the questions we actually wanted answered are behavioral ones — would she take the stairs or the elevator, would she rather get the email or sit through the meeting. Those come out the same for the whole family, so the recovery is as good as it needs to be. Complaining that the numbers aren't pinned down is like complaining that mass isn't pinned down until you pick units.

(3) Sam: the invariance only holds inside the dynamics you fitted on. A potential-based shaping term preserves the optimal policy in the original MDP but generally stops preserving it once the transition probabilities change — and the entire reason anyone cares about inferring human preferences is to reuse them somewhere else: a new task, a new environment, a system acting on someone's behalf. Exactly where you want to spend the reward, the equivalence class comes apart.

(4) Ines: then don't spend the reward, spend the policy; or fit in one environment and test in another. If a reward fitted on the kitchen trajectories also predicts what she does in the office, that's evidence you've got something real and not just a artifact of your own parameterization. That's just normal science — you broke the tie with data instead of with a stipulation.

(5) Sam: the test is weaker than it looks, because the fit already assumed the demonstrator is soft-optimal with respect to a reward that's linear in your features, with a single noise temperature. Suppose instead she's satisficing, or planning against a model of the world that's wrong. Then she still looks like a noisy optimizer of *some* reward, and the thing you fit is a blend of what she values and what she believes. You can't separate "she cares less about X" from "she thinks X probably won't work" — and that's the distinction the method was supposed to deliver in the first place.

(6) Ines: fine, but that's not special to IRL. A human watching another human has the same problem, and the way we sort it out is by intervening — change what she believes, see whether her behavior moves. IRL can do exactly that, and invariance-under-intervention just *is* what "preference" operationally means here. Also, the alternative isn't better: asking people what they want inherits every one of these problems and adds confabulation on top.

(7) Sam: even granting all that, look at the data. It's demonstrations — behavior that succeeded. You never see the attempts that failed, or what she'd have done with more skill or a better model of the world, so the fitted reward describes competent performance rather than the person. Which is precisely the problem if the plan is to hand that reward to something much more capable than she is.

(8) Ines: competent performance is still evidence about values, and nothing in the formalism requires the data to be optimal — the Boltzmann version assigns probability to everything, including the clumsy trajectories, so you can fit on the failures too. Anyway, no method here outputs revealed truth; it outputs a hypothesis with error bars, and you keep updating it. Demanding more than that is asking for something no inference from behavior could ever give you.

I mostly sat there nodding at both of them, which I don't think counts as moderation. Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what — which moves are clearly right, which are right but overstated, which are just wrong — and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing? I had a feeling while they talked that the answer might depend on whether you want the inferred thing for prediction or for delegation to a system that acts on your behalf, but I couldnt get it into words at the time and I'm not sure it's the right way to carve it up either.
