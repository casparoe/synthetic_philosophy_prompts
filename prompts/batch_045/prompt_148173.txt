I'm a research analyst at a mid-sized private foundation, and in three weeks I'm presenting to our program committee a proposal to replace the scoring template we use when we allocate across program areas. The committee votes at the end of that same meeting on whether to pilot the new method on one program area for a year, so the question period, not the slides, is where this is decided. The room is: two development economists of the experimentalist persuasion, who convert everything into external validity, publication bias, and whether anything not estimated off a randomized design counts as evidence at all; a former government cost-benefit analyst who will want a discount rate, a distributional weight, and an account of what happens under parameter uncertainty; two program officers with fifteen years of field experience each, who distrust aggregate metrics and will hear any formula as head office removing discretion from the people who actually know the partners; one board member who introduced our current framework to us and reads the effective-altruism literature, and who will defend it as cheap, transparent, and good enough; and the board chair, a retired software founder who thinks in terms of leverage and will ask what the framework would have told us to do five years ago.

My thesis is that the three-factor score we now use -- importance, tractability, neglectedness -- is not wrong so much as mis-specified, because two of its three factors are properties of a funder relative to a cause rather than properties of the cause, and because it has no term for the value of finding things out. I want to replace the score with an estimate of the expected marginal value of our next dollar to a cause, computed against an explicitly modeled counterfactual funding path, and then decompose it for presentation into four terms: a scale-weighted tractability indexed to our own access and comparative advantage rather than to the cause in the abstract; a saturation discount read off an absorption curve, meaning what the cost per unit of outcome does as cumulative funding in that area rises over a five-year window; a crowding term, since a dollar of ours that only substitutes for a dollar that would have arrived anyway does much less than a dollar that pulls in others or that no one else was going to give; and an option-value term for grants whose real payoff is resolving a disagreement before a much larger later commitment, which on my arithmetic is why a long-horizon funder like us should sometimes fund things that score badly on all three of the classic factors. The concrete thing I have to defend is a ranking flip: our largest line item, a well-evidenced and heavily funded global-health delivery program with a well-characterized cost per outcome, comes out below a small, weakly evidenced institutional-reform pilot that is very cheap now and that sits in front of a decision about a much larger commitment in about four years.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, in roughly the order they will arrive, each attributed to the person most likely to raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it -- the treasury analyst's objection should sound like a treasury analyst's, not like a philosopher's, and the program officers' should be about partners and legibility rather than about expected value. For each: what I should concede up front rather than being forced into it at the third follow-up, a reply I could give in about two minutes standing at a whiteboard, and a blunt note on where that reply is soft. Include what I should volunteer in the first two minutes to take the sting out of the two or three objections with real force, and tell me which objection I should simply grant in full. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks. My own candidate is the coordination worry: if every funder conditions on what every other funder will do, then the crowding term can make everyone rationally stand down at once and defund an area by mutual prediction, and I do not have a fixed point to point to. Tell me if you think the real killer is a different question -- I suspect the option-value term is vulnerable to something like always recommending more research and meta-work -- and argue for your choice over mine.
