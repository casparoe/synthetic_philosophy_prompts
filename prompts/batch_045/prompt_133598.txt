Here's the list, roughly in the order I thought of it:

1. Underdetermination is real. For any pile of data you like, even a huge one, there are infinitely many different theories or curves or models that hit every single point exactly and still disagree about anything you haven't measured yet.

2. And I think this shows up in real life, not just in made-up philosophy examples. In my last competition like six teams tied on the final score with models that were completely different — a gradient boosted tree thing, a linear model on hand-built features, a small transformer. I keep thinking that's just the philosophers' underdetermination wearing a hoodie. My friend says it isn't, because more data would eventually separate them, but I have a distinction ready: I call it data-limited vs world-limited underdetermination — data-limited means we just don't have enough observations yet, world-limited means no observation even in principle could tell the two apart — and I only think the world-limited kind is philosophically interesting.

3. I'm a scientific realist I think. Electrons are real, our best theories are at least approximately true, they aren't just instruments for churning out predictions.

4. But if two theories agree about every observation anybody could ever make, I don't think there's a fact of the matter about which one is right. They're just two descriptions of the same thing. (This is why I can't get worked up about whether spacetime is really curved vs. a field on flat space, if those come out observationally the same.)

5. I think simplicity is a genuine reason to prefer one theory over another and not just a tiebreaker we invented because we like tidy things. The world seems to actually be pretty simple, and that feels like evidence about what's true.

6. I also think the pessimistic meta induction is basically right: most theories that were wildly successful in their day turned out to be false in the end, so we should expect ours to go the same way. I don't think that should force me to give up 3.

7. The strongest argument for 3, in my opinion, is the miracle one — it would be astonishing if our theories predicted stuff this precisely without being at least roughly true. (Side thing I can't settle: if that argument works, does it also apply to an ML model that predicts something really well? Am I committed to thinking the internals of a net that nails protein structures are latching onto something real?)

8. And I think underdetermination being true is totally compatible with lots of science being finished and settled. We know smoking causes lung cancer, that's done. 'Here's another curve that fits all your data' doesn't reopen it.

So: which of these actually clash, and how badly? Please rank the clashes by severity instead of flagging all of them as equally bad, and for each one tell me what would have to give. I'd also like to know which ones only look like clashes but actually aren't, because I suspect my friend is pointing at one of those. And if there's a single revision that would clean up the most problems at once, what is it and what does it cost me? I don't have a favorite among these so don't worry about hurting my feelings — but I also don't want to be told that everything is in tension with everything, because that's not useful.
