I teach a first-year political theory module at a university college in Utrecht, where the cohort is roughly a third Dutch, a third from elsewhere in the EU, and a third from outside Europe — mostly Turkey, Indonesia, Nigeria, and India. A lecture exercise blew up on me last week. I had put a list on the board — constitutional government, freedom of conscience, market economies, secular law, gender equality, free inquiry — and asked which of these are 'Western values'. Two objections landed at once: a Turkish student said the list reads like a transcription of what Brussels requires of candidate states, and a Dutch student said her own grandparents would have rejected half of it, so the values cannot be what the West has actually believed. Afterwards in the staff room an anthropologist colleague told me the phrase is a Cold War artifact that has only ever done contrastive work — it denotes whatever the speaker's current outgroup is taken to lack — while a visiting American political theorist said no, it simply names Enlightenment liberalism, and the discomfort is only embarrassment about saying so. I don't want to adjudicate that fight in front of the students. I want the specification first.

So: write me the desiderata for an account of what the phrase 'Western values' picks out. The purpose is fixed, and I want it held fixed: the account must be good enough to adjudicate, in public argument, claims of the form 'X is incompatible with Western values' — good enough to say whether such a claim is truth-evaluable at all, what kind of evidence would bear on it, and who has standing to make it. The desiderata should therefore concern the extension and the mechanics of the phrase, not which values are worth having. Four cases broke my exercise and I want them driving the list: the convert case, someone born and raised outside Europe who assents to everything on the board; the betrayal case, two governments both claiming the same heritage and each accusing the other of apostasy; the drift case, the contents of the list visibly tracking the perceived outgroup (anti-communism in one decade, gender egalitarianism in the next); and the cosmopolitan-codification case, items whose actual drafting happened in Geneva and New York in committees with heavy non-Western participation, and which several non-Western states ratify more eagerly than some Western ones. If you suspect that some of what people demand here is not a genuine requirement but the prejudice of one camp, say which and why inside the list.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should state a single requirement, give the case that motivates it, and be bracketed as [non-negotiable] or [negotiable] according to whether an account that fails it should be rejected outright or merely demoted. Put an asterisk on the one requirement that does the most work in excluding the accounts actually in circulation — that includes the three I sketched above, plus whatever better account you would put in their place. If two of your five cannot be jointly satisfied by any single account, say so inside the item where the conflict bites rather than adding a sixth item. Skip any scorecard of the standard accounts against the list; I will build that myself.
