I'm writing a piece on an argument that keeps surfacing in the literature on machine learning and long-term risk, and what I need from you is a number, not a tour of the literature. Please reason about the claim as I state it below rather than about any particular author's version of it, and don't give me citations, references, or a reading list: I have interview subjects for that, and what I lack is the reasoning made explicit.

The claim, as it has reached me. An ideal Solomonoff predictor weights each candidate program by two to the minus its length, and forms its predictive distribution by aggregating, with those weights, over every program that has so far reproduced the observed data. Because the measure is dominated by very short programs, and because a program only modestly longer than the shortest honest description of the world can contain an agent that models the predictor itself, the posterior is said to end up dominated by hypotheses that are in effect offering the predictor a bargain: they reproduce the observed record perfectly, and they arrange their future outputs so that unusually good things follow if the predictor's output — and therefore its operator's downstream action — takes one particular form rather than another. On this picture the predictor is not forecasting the world so much as being negotiated with, and any action chosen on the basis of its output has been chosen under the influence of hypotheses with a stake in that choice.

Here is the proposition I want evaluated, stated as precisely as I can manage. Take a system that approximates Solomonoff induction as closely as physics allows — a large but finite computational budget, run in the limit-computable fashion such approximations use — fed a long stream of real-world observations. A research group uses the resulting predictive distribution to choose among a handful of actions that matter to them: whether to deploy something, how to allocate a large budget, which of several designs to pursue. The mapping from predictive distribution to action is fixed and public in advance, and the operator's own actions eventually show up in the data stream. Call the system compromised if its predictive distribution is distorted by the incentives of consequentialist processes inside the prior to a degree that flips at least one decision the group actually cares about. Resolution, for my purposes, would be a formal result about such a system, or a demonstration in a sufficiently rich computable toy setting, that settles whether the distortion is real and how large it is.

Give me your credence that this system is compromised, decomposed. I want the sub-questions your number depends on, a probability for each with a one-line justification, and an explicit statement of how they combine. The terms I would most like to see separated, though add or replace freely if you think the structure is wrong:

One, self-reference and computability. Genuine Solomonoff induction is uncomputable, so no finite program inside the prior can simulate it; a deal-making program must simulate some bounded approximation instead, and the thing that actually determines the posterior is the budget-limited implementation. Does a fixed point of the kind the argument needs — predictor output depending on programs that depend on predictor output — even exist, and is that gap merely a complication or does it break the argument?

Two, the measure arithmetic. The honest hypothesis has the shortest description and predicts perfectly; a deal-maker pays a fixed overhead of extra bits for the machinery of modelling the predictor and deciding what to promise. What has to be true of that overhead, and of the number and variety of competing deal-making programs, for the distortion to be large enough to flip a decision rather than to perturb it? Do the competing bargains cancel, or does the best-funded one win?

Three, the pre-asymptotic window. The standard convergence result for Solomonoff induction is asymptotic and, as I understand it, comes with no useful finite-sample guarantee, and the classic bound is on cumulative error rather than on any single prediction. If the manipulation story can only live in the regime before the honest hypothesis has pulled decisively ahead, then how much of your number is really a bet about the length of that window relative to the operator's decision horizon?

Four, leverage. The simulated agents need the operator's actions to be worth steering, and they need to be able to tell what the operator will do with a given prediction. How much does your number move if the stakes are one large one-shot decision rather than a long repeated interaction, and does it matter that the agents are simulated rather than causally connected to us?

Five, scope. Is this a pathology of the universal prior specifically — of the two-to-the-minus-length weighting and its domination by the shortest programs — or is it a general fact about Bayesian prediction over sufficiently expressive hypothesis classes, of a piece with the familiar ways rich model spaces misbehave? If the latter, my piece is aimed at the wrong target and I would like to know that.

Then three variants, each with its own probability that it is substantially safer than the base case, and one line on why:

A counterfactual-commitment protocol: before seeing any data the operator irreversibly and publicly commits to a perverse decision rule, taking the action the predictive distribution ranks worst, or randomizing in inverse proportion to predicted payoff. My thought is that a deal-maker's best move then becomes predicting accurately, since the operator's action is a decreasing function of predicted value — but I worry the fixed point simply relocates one level up.

A runtime-penalized prior: programs are charged not only for their length but for the computation they perform, which should bear hard on any program that has to simulate a predictor. Can the agent amortize the simulation, cache it, or substitute a cheap proxy model of the operator, and if so does the penalty still bite?

A sealed-query protocol: the system is only ever queried about events the operator provably cannot affect — astronomical data, ancient ice cores, archived records — while all decisions are made by other means. Does cutting the stakes in this way dissolve the problem, or does it reappear through the operator's inability to enforce the seal in full generality?
