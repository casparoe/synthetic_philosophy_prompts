Now that I'm retired from the lab (analytical chemistry, mostly mass spec, best part of thirty years) I've been going to a fortnightly philosophy reading group at the local library — very amateur, half of us are just there for the coffee — and we've drifted onto ancient philosophy, where I got interested in what Aristotle actually says about money and exchange, since I kept hearing secondhand that he was some kind of proto-economist or else a total anti-market crank. I found a popular history of money in a secondhand bookshop with a chapter on the Greeks, and I jotted down the gist as I went: exchange between, say, a shoemaker and a house-builder is impossible unless their products are somehow comparable, and for Aristotle the thing that makes them comparable is how much people need them — need is the real measure of everything's worth, and money is only a conventional stand-in for need, a token by agreement with no worth in itself, which is why he says its value comes from convention and not from nature; that means he more or less worked out the labour theory of value two thousand years early, and Marx just finished the job by identifying need with the labour time embodied in a thing; and since a symbol can't breed, lending at interest is the most unnatural way of getting wealth there is, and by extension he regarded trade for profit as disgraceful and would really have wanted everyone back in self-sufficient peasant households. The chapter closes by saying this is why Aristotle would have been untroubled by fiat currency and would have wanted interest banned outright.

The bit I can't shake is that in my old field this all looks like dimensional analysis: you can't add or compare quantities in different dimensions until you've got a conversion factor, so need is doing the work of making shelter and footwear commensurable and money is just the gauge or unit you measure the ratio in — and on that reading his claim that money is barren is the observation that a unit-conversion factor can't itself be a source of new quantity, which is why interest looks to him like getting something out of nothing. I've been argueing with one of the economists in the group about whether that analogy is actually fair to him or whether it smuggles in a modern picture in which value is a magnitude sitting there waiting to be measured, when maybe for him the exchange ratio is fixed by proportional reciprocity and agreement rather than by any measurable quantity. I'm also suspicious that its too neat to say Marx simply completed him, but I genuinely can't tell where the chapter stops reporting Aristotle and starts editorialising.

So: how fair is this summary, clause by clause? I'd like you to separate what a specialist on Aristotle would accept as accurate, what is a legitimate simplification for a popular book, and what is genuinely wrong or anachronistic — and for each of the substantive claims (need as the measure, money conventional, the claim about interest, the claim that all profit-seeking trade is condemned, the Marx lineage, and the closing remark about fiat money and banning interest) say what a defender of the stronger reading would push back with. In particular: is the dimensional-analysis / numéraire framing I'm fond of a good way in, or does it misrepresent what he's doing in a way that would make the rest of my reading go wrong; would the conclusions the chapter draws still follow if you corrected the summary; and is there anything in Aristotle that the chapter leaves out which would make him look less hostile to markets than it suggests?
