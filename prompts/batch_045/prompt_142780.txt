Hi — I'm on the allocations commitee of a small foundation. About $40m a year, four staff, monthly meetings, and we have to publish a one-paragraph public rationale for every grant we make.

We keep receiving what philosophers call Pascal's mugging: someone emails saying that unless we wire $5,000 to an account, they'll use their powers to cause some astronomically large amount of suffering, and when we ask how likely that is they just name a bigger number. We obviously say no, but we've never been able to say *why* in a way that doesn't also kill grants we actually want to make. Last month someone pointed out that if we're being consistent we either pay the mugger or we stop funding our pandemic-prevention portfolio, which has maybe a 1-in-10,000 shot at mattering at a civilisational scale. We also have a live $250k application from a group studying whether a certain industrial process could in principle set off a runaway atmospheric effect — real physics, genuinely speculative, maybe 1-in-a-million, catastrophic if real — and nobody can say whether that one is a mugging or not.

So we want one written rule. It has to be usable by a programme officer with no decision-theory background in about twenty minutes, it has to output a number or a yes/no (no "it depends"), and it has to survive being published, because donors read these things.

Here are nine candidate screens I've drafted, in my own words:

1. Value ceiling — any payoff above a billion units of value just counts as a billion.
2. Satisficing ceiling — once an option's upside clears a ceiling, we stop counting anything past it and rank options by what's under the ceiling.
3. Probability floor — probabilities below one in a billion round to zero.
4. Escalation screen — if the claimant can always name a bigger number in response to whatever probability we assign, the claim gets zero weight regardless of its content.
5. Mechanism requirement — we need a causal chain from our action to the outcome where every link has a probability we could defend to a sceptical colleague; one unspecifiable link and the whole thing is zero.
6. Track record — we only accept a probability if whoever produced it has a demonstrated calibration record at that order of magnitude; otherwise we throw their number out and substitute a base rate from a reference class of similar-sounding claims.
7. Median not mean — rank options by the median of the outcome distribution under our posterior instead of the average.
8. Robustness to our own disagreement — take whatever does best under the worst of the priors actually held around the table.
9. Information only — never fund on expected-value grounds at all; fund only to reduce uncertainty, and price that separately.

What I want back:

- Score each screen 0–10 on two things *separately*: (a) does it actually stop the mugger — including the version where he emails every month with a bigger number, the version where he offers to pay *us* an astronomical sum, and the version where the harm happens somewhere we could never verify; and (b) does it leave the real portfolio intact — and for each screen, name the one existing grant it would wrongly kill.
- I suspect two or three of these are the same rule wearing different clothes. Say which, and whether collapsing them loses anything that matters.
- A tier list: adopt as written / adopt with one patch you specify / drop. One sentence per screen, no essays.
- Under whichever screen you'd adopt, what fraction of the annual budget should we hold in reserve for mugger-shaped claims? Give me a number between 0 and 1, and say why it isn't 0 and why it isn't "as much as the biggest number implies."
- Where is the real work actually happening — in the decision rule, or in whatever tells us where the probabilities came from? One paragraph. If it's the second, say what the written rule should say about that instead.

Please don't rank them by how much decision theorists happen to like them. Rank them by whether a tired programme officer can apply them correctly at 4pm on a Friday, and by whether the sentence we publish makes us look like we know what we're doing.
