I need someone to make the strongest possible case for a position I keep botching in my reading group, so please just argue for it flat out: no hedging, no "on the one hand," steelman it and then some.

Background you can assume. There's a move Dennett likes to call a strange inversion of reasoning, and there are supposed to be two main instances of it. Darwin's: you don't need an intelligent designer to get design, you need a mindless algorithmic process and a lot of time. Turing's: you don't need a comprehending mind to get comprehension, you need a big stack of little mindless competences. And then people glue them together, roughly like this: mindless optimization produced real comprehension in us, so mindless optimization on GPUs can produce real comprehension in the things we build.

The position I want argued is that the glue is broken, and that it's broken for a structural reason rather than a contingent one. Darwin's inversion is an inversion about production: about how a system that has some competence came to have it. Turing's inversion is an inversion about constitution: about what such a system is made of, what's actually doing the work moment to moment. Call the mistake I think people are making the pedigree error, reading a system's present constitution off the mindlessness of the process that produced it. So argue three things. (a) The pedigree error is a real error and not just an uncharitable gloss on what people are saying. (b) Once production and constitution are pulled apart, the Darwin-to-Turing inference has nothing left to run on. (c) The part I actually care about: the two inversions are not merely about different subject matters, they are in tension with each other. In the Darwinian case comprehension has to be causally load-bearing, an adaptation that earns its keep, since organisms do better because they grasp how things hang together. On the Turing picture comprehension is at best a way of talking about a stack of competences and does no independent work of its own. One inversion needs comprehension to be real and efficacious, the other needs it to be idle, and you cannot quietly use both.

Two constraints so the result is actually useful to me.

First, do not let the argument rest on anything like "these systems are just statistics" or "it's autocomplete." Everyone in the group reaches for that and it kills the conversation. Run the argument at the level of the structure of the two inversions, such that your conclusion would still hold even if it turned out that something very much like current systems has genuine comprehension.

Second, there's an objection I can never answer and you should try to. The Darwinian lineage is shot through with constitution-level Turing inversions anyway: any evolved cognitive mechanism is presumably built out of dumber parts, and those out of dumber parts again, all the way down. Doesn't that mean production and constitution don't come apart at all, that Darwin's inversion just is Turing's inversion applied over and over, and that my distinction is an artifact of staring at too short a timescale? Handle that as charitably as you can and still land the conclusion, or, if you can't, tell me plainly which bit of the position has to be given up and what's left afterwards.
