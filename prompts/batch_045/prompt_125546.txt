I'm in a seminar on minds and machines this term and my position paper is due friday, and I've backed myself into a corner. We spent two weeks on the view that these systems aren't agents at all, they're simulators: what you get when you prompt a model isn't a single thing with goals talking to you, it's a character being played, and the next prompt plays a slightly different one. Someone in my study group thinks the whole debate is verbal and I half think she's right, but I can't say why exactly, and I don't want to hand in a paper that just restates the two slogans at each other.

I want a number, not a survey. Give me your credence distribution over these five options for current systems (big pretrained language models with tool use and some persistent memory, not hypothetical future architectures), and make them sum to 1:

1. no agent anywhere: there's a simulator and there are simulated characters, and calling either of them an agent is a category error.
2. exactly one agent, the system itself, and the characters are just how it presents itself or the roles it takes up.
3. many short-lived agents, roughly one per episode or persona, with no unity across episodes beyond whatever the memory store happens to carry over.
4. something else, and I mean specify it — the version I keep coming back to is that the agent is the whole deployment (weights plus scaffolding plus memory plus tools) rather than the model, so the same weights can be part of an agent in one deployment and not in another.
5. the question has no determinate answer: the concepts don't cut cleanly enough for there to be a fact of the matter, and the disagreement is really about which framing is useful for what.

Then decompose it. I don't need you to be exhaustive, I want the terms you're actually using: how much rides on what agency requires in the first place (a persisting, goal-updating control structure vs. mere dispositions to produce goal-directed-looking output), how much on whether loop closure matters (when a system's outputs get written to memory and fed back in as its own future inputs, does that make a subject or just a longer prompt), how much on whether there's any test that could tell one agent with a goal apart from many characters each locally coherent, and how much on whether the question is secretly normative — about responsibility, alignment, blame — rather than descriptive. I especially want you to deal with two things we argued about in section. First the counting problem: if the simulator story is right it looks like there are at least two agents in any conversation, the assistant-thing and a model of the user, and once you allow nested roleplay there's no obvious bound, so maybe is it an agent is the wrong question and how many agents at what nesting depth is the right one. Second, my own worry that there's no privileged self-report: if you ask the system what it was trying to do, that answer comes out of the same mechanism as everything else, so it can't adjudicate anything.

Give each term a rough number and a one-line reason, say how they combine into the distribution above, and tell me which single term your answer is most sensitive to. I'm definately sitting around 0.45 on option 4 and my friend is at 0.7 on option 3 — tell me where you think that disagreement actually lives, i.e. which sub-question the two of us would have to be disagreeing about for both of those numbers to be reasonable.
