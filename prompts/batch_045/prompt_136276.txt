I work as a statistician, mostly in causal inference and multilevel modelling, and I use Bayesian model comparison on a regular basis: marginal likelihoods and Bayes factors, prior sensitivity analysis, cross-validation, selection models of the Heckman type, post-selection inference, multiplicity and look-elsewhere corrections. I have essentially no training in philosophy of religion. I keep running into the fine-tuning argument in popular presentations, and I cannot tell whether it is a piece of reasoning my toolkit ought to recognise as sound once it is renotated, or whether it is doing something for which my toolkit has no slot. I would like you to translate it honestly and then give me the residue report. Please do not give me a bibliography, a literature survey, or a history of who has said what: I want the argument reconstructed and stress-tested, not reported on.

Proceed in three parts.

1. Translation. Restate, as faithfully as you can and in the vocabulary named above, the following three steps: (i) the claim that the constants and initial conditions are fine-tuned; (ii) the step from fragility — the target property holds only in a narrow region of parameter space — to improbability under a chance hypothesis; (iii) the step from improbability to confirmation of design, or of a multiverse, over chance. For each step, make the objects explicit: the sample space, the event whose probability is at issue, the hypothesis space, the measure or prior over it, the likelihoods, the resulting Bayes factor, and the sampling or selection mechanism that connects a hypothesis to what we could have observed. Where the argument needs something that has no name in my vocabulary, stop and say so at that point rather than smuggling in an analogue that behaves differently.

2. Residue report. Tell me what did not survive the translation. I have four worries I want you to work through explicitly, and I want you to add any further residue you find on your own.

(a) In my field, an astronomically small p-value attached to a region chosen after looking at the data is close to worthless unless one can say what other regions would have counted as equally significant. How does the argument's use of life-permitting as the target property fare under that discipline? And how does the argument change if I substitute a target property not defined by our own existence — say, the existence of long-lived bound structures, or of any system capable of storing and transmitting information — so that the post-hoc worry is defused?

(b) I want to stipulate away the measure problem. Suppose a future successor theory supplies a canonical volume measure on the space of possible constants, so that narrow region has an unambiguous meaning and the prior is no longer arbitrary. Does the rest of the argument go through under that stipulation, or does something else break once the measure is settled?

(c) A design hypothesis enters my Bayes factor only through a likelihood. What does a statistician do with a hypothesis whose likelihood is not merely vague but apparently unconstrained in either direction? Is there any standard practice — bounding, sensitivity analysis, imprecise probabilities, treating it as a model class rather than a model — that corresponds to what fine-tuning arguments actually do with it, or are they doing something else entirely?

(d) My toolkit treats observation selection — survivorship, truncation, selection on the dependent variable — as something to be modelled explicitly rather than mentioned afterwards. Is the anthropic objection best reconstructed as a selection model of that kind? If so, does the fine-tuning argument come out looking like an uncorrected selection effect or like a correctly conditioned one?

3. Verdict. I should tell you my prior inclination, which you should feel free to overturn: I suspect the whole argument is an uncorrected selection effect wearing cosmological vocabulary, of a piece with the inference from a double-six to the claim that the dice must have been rolled many times before. If that is a distortion, say so and say where. Then give me a bottom line: for each main residue, is it a defect of the statistical vocabulary — something the framework cannot yet express — or evidence that the original argument is less determinate than it looks? And finally, after translation, does the fine-tuning argument stand as defensible statistical reasoning, as reasoning that is defensible only within a framework carrying extra structure that statistics does not supply, or as something that does not survive translation at all? Commit to one of the three, and state explicitly which of my stipulations your verdict depends on.
