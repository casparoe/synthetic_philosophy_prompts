Please draft this as if you were both a scholar of Kant's political thought and the author of the deadpan institutional satire that political-theory blogs run once a year: before you write anything, spend a few lines listing the features of his 1795 essay on perpetual peace that become funny the moment someone has to administer them — the definition of a republic by the separation of the legislative from the executive, together with the consequence he draws that democracy in his strict sense, where all decide, is itself a form of despotism; the insistence that what is wanted is a federation of free states and not a world state, which would be a tyranny; the reduction of cosmopolitan right to a right of presentation rather than residence, argued from the fact that humanity shares the finite, spherical surface of the earth; the prohibition on contracting national debt for the external friction of states; the requirement that standing armies be abolished in time; the preliminary article forbidding forcible interference with the constitution and government of another state; the guarantee of the whole arrangement by nature, working through self-interest and the commercial spirit rather than through moral improvement; and the secret article under which states take counsel from philosophers but must never be seen to do so. Then write the piece itself.

The piece is a memorandum of roughly 700 to 900 words, dated in the 240th year of the Peace, issued by the Bureau of Republican Certification of the Federated Republics to the Federal Council, formatted as a one-paragraph cover memo from a Deputy Auditor followed by six to eight numbered findings, each with a compliance determination, in flat bureaucratic prose. Its occasion is the admission petition of the Meridian Union, a polity of about eleven million people that governs itself by continuous digital plebiscite: every citizen votes on every administrative act, including the issuing of warrants and the movements of its volunteer defence units. The Union is the most participatory state on earth, and it cannot be certified — work that irony without ever naming it, so that the auditors have to recommend that the Union install a representative legislature that does not execute, and have to explain to a population that votes on everything why rule by all counts, on the Federation's own founding text, as despotism. Distribute the remaining findings across the Federation's own embarrassments: the Volunteer Reserve, which maintains no standing army but sells households annual drill subscriptions and funds them with Peace Preservation Certificates issued against future commercial revenue; the Federal Enforcement Corps, which exists to secure republican constitutions in states it is forbidden to interfere with; the Orbital Habitation Question, in which nine thousand residents of free-orbit settlements claim the cosmopolitan right to present themselves and are advised that they enjoy a right of visitation but no right of residence, since the earth's surface is finite and originally held in common while orbital space is not; and the Bureau's dependence on an unnamed advisory panel whose conclusions bind the Council and whose members may not be acknowledged. Include two short quoted remarks, one from a Federal Council member described as the last avowed Hobbesian, angry at the enterprise for a reason a realist would genuinely give, and one from a member of that panel, bitter at being unthanked by order of the secret article.

Do not put any words in Kant's mouth inside quotation marks, and do not cite editions, sections, or page numbers; if a finding needs the doctrine, paraphrase it in the auditors' own jargon. The humour should come entirely from the articles being applied correctly, so that a graduate student who has read the essay recognises the satire as fair rather than merely silly, and the closing determination on the Meridian Union should land as a genuine reductio of something in the founding text rather than as a punchline about technology.
