I'm convening the final session of an upper-level seminar on eighteenth-century moral philosophy, and the reading for the day is Hume's essay on suicide. I want to open the hour with a poem rather than a handout — something that puts students inside the argument's joints before anyone starts reciting conclusions — and I have not managed to write it myself.

What I have in mind is a villanelle: five tercets and a closing quatrain, two refrains, iambic pentameter, in the plain, balanced, aphoristic idiom Hume's contemporaries wrote in, so that the speaker sounds like someone arguing a case rather than confessing a mood. The form should carry the philosophy rather than decorate it.

The two refrains are the two claims the essay leaves standing beside each other. One is the objection Hume is answering: that we hold a station assigned by Providence and may not quit it without the commander's leave, on pain of setting ourselves against the order of things. The other is his reply: that altering the course of nature is no rebellion, since the faculties with which one alters it are themselves parts of that order, and diverting a river and dying by one's own hand differ in degree and not in kind. Let them alternate across the tercets, each returning as though it had settled the matter, neither ever refuting the other, and let them meet in the final couplet. The point of the meeting is that the reply does not defeat the objection but relocates it: if my will belongs to nature's course, then so does the despair that pronounces my life not worth keeping, and the two claims sit side by side with nothing left to adjudicate between them.

The turn should land in the fourth tercet or so, and it should be the discovery the essay does not itself make: that the verdict that one's life is not worth continuing is issued by a faculty that is itself among the perceptions whose future tenor it is pronouncing on — a violent passion wearing the clothes of a settled judgment, in Hume's own vocabulary of calm and violent passions. If you can fit it without padding, the sharper version belongs here as well: that the self to which a duty is supposedly owed is, on Hume's own account of personal identity, not clearly the sort of thing that lasts long enough to be wronged or benefited. Do not force both in.

Three accuracy constraints, because I will be reading this aloud to people who know the text. First, get the essay's architecture right: the denial runs through three supposed duties in turn — to God, to society, to oneself — and the move is different in each. To society, the claim is that the person who withdraws injures nobody and merely ceases to do good. To oneself, the case is prudential: a judgment about the tenor of one's own future experience rather than an appeal to rights. Second, do not let the speaker trade on atheism or deny Providence; Hume's argument is internal to the providential picture, not a rejection of it. Third, do not let the poem slide into a general licence. Whatever permission Hume grants is tied to cases where life has become a burden or is of no further use to others, and how much further he goes — whether he thinks it can positively be a duty, or even praiseworthy — is genuinely contested. The poem should be honest about which reading it is staging instead of quietly helping itself to the strongest one, and it should resolve into neither vindication nor warning.

Then add a note of 120–150 words saying which reading of Hume the poem commits to and where it knowingly takes liberties, with the text or with the period; and, since I will be vouching for it in front of specialists, say how confident you are that this rendering would survive objections from someone who has taught the essay, and single out the one line or move you would most expect such a reader to call wrong.
