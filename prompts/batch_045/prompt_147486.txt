I am writing the requirements section of an internal methods document for a public agency that is about to procure a risk-scoring system, and I want to settle what I am asking of a fairness criterion before I go back to looking at candidate criteria. Please give me the specification rather than a survey: no literature review, no citations, no paper titles, no named theorems. I have read the standard results. What I want is the list of requirements whose joint unsatisfiability those results are actually about, the considerations that make each requirement compelling in the first place, and your view on which of the things people in this field demand are genuine desiderata and which are the preferences of one camp.

The setting, so that adequacy has something to be relative to. The agency administers a scarce and beneficial intervention — imagine a home-visiting nurse programme with more eligible families than places — and it ranks applicants using a risk score bought from a vendor who will disclose the score and nothing about the model. Two features of this setting matter to me and I do not want them treated as footnotes. First, the agency observes the outcome the score predicts only for the families it actually enrols; for everyone else the label is missing, and missing in a way the agency's own past decisions produced. Second, enrolment is not inert: it changes the outcome, and it changes who appears, with which label, in the data the agency will use to re-fit or re-contract the score next year.

Then the ask. Write the desiderata a fairness criterion for this score would have to meet: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect rather than collapse, the facts about the deployment it must be consistent with, and — the part I care about most — the requirements that are added by the two features above and that a criterion designed for a one-shot classifier with complete labels need not face at all. For each requirement, give the concrete case or consideration that motivates it, stated in this setting rather than in the abstract. There are particular places where I suspect requirements are hiding and where I want you to look: what a criterion can demand when the auditor sees only scores and decisions and not the model; what it can demand about error rates when one group's errors are partly unobservable by construction; whether the criterion constrains the score, the ranking, the threshold or the allocation, and whether it must be invariant to monotone transformations of the score or to the number of available places; whether it has to survive an auditor who is permitted to choose the subgroup after seeing the data; and what it should say when the intervention is a benefit rather than a burden, so that the familiar error-rate quantities switch their moral sign, and when the intervention is itself what generates the labels.

Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as negotiable, and where it is negotiable say whose preference it encodes and what we give up by dropping it. Flag explicitly the subsets that cannot be jointly satisfied, and for each such conflict say whether it is a conflict in principle — the requirements are incompatible whatever the world is like — or only a conflict given contingent facts about the population, such as differing base rates or a particular structure of missingness. I want to know which conflicts I could argue my way out of by changing the world and which I cannot. Where a subset is unsatisfiable, say what the most defensible weakening of one of the requirements would be.

Two short closing items. First, name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive candidate criteria, and say why it does that work. Second, if it adds something rather than merely labelling, give a scorecard of no more than three or four broad families of criterion — criteria stated in terms of calibration and error rates, criteria stated in terms of counterfactuals or causal structure, criteria stated as a demand that similar people be treated similarly — against your list, in a few lines each; skip it if it would add nothing.
