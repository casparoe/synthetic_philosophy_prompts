I'm staff (non-voting) to a state psychoactive-substances commission that assigns each drug to one of four regimes: criminal prohibition, decriminalized possession with a civil fine, a state-licensed retail market, or supervised access with no retail sale. I want a prediction and a consistency check on our next docket item, together with the reasoning that gets there.

Our track record, compressed:

- Cannabis: licensed retail market, potency caps, tax. Stated rationale: modest harm, prohibition had failed, a quality-controlled supply was feasible.
- Psilocybin: possession decriminalized, retail sale left criminal, state-supervised facilitated access for adults. Rationale: low toxicity and negligible dependence, but thin evidence for unsupervised use.
- Fentanyl and methamphetamine: possession downgraded to a civil infraction with treatment referral (Portugal-style), but no licit supply channel created; the commission said that manufacturing a legal supply of high-dependence drugs would itself generate harm.
- Kratom: left lawful but unlicensed, with no retail framework established, citing dependence liability and thin evidence.
- A class of synthetic cannabinoids sold as incense: criminalized outright despite acutely modest harm, because long-term human data were absent, with review deferred three years.
- Alcohol and nicotine: untouched, although both sit above several prohibited substances on the commission's own harm ranking; the reasoning given was that prohibition of entrenched substances predictably fails and that alcohol lies outside its remit.

New case: a two-year-old compound sold online as Vela. Orally active, about three hours, mild euphoria with reliably reduced anxiety and a next-day mood lift; across roughly forty thousand estimated user-months there are no overdose deaths and no withdrawal syndrome, and tolerance builds slowly; long-term data stop at eighteen months. The wrinkle I cannot fit to our precedents: Vela slows reaction time about as much as two drinks while leaving users feeling unusually clear-headed and confident — no odor, no slurred speech, no usable roadside test, and self-assessment of impairment is worse than with alcohol. So nearly all of its expected harm is third-party and, with current technology, unenforceable, while harm to the user looks lower than for anything we currently permit.

I've followed cost-benefit writing on drug policy for years and cannot reconstruct one principle behind these votes; every rule I fit to them has a counterexample in our own record. So: predict the ruling, identify the candidate rules our record is consistent with and say which you think the commission is really running and why, and give the decision that would be most consistent with its own precedents, naming the closest analog. Finally, tell me which hypothetical variant would best separate the competing rules — my own candidate is an otherwise identical compound whose impairment is cheaply field-testable — and say whether that is the right probe.
