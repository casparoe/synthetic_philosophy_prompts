I'm a first-year PhD student in cognitive science, and a few of us have been reading Dennett's paper "Real Patterns" alongside a well-reviewed popular book on AI and the mind that devotes a chapter to it. The chapter is the clearest thing I've read on the topic, but I have no way to tell how much of it to trust, and it characterizes the view roughly as follows:

"Dennett's three stances — physical, design, and intentional — form a hierarchy of prediction strategies ordered by how fundamental they are. At the bottom is the physical stance: specify the actual microphysical state, apply the laws of nature, and in principle you get perfect prediction. The two higher stances are shortcuts you fall back on when that computation is beyond you: the design stance, which is available only for things that were genuinely designed by someone and which treats the system as built to serve a function, and the intentional stance, which treats the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires. Because the higher stances are instruments for saving computational effort rather than ways of finding out how things are, the patterns they uncover are projections of ours rather than discoveries: anything predictable from the intentional stance is in principle predictable more accurately from the physical stance, and Dennett's willingness to call such patterns 'real' is really just a polite way of being an eliminativist about the mental. His positive criterion is bare compressibility — a pattern is present in a body of data whenever the data admit a shorter description than a bit-by-bit listing — and since even gerrymandered patterns, like the constellations, can be made to compress if you pick the right coding, the upshot is that patterns are in the eye of the beholder. Nor is this a live research program: the objection that any data set can be compressed given a friendly enough coding scheme, so that the criterion is either trivial or circular, was widely taken in the 1990s to have disposed of the proposal. The chapter then applies the framework to contemporary AI, concluding that because taking the intentional stance is the only tractable way to predict what a large language model will say next, Dennett's own account commits him to counting such systems as full-fledged believers and desirers."

I'm suspicious of parts of this, but not confidently. I'd previously been told that Dennett means "real pattern" as a genuine middle position — neither the furniture of the world nor a mere facon de parler — so calling him a discreet eliminativist sounds wrong to me; and I've also heard it said that on his view some patterns are visible only from the higher stances, which is the opposite of the claim that the physical stance always does better if you could just do the computation. To get a grip on the disagreement I invented a toy case of my own: a cellular-automaton world I call SLATE, whose update rule is short enough to write on an index card. In SLATE some regularities fall straight out of the rule; some are predictable only if you re-describe certain travelling configurations as persisting objects with trajectories and collisions; and there is a third, looser regularity in which the population of such configurations tracks the local availability of a resource, so that describing them as "seeking" it buys you some predictive grip. Would you go through the chapter's characterization sentence by sentence and tell me what Dennett does hold, what is a fair simplification for a popular audience, what is subtly wrong, and what is a live controversy rather than a settled verdict? In particular: is the three-stances picture a hierarchy or something more like a set of complementary strategies, is compressibility really the whole criterion, does the relativity-of-coding objection refute the account or is it one Dennett has resources against, is the design stance restricted to artifacts with designers, and does the conclusion about language models follow from the corrected version of the view or only from the chapter's distorted one? Please also run the corrected version past my SLATE case and tell me how many grades of reality I should count there.

Since I'll be repeating some of this to my reading group, please mark the places where your own account is most likely to be wrong or contested: where you are extrapolating from what Dennett says rather than reporting it, which of your readings a defender of the book's chapter could reasonably resist, and what would change your mind — a passage in the paper that says otherwise, a version of the relativity-of-coding objection that does real damage, or an argument that the intentional stance toward a language model fails one of Dennett's own conditions in a way that ordinary intentional ascriptions don't.
