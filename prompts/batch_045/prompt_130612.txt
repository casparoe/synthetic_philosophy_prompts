I am revising a paper on the bootstrapping problem and easy knowledge, and I have written myself into an argument I cannot fault and cannot accept. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The set-up. Say that a source S is *reliable* for a question-set Q just in case a high proportion — fix it at 0.9 — of the propositions S delivers about questions in Q are true. Let the No-Bootstrapping constraint (NB) say that a subject cannot come to know, or justifiably believe, that S is reliable by means of an argument all of whose factual premises are supplied by S itself. NB is what does the work in the familiar easy-knowledge complaints, and in the internalist demand that a knower be able to cite reasons for trusting her sources. It is what my paper defends. The following argument appears to refute it.

The case. Ana has exactly one route to the masses of the two hundred sealed vessels in a room: an instrument S with two channels, A and B. She has no independent scale, no testimony, no way to open the vessels. She can read S's display, but that tells her what S says rather than what is so, and I grant everyone that reading a display is not itself a deliverance of S about the masses.

(1) Every factual premise Ana has about the masses comes from S.

(2) A and B are causally independent: separate sensors, separate circuitry, separate power; neither is wired to the other, and a malfunction in one does not bring about a malfunction in the other.

(3) The questions in Q are heterogeneous — vessels of very different size and material, weighed at different times, under different ambient conditions, with the instrument re-zeroed between batches — so there is no single respect in which the readings could all be off together, no one stable feature of the arrangement for a systematic error to track.

(4) Ana queries both channels about each vessel. A and B agree on all two hundred.

(5) Where two causally independent channels agree on a question, that agreement is strong evidence that the agreed answer is true. This is just the ordinary logic of independent corroboration, the same logic we invoke when two measurements or two witnesses agree: agreement is far more likely if both are right than if both are wrong. And (3) answers the residual worry that they agree because they are wrong in the same way.

(6) So for each vessel Ana has strong evidence for the answer; and since reliability is nothing but a high proportion of true answers across Q, she has strong evidence that S is reliable for Q.

(7) Once reliability is established, S's deliverances are knowledge for Ana: for any further question in Q, S's saying that p gives her knowledge that p, by the core reliabilist claim. And she has it without any external calibration.

(8) Therefore NB is false. A source with internal redundancy certifies itself, and the easy-knowledge problem is not a problem but a consequence of the logic of corroboration.

Why I cannot accept (8). Nothing in (1)–(7) turns on what kind of source S is: any source stable enough to reproduce its own deliverances across two channels would be certified, including sources whose provenance gives me no reason at all to trust them. And a referee has told me that the argument is not even a counterexample to NB — if A and B are genuinely independent, she says, then this is ordinary cross-checking rather than bootstrapping, and NB was never engaged. I do not think that settles it, because whether the channels are independent in the sense the argument needs is not something Ana can settle from inside the room. But if the referee is right, I do not know what NB forbids.

What I want from you. Name the step at which the argument fails, explain why it looked all right, and tell me what it costs to give it up. I would prefer to keep the definition of reliability in the set-up and the reliabilist step (7), since both are load-bearing elsewhere in the paper, so my instinct is to locate the damage in (5) or in the move from (2) and (3) to (5) — but follow the argument where it actually breaks. Then tell me what survives of NB. In particular: if I give up that step, am I left saying that no source can ever certify itself, a claim that seems to spread to memory, to testimony, and finally to inference itself, since the only way to check a rule of inference is to infer? Is the right fallback a weaker, stable version of NB, or a non-evidential entitlement to trust one's sources, or a shift from an actual-frequency notion of reliability to a modal one, on which being right around here is not enough? And where does the diagnosis leave the standard claim that reasoning from perception to the reliability of perception is epistemically circular but not vicious?
