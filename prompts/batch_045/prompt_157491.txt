I'm writing a short piece for policy staff who commission studies and evaluations, and who mostly assume that if a report would substantially change what they believe, commissioning it is worth it. I want one case that pries apart how much a signal tells you from how much the signal is worth.

Construct a single ordinary decision problem — a hiring decision, a treatment choice, a procurement, your pick; no thought-experiment machinery and no perfect predictors, though a realistically imperfect test with known error rates is fine — and give it twice. In the first version the signal moves the agent's credence a long way, from roughly even odds to near-certainty either way, and is nevertheless worth exactly zero: expected utility conditional on receiving it equals expected utility without it, so the non-negativity that Good's theorem guarantees holds as an equality rather than an improvement. In the second version the same signal, with the same priors, the same likelihoods and the same utility function, is worth strictly more than zero. The two versions may differ in exactly one structural feature, which you have to name, and it cannot be the informativeness of the signal, the priors, the likelihoods, or the payoffs. Give the numbers and the expected-value calculations for both, so that it is visible that the belief change is identical while the verdict is not.

Then say which feature is carrying the case and what happens to the second version without it, and give the strongest defense of the worthless signal in the first version — value for decisions not yet foreseen, epistemic value, and so on — along with your reply to it. Finally, my readers will point out that the theorem only ever says information cannot hurt in expectation: is a third version available, in which the same signal, private and free, has strictly negative value for an agent who still updates by conditionalization and still maximizes expected utility? If not, say which assumption has to be given up and what conceding it costs.
