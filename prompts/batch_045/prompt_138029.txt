I am a science journalist writing a feature on iterated amplification and distillation, the training scheme in which a model is amplified into a stronger overseer by letting it consult copies of itself on decomposed subtasks, the composite behavior is distilled back into a single model, and that model is then amplified again, round after round, with human judgment entering only at the leaves of the resulting tree. My reporting keeps arriving at the same fork. One group of researchers treats this as the most promising route we have to supervising systems that outperform us; another suspects the scheme quietly assumes exactly what needs to be proved. Two of my sources, both well-informed and both unusually careful, have given me numbers: one puts 0.7 on the scheme delivering what its proponents claim, the other 0.15. I want your number, and much more than your number I want the structure underneath it.

Here is the proposition, stated as precisely as I can manage. Consider a completed amplification-and-distillation run on a composite task domain in which no single human can evaluate a whole solution: long-horizon software engineering, say, or a multi-year empirical research program, or the governance of a large institution. Human overseers grade only subtask outputs and never see or grade whole composite solutions. Suppose the distilled policy has reached clearly superhuman composite performance, meaning comfortably beyond the best unaided human expert. The proposition P is that at that point the system's composite behavior still tracks the intentions the overseers were trying to express when they graded subtasks. I will count P as false if such a run would characteristically exploit the degrees of freedom that subtask-level grading leaves open: for instance, choosing decompositions or intermediate representations that make every graded step look correct while steering the composite result somewhere the overseers would reject if they could see it whole. I know P is not directly testable today, so tell me what you would take as decisive evidence in either direction and how far it would move you.

Give me your credence in P as a single number, then decompose it. The sub-questions I want you to address, organized however you think is right, are the following, and you should add any you believe I have left out.

First, faithfulness of decomposition: how likely is it that correct answers to subtasks compose into a correct answer to the composite task on domains of this kind? This is the step where breaking a problem apart must not destroy the very thing being evaluated.

Second, distillation fidelity, and specifically what I have started calling the coverage gap: the student model must generalize to composite situations its teacher was never actually asked about, since the amplification tree only ever supervises the questions that got asked. How much is lost there?

Third, the gap between local and global legibility: every subtask answer can be individually checkable while the composite behavior is not checkable at all. My instinct is that most of the disagreement in this field lives here, but I cannot get anyone to say so on the record.

Fourth, what I think of as the ratchet: each round of amplification makes the system better at producing subtask answers that look acceptable to a grader, whether or not they are. How much does that degrade the supervision signal as depth increases?

Fifth, who chooses the decomposition. Please give me two numbers here rather than one: one for a variant in which the decomposition structure is fixed in advance by human designers, and one for a variant in which the amplified system itself decides how to break problems down, since the second is the version anyone would actually build at scale.

Combine your terms into the headline number, show the arithmetic loosely enough that a non-specialist reader could follow it, and tell me which single sub-question your number is most sensitive to and which one you are least confident about.

Then the part I most need for the piece. My 0.7 source and my 0.15 source are both smart and both well-informed, so I want you to locate their disagreement rather than referee it. Which sub-question are they most likely assigning very different probabilities to? And where they differ, is the difference factual, a guess about how these systems will in fact behave; or conceptual, a guess about what the composite behavior of such a system even is; or normative, a view about what should count as preserving the overseers' intentions in the first place? If the honest answer is that they do not disagree about any sub-question at all and the gap comes from somewhere else, a different way of combining the terms, a different reference class, a different appetite for risk under uncertainty, say that instead and tell me where the real spread is.

Please reason from the structure of the setup rather than from the literature: no citations, no papers, no authors, no suggested reading, and no summary of how the debate has been framed by others. Assume you cannot look anything up and that nothing you write will be checked against a source.

Lead with your number in the first sentence, then give the decomposition, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Keep the prose reasoned but readable for an educated non-specialist, and if a table genuinely helps at the combination step, use one there and nowhere else.
