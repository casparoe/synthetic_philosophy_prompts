I work in formal verification for safety-critical autonomous systems, and I want to understand the argument in AI alignment about worst-case versus average-case guarantees by translating it into the vocabulary I actually use, and then by seeing exactly what the translation loses.

What I have available: transition systems and Markov decision processes; temporal-logic specifications; the distinction in probabilistic model checking between a property holding on all paths and holding with probability one, which are not the same thing once the path space is infinite; quantitative bounds of the form P ≥ 1 − δ over a specified scheduler or sampling distribution; the distinction between an oblivious and an adaptive adversary, and in particular between one that must commit before seeing the system and one that sees the system, its specification, and its random coins; assume–guarantee contracts between components, together with the fact that such guarantees compose only to the extent the assumptions are actually discharged at runtime; runtime monitors, safety envelopes, and fallback controllers that take over when a monitored assumption is violated; and tail bounds, which I know stop being useful once the loss distribution is heavy-tailed enough that its first moment does not exist.

Two stipulations before you begin, because these are where I expect the translation to strain. First, take the loss distribution over outcomes to be Pareto-tailed with tail index below one, so that expected loss is undefined rather than merely large. I want to know what becomes of any position whose content is stated in terms of expected value, and whether that position has any reformulation in my terms at all. Second, note that in my systems the measure-zero set of violating paths is real: satisfaction with probability one permits a nonempty set of bad executions, and the reason I normally do not care about them is that nothing steers the system onto them. Suppose instead that something does — an optimizer that selects actions precisely by searching for the paths on which the property fails. Handle that case inside the vocabulary if it can be handled, and flag it if it cannot.

Please translate, one at a time, as faithfully as you can:

(a) the position that alignment work should aim at guarantees that survive adversarial optimization — that a system which is safe across the deployment distribution but not under optimization pressure has not been shown to be safe, on the grounds that the deployment distribution is not exogenous but is in part produced by the system itself and by other systems competing with it;

(b) the position that worst-case guarantees are the wrong target — that they are either unobtainable, since for any system one can construct inputs or environments on which it fails, or vacuous, since a bound that must hold everywhere says nothing about what will in fact happen — and that what we should be doing instead is improving expected outcomes under the distribution we will actually face, accepting that residual tail risk remains;

(c) a third position I keep encountering in outline and cannot reconstruct: that the right target is neither a worst-case guarantee over behaviour nor expected value over outcomes, but a small set of hard invariants the system may never violate — not taking irreversible actions, remaining corrigible, not enlarging its own action space without authorization — combined with deliberate indifference to the quality of everything else. Call it the thin-invariant view if you like. Say what it looks like when written as a specification in my terms, and whether it answers the objections to (a) and (b) or only relocates them.

Then give me the residue report, in three lists: what translated cleanly; what translated only after you quietly altered the meaning, with an explicit statement of what you altered; and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. For the third list I want your verdict on each item as to whether it reveals a gap in the vocabulary I use or a gap in the original dispute. In particular, assess these four candidates for untranslatable remainder:

(i) the adversary the guarantee must hold against is the system the guarantee is about — in my work the environment is indifferent to my proof, and the nondeterminism I resolve with a scheduler is not itself trying to find the counterexample;

(ii) the transition relation is not fixed in advance — the system may acquire new actions, revise its own objectives, or construct successors whose state space I cannot enumerate, so there is no model over which to check anything;

(iii) the specification is itself the contested object — I can prove things only against a formula someone hands me, and I have no vocabulary for a specification that is uncertain, learned, or legitimately revisable rather than merely buggy;

(iv) there is no repetition — my probabilistic guarantees are frequencies over many runs, whereas the deployment I am being asked to reason about is a single run with no resampling, so almost-sure properties and tail bounds have no long-run interpretation.

Finally, answer directly: is the worst-case-versus-average-case disagreement one disagreement or several that have been run together, and if several, does the translation let you say which part is genuinely about what to prove, which about what to build, and which about what to bet on under uncertainty? I would rather be told that some of the debate dissolves under translation than be handed a tidy translation of something muddled.
