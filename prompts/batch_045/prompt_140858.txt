Not sure if this is the right place to ask this, but here goes. I'm a 3rd-year PhD student in evolutionary biology (microbial experimental evolution — endless plates, serial transfer, patches that crash) and I keep getting the same shape of result out of my simulations and I have no idea what to call it.

Here's the shape. There are local populations — patches — and they're open: individuals migrate in from a common pool, and the patches don't really reproduce, they just persist for a while and then get washed out or crash. Now take a trait with two effects. (a) Within a patch, in any given round, the bearer does slightly better than its patch-mates — grows a bit faster, whatever, nothing dramatic. (b) The trait makes the patch it is in last longer before crashing. Both effects are perfectly ordinary individual-level effects; no spooky action.

What keeps happening is that the trait spreads mainly because of (b), and I don't think it's any of the usual stories. It isn't kin selection: I can push patch-mate relatedness down to basically zero in the sim and it still works, because the covariance isn't coming from who is related to whom, it's coming from the fact that bearers are physically sitting in the patches that don't crash, so they get more rounds of (a). And it isn't the standard multi-level story where the group with more cooperators pumps out more total offspring and that's the between-group term — because I can set things up so the long-lived patches actually produce *fewer* total cells over the whole run than the short-lived ones (slow growth, long life), and the trait still wins. What seems to matter is how many selective episodes happen inside the patch before it dies, not how much comes out of the patch.

Second case, different substrate, same shape as far as I can tell. A symbiont strain that extends its host's lifespan. The strain is only mildly better than its competitors within a host, and it gets diluted every round by immigration from the environment. Long-lived hosts host more rounds of within-host competition, so the strain ends up winning there — even though the extra rounds don't produce anything extra for the strain's own descendants in particular, and the hosts don't leave more offspring.

What I can't work out is how to talk about this. Is the patch (or the host) a unit of selection here, or not? It doesn't reproduce, and I can't see that it has a fitness in any normal sense — what it has is a lifespan, or equivalently a turnover rate, or a duration measured in particle generations. And the trait's success looks like it comes from the patch's duration acting as a multiplier on how much lower-level selection happens, not on which way the lower-level selection points. I've been writing 'episode-count selection' in my notes, which is probably awful, and I half suspect it's a special case of something — some version of niche construction (the trait lengthens the environment in which it happens to be favoured), or maybe just what falls out of a multi-level / Price-style decomposition once you put a clock in it, or maybe it's that old life-history thing about output and generation time coming apart, pushed up one level.

So: does this configuration have a name, or a home in the levels-of-selection literature? But honestly I don't want a label, I want the carving. Concretely, the things I'd want an answer to pin down:

- Is the collective doing any causal work at all here, or is it just a background condition on individual-level selection, the way temperature is? What experiment or what bit of formalism would settle that?
- How exactly is this different from the group-lasts-longer / group-is-less-likely-to-go-extinct version of multi-level selection, given that I can make output and duration come apart in either direction?
- Is it niche construction or ecological inheritance, or does the fact that the modification changes only how much selection occurs (and not what it favours) put it outside those?
- Could it be redescribed as plain individual selection with a strange fitness measure? If so, is there anything actually wrong with that redescription?
- And the edges: what would have to be true for a case not to be an instance of this? Say the patches were closed rather than open; or the trait's effect on patch lifespan didn't depend on how common the trait was in the patch; or the within-patch advantage were exactly zero; or the trait shortened patch life but still made its bearer more likely to be in whatever persisted.

Please don't spend words on references or citations or who said what — I can chase all that myself. I want the anatomy: what the thing is, what it is not, which of its near neighbours it's closest to and why, and if there genuinely isn't a term for it, a name plus a definition tight enough that I could look at a new case and say yes or no.
