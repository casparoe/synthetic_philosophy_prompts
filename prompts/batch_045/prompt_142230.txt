I keep arriving at an argument I don't like and can't defeat, and I'd like to know which step is rotten.

Queueing, jaywalking, and restaurant tipping all look like conventions in the philosophers' sense: each is one among several available solutions to a recurring coordination problem, and any of the alternatives would have served just as well. But if that is right, the content of the local rule is arbitrary, and so the only consideration in favor of conforming is that nonconformists get sanctioned — the rule itself has no grip on someone who is willing to absorb the dirty looks. Two consequences seem to follow, and I doubt both.

First, a visitor appears to owe the locals nothing. She never joined their practice, and it is hardly her fault that their equilibrium differs from the one she grew up with; so jumping a queue in Lisbon, crossing against the signal on a street where everyone else crosses, or leaving nothing on the table in a New York diner is at worst impolite — a breach of local etiquette rather than a wrong done to anyone.

Second, consider a bakery in a city I will call Marisburg, where a place in line may be given away or sold and a transferred place is treated by everyone as fully valid. If the locals there are entitled to treat standing-when as a tradable good, then a visitor from Marisburg who offers to buy a place in a first-come-first-served queue elsewhere seems to be doing exactly what her own convention licenses, merely in a jurisdiction with a different arbitrary preference.

Is the argument sound? If not, which premise fails? And does the answer come out the same for the queue, the crossing, and the tip?
