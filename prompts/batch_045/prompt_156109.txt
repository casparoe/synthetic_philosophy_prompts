You are a senior scholar of Greek and Roman biography who also works in moral psychology, and you have served on many fellowship committees: you know that a fundable two-page prospectus needs a question narrow enough to answer in a year, an angle nobody has already taken, a method stated as something a person actually does, and an honest failure condition.

I am applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need that proposal. Here is my half-formed idea, which I would like sharpened rather than merely endorsed.

The current philosophical discussion of learning virtue from lives — exemplarist views and their critics — seems to me to share one assumption: that a life teaches by supplying a pattern the reader admires and then imitates, so that the live questions are whether admiration can be a route to moral knowledge at all, and whether imitation is even available to someone in very different circumstances. But the genre everyone cites as the model does not obviously work that way. Plutarch sets his subjects in Greek/Roman pairs and appends an explicit comparison to each pair; and in his programmatic remarks he says he is writing lives rather than histories, and prefers small incidental details — a remark overheard, a jest, a gesture, a moment of temper — to the great public deeds, on the ground that these reveal character where battles do not. That looks less like pattern-supply and more like calibration: the reader is not handed a template to copy but given two lives to hold against each other and a stock of unguarded moments to measure them by, and what they come away with is a sense of direction in a space of contrasts rather than a model to reproduce. Call this, if it is genuinely distinct, the contrastive calibration model of moral learning from a life.

Write the proposal so that it does the following. State the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer within a year, and say why the standard admiration-and-imitation framing leaves that question open. Give the method month by month, including how one would test the calibration model against the text rather than merely illustrate it with favourite passages. Confront the awkward fact that Plutarch's own comparisons frequently slide away from comparing characters and end by declaring one of the pair greater: say whether that verdict is a different activity that undercuts the calibration reading, or a piece of the same one, and let that be a live branch point rather than a footnote. Describe what a positive result would look like and, separately, what would count as the project failing — and tell me which failure you think is likeliest. Keep the scope honest: if this is too big for twelve months, say where to cut.

Do not give me a bibliography or a literature review by name; I will add citations myself, so please refer to positions and arguments descriptively. Before writing, spend some time brainstorming two or three alternative framings of the question and choose the one that is most tractable and least already-worked-over; then write the proposal itself in a formal register, around two pages.
