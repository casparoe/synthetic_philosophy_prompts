Me and two people from my union local have been doing a sloppy little reading group on automation — I'm the one who writes internal tooling that quietly deletes a chunk of my teammates' workload, so for me this is less academic than it is for them — and last week one of them shut down an argument with "the hand-mill gives you society with the feudal lord; the steam-mill, society with the industrial capitalist." The work he wanted it to do was pretty specific: he said Marx thought technique is the engine of history, so if the current wave of automation really is a new productive force, then fighting over regulation or workplace bans is pointless, the relations of production will change to fit whether we like it or not, and the rational move is to push the automation as fast as possible and let capitalism break on it. I went and looked the line up afterwards and found it in The Poverty of Philosophy (1847, the polemic against Proudhon), where it comes right after "In acquiring new productive forces men change their mode of production; and in changing their mode of production, in changing the way of earning their living, they change all their social relations." That threw me a little, because the chapter's actual beef seems to be with treating economic categories as eternal ideas rather than as historical products — which isn't the same as a theory about machines driving everything — but I don't know how much weight that context can bear.

So: don't just tell me "Marx was a technological determinist" or "no he wasn't, that's a misreading," I've heard both from people who were very confident. Give me a menu instead. What are the distinct precise claims the mill line could be making, from the defintely platitudinous to the really strong, and can you sort them? The distinctions I keep chewing on are things like: is it a claim about co-variation within a whole form of life (a mill is shorthand for an entire way of living, so nothing causal is being said at all), or a feasibility claim (at a given level of technique only some property and exploitation arrangements can stably work), or a selection claim (among the arrangements that could work, the ones that actually get adopted and stick are the ones that keep developing the forces), or the fettering/destabilisation claim (relations that stop developing the forces get burst)? Does it require that the forces have their own independent tendency to develop, or could their development itself be explained by class relations and property forms — because if the second, the explanatory arrow looks reversed and the line becomes much less interesting? Does it say technique uniquely pins down a set of relations, or only that it constrains them, and does the fact that apparently similar tools and farming techniques show up under wildly different property systems kill the strong version or is the strong version only ever about epochs in the long run? Is it a claim about the economic base only, or is it smuggling in the claim about ideas and categories too?

For each reading I'd like a verdict: which is a platitude nobody would deny, which is substantive and actually defensible, which is false, and — the part I care about most — which one(s) my friend's accelerationist argument would need in order to go through, and whether the weaker readings get him anything at all. Two more things that would help. First, if the line is a schema, what would each reading fill in the blank with: "the ____ gives you society with the ____" — what's the AI-mill society on each reading, and are any of those answers things I'd actually want? Second, does anything in Marx's own account of machinery under capitalism complicate even the strong reading — machinery adopted to cheapen labour power and produce relative surplus value, deskilling, the reserve army of labour, and that Grundrisse passage where knowledge becomes a direct productive force and necessary labour time shrinks so much that the value basis of the whole system starts to erode? My worry is that all of that gives you a capitalism that keeps generating crises and immiseration rather than a capitalism that hands you a successor for free, in which case "accelerate and wait" is drawing a conclusion the slogan never licensed.
