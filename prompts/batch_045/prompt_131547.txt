Something about me, so you can pitch the answer. I am a retired fisheries scientist, forty years of stock assessment, and in the last couple of years I have started reading philosophy seriously for the first time — mostly philosophy of science and measurement, and whatever I can follow in moral philosophy. A friend put me onto the debate about whether violence has declined, the one carried on in the well-known popular book and in its critics: absolute counts versus per-capita rates, what gets to count as violence at all, and whether there is a fact of the matter. I have no stake in the outcome. What I cannot stop noticing is that the argumentative shape of this debate is one I spent a career fighting.

In my field we estimate abundance from an index. The classical case is catch per unit effort: the catch divided by how hard people fished, used as a proxy for how many fish there are. Two failure modes were drilled into me. In the first, the stock collapses while the index stays reassuringly flat, because the fleet keeps finding the last aggregations and the fishers get better at finding them; the ratio holds up while the thing it is supposed to track falls apart. In the second, the index falls much faster than the stock, because the easily reached concentrations are fished out first and the effort goes into unproductive water. There is jargon for these two directions — I was taught hyperstability and hyperdepletion, though I do not know how standard the words are outside my corner of the subject. The general lesson I took from them is not about fish. It is this: a ratio stops indexing anything when the denominator and the thing you are trying to measure are not independent, when the counting frame or the reference class is itself shaped by the process under study. And crucially, no additional measurement of the numerator or of the denominator separately will tell you the index has come loose; you only find out by some independent handle on the quantity itself.

I think I see the same configuration twice in the violence material.

First instance. The striking numbers about prehistoric and small-scale societies — the double-digit percentages of deaths attributable to violence that get quoted — come from skeletal assemblages. But the number of people who lived, the population you would need in order to turn injured skeletons into a rate, is estimated from the same sites, by the same excavations, often through the burial record itself. And whether a dead person is in the sample at all depends on burial practice: formal cemeteries in one period, mass graves and unburied dead in another, differential treatment of women, children, and outsiders. So the membership of the population at risk is partly an output of how violent the period was and of how the living disposed of their dead. The fraction of skeletons with weapon trauma can go up because burial got more inclusive, and it can go down because the dead stopped being buried where we dig.

Second instance. The long peace in the core of the state system is accompanied by the relocation of fighting to colonies, frontiers, and client territories, and the populations counted as peaceful grew by conquest and incorporation. Here the community over which the rate is computed is not a fixed background against which violence happens; part of how it became peaceful is that the violence was pushed outside the counting frame, and part of how the frame grew is the violence. When a critic says that per-capita rates flatter us because the relevant unit was redrawn, and a defender replies with better data, they seem to me to be talking past each other in exactly the way my colleagues and I did when one of us reported an index and another reported a survey.

So the thing I want a name for is this. An index of some quantity — a rate, a proportion, a per-capita figure — whose denominator, reference class, or counting frame is not given independently of the process being measured but is caused, selected, or reshaped by it, with the result that the index can move in the opposite direction from the quantity, or at a different rate, and no refinement of the measurements of either part settles which is happening.

Why I do not think I already have the answer. Goodhart's law and Campbell's law are about agents responding to a measure that has become a target; that is a feedback loop through beliefs about the indicator, and although something like it may operate at the margins of this debate, the coupling I am describing does not run through anyone's awareness of the statistic — nobody in a prehistoric burial ground is gaming an index. Simpson's paradox and the ecological fallacy concern reversals between subgroups and an aggregate when the partition is fixed and known; my problem is that the partition is what is in dispute. Selection bias and endogenous sampling are close but, as I have met them, too broad to be the concept I am after, and they are usually stated for samples drawn from a population whose existence is not at issue. A friend who reads political philosophy mentioned a boundary problem about who is in the demos, which sounded adjacent but seemed to be a normative question rather than a question about whether a number tracks anything. And please do not tell me that it all depends on what we mean by violence: I can see that the definition of violence is contested and that broadening it tends to pull in the cases whose trend is flat or rising. That may be one phenomenon alongside mine, or it may be the same phenomenon in different clothes, but it is not an answer on its own.
