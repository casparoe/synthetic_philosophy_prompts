Ok so a friend and I have been arguing about the van Meegeren affair and I want something more organized than what we managed. Facts as I have them — fix anything I've got wrong, but please don't send me to a book, I can't get to a library this week. Late 1930s, a Dutch painter Han van Meegeren starts turning out new "Vermeers", partly out of money and partly, he later claimed, out of revenge on critics who'd dismissed his own painting. The big one, a Supper at Emmaus, is authenticated by Abraham Bredius, about as authoritative a Dutch-painting scholar as there was, and bought by a Dutch museum for a fortune. Several more follow, and one ends up with Göring, who swaps a pile of looted paintings for it. After the war van Meegeren is arrested for selling Dutch cultural property to the Germans, is looking at something much worse, and so confesses to having painted the things himself — which means proving it by producing another one under watch. He's convicted, gets about a year, dies not long after. Later technical work on the pigment found modern synthetic hardeners in the paint — bakelite-ish stuff, I think, I'm not sure — so they were never undetectable, just never tested. And then the coda, which is the bit my friend and I can't get past: the pictures are worth real money again now, as van Meegeren forgeries, and they hang in museums.

What I want is the case run through the value-of-originals debate properly. The taxonomy I've been working with has four entries: the autographic or performance view, where what matters is that this object is the residue of a particular person's actual acts in a particular place and time; the documentary view, where the painting is a trace and a fake is a corrupted piece of evidence about the past; the surface view, where only what meets the eye matters, so an indistinguishable forgery is just as good; and the relational or conversational view, where a work's value is a function of where it sits in an ongoing art-historical and critical conversation.

Three things I actually want you to get to rather than gesture at. First, what I keep calling re-anchoring: after exposure these paintings didn't fall to the price of some anonymous 1930s canvas, they got re-catalogued under a new name and partly recovered, and some people argue they're more interesting now than they'd ever have been as mediocre van Meegerens. Which of the four accounts predicts that, which has to bolt it on as a separate fact, and does the recovery show that the original's premium was never in the object in the first place? Second, palimpsest value, which I made up but can't shake: once the deception is public the object carries both the history it pretended to have and the one it actually had, and it becomes in some sense a work about forgery — so can exposure add value, and if yes is that a reason to think the four-way taxonomy is missing a category? Third, the recoil. Fakes get destroyed or locked in vaults and curators talk about them as contaminating, which is stronger than worthless. Where does that extra negativity come from on each account, and is any of the four even trying to explain it?

Then run it the other direction. Does the episode confirm any of the four, embarrass any, or land in a blind spot? My friend says it refutes the surface view outright — people were having exactly the experience and then the value evaporated. I'd of thought that's too quick, since it wasn't a perfect forgery, since part of the collapse is just a market refusing to price objects whose attribution is unstable, and since the buyers' enjoyment itself seems to change on exposure in a way the surface view has to call irrational. Who's closer? One contrast I can't place either: the Hitler Diaries sit in a vault and nobody wants them, while van Meegeren's hang in museums. Same taxonomy, opposite fates — real difference in the objects, or just in how good they are at what they do? And be straight with me about where the frame goes quiet: which of these questions do all four accounts decline to answer, and what would you have to add to say anything about them.
