I spent a working life as an experimental physical chemist, mostly reaction kinetics, and since retiring I have been reading in the philosophy of science. I find that my judgments about which explanation is the best one are far more confident than my sense of why, and I would like help extracting the principle I seem to be using, or being told plainly that no single principle is available.

Here are the verdicts I trust.

First, Wegener's continental drift. The fit of the coastlines, the matching fossil and rock sequences on either side of an ocean, the distribution of ancient glaciation: all of it fell into place at once. His critics answered that he had no mechanism capable of moving a continent, and I find that I side with the critics. The absence of a mechanism was, to me, a genuine explanatory defect, and when seafloor spreading arrived with a mechanism attached, the standing of the theory changed, and changed rightly.

Second, Semmelweis in Vienna. He got the men in his clinic to wash their hands in chlorinated lime before entering the ward, the death rate from puerperal fever collapsed, and he had no account of what was being transmitted beyond something cadaveric carried from the dissecting room. His seniors refused to accept this, largely because the mechanism was unknown. Here I find that I side against the critics: withholding acceptance on the ground that no mechanism had been supplied was, to me, unreasonable. I take this to be in uneasy company with my first verdict, and I would like to know whether the two can both stand.

Third, the unseen planet. An anomaly in the motion of Uranus was used to posit a further planet, a position was computed, an astronomer pointed his telescope and there it was. The same style of reasoning applied to the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion posited an intra-Mercurial planet, which was never seen and whose job was eventually done by a different theory altogether. I regard the first inference as having been a good one when it was made and the second as not. I want to say this without leaning on how either one turned out, and I am not sure I can: in both cases the data were a residual anomaly, the posit was an invisible body, and the fit would have been perfect if the body had been there. My own diagnosis is that the difference is one of constraint: in the first case the anomaly fixed where the thing had to be, so an observation could have refuted it, whereas in the second a broad family of orbits and masses would have served, so almost nothing could have. I would like that diagnosis made precise and put under pressure.

Fourth, unification. I count it as a real virtue, not an aesthetic bonus, that one set of equations covers electricity, magnetism and light, and that it does so by way of a definite propagation speed which turned out to be a speed already measured in another context. Fifth, and in what I take to be tension with the fourth, breadth as a vice: when someone proposes that a long list of unrelated chronic illnesses are all really manifestations of one thing, or that one fluctuating quantity explains a whole catalogue of complaints, the more phenomena the account swallows the less I trust it. I would like to know what separates the unification I admire from the breadth I distrust.

My tentative principle, such as it is, has two clauses. The best explanation is the one under which what we actually observed was least surprising; and a posit earns its place only if it has what I have started calling independent purchase, meaning that something other than the phenomenon it was introduced to save could have shown it to be wrong, in a way that was fixed before anyone looked. The second clause is doing a great deal of work and I do not trust it. It is what lets me fault the theory with a knob for every anomaly, and it is what separates the two planetary episodes, and it is what I suspect separates unification from sprawl. But I worry that it eats the first clause: if a hypothesis is allowed enough free parameters it can make any observation unsurprising, so surplus flexibility has to be penalised, yet the penalty cannot simply be a count of parameters, since I do not admire the theory with fewer knobs when its one knob is doing the work of six. I also worry that I am reasoning in a circle, since whether a posit had independent purchase seems itself to be something I judge after the fact.

The case I return to is dark matter against modified gravity. Rotation curves are the anomaly; one response adds matter, the other revises the dynamics. I lean towards the added matter, and I initially counted the fact that it also bears on gravitational lensing, on the formation of structure and on features of the microwave background as corroboration of the Maxwell kind. Then I noticed that the additions have been shaped, case by case, to each new anomaly, and I began to wonder whether this is corroboration at all or the protean breadth I said I distrusted. I would like the principle to rule on this, and to say something about the multiverse style of explanation of the physical constants, which I cannot decide whether to admire or to despise.

What I am asking for, then, is a candidate principle stated precisely enough to be applied, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on cases I have not listed; an honest account of which of my verdicts it forces me to give up; and, if it can be said briefly, what the principle presupposes. It has begun to seem to me that my practice of choosing the best explanation is only sensible if the features I am calling virtues are the sorts of features a true theory would be expected to have, and I would like to know whether that is a commitment I can defend or only a habit my career trained into me.
