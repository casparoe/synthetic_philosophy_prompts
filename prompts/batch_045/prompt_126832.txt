I am the teaching assistant for a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and the unit that consistently leaves my students unable to see the forest is the accuracy-first or epistemic-utility-theory program. They can follow the individual arguments well enough — the dominance argument for probabilism, the expected-accuracy argument for conditionalization, the attempts to extend the framework to chance-credence norms, imprecise credence, and outright belief — but they come away unable to say which commitments are load-bearing and which are decoration, and they tend to assume that a view is a view because someone has a name for it rather than because it occupies a stable cell in the space of options. So I want a handout that does the opposite: build the taxonomy from the logical space first, and only then locate the actual positions in it.

Build me a decision tree, sized for a one-page handout plus a page of notes, with roughly ten to fourteen branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves, taking a student from the root commitment — that the epistemic standing of a doxastic state is a matter of how accurately it represents the world, and that norms are to be derived from that value — to a named position or to an explicit unnamed leaf. Every node must be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, so no node may be of the form do you accept the accuracy argument for probabilism, and ideally each split should be illustrated with a miniature case small enough for a margin: a three-cell partition, a coin of unknown bias, a single credence of 0.7 in rain. The distinctions I most want doing the work are these: whether accuracy is assigned holistically to whole credence functions or additively proposition by proposition, and relatedly whether inaccuracy is local (depending only on the credence assigned to the true cell) or global (depending on the whole profile); what strength of propriety the measure is required to satisfy, and whether anything weaker such as mere propriety or a no-sure-loss condition is admitted, since I suspect this node is where permissivism and uniqueness actually separate; which decision-theoretic principle selects the norms, state-wise dominance against ex-ante expected-accuracy maximization against something weaker; whether the framework is synchronic only or is also meant to govern updating, inquiry, and the value of information; whether accuracy is the sole epistemic value or is weighed against simplicity, informativeness, or cautiousness; and which attitudes are in the domain at all, with credence-only views separated from those that also treat full belief, comparative confidence, imprecise or mushy credence, and self-locating credence.

Three further requirements shape the deliverable. First, tag each leaf with which of four headline results it can still vindicate — probabilism, conditionalization or plan-conditionalization, a chance-credence norm of the Principal Principle kind, and a determinate verdict on whether one or many credence functions are permitted by a body of evidence — so students can see the cost of each branch. Second, identify at least two combinations your tree allows that nobody, or almost nobody, actually occupies, and give one sentence on why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Third, and most important for my purposes, audit the tree for measure and partition sensitivity: flag any node whose answer is really a choice of formalism rather than a philosophical claim, especially the choice between the quadratic and logarithmic families of scoring rules and the choice of the algebra or partition over which dominance is assessed, and say for each flagged leaf whether its verdicts would survive swapping one for the other. Also flag any pair of positions the tree cannot genuinely separate rather than forcing a distinction between them, and mark any node whose correct answer depends on a formal result you are not confident about instead of asserting it. I will supply the readings myself, so refer to positions and arguments only, with names where they help and none invented where they do not.
