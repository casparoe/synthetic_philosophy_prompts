I've sat for about nine years as one of the lay members of the ethics committee at a mid-sized hospital, and there is a shape to how we handle decisions for patients who can't speak for themselves that I've never been able to make sense of. I'd like to know what someone would have to believe — about persons, or about obligation, or about what a relationship actually is — for the practice to hang together as the sensible thing to do.

Here is the shape of it, as I've seen it in more than one place. When a patient can't decide, we go looking for a family member. Nobody proposes the best-informed stranger, or the attending physician, or a judge; the law's list of who to ask runs by closeness of relationship — spouse, then adult children, then parents, then further out — though a patient who has named someone else in advance has that name honored even over a spouse. If there is no one, we treat it as a misfortune and hand the decision to a public guardian, and everyone involved regards that as a falling-back, not an improvement. But the moment we have the family member in the room, we tell them something like this: this isn't your decision and it isn't about you; don't tell us what you want, and don't tell us what would be good for you or for the rest of the family; tell us what *she* would have wanted, judging by what she said and how she lived. And we enforce that. If it emerges that a son is declining a procedure because he cannot face his mother dying, or because a long convalescence would consume what he'd inherit, we treat that as a contaminant, in the way we'd treat a bribe — and if he won't set it aside, we go looking for a different surrogate. Our committee's worksheet has two columns. One asks what the patient would have wanted; the other asks what those left behind say matters to them. Both are filled in at every meeting. Only the first column goes anywhere near the decision.

What bothers me is that the two halves seem to pull in opposite directions. We choose the decision-maker on the basis of a relationship and treat the lack of one as a loss; then we require that person to reason as though the relationship mattered only as a source of evidence about somebody else's wishes. We treat a person's own stake in the outcome as a corruption — but only here. The same institution expects families at the bedside, funds bereavement counseling, and will spend an hour of a senior clinician's time on a family meeting. And it isn't that we take the family's interests to be worth nothing: when there is nobody at all, we don't think we have arrived at a cleaner and more objective decision. We think we have arrived at a worse one.

Two cases bring it out. A man in his sixties has said for years that he wants everything done, whatever the odds; his wife and daughter, who would be the ones caring for him at home for the next decade, say they cannot do it, and ask that treatment be limited. A woman of eighty-five said more than once that she would never want to be kept alive on a machine — but she said it in the years she spent nursing her own mother through exactly that, and the person now being asked to report her wishes is the daughter who watched her do it. In the first case the family's years of caring are, as far as I can tell, supposed to count for nothing in the answer. In the second, the relationship is doing enormous work and we still have to describe it as evidence.

So, my questions. What does this arrangement presuppose? Can you reconstruct the view of persons and obligations that it only really makes sense under, and say which rival views it quietly rules out — in particular, could someone who thinks that relationships are themselves where moral requirement comes from, and that partiality is not a bias to be corrected but the ordinary texture of a life, endorse the practice as it stands? Or is the practice compatible with a much thinner view than I've assumed: could somebody who holds that nothing matters morally except each individual's own wishes and welfare endorse everything I've described, on the grounds that close family are simply the best available evidence of those wishes and the most reliable people to act on them? If so, I'd like to know which parts of what I've described the view actually needs and which are just habits it happens to sit well with — I suspect some of it is doing no work at all, and I can't tell which. Is the tension I feel between the conflict-of-interest rule and the bereavement budget a real inconsistency, or does it dissolve once the practice is described properly? And if we replaced the implicit view with the relational one, what would concretely have to change: the second column, the rule against acting on one's own stake, who gets asked, what we say to a patient who has written down instructions that conflict with what the people around them need?
