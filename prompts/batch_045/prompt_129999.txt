I teach a seminar on moral psychology and I am planning to screen Christopher Nolan's Memento alongside the skeptical literature on self-deception: the arguments that ask how, if at all, a single subject can both know that p is false and bring it about that she believes it, given that the intention seems to give itself away and the resulting state seems to be one mind holding contradictory beliefs.

The use I want to make of the film is specific, and I am not confident the film can bear it. Call it the diachronic solution. In the standard cases the difficulty is supposed to be that deceiver and deceived are the same subject at the same moment; Leonard Shelby's strategy appears to dissolve that difficulty by separating the two roles in time. The man who writes the caption on Teddy's photograph and records the license plate knows exactly what he is doing and why; the man who later reads his own handwriting as the testimony of a stranger does not. At no instant does anyone believe both the note and its denial, and no one seems to be trying to fool himself in the sense the skeptics say is impossible — it looks much more like one person deceiving another, who happens to be his own later self.

So: does the film support that reading, or quietly undercut it? I want three things. First, whether the diachronic split is a solution or only a relocation — whether the skeptical puzzle reappears as a question about whether the writer and the reader are the same agent, the same self, or the same bearer of responsibility, and whether the film has a view on that, given how much of it is about personal identity over time, the Sammy Jankis material, and Leonard's closing remark about needing to believe that the world still exists when he is not looking at it. Second, where the film shows him as knowing, at the moment of acting, that he is manufacturing his own evidence — if it does, the paradox may return in temporal disguise, and the split is only apparent. Third, where the film resists the reading and an honest critic has to say so: it may be a film about memory, testimony, and identity rather than about self-deception, and pressing it into this argument may flatten it.

Tell me which scenes you would screen to make the case, and whether anything the film does with notes, photographs, tattoos, and the arrangement of his own environment adds to the philosophical debate something the arguments do not — for instance the thought that a deception can be something you build a world for rather than a state you are in.
