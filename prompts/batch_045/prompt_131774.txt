I'm writing the third chapter of my dissertation — roughly, whether determinations of death can be insulated from the practices they license — and I need a constructed case to anchor the section where I argue that the neurological and the circulatory criteria can come apart in a way that is neither a dispute about evidence nor a dispute about timing. The cases I build myself either collapse back into the familiar brain-dead-on-a-ventilator example or smuggle the conclusion in by stipulating a definition.

Here is what I need. One paragraph, set in a contemporary hospital, using only interventions that are in clinical use now: no speculative technology, no exotic pathology, and nothing settled by stipulating what the law or a guideline says. In the case there must be a patient who, at a given time t, is dead; and a counterpart, real or hypothetical, whose physiological state at t is indistinguishable from that patient's and whom nobody would count as dead. The difference between the two may lie only in what happened before t or in what is planned after it — not in any difference in their present physiological state, and not in anyone's epistemic situation.

The case must also block the standard reconciliation: the move that says the circulatory criterion is only a proxy for the neurological one, since circulation matters because its prolonged absence destroys the brain, so that the two criteria cannot genuinely conflict. Neither verdict in your case may be defended by appealing to one criterion's tracking the other. And the divergence should be uncomfortable for at least one of the three standard accounts — whole-brain death, the circulatory-respiratory criterion, and the view that the organism dies when it ceases to function as an integrated whole — in the sense that the account is forced to return a verdict its own defenders would not want. One route you might take, though I would rather have a case that meets the constraints than this particular case: after death is declared on circulatory grounds and an interval of asystole is observed, a circuit restores flow to the organs while the brain's supply is deliberately excluded; if you use any device, keep to technology available now and describe it accurately.

Begin with the scenario itself — no preamble, no restatement of what I have asked for. Then, leading each part with its bottom line in the first sentence:

1. What each of the three accounts says about the patient's status at t, and why; and which account is forced into the unwelcome verdict.
2. Which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if it is removed — specifically, which familiar and by now uninteresting case the construction collapses into.
3. The strongest reply available to someone who wants the declaration of death to stand, where the reply is not simply a restatement of their preferred criterion.
4. In one sentence: whether the case is best read as support for abandoning the determination of death in favour of a consent-based standard for procurement, or whether a death-based framework can absorb it.

I do not want references, citations, or suggestions for further reading — I know that literature. What I want is the construction, and the argument that it meets each of the specifications above.
