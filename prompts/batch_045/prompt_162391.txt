I am to give a single eighty-minute seminar to a colleague's second-year environmental studies class on whether the destruction of habitat might, on net, reduce the amount of suffering in the wild. The students will arrive with conservationist reflexes, a fair amount of field biology, and no formal ethics; I have one session and no follow-up.

Do not give me the argument or a survey of the literature. Give me a plan for the exposition, and in particular for the dependency structure: what has to be in place before the question even appears well-formed to them, and why those pieces have to come in the order they do. I have in mind at least three candidates for the groundwork: that the welfare of wild animals matters at all; that the value of a population and the size of a population are separate axes, so that a thriving ecosystem and a large total of welfare can come apart; and that a population can be demographically stable while being composed overwhelmingly of lives that end very early. Which of these is genuinely load-bearing, and which can be asserted in passing and picked up later?

The claim I want them able to state at the end is what I have been calling the subtraction gain: a possible improvement consisting entirely in there being fewer lives lived, so that no creature is made better off, no creature is spared a harm, and the beneficiaries are individuals who never exist. I take it that this gain registers only under a total view of welfare and disappears under an average one. Tell me where that conditional belongs in the sequence, and whether presenting it as conditional will simply license the students to withhold judgement and learn nothing.

I have drafted a carrying case: a low-lying coastal field, abandoned by a tenant farmer, that either reverts to wet meadow and marsh or is kept in improved pasture, with the difference made up of invertebrates, voles, frogs, and gulls rather than anything charismatic. I like it because it is bounded, because no one's livelihood hangs on it, and because the same mechanism that makes the loss of habitat a candidate improvement makes the restoration a candidate harm, which I hope will unsettle them in a useful direction. Tell me whether that case should carry the whole session, what else it needs to have and what it must conspicuously lack, and why I should not instead open with something they already have feelings about, such as a rainforest, a predator reintroduction, or a suburban deer cull.

Then the misconceptions, in the order in which you think they will actually arise, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off and an indication of any that should be allowed to run for a minute because they are doing useful work. The ones I anticipate are: that nature is balanced and the suffering in it minimal or merely functional; that more habitat and more animals is straightforwardly better, so that the question is perverse; that the suffering is confined to the end of a life and therefore outweighed by the rest of it; that a gain which helps no existing animal is no gain at all; that the view commits us to paving the countryside; that the empirical question must be settled before any of this can be discussed; and finally the slide from welfare-improving to all-things-considered required, which I want left standing as a visible remainder rather than dissolved.

I also need one question to put to them in the last ten minutes that would let me distinguish their having understood the claim from their having merely been made uncomfortable, and I need to know what I should explicitly decline to settle at this level and say out loud that I am declining it.
