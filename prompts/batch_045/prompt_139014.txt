I co-host a tiny podcast with my brother — we get one guest on for about an hour and just pick at a single idea untill it breaks. Next month's guest is someone I'll call Rowan, who defends what he calls "pessimism about borrowed verdicts": roughly, that when you take someone else's word for it that a thing is wrong — not because you can see the reasons yourself but because they said so — something is missing from your moral situation, even if the belief you end up with is true and you act on it. I've been turning this over on my own for months and I've got a pile of half-baked objections, but I've never studied this stuff, so I can't tell which of my objections are the ones people actually press and which are just me going in circles.

Can you write me nine or ten interview questions, each two or three sentences, in plain spoken language — I'll be reading them out loud, so no acadamese — plus a follow-up for each, keyed to what you think Rowan will most likely answer. Here's what I want covered:

- Rowan's favourite move, whenever I bring up the feeling that a borrowed verdict is hollow, is to say something like "that's a question about how the belief got there, not about whether it's a good belief." I want a run of two or three questions that makes that move harder and harder to lean on, ending with one where the distinction itself is what's up for grabs.
- The asymmetry with ordinary expertise. If a mechanic tells me my brakes are shot and I act on that, nobody thinks I'm missing something; I'm being sensible. He'll say morality is different because what matters is acting for the reasons, or that the act has no special worth if you do it on someone's say-so. Press that — and then push on whether that makes the whole worry kind of selfish: the person I helped doesn't care whether I understood why helping was right, so who exactly is this defect a defect for?
- A case I made up that I like: I've got two friends who agree with me that keeping promises matters and that not wrecking someone's life matters. In a particular mess I ask them which of those weighs more here, and they say the promise, definately. That feels like borrowing without borrowing the verdict. Is borrowing a weighting different from borrowing an answer, and why would his view treat them the same way — or differently?
- Another one: when someone tells me "that thing you did hurt me", I just take their word for it, and that seems completely fine, not hollow at all — but they aren't an expert, they're the person affected. Maybe all moral deference is secretly like this, or maybe this case just shows the worry is really about expertise and not about testimony. I want the question to force him to say which.
- Chains and crowds. What if I defer to someone who was themselves only passing on what they'd heard? And what if, instead of one trusted person, I go by what a bunch of people who disagree with each other roughly converge on — is that still borrowing, or is it more like reading a map?
- A worry I can't shake: if everyone in a community defers to the same one or two voices, you get a moral monoculture — everybody's reasons are the same reasons, so nothing corrects the mistakes. That looks like a harm of deference that has nothing to do with whether any individual's borrowed belief is defective, and I want to know where it fits in his picture, if anywhere.
- Looking backwards: I decide something is wrong on my own, clumsily, with my own reasons, and years later find out that someone whose judgment I now trust had come to the same conclusion. Does he have to say my original verdict was defective the whole time and just got lucky? That answer feels wrong to me but I can't say why.
- Kids. Most of us learned most of our morals by being told. If borrowed verdicts are defective, are we all walking around with a broken moral picture that we later repair — and what would the repair even consist in?
- Finish with one question about what would count as evidence against the view. Not "what would change your mind" in general — I hate that question — but something concrete he'd actually have to give up.

He's quick and he'll have a stock answer for the obvious stuff, so please skip anything that just asks him to explain what the view is.
