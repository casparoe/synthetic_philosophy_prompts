Bit of background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired physiologist, spent close to thirty years with electrodes in rodent brains — the old intracranial self-stimulation work, rats on a lever for direct stimulation to the point where they'd ignore food and water. Retired now, and a couple years ago I wandered into a philosophy reading group at the public library, got hooked on the AI alignment side of things, and I've been chewing on wireheading and reward tampering ever since.

I keep trying to sort the cases out in control-theory language, because that's how my brain actually works, and I've ended up with three failure modes that the stuff I've read seems to lump together under one word:

- meter tampering: the agent corrupts the channel that reports the value, so the readout stops tracking whatever it was supposed to be measuring. Short the thermocouple, the gauge reads 37.0 forever.
- setpoint tampering: the measurement channel stays honest, but the agent rewrites the target the controller is aiming at.
- plant tampering: meter and setpoint are untouched, but the agent rewires what's downstream so that moving the measured quantity becomes trivially easy and loses its connection to what the designer actually wanted. My rats at the lever were this one, I think — the reinforcement machinery doing its normal job, the animal just finding a shortcut around the whole business of locating food.

What I want from you: what are the most important implications of taking that three-way split seriously? Three things I care about in particular.

One: in reinforcement learning the reward signal looks like it's doing two jobs at once that my thermostat keeps separate — it's the measurement and it's the objective. So do meter tampering and setpoint tampering collapse into the same thing there? And if they do, does that make an RL agent more prone to this than other kinds of controller, or less?

Two, and this is the one I actually have to say something about out loud: I'm on a hospital committee looking at a proposed trial of closed-loop deep-brain stimulation for treatment-resistent depression. Device reads a neural marker off the patient; when the marker crosses a threshold it stimulates; the stimulation is supposed to relieve symptoms and, well, feel good. In principle the patient can learn to push that marker around — certain thoughts, certain activities. One of the psychiatrists on the committee keeps calling this "wireheading a human being," and I can't tell if its a real description or just a spooky metaphor. Which of my three modes would it have to be a case of for his charge to stick, and what would we have to look at or measure to tell which one it is?

Three: whether any of this is a genuinely new problem, or whether every homeostat with a writable setpoint was always halfway to being a wirehead. Does the "reward isn't wellbeing" lesson come out differently across the three cases, or is it the same lesson all the way down?

Please just argue it out — I'm not going to chase down papers, so I'd rather have the reasoning than a bibliography or a list of names to look up.
