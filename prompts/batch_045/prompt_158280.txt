I hold a two-part position about self-locating uncertainty, and I want to know what would have to be true for it to be false.

First part: the self-indication assumption is the correct treatment of indexical evidence. Hypotheses are to be weighted by the number of observers they contain, so that, other things equal, a hypothesis on which the world contains twice as many observers is twice as likely to be the one I am in. I also believe — and I would want to be told if this is wrong — that this is equivalent to the self-sampling assumption with the reference class widened from actual to possible observers; if that equivalence does not hold, count it as a defeater and say why.

Second part: the presumptuous philosopher result is a bullet to be bitten, not a reductio. That I exist at all is evidence of astronomically large strength for hypotheses on which there are very many observers, and it is defeasible in principle by ordinary evidence, even though in practice no body of evidence anyone could assemble would overturn it. I hold both parts because every rival I know of forces me to explain away sampling inferences that this treatment gets right, and because I have not found a version of the "unacceptable consequence" argument that does not simply restate the intuition the view denies.

Tell me what would count against it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender of the view would concede it as counting against them or would dismiss it — I care about what the view's friends grant, not only about what its critics say. Where the position survives only behind a qualification (a restriction to finite worlds, a stipulation about the reference class, a choice to count observer-moments rather than persons), mark that, and tell me whether the qualification has insulated the view into unfalsifiability and whether that should bother me in the way it would bother me for an empirical claim.

Among the pressure points I want you to consider — use them where they are load-bearing, do not merely hand them back to me, and do not feel obliged to cover all of them:

- the counting-unit problem: a case in which the number of persons and the number of observer-moments come apart. On one hypothesis a single subject is awakened twice, with amnesia and no way to distinguish the awakenings; on the other, two subjects are each awakened once with the same subjective experience. Person-counting and moment-counting disagree about the weight each hypothesis receives, so the view has to say what it counts; I do not know whether either answer is defensible, or whether the disagreement itself shows that the weighting is arbitrary.
- the staggered variant: the same structure, but the extra observers on the larger hypothesis are created one after another across geological time and never coexist. Does the view then depend on a simultaneity assumption it cannot state? And does the doomsday-style reasoning that the self-indication assumption is supposed to cancel return in some form once the observers are spread through time?
- the reference-class dial: if self-indication is self-sampling with a wider class, then how wide the class is — actual humans, all physically possible observers, all computationally possible ones — sets the strength of the update, and can apparently dial it from modest to astronomical. Is the view then voluntarist about how much my own existence tells me, and if so is that a defect in a theory of evidence?
- unlearnability: the likelihood ratios the view extracts from bare existence are enormous, and they are also sensitive to how finely the space of hypotheses is partitioned. Does the position thereby become unfalsifiable in practice and arbitrary in its numbers, and is that fatal, or is it what a claim about prior probabilities can live with?
- calibration: across all the agents who actually exist and apply the rule, would the credences it recommends be calibrated — would average credence track frequency? If not, is calibration simply the wrong test for self-locating credence, or is it the one consideration I should treat as decisive?
- the infinite case: the view breaks down when hypotheses posit infinitely many observers, and the associated measure problems generate the familiar paradoxical results. Does any of that count against a version restricted to finite worlds, or is the restriction gerrymandered to fit?

Also state, if there is one, anything the view predicts that its rivals do not, where the prediction could have come out otherwise — in cosmology, in the treatment of Sleeping Beauty, or elsewhere — and say how much weight that carries against the defeaters above. What would move me most is a case on which following the view leaves me worse off by its own lights, or on which it recommends that I stop trusting the reasoning that produced it.

Give me exactly five numbered items, ordered from most to least decisive, each one or two sentences long, each naming a defeater, whether defenders concede it, and whether it is fatal or merely awkward. No introduction and no conclusion.
