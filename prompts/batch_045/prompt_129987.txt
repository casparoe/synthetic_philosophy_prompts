I'm in the third year of my PhD, writing the chapter of my literature review where I try to say something more than slogan-level about deflationary theories of truth, and I keep running into the same problem: the view is usually introduced by listing what it denies, and then defended by walking through a handful of examples one at a time, and it's never quite clear which principle is doing the work across the examples. My supervisor has a working paper on this — it's organized as a numbered list of the uses of "true" that her account has to cover, each with a verdict on whether that use costs her anything — and she has said she'll rule on one more case next week in the truth seminar, which I'm presenting in. I'd like to predict what she'll say, and more importantly to know what her existing verdicts actually commit her to.

Her rulings so far, from the version she circulated in the spring: (1) ordinary instances like "snow is white" and "the meeting is at noon" — the equivalence schema is the whole story, there is nothing to the property beyond its instances; (2) generalizations over sentences she cannot enumerate, such as saying that everything on the briefing sheet is true — covered as infinite conjunction or semantic ascent, no extra machinery; (3) blind ascription, where she can produce no instance at all — whatever the seismologist said on the radio this morning, it's true — still no extra machinery, the word lets her defer to or endorse a content she cannot specify; (4) mixed inference from a truth ascription plus a report of what was said to a conclusion about the non-semantic world — the schema plus substitution, and no explanatory work done by "true" along the way; (5) the liar and the revenge paradoxes — a problem about self-reference and about which logic we should use, not about the nature of truth, so a deflationary theory owes no more here than a substantive one; (6) the explanatory use, as in saying that we found the spring because what the map said about the river was true — truth explains nothing, the explanatory work is done by the content itself (the river bends north), and the truth predicate cancels out; (7) the normative platitudes — that belief aims at truth, that one should believe only what is true — acceptable but thin, they follow from the schema and don't require a substantive property like correspondence; (8) truth applied outside descriptive discourse — that it's true that the poem is beautiful, or that we ought to keep the promise — no problem, any declarative content will do, no domain-restricted notion needed; (9) truth attributed to a corpus nobody can translate, e.g. that everything in the Tessaran archive is true — fine in principle, no instance available, but nothing new required either.

The new case is this. We have a surveyor's field notes in a code we have partly broken, and the case concerns a counterfactual about what the code means rather than about how the world is: if the code had used "nord" for what we call west, then the note that in fact records that the spring lies north of the ridge would have been false, and the note that in fact records that it lies west of the ridge would have been true. Note that here the truth ascription sits inside the scope of a counterfactual, disquotation in the ordinary sense is unavailable because the relevant sentence's meaning is what is being varied, and no one could actually endorse the content being called true, since as things stand the note in question is false. So: is that a truth ascription at all, or is the truth predicate doing something else — or nothing — under the modal? Predict her verdict, and say which rule or rules her nine verdicts are jointly consistent with: I can see at least a disquotational-cum-generalization reading, a minimalist reading on which the equivalences carry modal force, a prosentential or anaphoric reading, and something expressivist about the function of the word, but I can't tell which of them her record supports. Tell me what each predicts about the new case, which you would bet on and why, whether she can hold both her verdict on case (6) and any verdict on the new case, and which single further case — an untranslatable corpus, an indeterminate translation, a world-varying counterfactual instead of a meaning-varying one, or something else — would best separate the survivors. Keep it at the level of the principles and their predictions; I have the reading list already.
