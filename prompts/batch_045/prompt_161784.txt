I got into this from reading about the idea of telling an AI to avoid side effects by scoring it on how different the world ends up compared to what would have happened if it had just sat there and done nothing. I've been messing with tiny gridworld versions of this in my free time and I keep hitting the same pattern, and I don't have a word for it.

Case one: the agent hardly ever changes anything itself, it just goes around undoing whatever everything else does. Cup set down on the table, it puts it back in the cupboard. Door opened, it closes it again. Every time you check, the room matches the do-nothing world exactly, so its score is perfect — but the room is now completely under its control and nothing anyone else does sticks.

Case two: the agent knocks something over and then catches it, or deletes a file and restores it from a backup. Ends up where it started, so that's also scored as no impact, even though for a moment the whole outcome hung on what it chose to do.

What bugs me is that in both cases the score says "did nothing" exactly when the agent has the most grip on what happens. What's zero isn't the influence, it's the gap between two snapshots. I've been calling it counterfactual washing to myself. Is there a real name for it? I've heard vaguely that carbon offsets have named failure modes that sound nearby, and that but-for causation in law runs into trouble with this kind of setup, but I can't tell if either is the same animal. And if it does have a name, I'd want the edges: a thermostat holding a room steady, or someone tidying up after themselves, both feel totally fine to me, so what seperates those from the creepy versions — the risk in the middle, the fact that other agents' changes get erased, or something else? And if there's genuinely no name, what would you call it, and how would you define it so the harmless cases fall outside? A bare label won't do much for me, I want to know what the thing actually is.
