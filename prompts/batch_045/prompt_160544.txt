I want you to work through the Helicobacter pylori story as a case study in the ethics of belief — Clifford's evidentialism versus James's will to believe — and I want the mapping done properly, not the moral of the story.

Facts as I remember them (correct me if any detail is off, but I don't think the dates carry the weight): through the mid-20th century the working dogma was that peptic ulcers were an acid-and-stress disease; a widely cited 1954 biopsy study found no bacteria in the stomach and basically settled the question for a generation, even though people had been reporting spiral organisms in gastric tissue since the late 19th century and again around 1940. In 1981 a pathologist named Robin Warren and a young physician named Barry Marshall started looking at spiral bacteria on gastric biopsies in Perth; they got their first culture in 1982 more or less by accident, because a lab tech left a plate sitting over a long weekend instead of tossing it after the standard two days. Their paper was rejected by the Australian gastroenterology society in 1983 as bottom-decile. Marshall failed to infect piglets, so in 1984 he drank a culture himself, got gastritis, biopsied it, and cleared it with antibiotics — which satisfied Koch's postulates for gastritis but not for ulcers. Mainstream practice didn't really switch to antibiotics for another ten years or so, and they got the Nobel in 2005.

Here's what I want, in this order.

First, set the case up cleanly before you judge it. Who is the subject of the belief-attribution at each stage — Marshall, Warren, the reviewers, the field as a collective? What exactly is the proposition: that spiral bacteria colonize the human stomach; that they cause gastritis; that ulcers are an infectious disease; or all three run together? Those have very different evidence behind them at very different times, and I suspect the whole story looks more Cliffordian or more Jamesian depending on which one you track. Then run James's machinery on it honestly: was Marshall's option live, forced, and momentous, or was wait and gather more data a genuine third branch, in which case it was never a genuine option in his sense at all?

Second — and this is the part I care most about — I want you to split what I'll call the belief-question from the pursuit-question: what you're permitted to believe versus what you're permitted to investigate, fund, or act on provisionally. My worry is that the H. pylori case looks like a win for James only because the two get slid together: Marshall's stubbornness looks vindicated as pursuit, and then gets laundered into vindication as belief. Tell me whether that's what's happening and whether James's argument actually needs the slide.

Third, James's self-verification idea. Distinguish two mechanisms I want kept apart: one where believing a hypothesis causes the evidence for it to get produced, and one where believing partly constitutes the truth of what's believed, the way it might in a social or agential case. Which one, if either, is the H. pylori case an instance of? Does the first one do any real work for James, or is it a much thinner claim than the one he needs?

Fourth, a standpoint asymmetry I keep tripping over. Marshall's decision was first-personal and momentous for him; the establishment was gatekeeping a large stream of clinician claims, most of them false, and there's a natural thought that the evidential bar is legitimately higher for an institution than for an individual even about the same proposition. Call it the throughput point if you like. Is that a genuine principle or just a rationalization for conservatism? Does either Clifford or James have the resources to state it, or does it sit outside both of their frameworks?

Then the reverse direction, which is where I want you to be disciplined about what the case can't show. Two things specifically: (a) Clifford frames credulous belief as a social wrong, a damage done to the community of inquiry — but here it's arguable that the harm, a decade of acid suppression and ulcer surgery, came from the side that withheld belief. Can his own social framing be turned on the evidentialists, or does he have a clean reply? (b) We remember Marshall because he turned out to be right; unsuccessful dissenters don't get Nobels. Given that selection effect, how much does this case actually support either side — is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or just decoration on a position arrived at independently?

Finally, tell me where the lens goes quiet. The bottleneck here looks partly instrumental rather than doxastic: a culture discarded on a two-day schedule, a stain nobody was using, no animal model, plus commercial interests built around acid suppression. Does an ethics of belief have anything to say about a case where the real obstacle was method and incentive rather than anyone's poor epistemic conduct? I'd rather you say the theory is silent there than stretch it to cover it.

Please don't give me bibliography, further reading, or the keep-an-open-mind moral. Just the analysis.
