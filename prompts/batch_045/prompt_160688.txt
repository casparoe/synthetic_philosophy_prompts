Hi — I need a hand with a speech and I think I've boxed myself in.

I'm on the executive board of the hospital workers' local at Mercy General (about 400 of us in the barganing unit, roughly 90 of whom never joined and get the contract anyway, because we have to represent everyone in the unit whether they signed a card or not). We just ratified after an eleven-day strike. We got enforceable staffing ratios, which is what the thing was actually about, and we gave up most of the wage steps we'd asked for, so it's a win and not a win and everyone in the room knows exactly which is which.

The speech is at the ratification party on Saturday — union hall, potluck, kids running around under the tables, three minutes, four tops. The room will be: people who walked the line for all eleven days; about thirty members who crossed (my cousin among them, and honestly for reasons I can't say I'd have handled better); the 90 who never joined; families; the hospital's new HR director, who somebody invited and who I guess is actually coming; and my old labor-relations professor from community college, who is the reason I ever opened a labor law casebook and who will absolutely hear it if I say something incoherent.

Here's the part I'm stuck on. I keep trying to find a way to talk about the people who weren't there, and every version I write comes out as one of two things: either it's a debt speech (you owe us, you took what we paid for) or it's a forgiveness speech (welcome back, we forgive you, it's behind us). Both feel wrong, and I think I've worked out why — both of them treat the strike as a thing we made and then distributed, a benefit some people consumed without contributing to. But that isn't what happened. What happened was a few hundred people doing something together for eleven days. The raise is divisible, and it does get shared; the having-been-there isn't divisible, and you can't get it retroactively or pay it back. So the only honest register for that bit is forward-looking — the door is over there and it opens next time — and I can't figure out how to say that without it landing as a threat or a membership pitch.

So the rules I'm giving you, since they're what makes it hard: nobody gets named and the s-word doesn't get used. Nobody gets forgiven (that isn't mine to hand out, and it also concedes the whole debt framing). It can't claim we won. It can't turn into a grievance list or a set of demands for next round. It can't thank the HR director in a way anybody would read as sincere. And it shouldn't lean on 'solidarity' — my dad will visibly roll his eyes if I say it twice, so once at most.

The one thing I'd like my old professor to catch and nod at: somewhere in there I want the point that the strike wasn't four hundred people quitting. We were withholding labour and coming back, not ending the relationship, and that's kind of the whole thing — it's why the 90 are in the unit at all and why the contract binds all of us. If you can get that in without it becoming a lecture for the kids and the families, that's the part I'll care most about.

Please write it. And then underneath, be straight with me about the thinking instead of just polite: tell me where the speech takes liberties, where my bit about benefits being divisible and participation not is doing more work than it can really bear (I half suspect it's a feeling I've dressed up as an argument), and whether what I've described is actually different from the plain old free-rider complaint in the way I think it is. And if you think the setup is confused — that a speech like this can't be given, or that I'm asking three minutes at a potluck to do something three minutes can't do — say that plainly rather than writing something that sort of complies. I'd much rather hear it now than find out standing up.
