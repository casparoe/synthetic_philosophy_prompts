I work on a tiny behavioural insights team inside a state agency, and I've built an argument that I think proves way too much, but I can't find where it breaks.

In-house we split nudges into Rails — they change what's easy without touching what you want (defaults, ordering, pre-filled forms) — and Engines, which change how the options themselves look to you (a frame that makes one option feel like a loss). We're only cleared to use Rails. We also use the notion of a Hard Twin: two interventions are hard twins when they produce the same pattern of behaviour and roughly the same expected welfare gain, but one leaves your option set intact and the other doesn't (a ban, a mandate, a tax big enough to bite). The pitch for our program is that Rails get the good without the coercion.

(1) You can't present a choice without presenting it somehow: options come in some order, something is default, something is salient. So every choice situation has some architecture, and whoever sets it up inevitably influences behaviour. There's no neutral presentation to fall back on.
(2) If influencing behaviour is unavoidable for the architect, then the architect can't be criticised just for influencing behaviour.
(3) Rail nudges, Engine nudges, mandates, bans, bribes and outright lies are all ways of influencing behaviour.
(4) So none of them can be criticised just for influencing behaviour.
(5) For most any Rail nudge there's a Hard Twin that brings about the same good more reliably, since it doesn't depend on people being lazy or distracted.
(6) If it's permissible to bring about some good by a means that works only sometimes, and another means brings about that same good more reliably at no greater cost along anything else we care about, then we should use the more reliable one.
(7) So we shouldn't be running Rail nudges at all — we should be mandating, and by (4) we can't be criticised for that either.
(8) Mandating is the thing the whole nudge program exists to avoid, and (4) would also let us lie to people.

I'd like to keep (1) — try designing a form where the options are in no order whatsoever — and I'm also oddly attached to (6). Which step do you kill, and what does killing it cost me? Does the Rail/Engine split end up doing any real work here or does it drop out? And if the inevitability point survives at all, what's the strongest version of it? Please answer in American English.
