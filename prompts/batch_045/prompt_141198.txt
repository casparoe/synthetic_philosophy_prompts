Hey, I need help drafting a document. I'm on a tiny working group that has until March to write the decision rule for a citizens' assembly on housing and transport policy in my city; the assembly will vote on maybe forty proposals over two years, and we have to fix the rule now, before anyone knows what the proposals will actually be. Every proposal gets voted as a bundle: P = "the measure would cut district traffic emissions by at least 15% within ten years", Q = "the measure's costs would fall disproportionately on lower-income households", and the assembly's standing doctrine is that a measure should be adopted iff P and not Q. Our group has split into two camps that have now argued past each other for about a year. The "reasons first" camp wants premise-based aggregation: aggregate the votes on P and Q, then read the verdict off the doctrine, because on their view the point of the assembly is to give the city a set of reasons it can stand behind, and a group verdict that contradicts the group's own premises is worse than no verdict. The "verdict first" camp wants conclusion-based aggregation: take the majority on "adopt?" directly, because that's the question the assembly was actually asked, the premises are mostly the majority's rationalization after the fact, and a rule that lets a majority lose on the thing everyone cares about because of how the ballot happened to be carved up is a bug, not a feature.

Two complications that are specific to us and that I never see in the textbook examples. First: about a third of the assembly has said from the start that they will abstain on P and defer to a technical panel, but they insist on voting themselves on Q — so different propositions get decided by different, partially overlapping subsets of people, and I have a feeling that makes premise-based aggregation do something extra weird. Second: there's a live proposal to rewrite the doctrine with a moralized version of Q in it, Q* = "the cost burden would be unjust", and several members vote differently on Q than on Q*, so which premise set the doctrine routes through looks like it can flip the outcome all by itself. Oh, and because the assembly runs two years and early decisions get cited as precedent, whatever rule we pick, its outputs become inputs to later cases.

What I want is the document these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Concretely: a numbered section of claims both can sign — about what the discursive dilemma really shows; about whether the known features of premise-based versus conclusion-based aggregation (majority-inconsistent outcomes, the way results depend on which propositions are treated as premises, what the standard impossibility results with universal domain, collective rationality, anonymity and independence actually rule out) are genuine problems or just the price of admission — followed by a disagreements table where each side's position is phrased the way that side would phrase it and not the way the other side would caricature it. I know the classic three-person example where the two procedures come apart, so use it as a warm-up if you like, but what I really want is the smallest voter profile you can find where the partial-deferral setup makes premise-based aggregation output a judgment set that no individual member endorses and that no fixed electorate would have produced — and then say plainly whether that's a real pathology or a artifact of the way I've described it. Then give me the cruxes: for each one, a concrete case, test, or hypothetical assembly outcome that both camps agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. One crux I want addressed head on: a compromise somebody floated, where you run both procedures and if they disagree the measure simply fails and the status quo stands — can both camps sign that, or does it just relocate the disagreement? Be explicit about which parts of the fight, if any, are merely verbal. And please, no bibliographies, reading lists or citations — I've read the literature, I need the arguments, not pointers to it.
