I read a long blog post last week — one of those 4000-word rationalist-adjacent things with a lot of upvotes — that uses pain asymbolia to settle an argument about the badness of pain, and I can't tell how much of it to trust. The key passage goes roughly like this:

"Pain has two separable components. First there's the sensory-discriminative one: the burning or stabbing character, the location, the intensity, the ability to tell a pinprick from a burn. Second there's the affective one: how much the pain bothers you, its unpleasantness. Asymbolia, which results from damage to the cingulate cortex, knocks out the second and leaves the first fully intact. Asymbolics feel the pain, can tell you exactly where it is and how strong it is, still flinch and withdraw reflexively — but they report, with a shrug, that it doesn't bother them at all. This shows that the unpleasantness of pain is a separate add-on rather than part of the sensation itself: the sensation, considered on its own, is completely value-neutral, like the smell of coffee or the colour blue. It's a genuine double dissociation, too, since you also see the reverse — patients whose sensory ratings are low but who report the pain is unbearable. It follows that what actually matters is suffering, not pain, and that suffering is a kind of judgment the person makes about the sensation rather than a feeling. So pain as such isn't bad, and a hedonist who counts pain itself as a disvalue is tracking the wrong quantity. And since the cases prove the two can come apart in principle, you could strip away the second component and leave the first: imagine a patient with the full sensory profile of a bad burn, withdrawing normally, who says with total sincerity 'this doesn't bother me and it isn't bad for me.' There is nothing left there for the hedonist to count."

The bit that bothers me is the slide from "asymbolics aren't bothered" to "the pain isn't bad for them and they aren't worse off." That seems to be trading on something like "doesn't feel bad to them" versus "isn't bad for them," and I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just philosopher's wordplay. I'm generally sympathetic to the idea that pain is intrinsically bad, so I'm probably looking for a way out, and I'd rather know if the rest of it is solid.
