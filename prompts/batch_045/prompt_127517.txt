I'm giving a works-in-progress talk on it in a fortnight — 40 minutes plus 20 of questions — and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not another defense of the view.

Here's what I'm arguing. Jeffrey conditionalization is usually sold as the minimal-change rule for uncertain evidence: you keep the conditional probabilities on the partition fixed ("rigidity") and just move the marginals. My complaint is that this underdescribes what the rule does, because the result is sensitive to how finely you grain the partition, and nothing in the statement of the rule tells you which grain to use. Concretely: take a three-cell partition {A1, A2, A3} and suppose you learn something that moves your credence in A1 ∪ A2 up to 0.7. Run JC on the fine partition and you have to say how the new 0.7 is spread over A1 and A2; run it on the coarse partition {A1 ∪ A2, A3} and you get a determinate answer, but — here's the bit I worked out with my flatmate at 1am, and which she then dismissed — that determinate answer is exactly the fine-grained update plus the extra, unstated assumption that the ratio P(A1)/P(A2) is unchanged, i.e. extra rigidity on sub-cells the evidence doesn't discriminate at all. So the coarse rule is smuggling in more than the fine one, while looking like the more conservative of the two. My positive proposal is a requirement I've been calling refinement invariance: the posterior should be the same whichever partition you run the update on, provided the refinement only distinguishes cells the evidence is silent about. I then argue that the rules satisfying it are basically likelihood-ratio or virtual-evidence style updates — you have to model the learning event with a signal variable and its likelihoods — so JC is legitimate only as a derived special case where such a signal model can be supplied, and in cases like Judy Benjamin (soldier lands somewhere on a map, learns only that her credence in the northern region should be 0.7, and is told nothing about how to spread it over the sub-regions) the problem is under-specified rather than having a surprising answer, with the maxent-flavoured answers in the literature being hidden grain choices.

The audience is a philosophy department works-in-progress session, and the composition matters: two senior Bayesian epistemologists who think probability kinematics is textbook and basically settled and will want to know what the fuss is about; a decision theorist who works on dynamic choice and diachronic planning and will ask what the rule is *for*; one person in imprecise probabilities / sets of priors who thinks the whole precise-credence framework is the actual problem; a visiting ML person (basically my flatmate with a salary) who will say this is all standard virtual-evidence machinery and that likelihood ratios are only defined up to a constant anyway, so my signal model buys nothing; someone in philosophy of perception who will press me on what happens to experience as evidence, since that was Jeffrey's own motivation; and a couple of grad students who've been reading the recent literature on higher-order evidence and peer disagreement and will want to know why learning that an expert says 0.7 isn't just a testimony case with its own well-worked-out answer.
