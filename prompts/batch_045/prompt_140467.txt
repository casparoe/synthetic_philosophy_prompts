I came to all of this from a non-academic direction. I got hooked on popular books about time travel, followed the references backwards, and ended up on a chapter about Kurt Gödel in one of those large trade books about time; I then tried, badly, to explain it to two friends, one of whom has a physics degree and one of whom argues with me about everything. The chapter's account goes roughly like this — I have compressed several pages into a single paragraph, but I have tried not to change anything in doing so. General relativity is Einstein's theory of gravity written as equations for the geometry of spacetime, and different solutions to those equations describe different possible universes. In 1949 Gödel, the logician, produced a solution describing a universe that does not expand but rotates: homogeneous dust, turning relative to the local standard of non-rotation, together with a cosmological constant tuned to fit its matter density. Because of that rotation the light cones tip over as you move away from any given observer, and far enough out they tip so far that a spaceship travelling always below the speed of light can follow a path that returns to its own starting point in spacetime — a traveller could in principle set off, go around, and arrive in time to meet her younger self. Gödel then drew a philosophical moral: in a universe like that there is no way to carve spacetime into a single sequence of universal "nows", so physics supplies no objective present moment, and if there is no objective present then there is no objective passage of time either. The flow of time, he concluded, is not in the world at all but is contributed by our minds, which is more or less what Kant had claimed; or, as the chapter puts it a page later, time is an illusion, and every moment exists just as much as every other. The chapter goes on to say that working physicists have never been much troubled by any of this, since observation long ago established that the real universe is expanding rather than rotating, so Gödel's solution is a mathematical curiosity and not a description of us, and the whole episode is best read as a cautionary tale about a great logician straying outside his field.

Two things make me suspicious, and I cannot tell whether my suspicions are even the right ones. The first is that the "physicists shrug" passage seems to miss something: it reads as though the chapter thinks Gödel was making a guess about which kind of universe we happen to inhabit and guessed wrong, whereas my physics friend says the whole point was that the equations permit such a universe at all, so that discovering we do not live in one would simply be beside the point; my other friend replies that if closed time loops turned out to be impossible in any world the theory genuinely allows, the argument would collapse — and I have no idea who is right, or what "possible" would even have to mean in this context. The second is that the chapter slides from "time does not objectively pass" to "time is an illusion and all moments are equally real", and those strike me as different claims: one seems to say something about the world and about us, the other sounds like a flat metaphysical thesis about what exists. I am also unable to keep apart three things that everything I have read seems to run together: the point that special relativity already denies any absolute simultaneity, the point that in Gödel's universe there is not even a single global time order, and the point that there is no objective becoming. So: how fair is this chapter? Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on the foundations of physics or on the philosophy of time would accept as stated, what is a fair simplification for a trade book, and what is genuinely wrong or misleading — including whether Gödel's own conclusion is really the one the chapter attributes to him, and whether the inference from "no global now" to "no objective passage" is as immediate as the chapter makes it look? Does the dismissive verdict at the end survive once the errors are corrected, and does it matter for that verdict which of my two friends is right about possibility? I should add that I am not asking for anything to read — I have no library access and do not need recommendations — I only want to know which parts of what I have been told I should stop repeating.
