Hey, I could use some help sorting out an argument I keep having with myself. I'm on the board of a housing co-op — about 200 members, and anything above a few thousand dollars has to go to a general meeting where anyone can stand up and talk for two minutes and then we vote by a show of hands. Most of the time it's fine, and then every so often it goes completely off the rails: in the spring we voted in a fury to tear up our contract with a plumber who'd botched a job, and a month later voted to hire him back; a couple of weeks ago we approved a roof budget built on a number that somebody just said out loud in the room and nobody checked. So I started reading about Athens — the Mytilene affair, the Sicilian expedition, the trial of Socrates — because it's basically our setup with much higher stakes, and I want to know what the actual diagnosis is.

The two accounts I keep going back and forth between, which I'll name badly. First, the audience problem: the failure is in the moment of decision, and it's a failure of what a crowd can think while it's being watched. Nobody in a room like that wants to be the person saying the boring cautious thing, and whoever says the flattering thing — that we're strong, that we're owed something, that this will be easy — gets rewarded. On this account Mytilene is the odd case where the machinery half-worked (the assembly talks itself into killing every adult male in a rebel city, sleeps on it, talks itself back out of it, and the second boat only just gets there in time), Sicily is the rule (they vote for a huge overseas expedition on the strength of a picture of the island nobody in the room has actually checked, and the man who tries to warn them off by piling up the costs somehow makes the whole thing sound more worth doing), and Socrates is what happens when a mass jury with no one to rule anything out of order gets to pass judgement on a man's entire manner of life. Second, the continuation problem: the failure isn't in any single meeting, it's that the deciding body can't carry anything out or stick to anything over time — it has no institutional memory, no staff of its own, no way of binding its future self, and it judges people on how things turned out rather than on whether the decision was sensible when it was made. So the real damage happens upstream of the meeting: the people who have to execute the decision know they'll be blamed for the outcome and not for the reasoning, so they hide bad news, won't put unwelcome advice in writing, and steer toward the risky version of a plan because the cautious version gets them punished too if it happens to fail.

A friend says the second account is really just the first one wearing a hat — that if the meeting weren't performing for itself, the information problem would sort itself out. I don't think that's right but I can't say why. There's also a wrinkle I can't resolve: the second account seems to predict both that fear of punishment makes people timid and that it makes them gamble, since being the one who said no is its own risk — and I can't tell which way it should cut. Can you compare the two accounts properly, and tell me which of them is the more basic failure — which one you'd fix first if you could only fix one — and defend that, rather than telling me they both matter? If it helps to make it concrete, tell me which one my co-op is definately in danger from, since we have no Sicilian expeditions on the docket.
