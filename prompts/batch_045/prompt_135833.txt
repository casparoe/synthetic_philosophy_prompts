I sit on a hospital clinical ethics committee: roughly forty consults a year, usually answered within a day or two and signed off by the chair; the rest of us are clinicians, a nurse and a lay member, and none of us has any philosophy training. Again and again we are told what we morally ought to do by someone else — a senior intensivist, a specialist, a chaplain or faith leader, a hospital policy, or an outside expert — and I want a decision procedure we could run in the room, in under an hour, for when we should defer to that moral testimony and when we must work the judgment out for ourselves and own it. Ground it in what the epistemology of moral testimony actually says — the worry that taking another person's moral judgment as a reason is suspect in a way that taking their factual judgment is not, because moral understanding is not the kind of thing testimony can hand over — but make it usable by people who have never heard of the debate: give the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure refuses to allow deference, and where it hands the question to the full committee because what is at issue is which values to weight rather than what the facts are. It has to handle what I will call the entanglement problem: a single remark from one source often carries both a factual judgment only that source is placed to make (a prognosis, a claim about what the patient would have wanted) and a moral verdict, and it is not clear whether deferring to the one is deferring to the other. Then apply the procedure to three cases: a senior intensivist who says continued ICU treatment is futile and therefore must stop; a faith leader and a hospital policy speaking to a Jehovah's Witness adolescent who refuses a blood transfusion; and an outside expert's proposed ranking of patients for a scarce device. In at least one of them give an answer we will not want — my guess is the futility case, where we would find it easiest to treat the judgment as clinical and defer to it — and close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
