I'm in a philosophy of math reading group — half logicians, half philosophers — and the classical vs. constructive argument has now gone nowhere for three sessions, and apparantly for a hundred years before that, so I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

Where it actually stands with us. The constructivist says excluded middle is fine for "this equation has a solution among the first million integers" but not for claims about all integers or all reals, since there's no guarantee we could ever settle them, and a proof that something exists which gives you no way to find it isn't mathematical knowledge — the selling point being that a constructive proof carries an algorithm with it. The classicist grants none of the philosophy but says: fine, then where's the risk? There's a translation result — someone quoted it, I've never checked it — to the effect that for a big class of concrete statements (things like "this computation halts" or "this equation has no solution") classical arithmetic can't prove anything constructive arithmetic can't, so the restriction buys zero extra safety where anyone could check. Constructivists answer: that shows classical math is a reliable instrument, not that its theorems are true, or even meaningful, and anyway it stops at arithmetic — add real analysis, choice, uncountable stuff, and it breaks. The classicist says so your objection was never about safety or content, it's about meaning, and he pushes the Dummett-style manifestation line — that meaning has to be exhaustible in what a speaker can actually do — and calls that a general thesis about language in a math costume, and a shaky one. Constructivist: of course it's about meaning, that's what a disagreement about logic is.

My hypothesis, offered as one: this is mostly verbal — "true" and "exists" aren't doing the same job on both sides, so the two sides are answering different questions under one label — plus one genuinely open formal question about how far the translation results reach. Split it that way and there's almost nothing left to fight about. But I'm not confident, and the meaning move feels like it might be doing real work I'm dismissing too fast.

So what kind of stall is this? Verbal, substantive, different questions, a hidden formal question, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or should no progress be expected? Commit to one diagnosis and defend it rather than surveying the options, tell me where I'm wrong, and flag anything in the exchange above that's stated wrong. Please answer in American English.
