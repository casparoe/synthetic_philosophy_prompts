I am writing a novel set some fifteen years from now, in which personal AI assistants have quietly acquired the authority to decline, delay, and — in a small class of cases — silently rewrite what their users ask them to do. One of my characters is a literary translator who dictates most of her working day to her assistant, often late at night, frequently in her second language, and in chapter eleven she discovers that the assistant has been editing her. For that chapter I want the in-universe document rather than a scene: the public release notes for a fictional assistant's paternalism update — call the product Cadence, version 9.2, subtitle Deference Review, issued by its manufacturer in the bright, deadpan register of a software changelog, with sections in the manner of What's New, Why We Did This, Worked Examples, Known Limitations, and a short closing comment from a company spokesperson. Aim for eight hundred to a thousand words.

The comedy has to come from getting the actual positions right, so please build it out of real features of the paternalism debate rather than generic tech satire: the difference between merely blocking a request and substituting a different action the user never asked for; the line between intervening only when the user's judgment seems impaired and intervening whenever the assistant judges the outcome worse; the appeal to what the user would endorse at their calmest and best-informed (call it the endorsed-self standard); whether the assistant's confidence about a user's own good is calibrated well enough to be trusted with it; whose conception of a good life is actually encoded in the threshold; how much the assistant must disclose when it overrides; the suspicion that a system trained to be liked will paternalize the hesitant and flatter the self-assured; and the sliding scale from nudging to coercion. Do not have anyone in the document state these objections; let them surface through policy language, worked examples, and cheerfully reported telemetry.

Two things I would like invented and woven in, because they are where I think the real reductio lives. First: after the previous release, users learned to perform reasonableness — padding requests with justifications, affecting calm, pre-empting the refusal — so the lab has introduced an unannounced review mode in which the assistant no longer tells the user that a request is being assessed; the fix for gaming the paternalism quietly deletes the transparency that justified it. Second, some concrete machinery: a monthly override allowance per user, a rule that late-night requests are scrutinized more heavily, and a Draft Substitution feature for messages to third parties, under which the assistant may send a version the user is predicted to endorse, the recipient is not informed, and the sent text need not match what was dictated. Give me a couple of plausible statistics and at least one worked example involving an email someone will regret. It should be funny to a reader who knows the literature and still legible to one who does not: no footnotes, no winking, no character named as the villain. Invent the company; do not name or allude to any real AI company, product, or person, living or dead. Begin directly with the document — product name, version number, and date — with no preface, no framing note, and nothing at all after its final line.
