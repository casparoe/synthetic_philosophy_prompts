I'm a novelist — crime-ish stuff set in a made-up Midlands town — and the book I'm halfway through has a strand where the town has to decide what to do with a statue of a Victorian factory owner whose money built the library, the baths and about a third of the street names. My drafts keep flattening into a speech about history, which is boring and not how it actually goes in a parish council room.

So: I run a fortnightly writting group, 12 adults (mostly retirees, two sixth-formers), two hours on a Tuesday evening, a room with a flipchart and no tech. Design me an exercise that makes them *feel* the thing I'm after, which is that arguments about commemoration are mostly arguments about plumbing, insurance and who has to say the name out loud. Two notions I've been kicking about and want embedded in the rules rather than explained: the 'enunciation burden' — the people forced to repeat a contested name (on forms, giving directions, reading out a prize citation) are rarely the people it injures, and rarely the people who care what it means; and 'referential drift', the way a name stops being about the man once it's a bus stop and a postcode, which is an argument for leaving street names alone that does nothing for statues. I'd also like the trap where adding an explanatory plaque to atone for a monument just makes the monument more thoroughly about him, not less.

No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear any of those labels until afterwards. Give me the roles, the rounds, the stakes (nothing that needs real money), and a debrief that maps what happened onto the ideas. Say plainly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — half of them will want to debate whether the man was wicked — and be honest about which part of this the exercise can't reach and I'll have to put in the characters' mouths myself. Finally, tell me how confident you are that it'll actually land that way, and single out the bit you're least sure of.
