Question 4. Distinguish three theses: (S) substantivalism, the view that space exists independently of any matter in it and has its own structure; (R1) strict relationism, the view that there is no such thing as true or absolute motion, but only change of position relative to some arbitrarily chosen reference body; (R2) global relationism, the view that true motion is motion relative to the total distribution of matter, and that there is no space over and above the network of relations among bodies. (a) Reconstruct Newton's bucket argument, say precisely which of these theses it is aimed against, and say whether (S) follows if it succeeds. (b) Assess the following reply on behalf of (R2): the concavity of the water is explained by the water's rotation relative to the fixed stars, that is, relative to the total material distribution, not relative to the bucket; so the argument tells against (R1) only. Discuss this reply both with respect to Newton's companion thought experiment of two globes joined by a cord in an otherwise empty universe, and with respect to a case we invented in section, in which God annihilates all the matter outside the bucket one star at a time while the surface is concave: must the global relationist say that the surface flattens, and if so, gradually or all at once? (c) Assess the Leibnizian argument from the principle of sufficient reason: if (S) were true then God's creating the material world five metres east of where He in fact created it, or a year earlier, would have been a genuinely different creation even though no relation among creatures would have differed; God would have had no reason to prefer one of these to the other; hence there can be no such difference. Does this argument conflict with the bucket argument? Does it establish (R2)?

Answer. Newton's bucket argument appears in the Scholium near the beginning of the Principia. A bucket of water hangs from a long cord; the cord is twisted and the bucket released. At the first stage the bucket spins while the water has not yet taken up the motion, so the relative motion of water and bucket is greatest; the surface of the water is flat. By the last stage the water has acquired the rotation and travels round with the bucket, so that it is at relative rest with respect to the vessel, and its surface is concave, climbing up the sides. Thus the relative motion between water and bucket is greatest when the surface is flat and nil when the surface is most curved, which shows that the true motion of the water cannot be identified with its motion relative to the surrounding vessel. Newton concludes that the water is genuinely rotating, that is, rotating with respect to absolute space. This refutes relationism. A relationist cannot explain the concavity, since at the crucial stage the water is at rest relative to the bucket, the only body present, and the global version of the view cannot escape by appealing to the total distribution of matter or to the fixed stars either, because Newton's companion thought experiment of the two globes joined by a cord in an otherwise empty universe shows that the tension in the cord reveals the rotation even when there is no other matter for the system to be rotating relative to. Mach's response, that nobody is competent to say what would happen in a universe containing nothing but the globes, is not really an answer: it declines to grant the starting assumption of the thought experiment, and it is a version of (R2) anyway, so it falls with (R2). The Leibnizian argument from the principle of sufficient reason is in tension with all of this. If space were a substance, then God's creating the material universe five metres to the east, or a year earlier, would be a genuinely different creation although nothing in the relations among created things would differ, and God would have had no reason to prefer one to the other; so there can be no such difference, and space is not a substance but an order of coexistences. This argument and the bucket argument cannot both be right. Since the bucket argument is an empirical argument resting on what we observe, while Leibniz's is an a priori metaphysical argument, we should keep the bucket argument and reject Leibniz's, and conclude that substantivalism is true and relationism false.
