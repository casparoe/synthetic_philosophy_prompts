I write literary fiction — third novel is out on submission, fingers crossed — and I keep catching myself writing scenes where a character is obviously in some state and would, if you stopped them mid-paragraph and asked, sincerely deny being in it. The widower humming at the sink two weeks after the funeral who'll tell you he feels nothing yet; the woman who keeps making the same joke about her sister and swears she isn't angry. I got into an argument about this with a friend who works in philosophy of mind, and she said something like: well, it depends whether you think a state can be conscious without being *noticed*, and then whether the noticing has to actually happen or just be available to happen — and I realised I definately can't keep the options straight in my head. So I want a classification instrument, not a literature survey. No reading lists, no citations, no "see so-and-so 1997": I don't need to know who to read next, I need the map. It should work as a sort-yourself thing — me and two writer friends doing it on ourselves over dinner — and as a tree I can look at afterwards to see where we landed.

Specs, since I've got opinions about how it should be built:

- Fix the entry commitment rather than asking about it. Start from the datum that there is something it's like to be in a state you'd sincerely and competently deny being in. Everyone downstream has already granted that. If some positions simply reject the datum, make that one leaf at the top and move on.
- Six to eight branch questions, seven to nine leaves.
- Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names: give me the questions and the answer-combinations they permit first, then drop the named positions into the cells they actually occupy — higher-order views where the noticing must actually occur, higher-order views where a standing disposition is enough, first-order representationalism, self-representationalism, global-workspace/access-only views, the Block-flavoured view that phenomenal consciousness outruns access, and whatever else genuinely belongs — then mark the cells nobody lives in with one clause on why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Every branch question has to be answerable by a novelist with no theory vocabulary, phrased as a concrete case or a claim you can just agree or disagree with. A few of the cases have to be mine: the humming widower; a scene were the character is so absorbed in a task that she responds correctly to something — brakes for a light, answers a question — and afterwards has no idea whether she saw or heard it or what it was like; and one where she's wrong about what she's feeling, swears it's grief and it's actually relief.
- The distinctions I want doing the branching: does the noticing have to actually occur or is a standing disposition sufficient; does whatever does the noticing have to be about that particular first-order state or can it range more loosely; can it misrepresent, and what happens to a theory if it can, including the worry that it ends up making a rock conscious; whatever does the noticing — is it itself conscious, and how does the view stop that from going on forever; and finally, is the view trying to explain the what-it's-like-ness of the target state or only about it's availability for thought, report and deliberate action.
- Leaves carry the name of the position, or "unnamed" if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment as it bears on my kind of scene.
- Flag any two leaves the questions genuinely can't separate rather than inventing a split; I'd rather know where the tree bottoms out.

And then, since I may end up half-quoting this in an essay: say how confident you are that the map is complete, and single out the one branch or placement you're least sure of.
