Our city's Public Memory Commission is charged with judging proposals about monuments, archives, and traditions according to Nietzsche's distinction between the monumental, antiquarian, and critical uses of history for life, though our statute does not rank those aims. Our four precedents are as follows. First, we restored a bronze statue of the city's founder, who led a successful revolt and chartered the city, and we funded an annual civic procession in his honor. Second, we removed a plaque commemorating the city's worst cholera epidemic, a source of paralyzing grief, and replaced the site with a public health clinic. Third, we funded upkeep of the medieval cathedral where the charter was signed, which still hosts weekly concerts and a farmers' market. Fourth, we took down a portrait gallery of the previous dictator's family in city hall. A new proposal asks us to fund the restoration of a 300-year-old communal bread oven in a village square, still used weekly by residents, with no association with any great individual or event, and to designate it a protected heritage site. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own precedents? Please identify the candidate rules, the monumental, antiquarian, and critical standards, that fit our past decisions, say what each predicts about the bread oven, give your best recommendation with the reasons for preferring it, name the further case that would tell those rules apart, and state which precedent is the closest analog and what principle we would be adopting with either choice. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning; do not restate the question or add any preamble.
