Hey — I'm deputy lead at a small residential outdoor program for 13-to-16-year-olds who've been permanently excluded from mainstream school. Twelve kids, they live in with us Monday to Friday and go home at weekends, and the people actually making the calls are mostly outdoor instructors with a few weeks of training and no teaching quals. They're deciding things all day every day — do we make him do it, do we answer her, do we let the group watch this — and the stakes aren't abstract: our funding rides on kids not going back into the system, and one wrong call on restraint or on withholding a phone gets us shut down. Anything involving a sanction has to be signed off by our designated safeguarding lead, and whatever we use has to be runnable in the five minutes before a shift by someone who will never open a book.

I've been reading Locke's book on education — the 1693 one, written as letters to a friend about his son — and I keep thinking there's a usable procedure buried in it even though nearly everything about his setting is different from mine. The bits I have in mind: treat children as rational creatures and actually reason with them; never squash curiosity or fob a question off; example does more work than rules; the real engine is reputation, kids caring about credit and disgrace from the people around them rather than about beatings or bribes, with rewards like money or treats only teaching them to relish the reward; physical punishment basically reserved for flat obstinacy; harden the body rather than coddle it; and a private tutor at home beats school largely because of what boys catch off each other.

So: turn that into a decision procedure my instructors can run. Questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one.

Two things I want handled honestly rather than smoothed over.

First, what I've started calling the audience problem in my own notes. It looks to me like the whole reputation mechanism presupposes a small, stable audience whose good opinion the kid actually wants — parents, a tutor, one household — while what we've got is staff rotating on shift and a peer group that hands out status for the exact opposite of what we're after. Is there anything left of the mechanism in our setting, or does the procedure have to run on something else? Tell me if its not a real problem.

Second, our clinical consultant's rule is that we never deliberately make a kid feel ashamed — her line is that shame is what most of these kids are already drowning in. That reads to me like a straight ban on Locke's main lever, and I can't tell whether that hollows his framework out or whether the lever can be rebuilt as something like being taken seriously by an adult.

Then run the procedure on three real cases, and don't be nice about the answers:

1. Jayden, 14, refuses to split kindling before breakfast, tells the instructor make me, and the rest of the group is watching.
2. A 13-year-old who asks non-stop questions on the river walk — where the water goes, why the licence costs what it does, why we're not allowed to swim here — while we're forty minutes behind with the minibus booked.
3. Curtis, who'll do any task at all if it earns points on our level system that convert to Xbox time on Friday, and does nothing without them. Our funders love the level system because it gives them numbers to report.

I want at least one of those three to come out with an answer I won't want to hear, and I'd rather you told me which one than buried it.

Also tell me which Lockean distinctions have to survive the simplification and which I can just throw out — the cold baths and thin shoes, the gentleman's curriculum, the Latin, the manual trade, the assumption of one boy and one resident tutor — and where the procedure will misfire. I'm thinking especially of kids who've been beaten at home, kids who are autistic or have ADHD, and kids whose status economy at home runs backwards, but give me the ones I haven't thought of too.
