I'm a psychiatry resident finishing a PhD in philosophy, and in three weeks I'm giving grand rounds on Szasz's 1961 Myth of Mental Illness to a room that is not a philosophy seminar: roughly thirty clinicians, plus a bioethicist who has worked on commitment-law reform, plus a service-user representative from a local peer-run organisation who is also a neurodiversity advocate. My thesis is that Szasz's debunking argument works against the disease reading of psychiatric categories but does not by itself yield his libertarian conclusion about involuntary commitment — that needs a separate argument he never gives — and that once we reassign the categories a different job the whole issue redistributes. My sketch: a diagnosis does three separable things, which I call the explanation licence (it predicts and explains behaviour), the exemption licence (it reduces blame and reallocates responsibility), and the resource licence (it opens access to care, benefits, and, in the other direction, to coercion). Szasz showed the first licence is largely counterfeit for most psychiatric categories; he never noticed that the second and third might survive the debunking, because they don't require the category to be a discovery. Then I add an argument of my own, the identity ratchet: because people partly come to experience and organise their lives, relationships, and self-narratives around the categories available to them, a category can become load-bearing for the very people it supposedly misdescribes, so abolishing it isn't costless even for someone who agrees it was never a disease — and that gives us a reason to keep some of this vocabulary that has nothing to do with truth. My conclusion is that commitment should rest on something narrower and more honestly labelled than illness — acute impairment of decision-making capacity plus imminent serious danger to the person or others — which would justify coercion in fewer cases than current practice does, but would still justify it, and would leave psychiatry doing the thing Szasz always said it secretly does.

What I need is the question period, not a defence of the thesis. Tell me which two objections have real force rather than being occupational reflexes, tell me what the room's unstated shared assumption is that my talk will be read against, and end with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
