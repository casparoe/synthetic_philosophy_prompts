I am a staff software engineer at a company of about nine hundred people, and for the last eight months I have been one of three rotating members of the committee that allocates our employee hardship fund. The fund is discretionary: any employee, including contractors with more than a year of service, may apply once a quarter for help with an out-of-pocket cost — dental or medical work insurance will not cover, travel to a family funeral, adapting a home workspace after an injury, emergency childcare, replacing essentials lost in a fire. We have eighteen thousand dollars a month and typically between forty-five and sixty thousand dollars of requests. We meet for ninety minutes, read a one-page application (a free-text description, the amount requested, the applicant role and tenure, and, since last quarter, a self-rated need score from zero to ten), and decide. I then write a short paragraph of justification per decision into a shared document. The Head of People signs off, the works council can ask us to reopen any file, and a rejected applicant can request a fifteen-minute meeting with me. Committee members rotate every six months and none of us has any training for this. I have an engineering background and no philosophy; I can follow a precise specification, but I cannot go and read a literature.

The reason I am writing is that our Head of People has proposed two changes, and something about both feels wrong to me in the same way and I cannot say what. First, that we should simply rank applications by the self-rated need score and fund down the list until the money runs out, on the grounds that the applicants are the only people who know how much they need it. Second, that where we have to choose we should fund the biggest improvement per dollar. My worry, which I cannot sharpen, is that one person's seven is another person's four, and that the people who write the most florid applications are not the people who need the money most. I have also noticed that our committee is much better at picturing the life of a software engineer with back pain than the life of a night-shift warehouse picker, and that this seems to move money.

What I would like from you is a written procedure that the three of us can actually run, and that I can hand on to whoever replaces me. Please operationalise whatever the theory of interpersonal comparisons of utility has to say that is genuinely usable here: I have in mind the idea that a person's wellbeing has no natural zero or unit, that comparing how badly off two people are is a different job from comparing how much two people would gain, and that there is a well-known move where you ask the decision-maker to imagine having an equal chance of ending up as any one of the applicants rather than trying to climb inside their heads. If any of those are the wrong tools, say so and use the right ones.

Concretely, I need:

The questions to ask, in order, for a single application and then for a set of applications competing for the same money. For each question, what would count as a sufficient answer given that all we have is the one-page form and our own discussion, and what counts as no answer at all.

Where the procedure stops and returns a no, so that we do not end up inventing a justification for a decision we could not actually support.

Which comparisons we should refuse to make at all, in the sense that the honest answer is that we do not have and cannot get the information — and how the write-up should say that, so it survives review by the works council and does not read to the applicant as bureaucratic evasion.

Where the procedure must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. I would particularly like help separating two things I keep conflating: on the one hand, whether we are trying to maximise the total good we do with the money, and on the other hand, whether we are trying to direct it to whoever is worst off regardless of how much good the money does. I do not think measurement can settle that choice, and I want the procedure to say so plainly rather than smuggle in an answer.

The distinctions from the theory that have to survive being simplified for a committee of engineers with ninety minutes and no training, and the ones that can be dropped as making no difference to what we decide. Please be explicit about what we lose by dropping them.

A short, fixed set of concrete dimensions every application gets rewritten into before we compare anything, given that we cannot ask the applicant for more than the form already collects, cannot run any psychological measurement or calibration exercise on staff, and cannot require people to disclose a diagnosis. If rewriting free-text hardship into a common set of dimensions is a bad idea, tell me what to do instead.

A check for the failure mode I described: that we identify more easily with applicants who share our job, our education, or our way of writing about feelings. It has to be something a rotating committee can run without training.

Where the procedure will misfire, stated as plainly as you can manage.

Then run the finished procedure on three applications, showing your work at each step and including the write-up paragraph you would have me put in the shared document for each:

1. A nine-hundred-dollar ergonomic chair and assessment for an engineer with chronic back pain that will continue indefinitely, against twelve hundred dollars of travel for a warehouse picker to attend a parent's funeral abroad. Same pot of money, only one can be funded this month.
2. A standing desk for a senior engineer on a high salary with mild, recent discomfort, against emergency dental work for a contractor on the lowest pay band in the company. The dental work costs three times as much and the contractor's request is only partly covered by what we can give.
3. Two long-standing applicants with comparable requests and comparable costs, one of whom writes a measured, understated account and rates their need at four, and one of whom writes an intense account and rates it at ten. The understated applicant has lived with their condition for eleven years and describes it in their form as something they have simply got used to; the other applicant's problem began last month.

I would like at least one of these three to come out somewhere my committee will not want it to, and I would rather you chose which one that is than arranged the cases to avoid it. If the procedure's answer in that case is one I should overrule as a value judgement, tell me that, and tell me how to document an overrule so that it does not become a precedent for overruling the procedure whenever we find it inconvenient.
