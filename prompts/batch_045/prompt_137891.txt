I help administer a small grantmaking collective — about sixty members, consensus-ish culture, a charter we drafted ourselves — and we made what I now think was the characteristic mistake of people who have read about Nomic: we wrote the charter in the spirit of Peter Suber's game, the one where changing the rules is a move, in which rules start out divided into mutable and immutable ones with immutable rules taking precedence and a mechanism (transmutation) for changing a rule's own mutability. Our amendment article has two layers: section 7.1 says that amendments to the charter require a two-thirds vote of the membership, and section 7.2 says that section 7.1 may itself be amended only by a three-quarters vote. Last month we hit the failure mode I now take to be unavoidable for any self-amending system, and my roommate (a mechanism-design person, heavy LessWrong reader) and I have been going in circles on it ever since. Three proposals came to the floor. Proposal A was a single instrument, carried with 71% in favor — above two-thirds, below three-quarters — that both replaced section 7.1 with "amendments require a simple majority" and, in the same document, authorized a large grant. Proposal B, also a single instrument, lowered the general threshold to a simple majority while adding that any later amendment raising the threshold again would need 90%: a one-way valve, what I've started calling a ratchet. Proposal C would have required unanimity for all future amendments, i.e. full ossification. My roommate's position is that A is plainly valid — the old rule can validate its own repeal, so an amendment may bootstrap itself into force and then exercise the procedure it just created — and that the only interesting question is severability: if the threshold-changing half of A fails, does the grant survive, or does a single instrument stand or fall as a whole? I think that can't be right in general, because the same reasoning that validates A validates C, and C destroys the very power that was supposed to authorize it; but I also can't see where exactly to draw the line, since "self-amendments that shrink the amending power are invalid" seems morally attractive but legally arbitrary, and it has the flavor of the omnipotence paradox with extra steps (an omnipotent being making a rule it cannot later break). There is also a timing puzzle underneath all of this: at the moment you judge whether an instrument was validly enacted, which rule do you test it against — the one in force before, or the one it purports to bring into force?

So here is what I'd like. Please act as a legal theorist who is equally at home with analytic jurisprudence and with formal logic — someone who can talk about Hart's rule of recognition and Kelsen's basic norm without reaching for a library, and who also knows what a fixed point is. Write an essay of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words on the general question that our committee fight is an instance of: **can a self-amending system coherently bind itself — can an amendment power validly alter the terms of its own exercise — and if it can, by what mechanism; if it can't, what is actually going on when a purported self-amendment sticks?** Please reason from the structure of the problem rather than from the literature: no bibliography, no citations, no "as X argued in 1990" — I want the positions built and defended on their merits, since what I need is a map of the logical space, not a reading list. Before committing to a view, lay out the candidate answers explicitly (something like: self-application is legitimate and the old rule validates its own repeal; self-amendment is legally indeterminate until the subsequent practice of the officials settles it one way or the other; self-entrenchment is a fiction and every successful one is really a small revolution that retroactively constitutes a new rule of recognition), and give the strongest version of the one you reject — steelman it properly, don't knock down a strawman. Then commit: take a definite position on whether A is valid, on whether the ratchet in B differs in principle from the ossification in C, and on whether it matters that a self-amendment makes the amending body more powerful rather than less. One more wrinkle you should address if it fits: is there any difference, in principle, between a clause that sets one threshold for amendments that lower the threshold and a higher one for amendments that raise it (call it a Janus clause), and a clause that simply entrench itself? Is the Janus clause able to bind its own repeal, and what happens if a Janus clause is amended so as to swap its two numbers?

Please structure the response with the following sections, in this order and at these rough lengths: "The logical space" (~200 words, a numbered or otherwise explicit enumeration of the candidate positions); "Reading our charter closely" (~300 words, applying the candidate positions to Proposals A, B, and C, including the severability and timing questions); "The case that self-amendment is coherent" (~350 words); "The case that it isn't" (~300 words); "The strongest version of the view I'm rejecting" (~200 words); "My verdict" (~300 words, a definite answer to the general question and to the three proposals); "What we should put in section 7" (~200 words, concrete drafting advice for a real group that wants to avoid this mess without pretending it can be avoided entirely). Take your time and think it through carefully before you write; I'd rather have a slow, exact answer than a fluent one.
