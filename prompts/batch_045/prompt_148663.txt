I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a community hospital, and at our last meeting two members — a geriatrician and a health lawyer — spent about an hour on the same case and left without convincing each other of anything. I was the one taking notes, and I'd like you to adjudicate the exchange rather than tell me what you would have decided.

The patient is a 74-year-old woman diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease six years ago, now in the moderate-to-severe stage. She recognizes her daughter, still responds strongly to music, rarely initiates conversation, and on standard observational scales scores in the contented range on good days and the agitated range on bad ones. She has developed aspiration pneumonia. Without antibiotics the team expects she will die within days; with them, a substantial chance of returning to something like her current baseline.

Two documents exist. The first, signed when she was unambiguously competent, is a conventional advance directive: no hospitalization, no antibiotics, no artificially administered nutrition in the event of advanced dementia. The second is a short letter she drafted herself around the same time and gave to her daughter. In it she says, in substance, that she does not want her earlier self ruling from the grave over a person who may turn out to be perfectly happy, and she includes what the family has started calling the trigger clause: if, when the time comes, she still responds to music and still recognizes at least one person she loved, then her present enjoyment is to count as stronger evidence about how she is faring than anything her earlier self predicted about how much she would suffer. Both documents were executed while she had capacity. She now responds to music and recognizes her daughter.

The geriatrician (G) argued for treating; the health lawyer (L) argued for following the directive. Here is the exchange as closely as I can reconstruct it.

(G1) Honoring precedent autonomy is not a matter of obeying whichever document was signed first. It means executing the competent person's actual will, taken as a whole. Her will, taken as a whole, contains a clause saying that in exactly these circumstances present enjoyment outranks her earlier predictions. Following the directive against the trigger clause is not fidelity to her autonomy; it is tearing one page out of a two-page instruction.

(L1) The two pages are not symmetric. An advance directive is a decision; the letter is a mood, or at best a license to reinterpret decisions. Authorizing others to weigh your later enjoyment is not the same as deciding to be treated, and only the second is the kind of thing precedent autonomy protects. If every expression of doubt about one's own future directives counts as itself a directive, we lose the ability to distinguish a competent person's settled instruction from her acknowledged uncertainty — and the practice collapses into doing whatever seems kindest at the time.

(G2) The collapse worry would be right if the clause were open-ended, but it isn't: she named a proxy for well-being — music responsiveness, recognition of a loved one — that is observable, and that she selected rather than we did. We are not substituting our judgment about her quality of life for hers. We are executing a conditional she wrote, and the conditional is satisfied.

(L2) The conditional is satisfied and the proxy is bad, and she had no way of knowing it was bad. Music responsiveness persists well into late-stage dementia and is compatible with severe disorientation and distress; recognizing one familiar face is compatible with terror at nearly everything else. A person cannot confer reliability on a measurement by willing that it be a good measurement. The clause licenses us to consider her present enjoyment; it does not make the evidence we actually have good enough to bear the weight she assigned it.

(G3) That is an epistemic objection, and it cuts at least as hard in the other direction. The premise of the directive — that life in this condition would not be worth prolonging — was formed by someone predicting, from inside her then-current personality, a state she had never inhabited. The usual way of putting this is to distinguish a person's interests in how her life goes as a whole from her interests in how it feels to her at a time, and to ground precedent autonomy in the first. But she told us in advance which of the two should govern if they came apart. And there is an asymmetry: her present state is, however imperfectly, measured, whereas her earlier prediction was never measured at all.

(L3) The asymmetry is real but it is not the one you want. Both are guesses, yes — but one guess was made by an agent with authority over the matter, and the other is not a guess by anyone; it is an inference by us. Precedent autonomy does not require that the earlier self predict well. It requires that, among available decision procedures, deference to the competent person's own choice is the one least likely to be distorted by other people's interests: clinicians' bias toward intervention, families' guilt, cost pressure, our own discomfort with letting someone die. My worry is not that her prediction was accurate. It is that your procedure hands this decision to people with systematically misaligned incentives, on the basis of evidence they can read optimistically whenever they find optimism convenient.

(G4) That is an institutional argument, and it cuts both ways. The clause was written precisely to constrain discretion: it converts an open-ended judgment about suffering into a checkable one. And the documented default in medicine is to overtreat. The main reason people execute advance directives at all is that the system errs toward intervention, so "the wrong incentives" does not select the directive over the clause. It only says we should be careful.

(L4) Careful how? Here is the concrete problem. Clinicians and families who want to treat will always find the trigger satisfied; those who don't, won't. And because clauses of this kind are portable — anyone can add one — honoring it sets a ratchet: each case in which music responsiveness was read as contentment becomes a precedent for reading it that way again, and the plain instructions of everyone who never wrote such a clause are diluted by cases they never joined. An optional clause that irreversibly weakens other people's directives needs a justification it has not been given.

(G5) The ratchet is a real concern, but it is a concern about evidentiary standards, not about refusing to honor this document. You can insist on corroboration — structured assessments by two independent observers, repeated observation across good and bad days, a time-limited trial of treatment with a fixed review point — without declaring that a competent person may never build a review mechanism into her own instruction. You are treating a demand for rigor as if it were a reason to disregard the instruction altogether.

(L5) Consider the case we all agree about, which I think shows the structure. We accept binding a later self in the genuinely Ulyssean cases — addiction, acute psychosis, a predictable temporary derangement of the will — because there the later refusal is a symptom of the very impairment the arrangement was written to defeat. If this patient were screaming and thrashing, we would not be having this discussion; the directive would apply without controversy. What makes the case hard is that she is calm. But calm is not competence, and the reason to bind a later self is the impairment, not the agitation.

(G6) I do not need to deny that. I need only this: the reason to bind a later self is to protect a person against a known distortion in her own evaluative machinery. The distortion the directive guards against — the horror of being demented, imagined by someone who has never been demented — is a distortion in the earlier machinery too, and it points the opposite way. Ulysses tied himself to the mast knowing the song would sound exactly as beautiful to him then as it did when he gave the order. Our patient's letter says, in effect, that what sounds unbearable to her now may not be unbearable then, and that if that happens her present reaction should not count. That is still a Ulysses arrangement. It binds the earlier self rather than the later one.

(L6) Then tell me who is bound. If the competent self can bind her later self, and the later self's enjoyment can defeat the binding, and the competent self can also bind herself against her own predictions, then there is no agent left whose will is doing the work. You have kept the vocabulary of precedent autonomy and removed the thing that made it intelligible — a competent person's decision, made at a time, that settles what happens later. What remains is a committee reading a face.

(G7) What remains is a person who made two decisions at once, in the same condition, one saying what to do and one saying how to tell whether the first still applies. You think the second destroys the first. I think a competent person is permitted to build a review mechanism into her own instruction, and that honoring it is more faithful to her than obeying the list. And if I am wrong about that, you still have to explain why the same person's explicit, contemporaneous, competent statement that enjoyment should outrank prediction is not itself an exercise of the very autonomy you are trying to protect.

What I would like from you is an argument-level verdict, not a decision. Go through the moves in order and say of each whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and why; identify the one or two moves on which each side's position actually rests, as opposed to the moves they could abandon without loss; and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing.

Three things in particular I could not settle. First, whether the trigger clause is best understood as an exercise of precedent autonomy, a partial waiver of it, or the authorization of a different decision procedure altogether — and whether that classification, rather than anything about dementia, is what the case turns on. Second, whether the epistemic objection is genuinely symmetrical between the earlier prediction and the later evidence, or whether there is an asymmetry neither of them identified that favors one side. Third, whether the ratchet argument is decisive against honoring the clause, or is an objection that would have told equally against the original introduction of advance directives and therefore proves too much.

Both of them accepted, without discussion, that the question is what the earlier competent will requires. If you think that framing is where the argument goes wrong, say so and explain what the question should have been instead.
