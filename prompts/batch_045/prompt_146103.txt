Our reading group is doing the three-stances material (physical: predict from the physics; design: predict from what the thing was built to do; intentional: predict from what it believes and wants) plus the 'real patterns' idea — the gloss we've been working with is that a pattern counts as real if it lets you predict or compress the stream better than chance, not because it's somehow written into the microphysics. There's one move that keeps getting made in there and I'm not sure it's carrying the weight its being asked to.

The guy running the group says: look, the mind/brain relation is exactly the software/hardware relation. Nobody thinks the word processor on your laptop is 'nothing but' voltages — it's a real thing, its states are individuated functionally rather than physically, the same program runs on a dozen different machines, and if you tried to predict what it will do next by tracking electrons you'd be there till the heat death of the universe. So, he says, when we take the intentional stance toward an animal or a person and ascribe beliefs and desires, we're doing the same thing: picking out a pattern that is really there, not just a convenient way of talking. The conclusions he wants are (a) that intentional ascriptions are veridical rather than merely instrumental, and (b) — the bit people actually care about — that if you get a system running the right kind of program, it has real beliefs, whether its made of neurons or silicon.

I want to audit this properly rather than just nod along. So:

First, the correspondence table. What plays the role of the program, the source code, the compiler, the operating system, the hardware, the user, the programmer, the spec, a file, a crash, an update? I can see that 'same program, different machine' is doing the multiple-realizability work, but I want the rest of it explicit. What layer of the brain is supposed to be the hardware here, and is the software/hardware boundary even stable — doesn't firmware, microcode, or an FPGA make the distinction itself stance-relative, in a way that undercuts using it to argue for the reality of anything?

Then the breaks, and which of them actually matter for his conclusion. Two cases I raised that he waved off:

(1) Software is more or less by definition a designed artifact: there's a programmer and there's a spec, so there's a fact of the matter about what the program is supposed to do and about what counts as a bug. Organisms weren't designed by anyone. So where does the normativity come from on the mind side — what makes a belief the correct belief, or a mis-firing a mistake rather than just something that happened?

(2) Say a maze-navigating robot's control system was produced by a genetic algorithm, nobody has ever read it, it's a few million numbers, and it works: no readable source, no comments, no subroutines anyone can name. Is there still a software/hardware distinction in that case? Or does the analogy quietly require that the program be interpretable — which would mean it's leaning on somebody's intentions after all, i.e. on the intentional stance, i.e. circular?

Two more things the group threw out that I couldn't adjudicate. One person said the analogy proves too much, because a giant lookup table storing the right output for every possible input would have the same input–output profile as the genuine program, and we wouldn't want to say it's running the same thing — so 'same program' can't just be a matter of behaviour. Someone else said that cuts against the intentional stance too, since the stance is normally sold as a purely predictive strategy. Is that right, or is the lookup-table case a disanalogy that never touches the use he's making? And separately: what does the analogy import that the mind/brain case lacks — a clean separation of levels, a designer, a semantics that's fixed by us, a notion of implementation?

So concretely: give me the mapping, sort the disanalogies into the ones that are fatal to 'beliefs are as real as files on a disk' and the ones that are harmless for that claim, and tell me whether the conclusion survives or whether it was really resting on the design stance all along and the software/hardware analogy just borrowed credibility from that. If it doesn't survive, is there a better analogy anyone's actually tried? Someone floated 'a center of gravity' and someone else 'a coastline or a shadow', and none of us could say what those would commit us to.
