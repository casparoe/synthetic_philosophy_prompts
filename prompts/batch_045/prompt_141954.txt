I'm a high school senior, and I've ended up defending a fairly specific package of claims about the St. Petersburg game against the other people in my math club. I'd like to know what would actually count against it.

The package: the game is not a real decision-theoretic problem, only a demonstration that maximizing expected money is the wrong rule; Bernoulli's replacement of money with utility, using a concave and bounded utility function, dissolves it; and the residue of weirdness is an artifact of assuming a casino that can pay out unbounded sums.

Two replies I've learned to block, and one I can't:

- The finite-bankroll reply. I've restated the case so it can't help: the house is a standing consortium of sovereign governments with taxing and money-issuing power, so whatever figure comes up gets paid, and the offer is strictly one-shot, one player, one play, any finite entry fee you name. Call it the Sovereign Petersburg. I want to know what survives once the balance-sheet and repeat-play escapes are closed.
- The turbo version: if payoffs grow like 2^(2^n) instead of 2^n, then even log utility gives a divergent expectation. My move is that you just pick a more slowly growing utility function, and that in general no bounded utility function is safe, because a prize schedule can always be written to outrun it. I can't tell whether that is fatal to my view or just a fact of life for any decision rule.
- The time-average reply: that a player is averaging over time rather than over an ensemble, so the right quantity is a growth rate rather than an expectation. I don't have a settled answer to this.

What I want: what would count against the bounded-utility dissolution, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and given from the inside, meaning what an expected-utility theorist would themselves concede counts against it, not only what its critics say. Does the answer depend on closing off the escapes the way I have? Is there anything empirical at stake, such as whether actual attitudes to large, low-probability payoffs fit a bounded concave curve, and whether there is a result along the lines of modest risk aversion about small sums forcing absurd risk aversion about large ones under this rule? And, honestly: has the view been qualified into something that can't be wrong, and if it has, is that a defect here? I would be most moved by an argument that stays inside the one-shot Sovereign version, since that is the case I take to be cleanest.
