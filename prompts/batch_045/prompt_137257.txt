hey — I'm TAing a first-year historiography seminar next week and the session is built around that hoary old line about the past being a foreign country where they do things differently. Everyone reaches for it constantly and as far as I can tell it's doing completely opposite jobs depending on who's talking, so I want to take it apart properly instead of letting it skate through.

Two uses I keep running into. Use A, the tourist use: a student says we basically can't morally assess anyone before 1900 or so — they were working with a different set of customs, so blame is a category error, roughly like yelling at locals for not speaking your language. Use B, the prosecution use: another student says fine, it's a foreign country, but we don't suspend judgment about what goes on in foreign countries today — we condemn practices elsewhere all the time — so foreignness buys past agents nothing at all, and the analogy is meant to show that understanding isn't excusing.

My own half-formed addition, which I can't tell is a fix or a sabotage: the past isn't really a foreign country, it's more like a country we annexed and then built our constitution out of its rubble. Our moral vocabulary is partly a bequest from the people we're judging, so we're auditing them with a ledger they helped write — call it the double-entry problem if you want. There's nothing like that in tourism.

So: give me the correspondence table first — what plays the tourist, the border, the guidebook, the locals, the local custom, the return ticket, the home country's norms — and say whether Use A and Use B are even mapping things the same way. Then the disanalogies, and please sort them into ones that actually bite on the intended uses and ones that are just decoration. Ones I can see but can't weigh: the locals are dead and can't answer back, revise their story, or be negotiated with, and there's no present government to pressure; we're causally downstream of these people in a way we aren't from a country we visit; "they do things differently" pretends a whole society has one shared custom when half the time the contemporaries were screaming at each other about it, so who exactly is "they"; foreignness is symmetric but temporal distance isn't; and the traveler's home norms get treated as the correct ones rather than as just local too.

Tell me which of Use A and Use B survives once that's on the table, whether my annexation tweak rescues either of them or quietly concedes the whole question, and whether there's a better analogy — arguing with your younger self, joining an argument late, reading a family archive, whatever — or whether the honest verdict is that the foreign-country picture is carrying conclusions that nothing in it supports. Under 700 words including the table, and skip the bibliography entirely, I've got the reading list.
