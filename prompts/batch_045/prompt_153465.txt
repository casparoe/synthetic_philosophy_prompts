You are a professional philosopher who has taught Strawson's 'Freedom and Resentment' (1962) to many different audiences, and who is also good at watching a room and predicting where a discussion will go wrong. Before you start writing, spend some time brainstorming: sketch two or three genuinely different ways the session could be organised, say in a sentence each what would be gained and lost by each, and only then commit to the one you recommend. Think carefully about dependencies rather than producing a list of topics.

The setting: I run a monthly discussion evening in the back room of a bookshop — about twelve regulars, mostly working adults, a couple of retired teachers, a nurse, two engineers, a lawyer, none of whom have studied philosophy. They will have read a short handout of excerpts from Strawson's essay beforehand, plus nothing else. I have ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and no slides. I have no academic background; I came to this on my own after an argument with a friend who said that once you take psychology and neuroscience seriously, blame is just a leftover habit, and I have been chewing on Strawson's reply ever since. Please do not recommend any further reading — we only have the one handout, and that constraint is fixed.

Here is my own grasp of it, in my own improvised vocabulary, which I would like you to correct if it is muddled: Strawson's point is not really a theory of what responsibility is but a claim about what we are doing when we resent, feel grateful, feel let down, forgive — what I have been calling 'being inside the relationship' — and about what it would be to step outside it and treat a person as a thing to be handled, managed, treated, or avoided, which I have been calling 'stepping outside'. Excuses (he didn't mean it, he didn't know, he had no choice) keep you inside, whereas judgements like 'he is a small child' or 'he is not in his right mind' move you outside. Strawson then argues, as I read him, that we could not step outside wholesale even if determinism were true, because that would amount to giving up ordinary personal relationships altogether. Tell me plainly if any part of that is wrong or if my improvised labels will actively mislead the group, and give me better plain-English labels if so.

What I want from you is a plan for teaching the distinction between the two standpoints, not an explanation of it. Specifically:

The order the ideas have to come in, and what depends on what. I suspect the two-standpoint contrast cannot be introduced until the group has first felt, from the inside, what a reactive attitude actually is — but I am not sure whether to start with resentment or with gratitude, or with something smaller like being hurt by a friend's tone, and I would like your reasons.

The one case that should carry the whole session, and what it must have and must lack. My instinct is a friend or sibling who has wronged you in a way that is real but not catastrophic, where the excuse is genuinely unclear — but tell me what features the case needs for the two standpoints to look like live options, and what features would ruin it (too legal, too sympathetic, too extreme, involving someone the group has never been close to, and so on).

The misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Here are the ones I expect: that 'stepping outside' is the scientific or humane or grown-up attitude and that Strawson is defending mere vindictiveness; that on this view responsibility is just whatever people happen to feel, so a change in fashion would change the facts; that the reactive attitudes are raw feelings and therefore cannot be reasons-responsive or educable; that forgiving is simply the ceasing of resentment. Correct any item in my list that is not actually a misconception, and add the ones I have missed.

Three things I have got stuck on, and I want your judgement about whether each belongs in the plan, where, or whether it should be openly left out: (a) the 'thinning out' problem — someone whose capacities erode slowly, through dementia or addiction or years of grinding exhaustion, where the standpoint seems to fade rather than switch, so that there is no moment at which we step outside; (b) 'cold blame' — the judge, therapist, or exhausted parent who insists they feel no resentment at all while still holding the person responsible, and whether Strawson's picture can make sense of responsibility-talk continuing after the attitudes have been deliberately switched off; (c) the bystander asymmetry — the people who were not wronged are often angrier than the person who was, and I cannot tell whether Strawson's emphasis on how others' will is directed at us makes third-party and self-directed attitudes second-rate, or whether that is just a gap I am reading in.

Finally: a check, other than asking for a definition, that would let me tell at the end whether the distinction has actually landed, and an explicit statement of what I should refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I am refusing — I would rather leave them with one clear idea than three half-understood ones.
