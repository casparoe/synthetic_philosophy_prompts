hey — I'm on a working group that has to write down criteria for which conditions count as diseases for long-term disability coverage purposes, and the philosophy literature has been basically useless in a way that I find interesting rather than annoying.

The debate I mean: on one side the value-free biostatistical accounts (Boorse-ish: health is statistically species-typical functional contribution of a part, relative to a reference class, with no values smuggled in anywhere); on the other side the value-laden ones, especially the "harmful dysfunction" hybrid (a value-free dysfunction component plus a separate, socially valued harm component) and the more thoroughgoing normativist stuff where health is something like a person's ability to pursue their own vital goals. I know these roughly, I don't need a summary of them.

What happens in our meetings is that the stock examples do no work anymore. Everyone has a rehearsed line about homosexuality in the DSM, about drapetomania, about osteoporosis, about sickle cell trait, about masturbation. Bring one up and both camps produce their answer in under a minute and neither finds the other's answer even slightly tempting.

So I built a case I thought would actually break the tie, and it didn't, and that's why I'm writing. Here it is. A genetically isolated founder population of a few thousand people in which almost everybody carries a variant that turns the fear/stress response way down: no panic, essentially no PTSD after serious trauma, low baseline anxiety, and measurably worse threat detection, so more accidents and injuries. Nothing selected for it — it's drift plus a founder effect, and it's recent enough that it's not part of the species' design history either. It's the statistical norm inside the population and statistically subnormal outside it. Carriers mostly say it's fine or good, a minority say they'd pay to reverse it, and employers and insurers have loud opinions. And then a second version: someone with a statistically ordinary stress response pays to have it permanently turned down to the same level by a gene therapy.

What actually happened: the naturalist picked a reference class and got an answer. The harmful-dysfunction person said either "no selection history, so no dysfunction, so not a disorder" or "the mechanism isn't doing what it was selected to do, so yes" depending on who was talking. The normativist said ask the bearer and said the coverage question is just a separate question about what we owe people. Everyone got exactly the answer their account predicts, nobody's intuitions moved a millimeter, and we went to lunch.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't a substantive disagreement at all — either they're giving accounts of different things under one word, or each side has built in a dial (reference class on one side, "harm" on the other) that can absorb any case I hand them, so no possible case could ever adjudicate between them. But I'm genuinely unsure whether that's right, or whether something else is doing the work: a hidden empirical question, a difference about what a definition is *for*, or bedrock disagreement about whether medical categories track biology or ground entitlements.

So diagnose the stalemate rather than picking a winner. Back it up with what each side actually says when you turn the dials on them. And then the part I actually care about: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a principled constraint on reference-class choice, some kind of formal result, a change of question — or is the honest answer that no progress should be expected and our committee should just stipulate a definition for our purposes and move on? Please write in American English.
