I am finishing a novel set in a provincial archive, and I have come to a stop over a difficulty I cannot resolve alone, because I cannot tell whether it is a real difficulty or a private fussiness.

Near the middle of the book my protagonist, a junior archivist — not a detective, and not especially clever — comes to believe that a bundle of letters recently donated to the archive is a forgery. Her grounds are worthless. She has misread a watermark, which is in fact of the correct period; and the conviction settles on her in part because she has taken an intense dislike to the donor, a man she once saw behave badly at a funeral. She is nevertheless right. The letters are forgeries, made some thirty years ago by the donor's own grandson, for reasons that have nothing whatever to do with watermarks.

Everything afterward depends on that belief. Because she is convinced, she works through the donor's remaining papers, where she finds a receipt she would otherwise never have thought to look for; from the receipt she works out, by an inference I have checked and rechecked, where the forgeries were produced; and the book closes with the fraud exposed and her publicly congratulated for having seen what her superiors did not. Every belief she ends with is true, every step after the first is scrupulous, and the outcome is the one a better-equipped investigator would have reached.

My editor says the thing is fine as it stands. Her view is that the reader cares whether Nell is right and whether she acts, and that the route by which she arrived is a technicality. When I pressed her, she put it this way: you keep asking for something besides the truth, but if she has the truth and acts on it, and it all comes out as it should, what more could there be, and what would it even be?

I have two uneasements and I do not know whether either is sound. The first is that something is missing from her achievement — that she was right, but not right of her own accord. The only way I can make this precise is to say that in circumstances only slightly different from the actual ones, the same conviction would have formed in her by the same route and been false, and that this is somehow worse than being right for a reason. The second concerns what happens downstream. I had been working to a rule of thumb, that the book may contain one accident of this kind provided nothing after it is careless. My first reader says the opposite: that a chain resting on one unearned link takes on the defect of that link however carefully the later links are forged, and that my protagonist never, at any point in the novel, occupies a position in which the truth can be credited to her. She wants a late chapter in which the real explanation surfaces and Nell learns how badly she had reasoned. My editor thinks this would be an anticlimax, and that the reader will feel cheated of the ending.

Is my editor right that the difference I am reaching for is not a real difference? If it is real, what exactly is it, what would Nell have had to possess for it to be present, and does it matter that she would be of no use a second time? And is my first reader right that it spreads through the rest of the book, or does careful reasoning afterward repair the earlier fault? I would also like to know which way of staging the ending would actually show the difference, if there is one, rather than merely assert it.
