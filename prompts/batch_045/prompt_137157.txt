I work as a research assistant to a philosopher who is finishing a book manuscript on emotional responses to fiction, and I have been keeping a log of her verdicts on every case that comes up in the manuscript, in her graduate seminar, and in conversation. I want to predict what she will say about a case she has never discussed, and I want to know what she is really running.

Her verdicts so far:

1. Pity for the protagonist of a realist novel: genuine pity, the same emotion kind as pity for a real person. She explicitly rejects the move of calling our responses to fiction quasi-emotions or make-believe analogues standing in for the real thing.
2. Fear while watching a horror film: genuine fear, even though it produces no impulse to flee or to call anyone. On her view the missing action is explained by how the practice handles action, not by the fear being counterfeit.
3. Being moved by a passage of instrumental music with no story, characters, or propositional content: a genuine emotion, but not pity, fear, or grief — an objectless state she labels being moved. She insists on this because it gives her emotion without anything to imagine propositionally.
4. Pity for the hypothetical victim in a trolley-style example in a seminar: not genuine pity. We judge that the case would be sad and we feel something thin, but it is not the real thing. This is the verdict I find strangest, given how generously she grants emotions elsewhere.
5. A memoir later exposed as fabricated: while you believe it, genuine pity; once you learn it was invented, the pity normally collapses; if it survives, the residual state is, she says, the same kind of state you get from a novel. So the same emotion kind can be produced either by a false belief or by imagining, and the route does not change the kind.
6. Disgust at screen gore and the flinch at a jump scare: genuine, but she treats them as easy cases that plainly need no belief and sets them aside as doing no work for her view.
7. A character she invented herself, in a novel she is drafting, whose ending she has already decided and written in her notes: she reports genuine anxiety for the character while writing the middle chapters and does not treat this as a difficulty.
8. Wanting to visit an invented city from a fantasy novel: a genuine desire with a merely imagined object.

The new case. A subscription service sends you a weekly chapter of an ongoing serial novel. The text is generated by a language model working from a profile it maintains of you: your name, your job, your family, what you have responded to before. The protagonist shares your first name and much of your biography. In this week's chapter, the protagonist's brother dies. You know the text was generated, that nothing is meant by it, that the model has no experiences, and that the character is not a depiction of your actual brother, who is alive and well. Variant A: the app shows a continue differently button — you may re-roll the chapter and receive a version in which the brother lives, and the app retains whichever version you keep. Variant B: the identical story with the button removed.

My hypothesis is that she is running a rule about fixity — that grief requires the loss to present itself as settled rather than as something it is up to you to arrange — which would also explain verdicts 4 and 7 together, and which would predict attenuated grief in variant A and restored grief in variant B. But I can also imagine her running a rule about thickness of imaginative engagement, or about responding to a work rather than to an engineered stimulus, and those would not diverge on the button at all.

So: identify the rules that fit her eight verdicts, say what each predicts about variants A and B, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and tell me which single further case would best separate the surviving rules — I was thinking either a matched human-authored text with identical wording, or the same scenario presented as a bare summary rather than an unfolding chapter. If her verdicts underdetermine the case, say what she would be committing to with each answer.

Finally, on confidence: tell me how confident you are in the prediction and in the diagnosis, flag where you are extrapolating from thin evidence, and name the one question I could put to her that would most reduce your uncertainty. And if you think the exercise is misposed — if the rules I describe do not really make different predictions here, if genuine emotion is not the sort of thing that admits verdicts of this shape, or if verdict 5 already settles more than I think — say that plainly rather than giving me a polite prediction.
