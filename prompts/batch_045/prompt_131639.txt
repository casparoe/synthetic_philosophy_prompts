I serve on the committee rewriting the procedures for my school's Honor Council, and I have run into a technical question that I cannot settle on my own.

The Council has five members and hears cases under a rule with three elements. A finding of violation requires (1) that the student submitted work containing material produced by another person or by a generative tool, (2) that the student did not acknowledge it, and (3) that the material was more than trivial. The first two are questions of fact, usually settled by a document history. The third is a judgment call, and my sense is that members are barely better than chance on it. We also have a separate charge that applies if the student either submitted such material or shared the assignment with another student — a disjunction rather than a conjunction.

Having come across the discursive dilemma, I am now unsure how to structure the vote. As I understand the choice, we can either vote on each element and then apply the rule mechanically, or skip the elements and vote only on the final finding.

My instinct is that the second is both more accurate and more legitimate, and I would like to know whether that is right before I argue for it. On accuracy: the element-by-element route requires three separate majority votes instead of one, and each is another opportunity for the majority to be wrong, so it should be strictly worse than a single vote on the one thing we actually care about. My committee-mate says that people who study these questions generally conclude the opposite — that element-by-element is usually better at tracking the truth — but I cannot see why. Surely more majority votes means more chances to err. On legitimacy: element-by-element can produce a finding of violation that most of the Council would reject if asked directly about guilt, which seems to defeat the purpose of having five people rather than one.

Two complications I have not seen addressed anywhere. First, our elements are not equally hard, and my guess is that the difficult third element strengthens the case for voting only on the finding, since it contributes more error than the easy ones do. Second, I cannot tell whether the answer changes for the disjunctive charge; my argument as stated seems to cover both cases equally, which makes me suspicious of it. For what it is worth, the Council deliberates together before voting.

What, specifically, am I getting wrong?
