I'm taking a seminar on the ethics of force and I'm trying to work out what I actually think about humanitarian intervention by going case by case instead of from the top down, because the responsibility-to-protect framing we were given seems to settle the easy cases and do no work on the hard ones. My gut verdicts:

Kosovo, 1999: NATO's bombing campaign to stop the expulsion and killing of Kosovar Albanians was justified, even though the Security Council never authorized it and Russia would have vetoed.

Iraq, 2003: not justified. No atrocity was under way, and the case for it rested on weapons claims that turned out to be false.

Libya, 2011: the opening phase — the no-fly zone and strikes authorized to keep Qaddafi's forces out of Benghazi — was justified; the slide into regime change and his killing was not. I genuinely cannot tell whether that makes the whole intervention unjustified or whether one part can be justified and another not.

A case I invented: a state intervenes, stops an ongoing massacre, and does it partly to secure an oil concession. Justified. Motives look irrelevant to me.

The same intervention, identical in conduct, casualties and outcome, but carried out by one state acting alone rather than by a coalition: not justified, or at least much weaker. And it isn't hypocrisy that bothers me, because I don't think an intervener's domestic human-rights record disqualifies it.

One more: a recognized government invites foreign troops to help crush a rebellion, and those troops can foresee that the government will use the restored security to carry out mass killing in the rebel region. Not justified, even though invitation is normally the cleanest title to use force.

The pair I can't reconcile is the motive case against the coalition case. If what justifies an intervention is what it does — the people it saves — then why should it matter whether it is done by many states or one, when it doesn't matter why it is done? My own tentative principle is that an intervention is justified when it averts an ongoing or imminent mass atrocity, when it is the least rights-violating means likely to succeed, and when the intervener is not the sole judge of its own compliance with the first two conditions. But I can't see how that third clause does the work I want — it looks like it condemns unilateral action for being powerful rather than for being partial, which is not my objection.

Can you give me a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, that gets these verdicts or nearly all of them? Please state it precisely enough that I can apply it, say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed — arming a rebel group that would probably stop the atrocities and would probably commit some of its own — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up and why that one is the weakest.
