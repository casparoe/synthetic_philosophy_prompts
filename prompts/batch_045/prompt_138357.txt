I'm on the programming committee of a tiny repertory cinema — four of us, we pick about thirty titles a year, we meet for an hour a month, and the whole thing collapses if our one sponsor gets nervous or a pick turns into a local news story. Twice this year we've deadlocked over whether to show work by someone who's done something awful, and both times the same line ended the discussion: "you can just separate the art from the artist." It was doing real work — it was used to close the question, not open it — and I've come to think it doesn't mean one thing.

So I want the menu of precise claims that sentence could be making. Please don't give me a reading list or point me at papers; I can't chase them and I don't want the standard autonomism-versus-moralism essay anyway. Some readings I can half-see but can't state cleanly: that a work's moral faults are never artistic faults; that they sometimes are, but only when the fault is *in* the work instead of merely upstream of it; that even if it's a flaw that's no reason not to programme it; that the only real question is who gets paid and who gets credited; that we usually don't know enough about dead or private people to judge at all; and the one that actually worries me — that knowing what we now know changes what the work *means*, not just how we feel about it, which would make the separating impossible in principle rather than merely tactless. There are surely readings I'm missing, and some of these are probably platitudes wearing a costume.

Three live cases, invented but all close to arguments we've actually had: (1) a canonical 1970s film about the abuse of power, directed by a man convicted decades later of exactly that; (2) a documentary whose central scenes were filmed by deceiving the participants, who've since said they'd have consented but want a cut of any profits; (3) a restored silent film whose new score is by a living composer who's asked to have her name taken off it because of who bought the restoration, and where taking her name off visibly changes how the film plays. For each reading I want a verdict — trivial, substantive and defensible, or false — and I want to know whether it would settle any of my three cases or only the easy ones.

Give me exactly ten numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up. Every item should be one precise reading of the line, your verdict on it, and which of my three cases (if any) it would actually settle. Order them roughly weakest to strongest, and don't hide which of them my colleague was probably reaching for.
