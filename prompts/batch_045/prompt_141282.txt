I'm a retired quantity surveyor with more time on my hands than I know what to do with, and over the last couple of years I've fallen into arguing about the ethics of eating animals with two people who disagree with each other as much as they disagree with me: my brother-in-law, who raises cattle on permanent pasture, and my daughter, who hasn't knowingly eaten an animal product in nine years. I should say up front that I've picked up what I know from comment sections and long blog posts rather than from journals, so if there is a settled academic vocabulary for what I'm about to describe, it hasn't reached me. What keeps happening in these arguments is that a particular kind of consideration gets introduced as a tiebreaker — the wild animals affected by how we farm — and whichever one of us introduces it, the numbers reliably come down on his or her side. Two examples.

Take beef against chicken, compared per kilogram of meat. The chicken case means vastly more individual domesticated deaths per kilo, since the animals are small, but much less land; the beef case means a single large animal per several hundred kilos, but far more land per kilo, hence more habitat given over to farming and more field animals killed at harvest. So the direct-death count points one way and the land-use count the other, and nothing tells you how to weigh them against each other — that much I can see. But what I can't get past is that the land-use term doesn't seem to have a stable sign in the first place. On my brother-in-law's ground the pasture is full of voles, birds and insects that are there because the pasture is there. If that land went back to scrub, or to woodland, or to a housing estate, the animals on it would be an entirely different set, and some of those alternatives would hold fewer animals than the pasture does. So whether keeping cattle on it adds or subtracts wild-animal deaths depends on a counterfactual that nobody in the argument ever states, and each of us quietly supplies the one that suits our conclusion. The second example is that the whole ranking also reshuffles depending on what it is a ranking of: per kilo of meat, per gram of protein, per calorie, per meal, per hectare per year. Two diets can trade places when you switch units, and the choice of unit seems to carry a view about what is supposed to be minimised.

A friend who studied philosophy said the first example reminded her of the puzzle about future people in which no particular individual ends up worse off; but as she described it, that puzzle concerns who counts as harmed, whereas what bothers me is whether a single consideration has a direction at all before some comparison class is fixed. So I'm after the concept, not the verdict on beef. Is there an established name for a consideration whose sign is settled entirely by an unstated counterfactual baseline, and for the move of presenting such a consideration as though it were a plain empirical finding? Is the business with the units the same phenomenon or a different one? How does any of it differ from ordinary empirical uncertainty, from genuine incommensurability, and from simply picking the baseline that supports what you already believed — is the strategic version a separate thing, or the same thing done in bad faith? If nothing established fits, I'd rather you told me so and gave me a term with a definition and a couple of boundary cases than handed me a label that only half applies.
