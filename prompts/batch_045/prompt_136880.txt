I teach metaphysics, and I have been circling for some months on a short paper about what Hilbert's Hotel is actually supposed to establish. I have drafted the argumentative core of it below, and before I decide whether it is worth expanding into something I would send to a journal, I would like a referee-style reaction: the kind of response a good anonymous reader would give, not a summary and not encouragement.

Draft, §§2–5 (roughly 800 words):

**2. The deflation and where it stalls.**
The mechanics are familiar. A hotel with rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, … is full; a new guest arrives; the manager asks each guest to move from room *n* to room *n* + 1; room 1 is free. A party of countably many newcomers can also be housed, by doubling. And the Cantorian moral is equally familiar: *full* means only that every room is occupied, and the existence of a bijection between the guests and a proper collection of the rooms is not a contradiction but the defining feature of an infinite set — the Dedekind criterion, which given the usual choice assumptions is simply what it is to be infinite. On this reading the Hotel is not a paradox but theatre: it dramatises the failure, for infinite totalities, of the principle that a whole exceeds each of its proper parts. Entertain an actually infinite collection at all and you have already granted the story everything it asks for. Note also that the Hotel's hospitality has an exact ceiling: it takes countably many newcomers and no more, since there is no injection from the reals into the naturals. So whatever the story shows, it is a story about ℵ₀ in particular, and not about the infinite as such.

**3. Static and dynamic readings.**
I want to argue that the deflation is too quick, though not for the reasons its critics offer. Distinguish two claims. The *static* claim: there exists an assignment of guests to rooms such that everyone is housed and some room is vacant. The *dynamic* claim: the manager can get the hotel from its initial state to such an assignment. The first is a claim about the existence of a bijection and is, I think, genuinely unproblematic; the Cantorian is right about it. But it is not what the story asks you to imagine. Imagining the accommodation is imagining a procedure being carried out, and a procedure needs to be executable: either infinitely many coordinated simultaneous moves, or a completed infinite sequence of moves, which is to say a supertask. A referee will object here that simultaneous moves are not a supertask at all — a supertask requires an infinite sequence with a limit stage, whereas we have merely infinitely many events in parallel — and I concede this. But note what the concession costs: the possibility of the accommodation is now hostage to the metaphysics of simultaneous causation across an infinite domain and not to the arithmetic of cardinality. Either way, the argument against actual infinities that runs through the Hotel is borrowing its force from a separate thesis about infinitely many coordinated events or about supertasks; and that thesis is not free, since it looks uncomfortably close to the premise one would need to revive Zeno, and it is not obvious that a continuous or broadly Newtonian world can be described without infinitely many simultaneous events. The critic of the infinite thus owes us either an account of ordinary motion or an admission that the Hotel contributes nothing independent.

**4. A complication the story hides.**
Here is a consideration I have not seen discussed, and it makes me suspect the story is doing something other than what either side says. Among hotels with countably many rooms, Hilbert's is unusual. Index the rooms by the integers and the instruction *move to the room one greater than yours* frees nothing: it is an order-automorphism. A reassignment that does free a room is available — guests in non-positive rooms move down one, the rest stay — but it must treat some guests differently from others. Index the rooms by the rationals and there is no next room, so no successor-instruction can even be formulated, even though the rooms are still order-isomorphic to a proper part of themselves, by the familiar classification of countable dense orders without endpoints. What makes the standard Hotel vivid, I suggest, is not that it is countably infinite but that its rooms have the order type ω: a well-order with a first element, on which a single uniform local instruction — add one — realises a bijection onto a proper final segment. The shock, if that is the right word, is a response to order structure rather than to size. This is awkward for both parties. The critic of actual infinities is not really reacting to an infinite totality but to a very particular ordering of one. And the Cantorian deflation, pitched entirely at the level of bijections, never engages what the reader is being asked to picture.

**5. What is left.**
What survives, I want to say, is epistemological. No guest can verify, from anything locally available, that the new assignment is one-to-one and leaves exactly one room free; the fact is knowable only globally, by proof. Call this the unsurveyability of the infinite, and take the residual discomfort the Hotel produces to be the discomfort of a truth that is provable but not confirmable by any local inspection, even in principle. But unsurveyability is not peculiar to the infinite: whether some twenty-digit number is prime is equally beyond survey, and nobody infers that it is not determinately true. So the critic who reaches for unsurveyability must either accept the same conclusion about large finite numbers — becoming, in the strict sense, a finitist about what is feasible — or explain why unsurveyability damages a claim only when the claim concerns an infinite totality. I do not see how to make the second option out, but I am not confident, and I do not know which horn I would take.
