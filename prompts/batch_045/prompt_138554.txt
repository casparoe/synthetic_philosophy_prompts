I want to ask for a poem, but let me first say where I'm coming from, because it should affect how you answer. I have no academic background — no philosophy, and no physics past a single high-school course — and for three or four years now I've been reading, slowly and entirely on my own, about one thing: Kurt Gödel's 1949 solution of Einstein's field equations for a rotating universe, and what Gödel took that solution to show about time. I got started on an old popular book a friend lent me. I'm asking for the poem because I want to send it to that friend, who is a mathematician and who will check it, so I'd rather have something short on gesture and right in the details than the reverse. Please write for me rather than for a seminar: I can follow a hard idea if it's put in plain words, but I can't follow a hard idea that only gets gestured at.

The form I want is a pantoum: quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza reappear as the first and third lines of the next, so that the last line of the poem is its first line again. Five or six quatrains, rhymed ABAB, not padded out past roughly twenty-four lines. I want that form for a reason, and the reason is the subject: the return is not decoration here, it is the whole thing.

Here is what I think I understand, so you can see where I'm starting and so you can correct me if I've got it wrong. In Gödel's solution all the matter is turning — a homogeneous swirl of dust, with a cosmological constant tuned against the density of the dust — and the turning does something to the light cones, the local boundaries of what can reach what later. As I picture it, the cones lean farther over the farther out you go from the axis they turn around, and past a certain radius they have leaned so far that a circle drawn around that axis counts as a path forward in time: something moving always inside its own light cone, never faster than light, on what feels like an ordinary journey, can arrive back at the event it set out from, which is its own past. And this isn't a trick of the space being wrapped around itself — I gather the spacetime is simply connected, so you can't unroll it and be rid of the loops. Gödel did not offer it as a picture of our universe; ours doesn't appear to be turning as a whole, and I believe he granted that. He drew a philosophical conclusion from it anyway: roughly, that if the laws permit a world in which there is no single succession of "nows" for the whole of things, and in which an event's past is still there to be revisited, then the passage of time is not a feature of the world as it is in itself but something we bring to it. I believe he put that in Kant's vocabulary, as the ideality of time.

What I want the poem to carry is that metaphysical oddity and not the machinery of time travel, and I want the usual machinery kept out of it: no grandfather paradox, no altered past, no rendezvous with a younger self who is startled to be visited, no device that fails at the crucial moment. On the reading I've come to, the loop is uneventful: the traveler reaches the past and nothing is different, and the reason is not that the world defends its own history but that there was never a moving present for events to have happened in. The bad news, if it's bad news, is not "you cannot change what happened"; it is that nothing ever happened in the way we say it does. I don't want the poem to console anyone about that. No closing on unity with the cosmos, no wisdom extracted from the circle — the circle is the argument, not a symbol of something.

Put the turn at the moment the speaker realizes that the direction they have been calling "around" is now the direction of later: the cone has gone past the vertical. Let the repeated lines do the work of showing this — a line that meant going out should, when it comes back around, mean having arrived where it began without that being a second time. And let the final line's return to the first line land as a conclusion rather than as a resolution: not a round trip that was worth taking.

Two more constraints. Don't name Gödel in the poem, and don't let it state his conclusion as a slogan — no "time is not real," no "time does not exist." Someone who has read about this should recognize the claim from how the poem is built; someone who hasn't should still have a poem about going away and coming back. Keep the diction contemporary and unadorned; write it in the first person, in the voice of someone who has made the trip.

Where I need you to commit is this: the loops can be read as telling us something about time itself, or as telling us only that the theory permits geometries our own world doesn't have — an oddity of the mathematics rather than a discovery about time. Those are genuinely different claims, and I want you to pick one and build the poem so that the commitment shows in its structure and in what the speaker notices, not in a line that announces it. Then, under the poem, give me a short prose note: which reading you committed to and your reason for it, in a sentence or two I could defend to my friend; where the poem leans on a contested reading or knowingly simplifies the physics; where my summary above is garbled or wrong, said plainly; and what the form made you trade — if strict rhyme or a repeated line would have gone nonsense in its second appearance, tell me where you bent the form and what that cost. Please be specific and decisive throughout; I don't want a menu of options to choose from, I want choices with reasons attached.
