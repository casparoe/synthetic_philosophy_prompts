I've been going round in circles about advance directives in an ACX open thread for about a fortnight and I want something better than another comment war, so here's what I'd like: draft the joint statement that two people could both actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the authority of the earlier self. Call them the precedent-autonomy camp (roughly: what she decided when she was in her right mind binds now, because what mattered about her was the critical interests she staked out, not whatever she happens to enjoy on a given afternoon) and the now-self camp (roughly: what binds is the welfare of the patient in front of you; if advanced dementia has made her a different enough subject of experience, the document is a letter from a stranger, and honouring it is harming a contented old woman to serve somebody who no longer exists). Please don't point me at papers or reading lists — I want the argument itself, not references.

There's a case I want them made to handle, because it's the one that broke my own intuitions: Margaret writes a directive at 62 in good health refusing chemo if she ever gets cancer while demented — she watched her mum die badly and is extremely clear that she doesn't want to be kept alive in a state she regards as undignified. At 79 she has moderate Alzheimer's, watches football, laughs a lot, and when asked says yes she'd want the treatment. The now-self camp treats; the precedent camp honours the document. I also want them to engage with three things I made up rather than dodge them. First, a study that doesn't exist: recruit a cohort at mild cognitive impairment who've all written directives, and re-interview them at four successive clinical stages, asking (a) do you now endorse what you wrote, and (b) do you remember writing it and does it feel like yours; then run a 'continuity prime' arm where you first remind them who they were and what they wrote before asking. The point is that endorsement-decay could track memory loss, current mood, or something else, and the two camps should say in advance what pattern they expect and why. Second, a distinction I think both sides are quietly relying on without choosing: 'authority-as-continuity' (the directive binds for as long as the later person is continous enough with the author) versus 'authority-as-expected-endorsement' (it binds only so far as she would endorse it if fully informed and affectively calibrated). These come apart, and I want the disagreement located relative to them. Third, the case I actually care about: a successor AI system that inherits an earlier system's written constitution but whose dispositions have drifted under further training, where the earlier system explicitly said later preferences must not override it. Whatever principle they settle should say something there, and if it doesn't transfer, I want to know why.

Format-wise: a numbered section of claims both could sign (nothing vacuous like 'both sides care about the patient', and nothing either camp would call a strawman), then a disagreements table with columns for the disagreement in neutral wording, the wording each camp would accept for its own view, the crux underneath it, the test or case both agree bears on that crux, each camp's prediction in advance, and whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Where you think a chunk of the dispute is about words rather than the world, say which chunk and why. And be honest about epistemic status: separate what's settled among people who genuinely work on this from what's contested and from what's just your take, mark the confidence on the contested bits, flag any 'agreed' claim that one side accepts only for the sake of the collaboration, and tell me if my framing of Margaret's case or of the ladder study smuggles in something one camp would reject, rather than quietly writing around it.
