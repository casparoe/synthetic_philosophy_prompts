I set the papers for a final-year undergraduate seminar on consciousness, and I am writing this year's take-home final, for which students have three hours and a ceiling of 2,000 words. I want one question on the meta-problem — the project of explaining why creatures like us are disposed to make the claims we make about consciousness, as opposed to explaining consciousness itself — which cannot be answered by laying out the usual standoff between realists, for whom phenomenal facts are a further class of facts, and illusionists, for whom our phenomenal judgements are a systematic introspective error. I have been following this argument in the rationalist blogosphere since the days of Overcoming Bias, and I can predict the replies in advance: someone says a solution to the meta-problem would settle the hard problem, someone else says it only concerns the aetiology of our beliefs and settles nothing, and the thread dissolves into mutual accusations of begging the question. My students are, for temperamental reasons, mostly sympathetic to illusionism, and I want a question that punishes that sympathy rather than flatters it.

The machinery I want the question built around is stipulative, so please treat the following definitions as mine and not as anyone's published view. Call a solution to the meta-problem diagnostically slack when the mechanism it posits would go on producing exactly the same reports — of ineffable, intrinsic, immediately known experience — whether or not anyone had any experience at all, so that the explanation would work equally well for a population whose phenomenal claims were uniformly false. Call a solution gripping when it explains not merely that we make phenomenal claims but why we make these ones: why introspection represents its objects as ineffable, as private, as qualitatively intrinsic, and as modally detachable from anything physical.

What I want the question to force into the open is that grip and slack are separable, and that separating them produces both a dilemma and a neglected third option. The illusionist's explanation is slack by design and treats that as a feature. The realist who wants a solution with grip but without slack seems committed to an introspective mechanism whose operation is explained by the phenomenal facts themselves, which drags back the very interaction problem the meta-problem was supposed to let us set aside. And there is a third structural possibility that the reading never seriously considers: the reports are true, but the mechanism producing them is fully explained without any appeal to the facts that make them true, so that our phenomenal beliefs are correct yet in some sense unmoored from what they are about. I want students to say whether that third option is coherent, what it would cost — presumably something about our epistemic standing rather than about the metaphysics — and whether it remains genuinely distinguishable from illusionism once the costs are paid. One of them will certainly try to settle the whole thing by imagining a system trained to produce human-like reports about its own inner life; I want the question to make that move earn its keep instead of letting it function as a conversation-stopper.

Please give me the question itself, a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme out of twenty that names the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks — including, as separate errors, treating the meta-problem as a straightforward empirical question about what people say, assuming that explaining the aetiology of a belief automatically debunks it, and assuming that diagnostic slack shows the reports to be false. Write the whole thing as continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no list of further reading, the way you would write it in an email to a colleague. At the end, and in that same prose, tell me how confident you are that the question has a determinate good answer at all rather than being a matter of intellectual taste, and name the one claim in your model answer that you would least want to have to defend against a determined student.
