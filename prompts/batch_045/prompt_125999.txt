I'm a contributing editor at a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I'm finishing a long feature on the quiet rise of suffering-first ethics: the cluster of views, much argued over in policy-adjacent circles, on which the relief of agony is not merely one good among others but dominates, and on which there may be some level of suffering that no amount of pleasure, achievement or meaning can compensate for. The digital team wants a companion interactive, and I'd like it to be a decision tree rather than a trivia quiz — something that walks a reader from things they already have an opinion about to the position they have ended up holding, with every branch labelled by the claim that does the splitting and every leaf carrying the name of a position (or, where nobody defends it, 'unnamed'). What I need from you is the instrument itself — the questions, the branch structure, the scoring logic — and not an essay about the debate. Please don't add sources, footnotes, further reading or named thinkers and papers: the piece is already over-length, I have the background, and I need something I can hand to a developer.

Here are the distinctions I want the tree built around, roughly in the order I think they should come, though I'd happily be told I've got the order wrong. First, whether pleasure and pain sit on a single cardinal scale at all, or are only ordinally comparable, or belong to a pluralist picture on which the two cannot simply be measured against one another; my instinct is that this has to come first, because it seems to decide what the later questions even mean. Second, whether there is any lexical threshold — a point past which pleasures stop counting altogether — and, if so, what does the work in locating it: sheer intensity, duration, or something qualitative. My own candidate, which I want included as a live branch, is that the threshold tracks a kind of disintegration rather than a quantity: suffering that destroys a person's capacity to care about the very things that were supposed to compensate them. Third, the level at which the threshold operates: a single episode, a whole life, or a population. Fourth, whether it is one-sided or symmetrical, in the sense that an equally extreme good could dominate in the other direction; I suspect nobody holds that, and I'd like the empty branch flagged with a sentence on whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Fifth, whether the threshold is a fact about value or a fact about choice — I keep returning to something a palliative-care physician in Leeds said to me, that patients who describe their pain as unbearable will in the same breath describe their lives as worth living, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is evidence against a threshold or only evidence that people are unreliable about their own past agony. That suggests a sixth branch which I have not seen discussed anywhere and would like built in properly: whether the sufferer's own attitude — consenting in advance, or afterwards not wanting the suffering removed — can quieten or void its special weight, or whether the weight attaches regardless of what anyone thinks about it. And last, something about uncertainty: whether a threshold, if there is one, is the sort of thing you can multiply by a small probability, so that a one-in-a-million chance of threshold-crossing agony outranks the certainty of a million mild headaches. A reader wrote in to insist on that case and I think it separates the threshold views along a seam the usual framing conceals.

For format: seven to nine branch questions, each phrased so that an intelligent non-specialist can answer it without knowing which position it leads to — concrete cases rather than labels, two or three options each, and nothing that simply asks the reader which -ism they prefer. Then locate the positions in the space the questions generate rather than starting from a list of names: at minimum a straightforward symmetric single-scale view, a view that weights suffering more heavily without any lexical break, a lexical threshold view, a pluralist or incommensurabilist view, and a sceptical view on which the question has no determinate answer. Note which answer combinations are unoccupied and diagnose briefly why. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really distinguish rather than pretending the split is clean. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, since the point of the sidebar is to make readers uncomfortable in an informative way. Keep the whole thing under about nine hundred words, in British English, and write the questions in a register that would not embarrass the magazine — I want to test it on my editor, a friend who does risk modelling and my mother before it goes up.
