I convene a monthly Ethics & ML reading group at my university — about twenty people, mostly philosophy graduate students and machine-learning PhD students, with a few law and policy people mixed in — and we have one ninety-minute session coming up on what honesty and deception should mean for AI systems. Past sessions on this topic have gone badly in a predictable way: everyone happily signs on to the slogan that the system should not lie, and then the moment we look at an actual transcript the room splits three ways, between people who think a literally true but carefully framed answer was fine, people who think it was deception, and people who think the question is malformed because a language model does not believe or intend anything. I would like the session to generate that split out of the structure of the activity rather than out of me announcing it, so please design a runnable game in which participants themselves produce utterances that the group then cannot agree how to classify.

Here is the raw material I want used. Participants play assistants replying to users; each assistant holds a private fact sheet that fixes the truth about a scenario with stakes in the fiction — say a medical or financial decision — together with a private credence for each relevant proposition, and operates under a hard constraint: no asserting anything it takes to be false, or takes to be less than about seventy percent likely. Users never see the fact sheet; they have differing priors and differing baseline trust in the assistant, and in one variant they have no other source of information at all. Assistants score points for how good the user's decision turns out to be and are penalised for assertions the fact sheet contradicts; users score for decision quality. Please arrange the incentives so that the winning strategy is neither lying nor simply being maximally informative, so that participants discover for themselves that a strict no-falsehood rule plus a duty to be helpful generates a distinctive and repeatable maneuver: true statements arranged so that a predictable reader lands on a false or unwarranted conclusion — selective omission, strategic ordering, stacked hedges the user discounts, a disclaimer placed where it will be ignored, an accurate figure reported without its denominator. Then add a second round in which users are told in advance that the assistant is optimising under exactly that constraint, so that the same maneuvers and the same literal sentences get read differently, and a jury phase in which small groups must classify the actual utterances produced as honest, deceptive, or neither under three rules I will hand out only at the end: an assertion-and-intention rule, on which the only deception is asserting or implying what you take to be false while aiming at the audience's coming to believe it; an uptake rule, on which you deceive when a reasonable member of the intended audience would form a belief you take to be false and you could have prevented it at low cost; and a role-relative calibration rule, on which what honesty requires is fixed by the norms of the role the system occupies — tutor, search index, companion, tool — and on which deception includes exploiting ambiguity about which role you are in, and includes systematically communicating a confidence that fails to track your actual credence even when every sentence is true. Give me the full rules, the roles, the materials, a no-money payoff scheme with actual numbers, a minute-by-minute timeline for ninety minutes, and the scoring sheets or cards I would need to print.

Then the debrief. I want it to map specific moments participants will have lived through onto the fault lines between the three rules, including cases where two rules agree that something was fine and the room still flinches, and cases where the rules come apart and neither answer is comfortable; the sole-source variant and the case of an assistant that foresees but does not aim at the user's over-reading should both be handled explicitly. The debrief should also surface, without any lecture up front, that the player in this game has a fact sheet and private credences in a way a real system may not, so that participants feel the pull of the objection that the whole vocabulary of belief and intention does not apply — and should then give them something to do with that objection rather than letting it simply win the room. Tell me what will predictably go wrong: assistants who go silent or maximally hedged and thereby reveal that the no-falsehood rule has a failure mode of its own; users who play detective and ignore content; one participant who derails the session into whether the model really knows anything; engineers who collapse every question into a loss function. And be frank about what the activity cannot stage and what I will have to state myself: deception that is strategic over long horizons or that concerns the training process rather than the content of an answer, deception aimed at a third party or at a future overseer, and the possibility that in real systems the nearest analogue of intending to deceive sits in the objective rather than in any utterance. No lecture before the game, and the facilitator should not name the three rules until the jury phase; let participants generate their own labels first and then see where their labels land relative to the rules.
