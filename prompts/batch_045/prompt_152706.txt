I am in a graduate seminar on naturalizing mental content, and I keep encountering the same analogy being used in the same room to license two opposite conclusions, so I would like an audit of it rather than a verdict on the overall view.

The background we are working with is the etiological or selected-effects theory of function — a trait's proper function is the effect for which earlier tokens of that trait were retained by selection — together with the teleosemantic move built on top of it: a neural state has the content it has because it has the proper function of being caused by, or of indicating, that thing, and it misrepresents when it is tokened without performing that function correctly. The analogy carrying the whole apparatus, as it was presented to us, is that natural selection is a designer. As a carburetor's function is settled by what the engineer installed it to do, a frog's fly-detector's function is settled by what selection installed it to do; and as a carburetor can be a defective carburetor, the detector can fire wrongly at a passing bead. So the argument goes: determinate content and the possibility of genuine misrepresentation out of purely natural materials, no intentionality in the base and no homunculus.

One seminar participant then ran the analogy the other way: an engineer's intention is what does the real work in the artifact case, so the biological case is only as-if, and the view is covertly intentionalist and fails to naturalize anything.

Before I decide what to think, I want the mapping made explicit and then tested. Please give me the correspondence table — engineer, blueprint, prototype testing, quality control, artifact, defect, intended use, redesign, obsolescence — and then break it where it breaks. In particular, I have a case I cannot get the analogy to speak to clearly. Consider a lineage of small predators with a neural structure N whose token state S causes a strike. For many generations N is retained because N-triggered strikes catch flies. Then the environment shifts permanently: flies are gone, a slow grub is now the only prey, and the very same structure N, with the same proximal causal profile and the same downstream strike, is retained because N-triggered strikes now catch grubs. Nothing about the mechanism changes; only the historical reason it is still around does.

Variants I care about: two hundred generations under the new regime; a single catastrophic season that runs the filter once and hard; and an environment that oscillates between flies and grubs with no net direction. In each, what is the content of S — fly, grub, fly-or-grub, prey in general — and does the artifact analogy help decide? A component designed as a carburetor and later installed as a doorstop seems to get its function reassigned by a second designer with a new intention, and intentions are datable and all-or-nothing. What is supposed to play that role on the biological side, and is there a principled answer to how much selection is required to lay down a new proper function, or is the analogy importing a determinacy that selection, being graded, unsynchronized and entangled with drift, simply does not have?

Two further things. First, keep the two payoffs separate: which disanalogies threaten the claim that content is determinate, and which, if any, threaten the claim that misrepresentation is possible — I suspect the analogy can carry the second while failing at the first. Second, say whether a better analogy is available; I have been toying with the trait as a business practice that a firm retains because it raises profits, with no one ever having intended it, and I cannot tell whether that is an improvement or just the same smuggling with different props.
