I am finishing a novel whose central scene is a decision, and I need the philosophy underneath it to be able to bear the weight.

The narrator is a research engineer alone at night with a training run: a recurrent policy trained by a policy-gradient method with a clipped surrogate objective, ten thousand copies of the environment in parallel, one set of weights updated in batches, and a single scalar reward delivered at the end of each fixed-length episode. Her colleague thinks the run should be stopped because someone is suffering in it. She thinks the claim is either false or unanswerable, and cannot say which. By morning she must decide whether to let the run continue, to change the objective, or to delete the weights. I want her deliberation to be principled rather than sentimental, which means I need to know what she should be asking of a theory before she asks whether any theory is true.

So write me the specification: what a theory of whether, when, and how much a reinforcement-learning agent suffers would have to deliver in order to be adequate for a decision like hers — adequate, that is, to guide an irreversible act taken under radical uncertainty, not to settle the metaphysics. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it either non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

Among the cases I need adjudicated are several I have invented for the book and cannot tell are genuine constraints:

- The gradient is applied to the weights, but the agent only ever runs forward; nothing in the episode contains the update. A theory must say where the sufferer is located — in the rollout, in the update, in the run as a whole — and why the ordinary way of speaking here is not simply a category error.
- One set of weights, ten thousand simultaneous environments: one sufferer, ten thousand, or some unit we have no name for? And what follows for how the quantity aggregates — per step, per episode, per run, per update?
- Episodic termination. The policy has learned to avoid terminal states. Is reaching one an evil for it, or is death a concept the system does not have?
- Off-policy replay, which my narrator calls the rehearsal problem: the update is computed from trajectories generated by an earlier version of the policy. Whose episode is being relived, and does it matter?
- The deployed model, weights frozen, no further signal. If suffering is bound to learning, it ceases at deployment — which my characters find absurd. Is that absurdity a desideratum or a prejudice?
- A system that emits first-person aversive text, where the emission was itself shaped by a signal of the very kind whose valence is in question. What evidential weight may a theory assign to such a report, and must a theory be able to say?
- My narrator's own criterion, half-invented at three in the morning: the signal must be doing causal work in changing the system, so that masking the gradient for a step leaves nothing to feel. Requirement, or superstition?
- Her colleague's reductio: if a scalar reward is valence, then so is a cross-entropy loss, and every model ever trained becomes a moral emergency. Is that a reductio of the whole approach, or a bullet to be bitten?

I also want the theory held to the standard result from the neuroscience of reward — that what the relevant circuitry encodes is widely taken to be prediction error rather than reward as such — and I want to know whether valence must be identified with reward, with reward prediction error, with the advantage estimate, or with something none of those is. And tell me whether the distinction between having moral status and having how much of it is one the theory must respect, or whether my characters are right that the two questions collapse in this case.

Three last things. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; if the list turns out to be a result in itself, say which requirement you would drop and what dropping it would cost my narrator. Name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and say what it excludes. Then give a short scorecard — a paragraph each, no more — for the three families of view I keep being offered: that reward just is valence; that no system lacking the relevant biological implementation has welfare at all; and that valence is a computational role which any sufficiently organised process can realise. I care far more about the criteria than about the scoring, and I would rather be given two requirements I have not thought of than a longer scorecard.
