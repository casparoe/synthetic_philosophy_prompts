I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm planning a long feature — roughly 5,000 words, about three months of reporting — on what Kripke's rule-following paradox does and doesn't show about large language models. Take the dialectic as background rather than as something to re-explain: I know the skeptic's claim that no finite body of past usage, behavior, or dispositional profile determines which rule a speaker means (the familiar move of producing an alternative rule that agrees with addition on every case in some finite sample and diverges thereafter); I know that the communitarian and non-reductionist "straight" answers try to locate a fact where the skeptic says there is none, while the skeptical solution concedes the missing fact and tries to recover as much of our ordinary talk about meaning, correctness, and agreement as it can without one. I don't need a survey, and I'll handle citations and interviews myself — what I need is help deciding where the reporting should go.

Two things have pushed me toward the AI application specifically. The first is an argument I keep returning to and haven't seen stated cleanly anywhere: a trained model's weights are fixed by a finite corpus and a finite training procedure, and that corpus is, like any finite sample of a speaker's history, compatible with indefinitely many rules that agree on everything in it and diverge later — so if the skeptic is right in the individual case, then more data, more parameters, and more compute look like they cannot even in principle settle which rule the system is following. Call it the scaling-indifference argument; I suspect it is either trivially sound or rests on a confusion I can't locate. The second is a proposal a philosopher sketched to me over dinner at a conference, which I haven't been able to shake: that what a trained system means by a term is fixed not by its actual dispositions but by its counterfactual fine-tuning profile — which of its dispositions survive ablation, distribution shift, continued pretraining, reinforcement learning from human feedback, or retraining on a corpus in which the term has been systematically substituted. I like that it turns something apparently unmeasurable into something you could in principle run an experiment on, but I genuinely cannot tell whether it is a straight solution, a skeptical solution wearing a lab coat, or a change of subject.

So here is what I want from you: a defended agenda, not a literature review. I have perhaps a dozen interviews and three months; I can't chase every thread. Of the open questions this cluster raises — whether the skeptic's conclusion is really semantic rather than merely epistemic; whether the scaling-indifference argument is sound and what would have to be true to break it; whether the counterfactual-profile proposal is a genuine new option or a redescription of an old one; whether any of this bears on mechanistic interpretability, on alignment worries about deception, on legal questions about machine-generated text and intention, or on the folk claim that models "really" understand — which three or four should I actually spend the piece on, in what order, and by what criteria? Make the criteria explicit and argue the trade-offs between them: how much of each thread's value depends on questions upstream of it, which ones I could make real progress on in a piece pitched at an educated general reader rather than at specialists, which are neglected relative to how much they would shift what a reasonable person concludes, and which look deep but would leave the reader's view unchanged. Equally important: which of the paradox's alleged implications for AI are overrated, or don't follow at all — including, if you think so, the implication that any of this gives us reason to doubt that models follow instructions in the ordinary, unphilosophical sense. Finally, tell me what I would have to learn in the next month — a result, an argument, a person to call — that would move something up or down your ranking.
