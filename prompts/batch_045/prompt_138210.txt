I'm a first-year statistics PhD student, and I'm also auditing a graduate seminar on formal epistemology; the last few weeks of the seminar have been on higher-order evidence and on what it calls epistemic akrasia. I'd like you to restate the three positions we keep circling in the vocabulary I actually work in: joint priors and conditioning, hierarchical models, model uncertainty and model averaging (including the case where the true model isn't in my set), proper scoring rules and calibration, Dutch-book arguments, and the fact that my own future credences ought to look like a martingale if I expect to be self-consistent.

The three positions, as neutrally as I can put them: first, that higher-order evidence is just evidence, so the rational response is to condition on it and let my first-order credence in the proposition move; second, that higher-order evidence properly moves a separate second-order attitude while leaving the first-order credence exactly where the object-level evidence put it, so that high credence in p together with high credence that this very credence is unreliable is sometimes not a defect but the rationally required package; third, that a self-consistency requirement forbids that package, and the only real question is which level has to give.

Use as your test bed a distinction the seminar hasn't drawn. Call the first kind audit evidence: I learn something about the causal process that produced my credence, e.g. that my sample suffered selection on the dependent variable or that the model I fit was misspecified in a way I can name and hence model. Call the second kind actuarial evidence: I learn only that among reasoners who have read exactly what I read and reasoned as I did, the resulting credences have been badly calibrated in the past, with no story about what went wrong and no rival model to average over. If it helps, take the self-referential version in which the actuarial claim is delivered by the same suspect process, so that any update I make on the basis of it is itself called into question and the target looks less like an update than like a fixed point.

For each of the three positions, tell me what translates cleanly, what you had to distort to fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all — and whether that residue is a limitation of the framework I think in or a sign that the seminar's talk of levels is doing less work than it looks like it's doing. Please write in American English, at the level of a graduate methods course, and skip the literature: I want the translation, not the reading list.
