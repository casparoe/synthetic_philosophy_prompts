I'm a second-year PhD student in the economics of education, and I've been asked to convene a two-day adversarial collaboration between two people on my faculty who have been arguing past each other about higher-education finance for years. One of them defends universities that are free at the point of entry and funded out of general taxation: she takes the German and Nordic arrangements as her reference point, treats a repayable obligation attached to the person as a disciplinary device that steers working-class students away from enrolling in the first place and steers graduates away from care, teaching, and other low-paid work, and thinks the whole 'the graduate should pay' framing quietly assumes a degree is a private consumption good rather than something the rest of society also consumes. The other defends income-contingent graduate contributions: his line is that free tuition inside a system with a progressive income tax is an upward transfer to the people who already capture most of the earnings premium, that a repay-if-you-earn obligation with a write-off date is insurance against a bad draw rather than a debt in the morally loaded sense, and that open-ended free enrolment produces long enrolment spells, cheap re-entry, and credential inflation. They have agreed in advance to sign whatever joint memo comes out of the two days, and my job is to put a draft in front of them on the first morning.

What I want from you is that memo. Give me a numbered section of claims both could sign without hedging — I'd hope it includes something about how much of the graduate earnings premium is real human capital and how much is positional or signalling, something about tuition finance and maintenance finance being two separate instruments that are almost always argued about as if they were one, something about the effective marginal tax rate faced by a graduate who would otherwise take low-paid work, and something about the cross-border case in which one country trains the nurses and engineers another country employs. Then give me a table of the residual disagreements, each row phrased the way its own side would put it and paired with the phrasing the other side insists on. Then, for each disagreement, the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them — together with a concrete case, comparison, or piece of evidence both would accept in advance as bearing on that crux, and each side's prediction of what it will show, written down before the fact.

Two things I want handled explicitly rather than left between the lines. First, a lot of their argument is about what a 'debt' is: she uses the word for any obligation that follows the person and reshapes their options, he reserves it for obligations that cannot be discharged, carry default risk, and are underwritten by a balance sheet. Say plainly whether part of this dispute is verbal, and which part isn't. Second, both of them have started flirting with a hybrid neither of them likes: entry free at the point of use, combined with a time-limited obligation on graduates — a fixed percentage of income above a threshold for a fixed term, after which the balance disappears — where the write-off is defended not as a gift to the graduate but as already recovered through the future progressive income tax on the premium. Give that hybrid its own section, including the variant in which the obligation is levied on the graduating cohort rather than the individual, so that a cohort entering the labour market during a recession does not carry a heavier individual burden: I suspect his 'insurance, not debt' claim is only literally true under the cohort version, and I want to know whether the version he actually defends survives that. For each version, say what each of them would argue for and against, whether it collapses back into one of their pure positions under pressure, and roughly where the parameters (threshold, percentage, term) would have to sit before each of them stopped objecting. Keep the whole thing in a register both could actually sign — no verdict on who is right, no citations or references, no balancing language of the 'both views have merit' sort — and end with a single line beginning 'Crux:' naming the one disagreement both parties would agree the memo turns on.
