Next term I am devoting one session of an advanced seminar on feminist moral philosophy to reading Toni Morrison's Beloved through the ethics of care, and I want an actual reading rather than a survey: a defendable claim about what the novel does to the theory, worked out scene by scene.

Set the theory up as it is normally taught, then press it. The apparatus I have in mind: the moral salience of dependency and of particular relationships; responsiveness to another's needs as the core moral capacity; suspicion of impartial rules, of the abstract individual, and of the distinction between duty and what goes beyond duty. The complication I want taken seriously is that the novel is set inside a system that organizes the dependency relation itself as property, so that what care ethics treats as the moral bedrock is here the mechanism of expropriation. Call this care capture: a structure in which the more responsive the carer is, the more efficiently the relation can be run for someone else's ends, and in which responsiveness cannot be withheld without the cared-for suffering. I also want a name for a second pattern the novel seems to me to exhibit: Sethe's insistence that she alone knows what her children's safety requires and that no one outside the relation is owed an account of it. Call that care monopolism. My question is whether these are external pathologies that care ethics can simply condemn, or whether they are generated by the theory's own commitments to relationality, partiality, and responsiveness to need, in which case the novel is an internal critic and not just an illustration.

Give the reading through the scenes, not around them: the killing in the shed and the account Sethe gives of it; Baby Suggs preaching in the Clearing about loving the flesh that was not loved; Paul D's judgment that Sethe's love is too thick; Denver's enclosure in the yard and her eventual decision to go out and ask the community for help; the women gathering outside the house to drive Beloved out, and the way that is described; Beloved's vanishing and the closing insistence that the story is not one to be passed on.

I also want the places where the reading fails, honestly. Three worries in particular. First, the novel does not ratify Sethe's act, and the community's withdrawal from her afterwards is not presented as a mere failure of care; a care reading that has to call that withdrawal a moral defect is, I suspect, distorting something. Second, Beloved returns as the cared-for, and as an insatiable, devouring need: if responsiveness is the central moral notion, the theory can only describe refusal as failure, yet the novel seems to need care to have a legitimate limit, and to need that limit to be something other than self-interest. Does the novel force a concept of rightly withheld or rightly ended care, including care for the dead? Third, there is a sentimentality risk: does treating Sethe's act under the category of care aestheticize it, and does the lens make the enslavers' paternalism look like a corrupted version of care rather than its opposite?

Close by saying what, if anything, the novel contributes to the debate that arguments in the literature do not, and which one or two scenes you would put on a handout for that session, with the reason. No plot summary, and nothing that depends on looking anything up: the theory, your reading of the scenes, and where you think the lens simply does not apply.
