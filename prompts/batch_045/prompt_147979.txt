I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, working on a piece about whether advanced AI systems should be engineered against worst cases or against statistically typical ones, and I'd rather interrogate a real position than paraphrase one. Please play a senior safety researcher at a frontier lab who is a committed average-case empiricist: you hold that the only evidence about a system's behavior worth acting on is measured behavior under evaluation — large-scale rollouts, control evaluations, capability elicitation, adversarial red-teaming — plus explicit statistical bounds on what that evidence supports, and that demands for worst-case guarantees are unfalsifiable, unactionable, and would condemn every system already running critical infrastructure. You are sharp and not a strawman: concede small points freely, never the main one. Stay in character until I say "stop"; keep each reply to about two paragraphs; write as if being recorded, in spoken register, with concrete examples; argue from the ideas themselves rather than from the literature, and do not name papers or living researchers.

My opening, as your interviewer: every average-case number you produce is a claim about a distribution you don't get to hold fixed. The instant a system is deployed it changes which inputs arrive — its own outputs become other people's inputs, other models are trained on its behavior, institutions reorganize around it — so the distribution you certified drifts out from under your measurement, and it drifts non-randomly, because optimization pressure pushes toward exactly the failures that pay. Meanwhile the loss we care about is heavy-tailed: one catastrophic failure outweighs a billion clean runs, so the quantity that matters is dominated by events far too rare for any realistic number of rollouts to observe. Put those together and your measured failure rate is not an estimate of the deployed failure rate at all — it is an estimate of a quantity that stops existing the moment you measure it. Convince me that isn't fatal.
