Ok, Zeno question. Suppose Achilles is under a curse: he can only move by performing a deliberate act of initiating that movement, and every act of initiation takes some fixed minimum time ε > 0, however tiny the movement it initiates. To cross the stadium he has to cross half of it, and to cross half he has to cross a quarter, and so on; each sub-crossing is itself a movement, so it needs its own act of initiation. So there are infinitely many acts of initiation to get through before he's crossed anything, each one costing ε, and he never gets going.

My credence that this is a genuinely different argument from the standard dichotomy — not just the old one in a costume — is about 0.6, because the usual reply (the halving intervals sum to a finite time) doesn't obviously help: what blows up here is the sum over acts, not over distances or times.

What's yours? Give me a number and decompose it: (a) how likely it is that the standard dichotomy was ever really about the convergence of the interval-series, rather than about there being no first sub-task in a densely ordered sequence; (b) whether my setup cheats by helping itself to a way of counting tasks — i.e. whether you can just decline to treat the sub-crossings as separate acts; (c) whether a fixed positive minimum cost per act is even coherent when the acts are densely ordered; (d) whatever else you think belongs. A number plus one line of justification each, then how they combine, then which term you're least confident in and what would move it most. Also say what would count as settling this, since it's not obvious any evidence bears on it.
