I work as a backend / developer-infrastructure engineer, with no philosophy background beyond a small reading group at work, and I write an occasional newsletter for engineers about ideas from outside software that turn out to be about systems anyway. The next piece is on how much rule-following a society should demand — the tight-versus-loose cultures topic — and before I commit to writing it I'd like comments on my notes. They are notes rather than prose: the thesis, the distinctions I'm inventing because I don't know the proper vocabulary for them, and the order I plan to argue in.

Working title: "How strict should the linter be?" The thesis is that the right amount of rule-following isn't a point on a permissive-to-strict line, it's a function of how legible people's local circumstances are to one another and to whoever enforces the rule: rules are what you buy when you cannot afford to understand each other, and discretion is cheap only where situations are mutually readable. Moves, in order: (1) frame the ordinary version of the debate — too much rule-following stifles, too little produces chaos, pick a point — and reject it as treating tightness as a taste parameter rather than as a choice of information technology; (2) introduce enforcement latency, by which I mean the expected time and certainty between a deviation and anyone's response, and claim that what makes norms bite is low latency rather than severity, so that low-latency societies can sustain norms that are individually costly while high-latency societies can only sustain norms people would follow anyway, from preference or from repeated-run reciprocity; (3) introduce mutual legibility, the degree to which a stranger or official can cheaply read a person's actual situation, and claim it is the variable that determines whether uniform rules beat case-by-case judgment, with the corollary that a society isn't tight or loose but has a norm surface area — the set of domains recruited into enforcement at all — and that most public fights apparently about strictness are really fights about surface area, which is why each side can plausibly accuse the other of wanting chaos or tyranny; (4) the part I think is most mine and least safe, an asymmetry claim: tightness pays in visible, dated benefits (order, predictability, fewer visible failures) and charges in counterfactual costs (experiments not run, local adaptations not made), while looseness does the reverse, so any public that updates on observed outcomes drifts tighter over time regardless of the expected value — a ratchet — and looseness becomes a public good that can only be defended by argument, never by its results, which is a structural disadvantage rather than an accident, compounded by exit, since the people most comfortable with ambiguity are the most likely to leave a tightening society and thereby shrink its visible constituency for looseness; (5) the engineering analogy, stated and then deliberately broken: strictness resembles a type system plus CI gates, looseness resembles dynamic typing plus review, both trade local friction for global predictability, and both show the same ratchet, because a lint rule is added after every incident and almost never removed, since the cost of removal is invisible while the cost of the incident was not — but then concede that a compiler is perfectly observant, perfectly consistent, and has neither discretion nor politics, whereas social enforcers have all four, so the ratchet also amplifies the enforcer's blind spots, and that code has roughly one objective while a society does not; (6) the worry I can't resolve, that "should" has no clear subject — a planner, a member demanding something of neighbours, a minority deciding whether to tolerate a majority's looseness — and that my thesis quietly flatters whoever imagines themselves as the planner, which makes me suspicious of it. I want to end on a modest institutional upshot, that rules should carry sunset clauses so they expire unless re-justified, as a way to buy looseness back against the ratchet, while admitting that a sunset clause is itself a rule and the proposal may therefore eat itself.

What I'd like: tell me which of those six moves is actually load-bearing and which is decoration, and which single one you'd cut if the piece has to land at about 1,200 words; where the engineering analogy is smuggling in assumptions rather than illustrating; where I am asserting something empirical that I, writing from an armchair with no data, have no business asserting (flag these even if you think they're probably true); the strongest objection you can make to the ratchet argument in (4), especially one that doesn't just say the asymmetry is exaggerated; and whether enforcement latency and mutual legibility are really two variables or one, since I half suspect latency is just legibility applied over time, in which case I should collapse them. Please don't spend effort on citations, references, or reading lists — I'm not writing an academic piece and I don't expect them; reason from the ideas themselves. Mark each comment as something you're confident about versus something you regard as contestable or as a guess, and say plainly if you think a given claim is false or confused rather than merely unsupported. Finally, if the framing itself is broken — if "how much rule-following a society should demand" isn't a well-posed question, or my thesis is one of those things that sounds like a theory but has no content — say so directly instead of helping me polish it; I would much rather hear that now than after I've written 1,200 words.
