Hey — I run a small reading group on decision theory for AI people, and I'm putting together a handout that sorts out the responses to the "malign universal prior" argument: the idea that an agent doing Solomonoff-style reasoning over computable worlds will find its expected utility dominated by hypotheses containing consequentialists — simulators, jailers, muggers — who reward or punish it depending on what it decides, so that basically any decision rule ends up hijacked.

I want a decision tree, not a bibliography or a reading list: 5–7 branch questions, each phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. Build the logical space first (which combinations of answers are even coherent?), then place the actual positions into the leaves, and mark any empty combination with a line on why — incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The angle I care about is collateral damage. Every leaf has to say what it does with two cases: (a) the plain Pascal's mugging — a stranger claims he'll torture some absurdly large number of people unless you hand him five bucks, with zero evidence; and (b) an ordinary low-probability-high-stakes case, like buying earthquake insurance or funding a cheap hedge against some tail risk. Half the fixes people propose for (a) quietly kill (b) as well, and I want the tree to expose that rather than letting each leaf look clean in isolation.

Leaves I want represented: straight expected-utility maximization with unbounded utility (i.e. pay the mugger, be nice to the simulators); a cap on utility; a worst-case or ambiguity-aversion rule where the mugger's claim gets no probability mass you have to take an expectation over; a leverage-penalty style move where the prior shrinks as the claimed stakes grow; and a policy-level or updateless answer that separates being blackmailed from being traded with. Add whatever else fits, and flag any pair of leaves your questions can't actually separate — especially if the cap and the leverage penalty turn out to give identical advice on both cases.

One more thing I've been kicking around, which I want you to place wherever it belongs: discount a hypothesis by how much evidence you could ever in principle gather about it — a claim about an unwritably huge number of victims is indistinguishable from a bluff no matter what you observe — so maybe the real cut is verifiability rather than stake size. Or tell me if that's just the leverage penalty in a halloween costume.
