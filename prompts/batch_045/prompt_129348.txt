Context. I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, writing a short symposium piece on whether the multiple drafts model (MDM) can state its own positive claims without reinstating, in distributed form, exactly what it rejects about the Cartesian theatre. My supervisor says the argument below is "one equivocation and a lot of bravado", which I suspect is right, but I cannot locate the equivocation, and I do not want to be told where it is by way of a hint. Two cases I lean on, both stipulative.

The late-speciation case. A homonym such as "bank" is flashed and masked at t0; a disambiguating context word arrives at t0+180 ms. At t1 the subject reports having seen the financial sense all along, with no experience of a switch. Neural measures at t0 show the word form with both senses partially active; sense-specific content appears only after the context arrives. So the question is not merely when a content became conscious, but which content it was: the identity of the experience, not just the memory of it, seems to be fixed downstream.

The divergent-probe case. Same set-up, engineered so that the downstream measures come apart: the river sense dominates priming and action-guidance (it has wider influence on subsequent processing, more of the fame-in-the-brain condition), while verbal report and a forced-choice probe at t0+400 ms favour the financial sense. Now which content is conscious depends on which downstream measure is treated as authoritative.

Call a content probe-invariant when all downstream measures agree on it and probe-relative when they conflict. The ordinary run of perception, I take it, is probe-invariant; my two cases are not.

The argument.

(1) The regressargument against the theatre: if being conscious required a content's arrival at a place where it is displayed to an inner observer, then what happens at that place takes time and would itself need to be observed, and so on; so there is no such place, and no central experiencer who confers a durable stamp of approval on a draft.

(2) The Orwellian/Stalinesque argument: take two hypotheses that agree on every neural event and every behavioural disposition and differ only over whether a mistaken content entered the causal pathways before or after the finish line. They are empirically indistinguishable; and the conclusion drawn is not that we cannot know which happened, but that there is no fact of the matter, because the finish line they disagree about is a fiction.

(3) Why there is no fact of the matter: a content's status as conscious is not settled by arrival anywhere. It is settled by its later fate — its uptake into memory, its influence on subsequent action and report, its becoming famous in the brain by leaving consequences behind.

(4) Generality: nothing in (3) confines the dependence to laboratory cases. In the late-speciation case what is downstream-fixed is the identity of the content, not merely the timing of its arrival. And every content is in that position in principle: any content can be reconstrued, overwritten, or re-identified by processing that comes later.

(5) So for any candidate content c and time t, whether c was consciously experienced at t is fixed, if at all, only retroactively — by later events whose own status is in turn fixed only by still later events.

(6) Either that sequence of editorial events terminates in some event whose deliverances are not themselves revisable — in which case we have a finish line again, a place where the order of arrival equals the order of presentation, i.e. the theatre with the audience removed but the screen kept — or it does not terminate, in which case no content is ever determinately conscious, and MDM is eliminativism about consciousness, which Dennett explicitly denies; he takes himself to be explaining consciousness, not explaining it away.

(7) There is also a reflexive cost. MDM's own evidential base is verbal report, treated heterophenomenologically as data rather than as incorrigible testimony. But if the identity of a report-content is itself fixed only by later processing and no report-content is privileged, then the data are as indeterminate as the states they were meant to be evidence about, and the theory saws off the branch it is standing on.

(8) The obvious third option is that I have over-generalised: perhaps there is a fact of the matter whenever the probes converge and none when they diverge, so that indeterminacy is local to probe-relative cases like mine. But then the no-fact-of-the-matter claim does no global work, the Orwellian/Stalinesque argument reduces to the observation that in artificial cases the probes conflict, and the anti-theatre case has to be carried by something else — and I cannot see what that something else is.

(9) Therefore MDM either proves too much (global indeterminacy, hence eliminativism, plus the reflexive problem in (7)) or too little (local indeterminacy, which leaves a determinate, de-centred but perfectly real finish line intact and is arguably consistent with a theatre that has been relocated rather than demolished).

What I most want to keep, in descending order: that there is no inner observer and no central display; that MDM is not eliminativism and consciousness is not being explained away; that verbal reports are genuine data and not mere noise. I would give up the global no-fact-of-the-matter claim before I gave up any of these.
