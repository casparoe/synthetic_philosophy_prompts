I'm taking over the decision-theory slot in a master's module on evidence and policy next term, and I'd rather build the whole ninety-minute session around one result than lecture about it. The group is about twenty: a mix of philosophy and public-health students, perhaps half of whom have done a little probability and none of whom have done any formal decision theory. The result is the one usually called Good's theorem — roughly, that for an agent who updates by conditionalisation and then takes whichever act maximises expected utility under their own prior, being handed information for free cannot leave them worse off in expectation, and a test that costs something is worth buying exactly when it raises expected utility by more than its price. I don't want them to hear that name, or the phrase value of information, until afterwards. What I want them to run into first, and only later be able to name, is that what makes a test valuable is not its accuracy but whether it could change what they would do; that value is denominated in the currency of the decision, so a very accurate test can be worth nothing and a crude one worth a great deal; and that the guarantee rests on a premise — it holds because the agent re-optimises once the result is in, and it lapses when they cannot or will not.

So: design me a game for that room and that slot. It needs a concrete binary decision with two states of the world and a payoff table the students can read in a minute and take seriously — something in their idiom, approving a screening programme or stockpiling a vaccine or closing a beach, not a urn of coloured balls — and a market in which teams spend points from an endowment on tests of differing accuracy and price before committing to an act, with no real money and only a trivial prize at the end. Among the items on sale I want at least one test that is free and quite accurate but worth exactly zero because the decision is the same on either result, one that is expensive and coarse but worth a great deal, one that is dominated by another test on offer, and one cheap screening test whose entire content is a noisy indication of whether one of the expensive tests would come out positive, so that the question of whether the value of the pair is the sum of the values arises on its own. Please give me the actual numbers — the prior, the payoffs, and the expected values with and without each test — worked out, so I can check the design really does have the properties it is supposed to have, together with a minute-by-minute timeline, the materials, and any roles. Then a second phase in which the rules change so that the premise fails: either the scoring switches to something like minimax regret, or teams are made to commit to a decision rule before seeing the result. The same test that was worth buying should stop being worth buying, and nobody should have to be told why in advance.

Finally, the debrief: map particular moments in the session onto the parts of the result they correspond to, including where the arithmetic and where the behavioural surprise do different work. Tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it — my guesses are that they bid for accuracy rather than value, that they treat free as automatically worth taking, that they under-update on a noisy result, that one team corners the market in a test, and that anyone who has taken an evidence-based medicine course tries to import a hypothesis-testing frame that doesn't belong here — and how to handle each. And be frank about which features of the result the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply by hand: I suspect the main one is that the guarantee is an expectation taken over a single agent's own prior, whereas my students will arrive with twenty different priors and will have seen each other's results, and I'd like to know what else is lost in translation from the theorem to the room.
