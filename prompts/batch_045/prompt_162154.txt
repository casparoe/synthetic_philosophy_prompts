I'm a journalist working on a piece about persuasion that goes *through* someone's own reasoning instead of around it — Socratic-style sales coaching, those ask-don't-tell management fads, an AI companion that gets you somewhere by getting you to say it first. I've been banging my head agains this for a week and I need a thought experiment built to spec, and I need you not to cheat.

Here's the split I want the case to force. I'm defining the two views myself, call them whatever you want:

- The *channel* view: an influence is manipulation iff it doesn't run through the target's capacity to weigh reasons. If the person really does reason — however badly — from considerations they take up to a conclusion, it's persuasion, not manipulation, end of story.
- The *exploitation* view: an influence is manipulation iff it works by exploiting a known fragility in *how* the target reasons, rather than by the strength of the considerations themselves. Whether reasons were "engaged" is beside the point; what matters is that the mechanism is a defect in the machinery.

The whole reason I'm asking: in nearly every case I can think of, these two agree. Anchoring, framing, defaults, ambient cues — the exploit works below reason, so both say manipulation. Plain argument with some rhetorical gloss — no real exploit, both say persuasion. So build me ONE case where they come apart in the direction that's hard to construct: the channel view has to say "legitimate persuasion," the exploitation view has to say "manipulation," and the exploitation verdict can't secretly reduce to "the reasoning was bypassed after all." The exploit has to do its work *through* the target's reasoning, not instead of it.

Constraints:

- Ordinary life — a workplace, a clinic, a family. No sci-fi, no neurotech, no hypnosis, no cults.
- No lies and no withheld facts. Every factual claim the influencer makes is true, and the target has access to everything they'd need.
- Nothing hidden: the target knows they're being influenced, knows by whom, and knows what the influencer stands to gain.
- The outcome is mixed — mildly good for the target, clearly good for the influencer — so the case can't lean on the target being harmed.
- The target is a competent adult who, told about it in the abstract, would cheerfully agree that people over-weight arguments they generated themselves; they just don't apply that to themselves in the moment. If you've got a better documented quirk, use it and say why.
- One paragraph for the scenario itself. Then do the work.

After the scenario: what each view says and exactly why; name the one structural feature doing the work; and show it's doing the work by deleting it — if the influencer just made the argument straight out, or skipped the target's reasoning entirely, the two views should collapse back into agreement. Then give me the strongest thing a defender of the channel view can say to wriggle out, and whether they get out.

Cheating would be: stipulating the verdicts, sneaking in a lie or an omission, making the quirk something clinical, or making the influencer's move something the target couldn't in principle notice.

Last two things. Tell me which of the two views you think is actually closer to right, but flag clearly what's settled among people who work on this versus what's genuinely contested — I don't want to run a line in the piece presenting one side as the consensus when it isn't. Is "manipulation" even a moralized concept? Does transparency defeat it? Do these two views really come apart at all? Say which of those are open questions and which have rough agreement. And no citations, bibliographies, or "see so-and-so 2014" — I can't check anything from here and I won't print what I can't verify, so argue from the cases themselves.
