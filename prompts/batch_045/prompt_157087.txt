hey — I'm on a tiny committee at a land-grant university that's writing about three pages of guidance for labs on campus that fly autonomous equipment, and I need help pressure-testing an analogy that keeps ending every discussion.

The case that started it: an ag-engineering lab runs maybe forty small drones for crop scouting and spot-spraying, all running one shared learned policy that gets retrained weekly on fleet telemetry. After one retrain the fleet picked up a habit — shaving corners near the fenceline to save battery — and over roughly six weeks the cumulative effect was that a neighbor's goat herd got buzzed over and over, and one animal got hurt badly enough that the neighbor is, understandably, furious. No single flight decision in the log looks unreasonable. Nobody intended it, nobody predicted it, the behavior isn't in any spec, and the operators authorize flights in batches of hundreds, so the human in the loop is doing something pretty thin.

Whenever I bring up the responsibility-gap stuff, one member says none of this is new: it's just the many-hands problem from organizational ethics, and we already have a fix for that one — assign roles, hold the manager, build a compliance structure, move on. Another member says the drone is like a dog: you don't blame the dog, you hold the owner, it's basically strict liability, stop hand-wringing.

So: audit both analogies. I want real correspondence tables. In the organization picture, what plays the role of the employee, the middle manager, the corporate culture, the compliance officer, the board — and what are the weekly retrain, the telemetry, the kill switch. Same for the dog picture: who's the owner, what's the leash law, what corresponds to the dog being provoked. Then where each breaks against a case shaped like mine. I think three features are doing the damage: the harm only exists in aggregate (no token event is the harm), the thing that acted at time t isn't quite the thing anybody tested at t-1, and the causal chain runs through a training process nobody can narrate after the fact. Tell me which of those actually matter for the conclusion each analogy is being used to reach and which are just decoration.

Two more asks. First: does either analogy hold far enough to license the conclusion "no gap here, just assign it up the chain," or is the conclusion doing all the work and the analogy is scenery? I'd especially like blame separated from compensation separated from answerability — my suspicion is that the organization analogy works fine for paying for the goat, badly for blaming anyone, and that the people saying "no gap" are sliding between those without noticing. Second: my own half-baked alternative is that a fleet like this is less like an organization and more like a river — nobody is responsible for the flood, but we still build levees and still tax for them, and who is to blame for the flood is just an ill-formed question. Is that better, worse, or a different evasion wearing nicer clothes? If neither, what would be the better comparison.

I have to turn whatever comes out of this into actual guidance language, so be concrete about what the guidance should say, and tell me how confident you are in each call — I definately don't want evenly-weighted hedging on all of it. And if my framing is the confused one — if there really is no gap here and I'm just relabeling ordinary negligence, or if the colleague who says nothing is new is simply right — say that plainly instead of being polite about it.
