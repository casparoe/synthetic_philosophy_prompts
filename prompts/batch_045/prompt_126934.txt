I am giving a paper next week in a joint philosophy–economics workshop, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defence of the view.

The paper: Parfit's hitchhiker, I argue, gives no support to the claim that rationality can require a disposition to act irrationally. The appearance that it does depends on how finely the driver's prediction is assumed to be tuned to the agent's occurrent practical stance in the desert. I distinguish snapshot-visible dispositions, which a predictor could read off a single momentary snapshot of what the agent is presently set on doing, from profile-visible dispositions, which are detectable only from the pattern in which the agent resolves a range of decision problems over time. Roughly: the rational-irrationality reading is tempting only for snapshot-visible dispositions; but a driver who is merely a very good judge of character, as in the original case, plausibly tracks profile-visible ones; and once the case is varied so that the agent may install a commitment device before any prediction is made, the best-performing disposition is one a fully rational agent can already have. So the lesson is not about irrationality at all. It is that the payoff of a disposition tracks an empirical fact about what predictors can detect, and that normative conclusions about rationality should not be read off that fact.

The audience: about thirty people, mostly behavioural and experimental economists who work on social preferences, commitment devices and mechanism design, plus three decision theorists — a committed causal decision theorist, someone who works on deliberation and ratification, and a formal epistemologist — and two philosophers of action who treat intentions as elements of plans. Almost nobody there uses rationality as a normative notion; several will hear any appeal to it as at best a façon de parler for expected-payoff maximization, and at least one will think the whole problem is a screening problem with a known solution.

What I want: the eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased as that kind of person would phrase it in their own idiom; a two-minute reply for each; a note on where each reply is soft; what I should concede in the first two minutes rather than be made to concede in the twentieth; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the step in my argument I should be ready to defend rather than hope goes unnoticed.
