I am marking a mid-term for a final-year applied ethics course and want an independent read on one answer before finalising the marks. The question (which I set) and one student's response are reproduced below.

**Question 4 (25 marks).** Consider a visitor who is in a city for a single afternoon, will never return, and whose conduct no one there will be able to identify as an outsider's. In this city: restaurant bills include service, and leaving a tip is widely taken to imply that the server depends on the customer's charity; people form a queue at bus stops and board in order of arrival; and pedestrians cross where and when they please, with drivers forming their expectations accordingly.

In your answer:

(a) State the account of "convention" you are working with. (The course used the standard one: a pattern of behaviour that is arbitrary among the available alternatives, and is sustained because each person prefers to conform conditionally on others' conforming.)

(b) Defend either (i) the claim that the existence of these conventions gives the visitor a moral reason to conform that she would not otherwise have had, or (ii) the denial of that claim.

(c) Say whether your answer is the same for all three practices, and explain why or why not.

(d) Reply to the objection that, since no single person's conforming makes a difference to whether a convention persists, the visitor cannot owe conformity to anyone.

**Student's answer**

I defend (ii).

(a) A convention is a self-reproducing pattern of behaviour: arbitrary among the alternatives once we ask which rule would be best, but stable once it is in place, because each person does best by conforming given that the others conform, and that is what keeps it going. Two things follow. First, conventions are facts about expectations, and facts about expectations are not by themselves normative: that a great many people expect me to tip, to queue or to wait at the light is not yet a reason for me to do any of those things. Second, the arbitrariness of the rule's content is not what stops it from binding me. Given that others conform, conforming is usually what I have most reason to do; the fact that a different rule would have been equally workable does not show that I may ignore this one. So I rest nothing on arbitrariness.

(b) If the convention as such gives no reason, the reason has to come from somewhere else, and the only candidate is the effect of my action on people's interests. Frustrated expectations are not themselves a harm; they matter only where someone is made worse off. My test is therefore: does non-conformity set back anyone's interests?

(c) The test gives different results across the three cases, but not because they are different kinds of convention — only because the interest-effects differ.

*Tipping.* Leaving money would give the server more than she is owed, and would not set back her interests. But it would offend her, and it would put pressure on a wage arrangement this city has chosen. That is a real setback, so the visitor should not tip — though the reason is the offence and the pressure, not the convention.

*Queueing.* Here the convention does bind, again not as such: the people already waiting have given up time and taken up places, and if I push in they lose a place they would otherwise have had. Because identifiable people are made worse off, the visitor must queue. Where the bus is half empty and no one loses a place, nothing is at stake and the convention is inert. That is a consequence of the test, not a difficulty for it.

*Jaywalking.* Crossing mid-block sets back no one's interests, since drivers expect it; waiting at the lights sets back no one's interests either, since it merely delays the visitor herself. This convention is morally inert and the visitor may do either.

(d) I accept the objection, and it supports my conclusion. A convention persists because most people conform, and one visitor's act cannot change that; she cannot owe conformity to a practice her own conduct cannot affect. What she owes is not conformity but regard for interests. This also explains why her being an outsider, and never returning, is beside the point: it is not membership but effects on interests that do the work.
