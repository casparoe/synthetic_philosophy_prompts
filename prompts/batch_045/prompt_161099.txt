I have followed the argument about innateness and the poverty of the stimulus since the early rationalist-blog days, and I still cannot tell how much of it is an empirical dispute, how much is a dispute about what the word innate means, and how much is a dispute about which hypothesis space you are allowed to assume. What I want is the document a real adversarial collaboration would produce.

Please draft the joint statement that a committed nativist about language — the camp holding that a child's linguistic input underdetermines the grammar, so that a substantial, species-specific part of syntax is built in — and a committed domain-general learner — the camp holding that rich input plus general-purpose statistical, semantic, and pragmatic inference is enough — could both sign after arguing in good faith for a year.

Structure it as follows.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, phrased so that neither side is signing away anything it currently holds. I would like at least the following made explicit: the logical form of the argument itself (it is a gap argument — children end up knowing X, the evidence available does not determine X, so something else supplies X — and both sides should concede that its force depends entirely on how wide the space of candidate grammars and candidate inference strategies is taken to be); the point that any system learning from finite data needs inductive bias, so that the disagreement cannot be about whether learning is constrained but only about whether the constraints are specifically linguistic; what both accept about the input, including that it is far richer than the slogans imply and that absence from a sufficiently large sample can itself carry information; and what both accept about the much-cited cases on either side, including at least one famous deprivation case both camps now agree is too confounded to carry weight.

2. A disagreements table. Each row: the disagreement restated neutrally; then the nativist's own formulation; then the empiricist's own formulation, with each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording as fair. I expect rows roughly on: whether the argument must be run construction by construction, with a formal learnability result for each, or may be run globally; which notion of innateness is doing the work — genomic encoding, canalized development, invariance across environments, or simply not being learnable from the available evidence; whether domain-general biases can do the job; and how much weight may rest on the speed, cross-linguistic uniformity, and robustness of acquisition under degraded input.

3. A note on which part of the dispute, if any, is verbal. I suspect at least one row is really a disagreement about the meaning of innate or about what counts as being in the input, and I want you to identify it and to state what substantive question survives once the words are disentangled.

4. Cruxes, with predictions written down in advance. For each genuine crux, give the test, case, or argument both sides agree would bear on it, and what each side predicts. Two cruxes I especially want handled:

   - Acquisition under degraded channels: children born blind who nevertheless acquire the syntax of the language they hear; children who produce almost no speech for motor reasons but turn out to have acquired the language around them; and a sign language arising among deaf children brought together in a school setting with no shared language. Which of these does either side actually think discriminates between them, and how?
   - The large language model case. Systems with a generic architecture and a next-token-prediction objective, trained on internet-scale text — orders of magnitude more language than the few million words a year a child hears — now score well on many measures of grammatical competence. Both camps should agree this bears on the crux, and both should disagree about how: about whether the architecture and training objective are themselves playing the role of the built-in structure, about whether the training signal supplies something a child never gets, and about what our competence measures actually measure. Record each side's prediction, and then design the test that would separate them — my guess is something like training on a child-sized, child-directed, speech-only corpus with an architecture not hand-engineered for language — and say what each side predicts and which outcome would move whom.

5. For each camp, the finding or argument it would count as evidence against its own position, stated in its own voice rather than in its opponent's.

If you conclude that some part of the disagreement cannot be settled by any test either side would accept, say so and explain why. Do not adjudicate: I am not asking who is right, only for the text both parties could put their names to. Please write in formal American English, and do not include citations, references, or reading lists — I want the argument, not the bibliography.
