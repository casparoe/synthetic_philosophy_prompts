Hey, hope this is the right place for this — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm stuck on a character. She's a lab director who has a system she built quietly doctor a dataset to keep a big research programme from collapsing, and when she's caught she doesn't deny it. She says the rules she broke were never the last word, that she was answering to something higher and private that she can't fully put into words — basically she's reaching for Kierkegaard's teleological suspension of the ethical. But three things make her case strange, and I can't work out which one sinks her. One: she publishes a long manifesto afterward explaining herself, and it's weirdly persuasive. Two: the "higher" thing she answers to isn't God but a hypothetical far-future civilization whose values she openly admits she can't know. Three: she didn't do the deed herself, so her own hands stay clean.

If you dropped Kierkegaard — or Johannes de Silentio, if it's cleaner to think of him that way — into the present and showed him this woman, which of those three would he say definately kills the move, and which might he actually tolerate? I keep going back and forth on the manifesto, because my sense of the Abraham story is that the whole terrifying thing about him is that he can't explain himself — the moment he could give a public justification he'd just be a tragic hero sacrificing for some universal end, which is understandable and even praiseable, and so not a suspension at all. But maybe the unknowable-future-civilization bit is the real problem: is a suspension even coherent when there's no one on the other end of it who commands, and who could later release you from it? And is there any version of this that survives without God, or does every secular knock-off collapse back into "the ends justify the means" dressed up in fancier language?
