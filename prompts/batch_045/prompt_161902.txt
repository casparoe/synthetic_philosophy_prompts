I'm on the five-person board of our union's hardship fund — members chip in 1% of wages, we vote on claims, and the only written guidance is one line in the bylaws about helping members with misfortunes they didn't bring on themselves. No rubric, just precedent, and we argue it out in the meeting. Our record so far:

- Marta, born with a congenital condition, $40k surgery. Paid in full. Nobody even argued.
- Dev, pack-a-day smoker since he was 18, lung cancer. Denied.
- Priya, never smoked, same cancer, radon in co-op housing the co-op never fixed. Paid in full.
- Ines, celiac, asked for the difference between normal and gluten-free groceries. Paid the difference, not the whole bill.
- Tomas, says he can't be happy without single malt and opera tickets. Denied — the minutes call it a taste, not a need.
- Sam, quit a steady job to start a food truck in a city with a well-publicized 60% failure rate, folded in 14 months. Denied.
- Yusuf, took a job at the mill, the only employer within commuting distance, hurt when a cable snapped. Paid in full, one dissent.
- Grace, walking home from a late shift through a dark stretch to save bus fare, hit by a drunk driver. Paid in full — the board's line was that the driver's choice, not hers, is what hurt her.

New claim, and it's a messier one. Marisol, 52. Twenty years ago she chose house painting over two other jobs she'd been offered — better pay, and she liked it. Solvent exposure carries a documented elevated risk of chronic illness and she knew that; but the number she'd have been quoted at the time, and the one on the safety sheet she signed, was an average, computed from a study sample that happened to include nobody with her profile. Two years ago she was diagnosed with a rare genetic enzyme deficiency she's had since birth, undetectable by any test that existed before 2019, not caused by anything she did. It doesn't cause illness on its own; what it does is turn the exposure level that would have given an ordinary painter a mild, reversible condition into irreversible kidney failure. All the exposure that mattered happened before the diagnosis, so quitting then wouldn't have helped — though I garantee somebody on the board will make a lot of the fact that she kept painting for another year after her doctor told her to stop, because retraining at 52 with her mortgage would've cost her about a third of her income and she'd probably have lost the house. She also says two things that fit together awkwardly: that she'd take the job again, and that if she'd known her own odds were ten times the average she wouldn't have.

So: full denial, full payment, or something like paying only the gap between the mild condition she seems to have been gambling on and the kidney failure she actually got? What I really want is the rules we'd be committing to, not just the outcome — give me the two or three principles that fit all eight precedents and come apart here, say which precedent is the closest analog, and tell me what we'd be adopting either way. Also, is there a further case that would actually tell those principles apart? I suspect that's where the real disagreement lives. And if how finely you describe the gamble she accepted turns out to matter — painting-with-elevated-solvent-risk versus a small shot at a mild reversible condition — say so, because I think that's doing more work in my own thinking than I've admitted. I know there's a whole literature on this general shape of thing, chosen versus unchosen and so on, but I don't need the reading list; I need something I can say out loud on Tuesday. Please flag which parts are settled among people who actually work on this and which are live fights — I don't want to walk in quoting a contested view as though it were received wisdom.
