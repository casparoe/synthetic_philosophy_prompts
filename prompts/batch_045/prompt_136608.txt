I am a retired geochemist — thirty-odd years spent measuring how trace elements partition between silicate melts and crystals, which is to say a career in which I was periodically assured that what I was measuring was nothing but electrostatics and quantum mechanics, and in which I never once found that assurance helpful at the bench. In retirement I have attached myself to a philosophy of science reading group run through the continuing-education programme at the local university, and I have let myself be volunteered to lead one of its sessions. The group is fourteen to eighteen adults, mostly retired scientists and engineers with a scattering of other backgrounds, essentially no formal philosophy, and we have ninety minutes in a room with a projector, a whiteboard, and movable tables. The topic I was handed — I suspect because it sounded like safe ground for an ex-scientist — is reduction and emergence in science.

I do not want to lecture at them, and I do not want a session that ends with everyone agreeing that of course everything supervenes on the physics and that the rest is bookkeeping. I want them to run into the difficulty in their own predictions before it is named, and I would rather nobody hears words like supervenience, bridge law, or multiple realization until the debrief. Three things I would like them to feel rather than be told. First: that a higher-level generalization can be the only thing anyone in the room can actually use to predict, even when every participant grants from the outset, without argument, that the lower-level story is complete — the nothing-but move settles nothing and yet is not in conflict with anything, and I want them to feel both halves of that. Second: that the live questions are not about derivability but about invariance and about which counterfactuals a given description licenses — whether the pattern survives swapping the micro-rules for different ones, whether it survives a defect introduced into a single cell, whether it survives doubling the size of the system, and what each description can and cannot say about a run that never happened. Third, and this is the part I most want separated out: there seem to me to be at least two things that get called emergence which are not the same thing — a pattern whose derivation from the micro-rules is merely out of computational reach for creatures like us, and a pattern that is realized by many different micro-rule sets and is therefore robust to changes in those rules. I would like the session to prise those apart, and then to bring them back together in at least one case, because my private suspicion is that they are less independent than the literature I have skimmed assumes.

My own rough skeleton, which I would be glad to have improved on or replaced outright, is a prediction game built around a grid of counters that topple into their neighbours once a cell exceeds some threshold — a sandpile sort of arrangement, which at least these people will enjoy and which I can drive from my own laptop the night before. Participants would be split into tiers with deliberately unequal information: one tier receives the complete micro-rules and the exact initial state, one receives only aggregate time-series recorded from earlier runs, and one receives only the phenomenological generalization previous observers wrote down, whatever the honest version of that turns out to be for the system we use. All three then predict the outcome of a fresh run from a novel initial condition and answer a counterfactual about a run that did not happen. Then, and I think this is where the real work is done, they repeat the exercise under three perturbations in turn: the micro-rules quietly swapped for a different rule set that reproduces the same aggregate pattern across most of its range but comes apart in the tail; a single cell's rule altered; and the grid doubled in linear size. The point is that each tier should be vindicated somewhere and humbled somewhere, and that the pattern of who wins when is itself the finding.

What I need from you is a session I can actually run: concrete parameter values for the simulation and for each rule set (grid size, thresholds, seeding, number of steps per round, and how long a round takes in real time), a materials list assuming no computing beyond my own machine, the information sheet to hand each tier, a scoring scheme that needs no money and makes the asymmetric information bite, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and above all a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto particular features of the debate, saying which moment is supposed to produce which intuition and what the facilitator should ask at each point. Please also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I assume the fully informed tier will try to simulate the thing by hand and run out of clock, that someone will notice the rule swap and spoil it, that the tier given only the aggregate data will overfit a small sample, and that at least one participant will announce that the whole exercise is merely about ignorance. And be frank about what the activity cannot deliver: I take it that ninety minutes of toppling counters cannot produce anything in the neighbourhood of genuine ontological emergence or downward causation, and that the real historical cases involve limiting arguments that a tabletop simulation does not even gesture at. Tell me what I will have to say in my own voice at the end, and how to say it without undoing what they have just experienced. Please do not point me toward papers or reading lists; I have no library access from here and would rather have the activity itself. Please write in American English.
