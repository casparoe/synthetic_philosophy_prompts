Hey, I need a document rather than an essay. Two people in my reading group have been going at each other for months about concepts, and it's become pure repetition. Call them R and C. R is the Wittgensteinian: a concept is held together by criss-crossing similarities rather than by a set of necessary and sufficient conditions, and to have the concept is to be able to go on in a shared practice, not to be able to recite a definition. C is the stubborn classical theorist: any genuine concept has necessary and sufficient conditions, borderline cases show either our ignorance of where the boundary lies or mere vagueness, and our inability to state the conditions is a fact about us, not about the concept.

I want to force them onto a case neither has seen. Here it is, and it's made up, so don't go looking for it:

An anthropologist is studying the fishing town of Halvik, where people apply the word *lomping* to some activities and withhold it from others. Everyone agrees these are lomping: mending a net while perched on an upturned crate waiting for the tide to turn; scraping barnacles off a hull at low water when the boat isn't going anywhere yet; kids hopping from stone to stone across the inlet pool; tapping a spoon against a mug in time with the bus engine outside the depot; laying driftwood out in rows along the sea wall; two men tossing a coil of rope back and forth while the late ferry is still a speck; humming under your breath while kneading dough; sorting coins by year on the tavern table. Everyone agrees these are not: hauling nets onto the boat for the morning run; counting the catch into the ledger; scrubbing a hull with a brush because the owner is coming at noon and you're being paid; a child hopping because her mother told her to get to the door; sorting coins at the bank counter during your shift; a gull pecking at a shell on the rocks. Two cases split the town: a dog chasing its tail in the yard (about half say yes), and a councillor drumming his fingers on the table during a meeting (some say yes, some say that's just fidgeting, some say it is lomping but rude).

Her notebook lists these features: hands involved; repeated rhythmic motion; nothing produced or earned; done while waiting or killing time; done with whatever is to hand; mildly disapproved of by older people; solitary or social; stops the moment the thing you were waiting for arrives. Stipulate that no one of these is present in every agreed-yes case, and that each of them is present in some agreed-no case. Note especially that coin-sorting in the tavern and coin-sorting at the bank involve the same visible movements and get opposite verdicts.

Now two more stipulations. First, unknown to the townsfolk: every agreed-yes activity involves contact with something that has been below the high-tide line earlier that day, and no agreed-no activity does. The dog had been swimming that morning; the council table had not been near the water. Second, two probes: a shopkeeper restocking shelves from crates unloaded off the morning boat — everything he handles has been in the sea that day — is definitely called work, not lomping; and a woman up at the chapel on the hill at dusk, doing exactly the coin-sorting hand business with dry coins brought inland, idle and waiting, would be called lomping by everyone.

So: does the hidden high-tide property settle anything for either of them, given that the townspeople would overrule it in both probes? Does the pattern of agreement and disagreement in Halvik support R's picture, or does it only show that the word is vague, or that the town is tracking something they can't articulate? And does either of them have a story about why the same hand movements count in the tavern and not at the bank?

Write the joint statement they could both put their names to, in this order:

1. Agreed claims — a numbered list, 200–250 words. At least one of them should separate three things that keep getting run together: there being no feature shared by all the cases; there being no sharp boundary; there being nothing that explains why speakers judge as they do. Say which of those the Halvik data actually establishes.
2. Disagreements — a table, about 350 words. One row per point of difference, with R's position stated in wording R would sign and C's in wording C would sign. No strawmen, no hedged mush.
3. Cruxes and predictions — about 400 words. I expect at least three cruxes, and for each one give a concrete case, observation, or argument that both would accept as bearing on it, plus what R predicts about it and what C predicts. If one crux turns out to be empirical rather than philosophical, say so and say what observation would settle it.
4. Verbal or not — about 150 words. Which parts, if any, are really about how to use words rather than about how concepts work, and how would you tell?

Keep it under about 1200 words in total, and don't just rerun the standard board-games example at me — argue it on the Halvik material, then say how far, if at all, the conclusion is supposed to generalise.
