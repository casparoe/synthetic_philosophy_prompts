I write about science and ideas for a living, and right now I'm working on a piece about long-run forecasts that didn't land — peak oil, AI timelines, that sort of thing — and how much credit a forecaster deserves when they say afterwards that they were right but early. Malthus is my test case. I had two sources on a group call last week and they got into it, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Names changed, and I've got the recording but its basically unusable, so this is from memory — paraphrased, but the moves are theirs.

(1) Callum: Malthus wasn't making a date-stamped prediction, he was describing a mechanism — population presses against the means of subsistence and gets beaten back by famine, disease and war, or held back by people marrying late and having fewer children. Read that way, the last two centuries aren't a refutation, they're the mechanism running in slow motion. We avoided the crash by spending a one-off inheritance: fossil fuels, plus industrial nitrogen fixation, which turns gas into the plant-available nitrogen that a huge share of the world's food now depends on, plus mining finite things like phosphate rock, which as far as anyone can tell has no synthetic substitute. Population never stopped pressing on subsistence; we just raised the ceiling, and we raised it with a stock rather than a flow. Stocks run out. That's what early means here — not famine next Tuesday but the reprieve has an expiry date, and Malthus gets credit for the shape of the thing even if his clock was off by two centuries.

(2) Miriam: That's a rescue, not a prediction. If escaping by inventing something counts as confirmation, then nothing could ever disconfirm it — and that's before we notice that the specific thing he expected, in England, in the centuries right after he wrote, just didn't happen: numbers didn't outrun food there and get beaten back by famine. There's a positive reason it didn't happen, too. Malthus had the causal arrow backwards. People aren't only mouths, they're hands and brains, and the pressure itself is part of what calls forth the innovation that relieves it — more people means more people to have the idea, and the scarcity is what makes the idea worth having. A theory that gets the sign of the main feedback loop wrong isn't early, it's wrong.

(3) Callum: On unfalsifiability — every long-run mechanism looks like that, because it's a tendency with a ceteris-paribus clause stapled to it. The way to cash out early is to ask whether the escape is repeatable. If the reprieve runs on a finite stock, the theory has a deadline rather than a loophole, and deadlines are auditable in principle: does a substitute exist, how fast is the stock drawn down, can the flow ever be rebuilt. On your causal arrow, I'll grant endogenous innovation and it still doesn't get you out. The constraint is a fixed flow — arable land, freshwater, phosphorus, the energy you get back per unit of energy spent getting it. More researchers, same fixed thing being researched against: that's faster running in place. And innovation itself looks like it has diminishing returns, more scientists per unit of progress, more money per new drug, which is a Malthusian dynamic in idea space.

(4) Miriam: You just swapped theories. If the binding constraint is freshwater or phosphate or energy return, that's a depletion story — and two billion rich people with the same technology would strain it as much as eight billion poor ones. So Malthus was right is doing no work; what survives is the industrial escape was funded by drawing down a stock, which nobody disputes and isn't his theory. And here's my actual objection to right but early: the asymmetry. Check arrives in year 200, the forecaster was prescient; check arrives never, they were merely deferred. A claim with an unbounded expiry date isn't a forecast. So: a long-run forecast should come with a pre-registered concession clause — say in advance which observations would make you concede the mechanism itself is wrong, not merely postponed. No clause, no credit.

(5) Callum: Concession clauses are right in spirit and useless in practice, because any clause you can write gets wrecked by an outside shock — a pandemic, a war, cheap fusion — that changes what the clause was even about, and if you tighten it enough to be checkable you've made it short-run, which is exactly the horizon where nobody claimed Malthus was making a call. But the more interesting thing: I don't think the mechanism died, I think it moved. In rich countries nobody is short of calories; what's scarce is position. And some of that scarcity is fixed by construction — no amount of growth raises the share of people in the top one percent, or the number of seats at a handful of universities, or the housing in six or seven superstar cities. So you get escalating effort with no rise in the thing being competed for, and the checks come back as anxiety, credential arms races and collapsed fertility. Call it positional Malthusianism: same structure — more effort, same per-head return, competition over a fixed quantity — but the fixed quantity is social instead of material. If that's right he was onto something real, we just built enough calories to stop noticing which quantity was fixed.

(6) Miriam: It's a nice idea and it isn't Malthus. Material checks run through biology — you don't eat, you die, and no institution changes that. Positional scarcity runs through preferences and rules, which makes it a policy choice all the way down: you can create more slots, stop letting one exam sort a life, build the housing. A check you can repeal on a Tuesday afternoon isn't the same object as a check that arrives with a famine. And it inverts the politics — Malthusian scarcity says restrain the numbers, positional scarcity says change how status is handed out, and your framing quietly swaps one for the other while keeping his name on the label. Also, the fertility point cuts against you either way: rich-country fertility went below replacement because of the opportunity cost of children, not because people couldn't feed them. That's the preventive check showing up for reasons that have nothing to do with subsistence. You keep the name, lose the mechanism, then claim the win.
