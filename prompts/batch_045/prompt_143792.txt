I have been trying to get clearer about the explanatory reach of David Lewis's account of convention, and I want to test it against a case that looks, on the surface, like a textbook instance of it: Sweden's *Dagen H*, the switch from left-hand to right-hand road traffic. I'd like an analysis that runs in both directions — the theory illuminating the episode, and the episode telling us something about the theory.

The facts I'm working from, so that nothing here turns on your being able to look them up: more than ninety percent of Swedish cars were left-hand-drive while the country drove on the left; Norway and Finland, its land neighbours, drove on the right, with millions of vehicle crossings a year; in a 1955 referendum eighty-three percent of Swedes voted to keep driving on the left; the Riksdag legislated the change in May 1963 anyway; and on Sunday 3 September 1967 all non-essential traffic was banned from 01:00 to 06:00, the vehicles that were on the road had to come to a halt at 04:50, move over to the right-hand side, and set off again at 05:00, behind a four-year public information campaign and roughly 350,000 replaced road signs. Accidents and insurance claims dropped sharply in the first weeks, then drifted back to something like the pre-change baseline within about two years. The railways and the Stockholm metro simply carried on running on the left.

Here is how I understand the Lewisian machinery — a regularity in behaviour, in a recurrent situation, within a population, where almost everyone conforms, almost everyone expects almost everyone else to conform, and that expectation is itself part of each person's reason for conforming, the whole thing being common knowledge; and where the regularity is arbitrary in the sense that a different coordination equilibrium could equally well have been the one that got selected. Correct me if I've garbled that, but don't give me textual exegesis; I want the apparatus applied.

So, first the mapping, done carefully rather than loosely. Who exactly is the population and what is the recurrent situation — a single driver approaching a single oncoming car, or a whole national traffic system, or something at both levels at once? What are the options and the payoffs, and is this genuinely a pure coordination problem with coincident preferences, or is there a distributional residue that Lewis's definition struggles to accommodate: the owners of left-hand-drive cars against the bus companies with their right-hand-drive fleets, the motorists who had to buy new headlamps, the cities that had to rebuild bus stops and intersections, the tram systems that were simply scrapped and replaced with buses? And if Swedish cars were mostly built for right-hand traffic, then the two equilibria were not equally good — so is the arbitrariness condition violated, or does Lewis's framework already allow for conventions stuck at an inferior equilibrium, with the interesting question being why nobody could get there one driver at a time?

Second, where the theory speaks and where it is silent. It should predict stability-by-expectation and lock-in, and it does predict that no individual gains by deviating alone. But say what you think it cannot say about the transition: Lewis's account is an equilibrium account, and the hard problem here is getting from one equilibrium to another when every unilateral move is catastrophic. I have two speculative suggestions I want you to take seriously enough to tell me whether they are worth anything. The first is that the state's dated declaration functioned less as a command than as a public correlating signal that manufactured the common knowledge Lewis needs — and that it worked only because it was backed by a prior, second-order convention of deferring to the state on traffic rules; on that reading a convention can be bootstrapped by authority in a way that Lewis's talk of precedent and salience does not really cover. Test this against the obvious probe: if the Riksdag had fixed the date but the announcement had reached, say, four drivers in five, with nobody able to tell which ones, what does the theory predict, and is that the right prediction? The second is a distinction I want to propose between conventions sustained by expectations about what others will do and regularities sustained by what I'd call material sediment — road markings, bus doors, headlamp beams, intersection geometry — which make nonconformity physically awkward rather than merely unreciprocated. After 1967 a good deal of the regularity was built into the hardware. Does that mean the post-change regularity was a convention at all in Lewis's sense, or something else that merely looks like one, and does the same worry reach the continuing left-hand traffic of Britain and Japan?

Third, the verdict. Having done the mapping, tell me whether Dagen H is a confirmation of Lewis, an anomaly for him, or a case his theory was never designed to describe. I'm especially interested in whether it should count against the broader evolutionary story about norms — path dependence, basins of attraction, the difficulty of escaping an equilibrium without a shock — that one of the largest convention changes on record was achieved by deliberate, centrally planned, one-morning fiat rather than by anything resembling bottom-up evolution, and whether the 1955 referendum result is best read as evidence about what people wanted, about what they expected others to do, or about neither.

Please argue to a conclusion, say plainly which parts of the episode your theory cannot model, and don't pad the answer with general exposition of Lewis that isn't doing work in the analysis.
