I'm in a small reading group — half of us work in or adjacent to machine learning, the rest in policy and philosophy — and we have stalled out on AI existential risk. One member treats it as the defining problem of the century; another treats the whole discourse as a self-selecting community with an unusually strong taste for improbable-but-enormous stakes. I don't want another round of that. What I want instead is the specification: what would an argument about AI existential risk have to get right in order to count as an adequate case for, or against, taking it seriously? Fix the purpose as follows — the case should be good enough to decide whether a government should impose, and whether I should publicly support, a binding cap on frontier training compute, and good enough to tell a junior researcher whether to redirect a career into technical safety work. Adequacy here means adequacy for a decision, not adequacy as prophecy.

So write the requirements list, each requirement paired with the case or consideration that motivates it, and be willing to add to, correct, or demote the ones I sketch. The ones I keep coming back to: it has to decompose the risk rather than assert it — separating the hazard (what the technology itself can do when it fails), the exposure (how many actors, systems, or deployment contexts put us in the way of that hazard), and the vulnerability (how much of our civilisation's long-run trajectory is genuinely fragile, as opposed to resilient or able to reconstitute) — since these are moved by different interventions and a case that conflates them will recommend the wrong lever. It has to make marginal claims, not absolute ones: probabilities conditional on this policy versus that policy, rather than a headline figure about how the century ends. It has to say which risk it is even talking about — deliberate misuse, loss of control of a single system, or structural and competitive dynamics among many actors none of whom wants the bad outcome — and it must not let the intervention appropriate to one stand in for the others. It has to fix what "existential" is doing: extinction, permanent curtailment of humanity's potential, or unrecoverable civilisational collapse, with the policy conclusion re-checked under each reading. It has to survive having its historical analogies removed while its mechanism-level story is kept, and survive the reverse operation too, because I suspect each of those two halves is doing more work than its holder admits. It has to take a position on the fanaticism problem: whether astronomically large stakes over tiny probabilities are to be multiplied out, bounded, or discounted, and what follows for the argument if they are. And it has to handle two scenarios I want used as test cases: the quiet decade, in which capabilities plateau for reasons unrelated to any safety work, and the fire-inspector's problem, in which a successful intervention leaves no observable evidence that there was ever a fire, so that a high-risk world and a safely-managed world can look identical from inside.

Then tell me which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the methodological prejudices of one camp or another, and flag any subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I care about most, since if the list is internally unsatisfiable, that result is worth more to me than any scorecard. My own suspicion is that the demand for empirical constraint and the demand for a decision-relevant number pull apart: the event is unrepeatable, near misses may be either invisible or fatal, and any surviving observer is selected in a way that makes the absence of past catastrophes hard to read. Close by naming which single requirement excludes the largest share of what people actually say on this topic, and then, in a few lines each and no more, where three live lines of argument fail your list: the it-is-just-another-general-purpose-technology line, the default-misalignment-of-superintelligent-systems line, and the competition-makes-restraint-impossible line. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring is for me to finish.
