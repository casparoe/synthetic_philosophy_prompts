Im putting together a session for a mixed reading group — half philosophy grad students, half ML engineers — on the whole competence-without-comprehension thing, i.e. the Darwin/Turing strange inversion: you get all the outward signs of understanding out of a process that does no understanding at all, and the live question is whether that inversion eats only the *producers* of competence or also its *products*. I want a diagnostic instrument rather than an essay: a decision tree / sorting quiz that takes someone from a handful of concrete cases to a named position on what comprehension is. It should sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they think they like, so no node should just ask "do you think the machine really understands?"

Concretely: eight to ten branch nodes. Every node should be phrased as a question a smart person who has never read the literature could answer on the spot — plain English in the question stems, jargon only in the leaf labels.

Two cuts I especially want the tree to make, because they're where I think people secretly disagree:

1. Whether comprehension is categorical or scalar. If scalar, who or what sets the cut, and is there a fact of the matter about how much a given system has? Does the existence of a smooth gradient from frog-catches-fly up to human-mathematician force the scalar answer, or can a categorical theorist just place the threshold wherever the interesting machinery kicks in?
2. Where the reason-tracking has to live. Does it have to be in the system's current operation *and* accessible to the system itself (so it could in principle cite its reasons and notice when they don't apply)? Or is it enough that the tracking sits in the causal/etiological history that produced the system? Or is it enough that it's available to an outside interpreter who can exploit the pattern?

Cases I want doing the work (use most of them, add better ones if you have them): a system that generalizes arithmetic to numbers it never saw while its internals contain nothing you'd call a representation of number; a giant cached lookup table that mimics competence by brute storage; a system that's genuinely flexible but, asked why, has nothing, and can't tell when it's out of its depth; a system built by an evolution-like search with no comprehending designer anywhere in the causal chain, which nevertheless passes the second-order tests (flags its own uncertainty, produces explanations that track the real reasons); a system whose reasons-tracking exists only in its training history and not in how it now works; a subtractive case where you remove reason-access while holding behavior fixed; the frog; two systems with identical behavior where one runs on structured inference and the other on pattern matching; and the Darwin-versus-Turing asymmetry itself — does it matter that in Darwin's case the *process* is comprehension-free while some of the products do comprehend, whereas with the machine it's the comprehension of the product that's up for grabs. A burden-of-proof node would be useful too: is comprehension an extra posit that needs positive evidence, or the default attribution we retreat from only under pressure?

Leaves that need to be there: an interpretationist / intentional-stance leaf; an inferentialist leaf (grasping = being in the business of giving and asking for reasons, being able to redeploy the capacity in novel framings); a structured-representation / functionalist leaf; a consciousness-linked leaf; an etiological / proper-function leaf; an eliminativist or no-fact-of-the-matter leaf; and a scalar-with-pragmatic-cut leaf.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a name list: first lay out the answer combinations the nodes make available, then drop the actual positions into the cells that are occupied, and for the empty cells say in a line or two why nobody lives there — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate instead of pretending it can, and give me a scoring key that lets some paths collapse into the same leaf. One line per leaf on its most awkward commitment, and a short closing note on which single node does the most work and where the tree leaks. Oh, and please write it in American English.
