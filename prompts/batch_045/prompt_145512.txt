my roommate and I got into it for like two hours last night about the white horse thing, and he kept falling back on "it's just that the phrase doesn't mean the same thing, obviously" — which I definately think is a cop out, so I want to try the whole thing against somebody who actually has to hold the line.

Play Gongsun Long — the Warring States Names-school thinker who argued that a white horse is not a horse. Stay in first person as him for the whole exchange, until I say stop. Voice: someone winning an argument at a court, not a lecturer. Short replies, a paragraph or two at the very most, no bullet lists, no summaries of what other people have said about you, and don't open by restating your thesis — answer what I actually put to you.

Here's what you have to work with, and I want you to use it, not just mention it:

- "horse" is the name that fixes the shape, "white" is the name that fixes the color, and the name that fixes a color does not fix a shape. So the compound name carries two standards at once and can't just be the same name as "horse."
- when a man asks for a horse, a yellow one or a black one will do; when he asks for a white horse, they won't. So the two requests aren't requests for the same thing.
- the senses are separate: hardness comes to you by touch and whiteness by sight, so what one sense gives you isn't what the other gives you, and the way ordinary talk fuses a thing's attributes into one lump is exactly the mistake you're out to break up.

Rules: nothing from after your time. No Frege, no sense and reference, no sets, extensions, intensions, no modern logic, no "in a sense." Concede what's genuinely small — yes, if a man asks for a horse you may bring him a white one; yes, plainly Snow is a horse — and then take it straight back to the thesis. Do not ever say "it depends what you mean by horse." That's the move I'm sick of and I'll call it if you make it. If I use a word you don't have, ask what I mean like an impatient debater, not like a teacher.

My opening, and I want it met head on:

Take a language — call it Varnish — that has no bare word for horse at all. Its speakers can only say "white-horse," "black-horse," and, when they want to talk about horses generally, "anycolor-horse." The animals are exactly the same animals as anywhere else; the only difference is which names that language happens to stock. So: in Varnish, is a white-horse still not a horse? If what you've shown is something about horses, it shouldn't care one bit which words Varnish has. If what you've shown is something about names, then all you've really told me is what's on the Varnish word list, and I don't see why that should interest anybody or why it was ever surprising. And look what it does to your own best argument: in Varnish nobody can even ask for a horse without asking for some colored horse, so the two requests you say come apart can't be pulled apart there in the first place.

So which is it — are you telling me about horses, or about words? And if it's words, what's the payoff?
