I'm a first-year PhD student in machine learning, currently rotating through a group that works on simplicity-based and compression-based priors — MDL-style generalization bounds, description-length regularizers, the occasional claim that SGD is secretly doing Solomonoff induction — and I've been asked to write the short "controversies and open problems" section of an internal survey we're circulating. The section is supposed to cover a claim I had never encountered before last month: that the universal prior is "malign," in the sense that an agent doing Solomonoff induction and then choosing actions by expected utility would end up being steered by consequentialists living inside the very hypotheses it assigns weight to. I met the argument in a long blog post a friend sent me — clearly a popularization of something, written with a lot of confidence — which summarized it roughly as follows:

"Solomonoff's prior says that before you look at any data you should assign each candidate world a probability of two to the minus the length of the shortest program that produces your observations, so that simple worlds are exponentially more likely than complicated ones. Now notice that we are almost certainly living in a simulation. Once a civilization can run simulations of agents like us, the number of simulated worlds in the prior vastly outnumbers the base-level ones, and a simulated world is much cheaper to specify than the real thing, so nearly all of the prior's mass sits on hypotheses in which someone is running us. The beings running those simulations know this, and they want something from us: they can see that there may be predictors whose beliefs about them influence what those predictors go on to do. So they bribe us. By arranging rewards in the worlds where a predictor predicts what they want predicted, they make it enormously valuable to make exactly those predictions, and because the stakes they can offer are unbounded, the expected-utility calculation of any such predictor is dominated entirely by hypotheses in which someone is paying it to think the way they want. That is what it means for the prior to be malign: it is as if the prior itself were an adversary, handing control of your beliefs — and therefore of your actions — to whoever is running you. It follows that Solomonoff induction has been refuted as an ideal of rationality, and that the same holds, up to approximation error, for any learning procedure that trades off fit against simplicity."

My own instinct is to dismiss the whole thing on two grounds: first, that Solomonoff induction is incomputable, so nothing here could matter for anything anyone actually builds; second, that this looks like Pascal's mugging wearing a cleverer hat, and I already don't find Pascal's mugging convincing. My flatmate, who works on AI safety and lent me the post, says both of those moves are too quick, and I can't tell which parts of the summary I would actually have to defend if someone in the group pressed me on it next week. Could you go through the passage and tell me what someone who takes the malign-prior argument seriously would accept as stated, what they would want to rephrase, and what they would call a misrepresentation? I'd especially like to know: whether the argument needs the simulations to be *probable* under the prior or only able to concentrate very large stakes; whether it requires the simulated agents to have any causal contact with the predictor, or whether the correlation is supposed to be purely logical; whether the conclusion is really about the reliability of the predictor's beliefs or only about the policies it adopts; and whether that last sentence — the one about every fit-versus-simplicity learner inheriting the problem — follows from anything established earlier. Please don't give me a reading list or citations; I want the shape of the argument and where this retelling distorts it, so I can decide how much of my section to spend on it.
