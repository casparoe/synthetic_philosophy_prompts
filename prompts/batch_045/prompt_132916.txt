I direct a small policy-planning unit inside a foreign ministry, and effectively all of our work arrives in the form of a claim that some present situation is a repeat of an earlier one: this is Munich, this is Vietnam, this is 1914, this is a rising power frightening an established one, this is the Cuban Missile Crisis and therefore we should blockade rather than invade. These claims come in weekly, from ministers, from allied governments, from our own staff, and increasingly from advocacy groups. They are not offered as decoration; they are offered as evidence that a particular policy will succeed or fail. My team has no framework for grading them, so the loudest analogy wins.

What I want from you is a written procedure my analysts can actually run, not an essay about analogy. Constraints on its use: one or two generalists with no graduate training in history, roughly forty-five minutes, open sources only, no archives and no specialist consultation; the write-up has to fit on one page and be readable by a minister who has never thought about analogical reasoning; and it has to terminate in one of three verdicts — the analogy earns weight in the forecast and licenses a named prediction; it is only a reframing device, good for generating questions but not for answering them; or it is doing rhetorical work and should be struck from the paper. Please give me the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the sequence stops with a no, and the points where the remaining question is one of values or of irreducible judgment and must be handed to a named human rather than settled by the checklist.

Four constraints on the design itself, because the easy versions of this fail.

First, the procedure cannot rest on an agreed reading of the source case. Historians dispute what Munich teaches, what Vietnam teaches, and whether the Peloponnesian War really was a case of an established power reacting to a rising one; if your checklist requires the analyst to know what actually happened and why, it will just reproduce the analyst's politics. It has to work when the source case is contested.

Second, it must sort surface resemblance from mechanism portability: the analogy has to name the mechanism that did the causal work in the source case, state the conditions under which that mechanism operates, and then check those conditions in the target rather than counting shared features.

Third, it must be symmetric. For every analogy offered, the analyst should have to produce the best-fitting analogy that supports the opposite policy and run the same steps on it; a procedure that only ever interrogates the other side's analogy is worthless to me. Say explicitly what the analyst should do when the two analogies come out with comparable fit and opposite recommendations, since that is the common case and not a degenerate one.

Fourth, it must handle two pathologies I see constantly. One is selection: the cases we remember were filtered by outcome, and the lessons that survive in public argument were filtered again by how useful they are to people arguing for action, so a procedure that asks only whether the target resembles the source inherits both filters. The other is reflexivity: the other side has read the same history. A government may stage a provocation expecting us to read it as Munich, or refrain from something expecting us to read it as 1914, or invoke our own archival analogy back at us. The procedure needs a step for the case where the target is reasoning about our analogy use, including the possibility that the analogy is being deployed as a weapon rather than offered as a forecast.

Please also build in a pre-registration step, since we nearly always see the analogy after the recommendation is already drafted, and a step that asks what the expected loss is if the analogy is wrong and the action it recommends is irreversible.

Then run it, honestly, on three live-shaped cases: a hegemon-and-rising-power framing applied to the current competition over Taiwan; a Munich framing applied to a demand that a neighbouring state cede territory and cap its alliances; and a Vietnam framing applied to a proposal to send a few hundred advisers and some air support to a government losing a counterinsurgency. My unit has a house bias — we are the restraint-minded desk, we reach for 1914 and Vietnam, and we are reflexively hostile to Munich framings — so if the honest output is that our own house analogy is the weakest of the three, say that plainly, because it is the answer I will be most tempted to reject.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, where it will misfire, and which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as method. And note that the failure mode I most want to avoid is a checklist whose real content is check whether the analogy is accurate, or one that amounts to general scepticism about learning from history; the verdicts have to be generated by the structure of the case and the mechanism, not by the analyst's prior view of the policy.
