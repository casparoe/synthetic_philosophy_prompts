I am a journalist working on a long piece about how researchers, funders, and policy people who study risks to humanity's long-term future talk about human extinction, and I have now run into the same shape of claim in enough interviews that I want to know whether it has a name and, if so, where its edges are.

Here is the shape. Everyone I speak to agrees that extinction would be a catastrophe. When I strip away the deaths and the suffering — suppose the end were instantaneous and painless, and suppose everyone alive had already lived a full life — they converge on something like this: what is lost is not a matter of anyone being made worse off. Pressed to say more, the answers split. One group says the loss is retrospective: the work done this year, the symphony, the vaccine, the raising of a child, would turn out not to have had the point it was thought to have. Another group says no, their projects would still have had their point; what is lost is prospective and victimless — an enormous quantity of good that would have happened simply will not happen, and that is a disaster even though there is nobody it is a disaster for.

What I cannot place is that these same people, often in the same conversation, deny the mirror-image claim. They explicitly reject the thought that we lose something by not bringing additional flourishing people into existence: a society with a falling birth rate is not, on their view, incurring a loss of value on that account. Yet the non-existence of future people is precisely what they count as the central loss in extinction. When I offer them the replacement case — humanity ends, but the planet is immediately occupied by other beings whose lives go just as well, and who remember nothing of us — they divide sharply, and the division does not track any of their other disagreements. One further boundary I cannot locate: several said the retrospective version only bites if the end is close enough to fall inside the world they are living in, and that an end ten centuries out threatens nothing they care about.

I read a review some time ago of a philosopher's book — I believe it grew out of lectures on death — arguing that nearly everything we value silently presupposes that people will be around after we die, so that extinction would hollow out our present concerns. I assumed that was what my interviewees were reaching for. It is not quite: when I put it to them, more than one said that their projects would still matter, and that what would be lost is the future itself.

So: is there an established concept for this configuration — treating the non-existence of possible future people as an enormous loss in one direction while treating the non-existence of merely possible additional people as no loss at all — and is what I have described one phenomenon or two? If there is a name, I want the carving rather than the label: how it differs from the nearby views about who can be harmed by not being brought into existence, and how it differs from the view in the book I have described. If there is no name, I would like you to coin and define one, to say whether the retrospective and prospective articulations are genuinely separable, and to say whether the split over the replacement case shows a single intuition or several. I also need to know what would count as evidence that this is simply an inconsistency in how this community talks, since that is the deflationary explanation I would have to take seriously in the piece.
