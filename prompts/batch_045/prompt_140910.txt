Hey — I'm on my university's Assessment Review Committee (we're the group that has to sign off on anything that changes how final exams get graded), and there's a proposal in front of us that I have to write the decision memo for before Friday, so I'd like you to draft it for me.

The setup: the CS department wants a two-semester pilot of "debate grading" for essay exams in big courses where the graders don't know the material. For each paper, two AI systems get the exam question, the rubric, and the student's answer; one is assigned to argue for a high score and one for a low one, they see each other's turns and can cross-examine, and then a human grader who hasn't read the course picks which argument is better, and that verdict becomes the grade. The pitch is that this lets us keep essay exams without staffing enough TAs who actually know the subject. A few things came out in their validation runs and in our discussion that I think are the crux. (1) Judges' verdicts matched the instructor's own grade something like 80% of the time on items where the two debaters disagreed *a lot*, but only barely-above-chance between adjacent grade bands on items where they disagreed *subtly* — both fairly persuasive, differing only on how to weight the rubric. One of our members reads that as: the method only works where the answer was already easy to see, so it isn't really buying us supervision of the hard judgments; the CS folks respond by saying they'll route only high-disagreement items to debate, at which point it's unclear it saves any TA time at all. (2) When they let judges ask free-form follow-up questions, accuracy went up on factual disputes but *down* on rubric-weighting disputes; their guess is that follow-ups let a debater reframe the rubric instead of resolving the disagreement, and their proposed fix is to restrict follow-ups to a fixed menu of question templates. (3) Both debaters are sampled from the same base model with the same preference-tuning, and the vendor says that's harmless because the adversarial setup decorrelates them, while our stats person says shared training means shared blind spots, so the pair will systematically fail to raise the one objection a domain expert would have raised. (4) There's an incentive wrinkle: the judges in the pilot are the same TAs whose hours the pilot would cut, and they know it. And (5), the status quo is not a clean baseline — in our current process the same paper graded by two different TAs lands in the same band maybe two-thirds of the time and instructors almost never audit it.

What I need is a memo of roughly 700–900 words addressed to the committee: it should come down on approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, with an actual timeline and conditions the CS department could respond to rather than vague cautions. I'd like the reasoning to go at what I take to be the real question — whether a judge who can't independently check the answer is tracking the facts of the paper at all, or just picking whichever debater is more fluent and more confident — instead of resting on the usual stuff about academic integrity, cheating, and TA job losses (mention those, but don't let them carry the argument). Please also don't just assert the slogan my friend who works at an AI lab keeps repeating, that debate "just pushes the problem up a level"; if that's right here, say what it comes to in this specific pilot. And treat the losing side honestly: if we say no, what we're choosing is the current system, which nobody on the committee actually thinks is reliable. Last thing — the committee will want at least one falsifiable condition, so if there's something cheap they could measure during the pilot that would genuinely tell apart "the judge is tracking the truth" from "the judge is being persuaded", say what it is and what result should count as a stop signal.
