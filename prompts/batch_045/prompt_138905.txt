My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about a mutual acquaintance, and the argument kept coming back to a phrase neither of us could pin down. The acquaintance — call her R. — has spent fifteen years running a small hospice charity, and has now decided to leave, retrain as an actuary, and give away most of a much larger salary. She described the decision as the upshot of months of explicit comparison: she tried to work out, in a deliberately rough expected-value sort of way, which of the careers open to her would do the most good, and the answer that came back was not the one she was already living. My friend's reply was immediate: that, he said, is precisely Williams's one thought too many. On his reading, if you cannot go on with the thing you care most about without first checking that impartial morality permits it, then morality has asked one question too many of you, and the point generalises: your deepest commitments are not answerable to that kind of audit at all.

I pushed back on two fronts and got nowhere. First, it seemed to me that R.'s case differs in structure from the case Williams discusses at the end of his essay on persons, character and morality — the man who can rescue only one of two people in equal peril, and rescues his wife — because what R. cares most about is already other-directed and aggregative in content. Her project is the relief of suffering, not the pursuit of something whose point is independent of the general good. If the project already has that content, I said, consulting the calculus is not a foreign import into her life but an expression of it. My friend said I had missed the point, which concerns the standing of moral thought over practical thought generally, not the content of anyone's projects.

Second, I asked whether the complaint is even about occurrent deliberation. R. does not, at the moment of acting, run through an impartial argument; she settled it months ago and now acts on the settlement. And I improvised a further case: suppose she had never done the calculation herself, but had asked a friend she trusts to do it and simply taken his answer, so that no thought of that kind ever occurs to her at all. Is that better, because the thought is absent, or worse, because the deference alienates her from her own life even more thoroughly? He said my example was 'sociological'; I said his reading was phenomenological, and that Williams's discussion does not obviously support it.

So I would be grateful for the following. Set out, as a menu, the precise claims that the one-thought-too-many line could be making: a claim about what morality may demand of an agent, about what an agent may act for, about the phenomenology or psychology of deliberation, about the jurisdiction of moral thought over practical thought, about what an agent may be required to be able to justify. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and which comes closest to what Williams was actually doing. Then tell me whether any of the defensible readings really gives my friend what he wanted — that a demanding impartial morality has no standing to audit commitments like R.'s — and whether the objection is aimed at utilitarianism specifically or at the morality system in Williams's broader sense, Kantianism included. Finally, say how the readings differ on my two difficulties: a ground project that is already consequentialist in content, and deliberation that is background, merely remembered, or outsourced to somebody else.

Please write your answer in British English.
