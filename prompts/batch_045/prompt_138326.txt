I've somehow ended up chairing the on-stage interview at the one-day workshop on cooperation that our philosophy department is co-hosting with the biology crowd next month, and I want to get the questions right rather than recieving them the morning of. Our speaker is a young philosopher of biology — call her Marisol Vega, she's a postdoc in an evolution-and-behavior program — whose paper argues something like this: that kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity and group selection are not five rival explanations of cooperation but five ways of producing the same underlying thing, namely positive assortment (a correlation between the strategies that actually meet each other), and that once you see this the prisoner's dilemma itself is doing almost none of the explanatory work. Her slogan is that we should be assortment-first: the payoff matrix is a fixed background condition and all the interesting variance lives in whatever mechanism generates the correlation, so a literature organized around the dilemma has been measuring the wrong variable for forty years. My colleagues in the bio department mostly think she's right but that she's picking a fight with a strawman, and one of them has already told me he'll ask from the floor why, if the dilemma structure is so irrelevant, the models built on it keep producing results that survive contact with actual data.

So: draft me eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one on the one-shot case — put to her that the dominance argument for defecting is exactly the part of the literature her story seems to leave untouched, and when she replies that a one-shot game against a stranger is a degenerate case that never occurs in the populations she cares about, press her on the variant where each player is handed a verified copy of the other's decision procedure (or, if you prefer, a tournament where agents submit code and each program gets to read the other's source before moving): here the correlation between strategies is manufactured by mutual transparency rather than by any population structure, so does her framework have a place for it, or does she have to say it isn't a prisoner's dilemma anymore? I also want one question on the shadow of the future: how does she answer the backward-induction argument for a known finite horizon, and how does she answer the more interesting case where nobody knows how many rounds there will be and cooperation is sustained by each side's uncertainty about whether the other is a reciprocal type — with a follow-up ready for her almost certain move that this is just a fancy way of relabeling an irrational disposition as rational. One question should go at the extortion result, the discovery that in the iterated game a player can unilaterally enforce a fixed linear relation between the two players' payoffs, and ask her whether that supports her picture (payoff structure is nearly irrelevant; it's all about what your strategy is correlated with) or undermines it (the subsequent work showing that such strategies are dynamically unstable and lose out to generous ones looks like a specifically historical, population-level fact that her assortment talk doesn't predict). And one should press directly on the idle-matrix worry: ask her to run her own account on a game with no dilemma in it at all — say a pure coordination game with aligned interests — and tell us what changes; if the answer is nothing, follow up on whether she's really offering an explanation of cooperation or just a redescription of every case where like meets like.

A couple more things I'd like folded in. Last week in my decision-theory seminar a student asked why, if my reasoning and yours are the same reasoning, I can't simply reason my way into cooperating in the one-shot game, and I fumbled the answer; if one of the questions can get her to say something crisp about that, I'd be grateful. We're also having a badly behaved side-discussion in the department about what any of this tells us about AI systems that have to deal with each other — teams of learning agents, or language-model-based agents negotiating with a counterpart they'll meet again and can partly inspect — and whether the assortment story gives you any design advice there, or whether it just says arrange for the right correlations, which is the advice itself. Please give me a question on that too, phrased so that she can't get away with gesturing at the literature, plus a closing question about what observation — in a simulation, in the field, or in a lab — would actually make her abandon the assortment-first program. Assume she's quick, slightly combative, and very fond of saying that's a modeling artifact, not an objection: I'd like at least one sequence where that reply gets steadily harder for her to use. Don't bother with anything about how she got into the field; we're recording and we'll cut that anyway.
