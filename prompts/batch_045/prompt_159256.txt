In the graduate reading group I am leading on diachronic rationality, one participant has been defending Van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, that your credence now in a proposition A, conditional on the hypothesis that your credence in A at a specified later time will be r, should itself be r — by way of a financial analogy, and he uses it to settle rather than to illustrate. The analogy: your credence function at a time is like the market price of an asset. On the efficient-market picture today's price is already the market's expectation of tomorrow's price once tomorrow's information arrives; that is just the martingale property, and denying it would amount to claiming a predictable arbitrage, an identifiable trade that extracts money for sure from your own future self. Reflection, on this telling, is nothing but no-arbitrage applied to belief: it needs no independent support from diachronic Dutch-book arguments or from deference to a better-informed later self, and the standard hard cases must be confusions, since "the market will be wrong tomorrow in a way I can already identify today" is not a coherent thing to say. The analogy is doing real work for him: it is what lets him treat the future self as informationally dominant by default, and it is what lets him dismiss the hard cases without examining them.

I would like that analogy audited rather than either accepted or dismissed. Please give me:

1. An explicit table of correspondences — what plays the role of the asset, the price, the traders, the arrival of information, the filtration, market clearing, the arbitrageur, settlement, and the closing price. In particular, say what corresponds to the proposition A's being true (is it a payoff that settles, or is it something else), and what corresponds to the later total evidence, as opposed to the later credence.
2. Where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks bear on the use he makes of it. Three I want addressed directly: (a) martingales are defined relative to a filtration of information, whereas Reflection conditions on the coarse event that your later credence equals r — so the analogy has to treat the later credence as a sufficient statistic for the later evidence, and I want that assumption stated and tested; (b) prices are public, recorded, and disciplined by settlement, while credences are private, can be lost, and need never be settled; (c) markets aggregate many traders with dispersed information so that idiosyncratic error cancels, whereas a single mind has no such mechanism, so "efficient" for one agent requires an argument that the market case does not supply.
3. Run the analogy on three cases rather than gesturing at them. The noisy memo: at the later time you will be handed a one-line memo reporting that the trial succeeded, no other record will survive, and you know the memo is accurate ninety per cent of the time. The erasure: you know that before the later time you will lose access to a specific part of your present evidence, so the later self is strictly worse informed and you know exactly which part it will lose. The duplication: you will be divided into two successors, each of which will assign the same credence r. Does the market picture give the same verdict in each, and if it gives no verdict, say so.
4. A verdict on whether Reflection survives the audit or whether the analogy was carrying it, together with a better analogy if there is one — a forecast market you yourself will trade in later, a measuring instrument scheduled for recalibration, a witness you will later become, or something else — and a statement of what that better analogy does and does not support.
