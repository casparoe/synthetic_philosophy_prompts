I write novels, and for a long time I have made my living doing something I would be hard-pressed to define: inventing people who do not exist and reporting what they do and say. So the argument over whether large language models understand anything is not academic for me. If the strong version is right, it is partly an argument about whether my own skill is what I think it is.

When the subject comes up among writers I know, one picture does all the work. A model, the story goes, is not a mind but a compressed and slightly degraded copy of the library it was trained on, the way a heavily compressed image file is a degraded copy of a photograph. Generation is decompression. The confident, invented passages are compression artifacts. The conclusion everyone draws from it is the one I care about: nothing behind the fluency, no one home, no one who means the sentences.

I would like that picture audited rather than applauded or dismissed, and two things make me suspicious of it. First, a friend who works in image restoration tells me the analogy misdescribes the target: a compressed file stores the image together with a fixed codec, whereas a trained model stores not the data but a procedure for producing more of it, and a procedure is the kind of thing that can be right or wrong in ways a file cannot be. Second, consider what happens when a scene of mine works. I can say what my characters would do in a room I never put them in, and, more tellingly, what they would refuse to do. If a system can do that reliably for people it has never encountered, "degraded copy" looks like the wrong description of what it has.

Please set out the correspondence explicitly, as a table: what plays the role of the photograph, of the pixels, of the codec, of the file, of the artifacts, of the decompression. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion my friends draw from the ones that are merely cosmetic, and name what the picture quietly imports from image compression that the target does not have: a single fixed original to measure error against, error that is local and measurable, a reconstruction of the same kind as its source, a decoder that cannot be asked questions about the scene. Then say whether the conclusion survives the disanalogies — no understanding, nothing meant, mere pastiche — and if the picture will not carry it, offer one that might.

Finally, tell me whether the analogy leaves me in any better position than it leaves the machine. I write about centuries I never saw, working from letters and court records and other people's research. I reconstruct too.
