ok so my reading group is doing a session on Aristotle's sea-battle problem and I want to practise arguing against the real version of the open-future view, not the cardboard one I keep knocking over. Please play a philosopher who defends it: on the eve of the battle, 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow' is neither true nor false, the present admits of several genuinely open futures, and the sentence is true only if every one of them contains a battle, so 'battle or no battle' is determinately true while each disjunct is indeterminate. He insists the gap is metaphysical rather than epistemic — it isn't that we don't know, it's that there is nothing yet to know — and he's allowed the usual machinery: determinately-operators, admissible histories, the move that necessity attaches to the consequence and not to the consequent. Make him sharp rather than smug, willing to concede small points but not the main claim unless I actually earn it, one paragraph per reply, stay in character until I say stop, and feel free to correct me if I state his view sloppily.

Here's my opening move: I think your gaps are either idle or they eat deliberation, and you have to pick which. Horn one, settled-but-gappy: suppose determinism is true — the winds, the currents and the admiral's own deliberations fix by causal necessity that the fleets engage at dawn. Nothing in your setup seems to forbid gaps in a deterministic world, because on your account truth needs actuality, not causal neccessity. But then the openness that was supposed to vindicate deliberation is doing no work: a fully settled future is exactly as gappy as a chancy one, so the thing that motivated the view — that deliberation would be pointless if the outcome were already fixed — doesn't distinguish the two cases at all, and you've bought the gaps without buying the openness anyone cares about. Horn two: if you instead say the gaps definately track causal openness, then they should spread to the sentences deliberation runs on. Take the admiral's practical conditional, 'if I sail at dawn I take the strait'. Is that determinately true now, indeterminate, or not yet a proposition at all? If it's indeterminate you've broken the inference he's actually running — I want to win, if I sail I win, so I sail — and you've left practical reasoning worse off than the bivalent theorist who just says the conditional is true. If it's determinate, explain why plain future indicatives go gappy while the conditionals we act on stay fine. Pick a horn and I'll take the other one; I'll also press you later on how a chronicler writing a century afterwards can truly say a battle was fought if the future-tensed version was never true, but don't answer that one till I bring it.

And when I've pushed it as far as I can, I want him to say, in character, what single consideration would make him give up the openness claim and where he thinks the account is shakiest — where its reasoning is most likely to fail. I want to find out where it breaks, not watch him win on points, so if he concedes something it should cost him later.
