I'm writing a novel — a hospital novel, one of those institutional ones where the real antagonist is a committee — and I need a scene where an honest person loses an argument to a dishonest one and loses it fair and square, in front of a judge who is smart, careful, well-meaning, and has about an hour.

The reason I'm being annoying about this: I fell down a rabbit hole reading about that AI-safety-by-debate idea, where instead of making a human grade a system's whole answer you have two systems argue opposite sides in front of a judge who can only look at a little bit of it, on the theory that it's much easier to spot one bad step in an argument than to verify a long answer from scratch. The standard worry people raise is the obfuscated argument: a debater who never actually lies but buries you anyway. I want my scene to be a real, non-cheating instance of that failure mode at human scale — and I want you to prove to me that it's real and not just rigged.

What I need:

1. The scene. One paragraph of setup, realistic and contemporary, no sci-fi, a real institution with a real decision at stake where there are actual procedural rules about who may speak and when. A mortality review, an insurance appeal, a workplace safety board, a credentialing hearing — your pick, but it has to be a setting where the outcome matters to somebody who isn't in the room.

2. The trick. Here's the one I have in mind, use it unless you've got something better that meets every constraint below: the dishonest character carves the space of possible explanations into three or four branches, presents the carving as if it were exhaustive, and then rebuts each branch in turn. The truth lives in a branch that never gets properly put on the table. If you substitute a different trick, it has to have the same shape: cheap to check any single move, expensive to see that the moves don't add up.

3. Show me the liar is innocent. Go through their three or four key moves one at a time and, for each: what's asserted, what the judge can check in under a minute with the documents in front of them, and why it's true. Not "arguably true" — true. If any of their sentences is false, or misleading in a way a careful generalist would catch in the time available, your case fails, and I'd rather you tell me it fails than paper over it.

4. The symmetry requirement, which is the part I care most about. The honest character's objection ("that's not everything that could have happened") has to be one the dishonest character can fire back word for word. Both of them can gesture at the gap; only one of them can fill it in with the actual mechanism and the actual line in the record that confirms it — and filling it in takes digging the judge won't sit still for. So the judge can't break the tie on tone, credentials, or who seems more reasonable.

5. Then walk me through the judge's actual reasoning: why they rule for the wrong person, and why they feel fine about it afterward.

6. Two repairs that don't work: (a) give the judge twice the time; (b) let the honest side cross-examine freely, with the liar held to everything they've already said. A few sentences each on why the scene survives both.

7. One repair that does work, without a smarter judge or a longer clock: shift the burden so that whoever asserts the negative has to construct the space of alternatives themselves and then live with it — every gap in their map is their problem, not the other side's homework. Show me how that plays out in the scene, ideally in dialogue.

8. Then the honest bit. Which single feature of the case is doing the work? What collapses if you remove it — if the truth sat inside a branch that got named, or if the burden were on the positive claim instead? And how would someone who thinks debate basically works attack your case? My guess is they'd say the honest debater can just name the missing branch out loud and the judge should update on that, so tell me whether that reply actually lands against your version or whether it's the one place your case is soft.

Some constraints:

- Nobody here is superhuman. Both arguers are about as good as a sharp human expert, the judge is a competent generalist, no exotic anything, no perfect predictors, no convenient amnesia.
- The substance — the medicine, or whatever it turns out to be — has to be followable by a smart reader with no training in it. My readers aren't doctors and Im not going to become one.
- Give me two or three lines of actual dialogue I could steal.
- The dishonest character shouldn't be a villain. Somebody who'd pass a polygraph, because they believe their own framing. The obfuscation should be the kind of thing they'd do again on reflection, not a slip of the tongue.
- And the verdicts have to be generated by the setup, not announced. If I can swap the two characters' names and the scene still "works," you've cheated and I'll be able to tell.

Aim for 700–900 words all in. Don't send me off to read anything or give me a list of sources — I want the argument itself, not the literature around it.
