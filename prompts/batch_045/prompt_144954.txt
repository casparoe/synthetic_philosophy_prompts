Got into a two-hour argument with my mate Dave last night about Hempel's raven paradox, and it ended with him betting I couldn't make a room of normal people actually feel the pull of it. So I've stupidly agreed to do the next brown-bag talk at work: twenty minutes, a whiteboard, people eating sandwiches. The room is about twenty backend engineers and data folks, one genuine statistician, and one guy who reads philosophy blogs and will definately get "grue" in at some point. None of them have done philosophy since school, they're all sharp, and they'll smell a verbal trick in about four seconds and then stop listening.

So don't explain the paradox to me — I more or less get it. Plan how I should build it in that room. What I need:

The order, and what depends on what. My instinct is: put "all ravens are black" on the board, get them to agree that observing a black raven is evidence for it, then get them to agree that whatever confirms one statement confirms any logically equivalent one, then spring the white shoe. But should the equivalence bit come first, so the ending feels forced rather than like a trick I pulled out of a hat?

The one example that has to carry the whole thing, and what it needs to have and not have. I was going to swap ravens for something from their world — "every request returns in under 200ms", say — but I suspect that quietly smuggles in a difference that matters and I can't see which one.

Dave's objection, which is the bit I couldn't answer: he says the whole thing is an artifact of ravens and non-black things being open-ended classes, and that in a closed finite world — a bag of a hundred tiles, drawn without replacement — the shoe stops confirming, maybe even lowers the probability that all the ravens are black. Is he right? And more to the point, does the countable bag belong at the start, as the carrying example, or at the end, as the thing that dissolves it?

The misconceptions in the order they'll form, and where in the sequence to kill each one. I can predict three — "this is just the word confirm being vague", "ok fine, a white shoe is evidence, so what" (agreeing way too fast, without seeing that the amount of confirmation is the whole point), and "so this is why science is fake" — but there's probably a fourth I'm not seeing, and I don't want to burn minute two on the semantics complaint and lose the room.

One question at the end that tells me whether they actually got it rather than just going along with me. And what I should flat out refuse to get into — projectibility, the statistician wanting a full prior-and-likelihood derivation on the board, whatever — phrased as something I can say out loud: out of scope, come find me after.
