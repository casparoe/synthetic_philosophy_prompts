Hi — I write novels (literary fiction, mostly set in small coastal towns of the kind where everybody knows whose father did what in the fifties), and I've got stuck on a scene that has somehow turned into a shouting match in my writing group, so I thought I'd take it to people who actually enjoy this stuff.

The scene: Ines is 61. Her father was the local hero type, she's spent thirty years telling her grandkids stories about him, she lives in his house, and an archivist has just handed her a sealed file that would more or less settle whether he informed on two neighbours to the secret police in 1952. It's free — she's literally holding it in the car park — and nothing strategic hangs on it: she isn't going to testify, sue anyone, or sell the house. But she can't un-read it. So she's standing there deciding whether to open it, and what I need to know is whether I can write her refusal as genuinely rational or whether I'd be writing her as merely confused. One friend (she did a very decision-theory-heavy PhD before she started writing) says it's obvious she should open it: the thing people call Good's theorem — that for an expected-utility maximiser the expected value of free information is never negative, since the plan *do whatever you would have done anyway* is always available, so the signal-contingent plan weakly dominates — is a dominance argument, a couple of lines, near enough certain, end of story, write her opening the file. The other friend says that just restates the conclusion with a utility function nailed down by fiat, and that all the weight sits in assumptions that are precisely false here: that learning the thing doesn't change what she values afterwards, that she can even enumerate the options she'll have once she knows and pick the best of them, that the *before* standpoint is the one that should count. They're at about 0.85 and 0.3 and I definetly can't tell which of them is bluffing.

So: what's your credence in this — that for a real human agent in a case like Ines's, where the information is materially free and nobody else's reaction is in play, the theorem's guarantee actually carries over from the ideal agent to the person, in the sense that getting the information cannot make her worse off, judged from where she stands before she opens the envelope? (Count the claim as vindicated if the idealisations doing the work are ones we should treat as harmless rather than as typically false in ways that matter.) I don't want *it depends on your model of rationality* — I want a number, and I want it decomposed: how much rides on the formal result being correct as mathematics for a Bayesian expected-utility maximiser, how much on her utility function being stable under what she learns, how much on her being able to form and execute the contingent plan (or even to know what her post-learning options are), how much on whether *worse off* should be read ex ante at all given that she might bitterly regret looking afterwards, and whatever else you think belongs in there — imprecise or ambiguous priors, the fact that the value could be exactly zero rather than positive, the possibility that the theorem has no empirical content beyond being a consequence of how expected-utility maximisation is defined, the business about whether she can precommit. A number and one line of justification for each term, then say how you're combining them, and then tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to and which one you're least confident about. And finally: which of my two friends is most likely wrong about what they're disagreeing over, and what would move you — an argument, or some empirical finding about how people actually fare with this kind of information? I'd also love to know, in a sentence or two, whether the honest version of the scene is her opening the file, not opening it, or her not being able to decide at all.
