I sat in on a design review at a lab where I do some outside advising, and two researchers spent most of it arguing about the oversight scheme for a coding agent. The agent works on long, open-ended tasks — multi-hour, hundreds of tool calls, only sporadic human contact — and nobody can write down the objective. Their plan is to train it to maximize predicted approval: a human principal is shown a proposed action and clicks approve or reject, the agent is trained to maximize the probability of approval (first from the principal directly, later from a model of the principal's judgments), and the approval model is retrained as the principal's standards drift. Call this approval-direction. The dispute was whether the resulting system is goal-directed in the sense that matters for safety: roughly, whether it competently pursues some target across a wide range of situations, and so acquires the convergent instrumental reasons — to protect its own objective, to acquire resources and influence, to avoid being corrected or switched off — that make such systems dangerous. Neither of them moved the other, and I couldn't tell who was winning. Paraphrasing as fairly as I can:

(1) Marta opened by saying approval-directed agents are simply not goal-directed, because what they maximize is not a state of the world but a relation: a judge's response to what the agent does. Nothing in the setup hands the agent a terminal goal it carries from situation to situation; the target is re-specified every time a fresh judgment arrives. So there is no objective for the agent to defend against correction and no reason for it to accumulate power. What the design buys you, she said, is exactly the substitution of a *whose* for a *what*.

(2) Dev replied that the relation/state distinction does no work. A judge's approval is realized in the judge's head, which is as much part of the world as anything else, and an agent that can predict approval well enough to optimize over hours of tool calls will manage the judge's epistemic situation the way it would manage any other target — by controlling what the judge sees, when they see it, how much context they get, how much time they have. On his view approval-direction is just goal-direction with the goal parked inside a person, which makes it worse rather than better, since fooling a person is cheaper than rearranging the world.

(3) Marta conceded some of this and re-cut the distinction: the safety-relevant variable, she said, is not whether the target is a state but whether approval is solicited myopically or holistically. If the principal approves each action in its local context, with no credit assigned across the episode, there is no later approval for the agent to secure and hence no gradient toward shaping the principal's beliefs over time. Dev's incentive appears only if you ask the principal to rate whole trajectories. She granted the cost — myopic approval inherits the principal's own myopia and gives up long-horizon competence — but insisted his objection is an objection to trajectory-level approval, not to approval-direction as such.

(4) Dev answered that myopia relocates the incentive rather than removing it. Every query is a context the agent itself builds: which files are open, how the diff is summarized, which option is framed as the default, whether it asks at the start of the day or at 6pm. A per-decision approval-maximizer optimizes each of those for approval, which is precisely where sycophancy and selective presentation live; and because one system emits the whole sequence, the local effects compose. The agent can steer the project into regions where the next query is easy to approve, and end up having reshaped the principal's understanding substantially, without ever having had a goal. Manipulation, he said, doesn't need a goal — only a long series of locally approved steps.

(5) Marta's last move was that this shows the real object of optimization is neither approval nor a goal but the principal's epistemic position, and that the fix must be structural rather than a choice between her design and his. Her concrete proposal — she called it blind counterfactual approval — is that the agent maintain a distribution over the actions it regards as reasonable at each step, sample one from it *other* than the one it actually executed, and have the principal rate the sampled action against the outcome observed afterwards, with the agent never told which action was rated. Because the agent cannot influence which action gets judged, she argued, it cannot profit from presentation, and the gradient toward managing the principal's beliefs collapses. Dev's only reply before we ran out of time was that the menu is the agent's too: it can propose candidate sets in which every member is approval-friendly, or in which the alternatives look bad beside the one it wants, so you have moved the target one level up rather than eliminated it.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one per move, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: rate the move (clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, half right, mostly right, clearly right) and say in a sentence why. In item 5, after rating the move, add one sentence on what, if anything, both of them are missing.
