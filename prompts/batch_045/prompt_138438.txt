I am a novelist with one major revision left on a literary novel: about six months of full-time work before copyediting, and an audience that reads fiction for character and style rather than for argument. For the sake of this project I have become persuaded by W. E. B. Du Bois's argument in his writings on art that art cannot avoid being propaganda. On this view, even a novel that never mentions politics still advances some vision of who counts as a person, which lives are narratable, and what a reasonable social order looks like. Assume that this is true for the purpose of the question. My end is narrow and practical: I want the finished book to leave its readers with a slightly different imaginative sense of what is possible in housing policy, not by inserting speeches or converting the book into a tract, but by shaping the material where its propaganda actually lives.

To think about that, I have been using a rough distinction of my own. Call the first the thesis level: the political views characters state, endorse, or refute in dialogue and narration. Call the second the formal level: choices about point of view, which characters are granted interior depth, what motives are treated as intelligible, how the plot distributes sympathy and resolution, and what the ending presents as livable or unlivable; at this level, propaganda can be carried by craft rather than by assertion. Call the third the reflexive level: whether the book admits and foregrounds its own partiality, or presents its framing as simply the way things are, and how that admission changes a reader's trust.

My question is an agenda-setting one. Given limited revision time and a novelistic rather than philosophical toolkit, which two or three open questions about how to turn Du Bois's claim into practice should I investigate first, and in what order? I do not want a survey of everything one might say about propaganda in art, and I do not want a reading list or citations; I cannot follow them. I want a defended priority list of at most three lines of work. For each, tell me: what exactly the question is; why it is upstream of other artistic decisions, in the sense that settling it would make many later choices easier or would change what counts as a good later choice; how tractable it is for a working novelist using only reflection, drafts, and perhaps a few trusted beta readers, rather than for a philosopher with unlimited time; and what concrete evidence over the next six months would move it up or down the list, such as which passages early readers call preachy, or which characters they say they could not understand.

Please also make the criteria explicit: say how much weight you are giving to importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many other decisions depend on the answer, and describe one trade-off where you deliberately deprioritize a question that is philosophically deeper but would not change my revision. Then give the ordering.
