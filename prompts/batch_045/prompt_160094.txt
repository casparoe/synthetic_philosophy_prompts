Hoping you can help me think something through. I help moderate a fairly big volunteer organisation (about 40k members, lots of sub-groups, we have a code of conduct and an informal habit of enforcing it very badly), and I'm writting a policy on how we handle it when a member says something that sets off a big public reaction. Before I write anything down I want to understand what the informal practice we've drifted into actually presupposes, because every time I try to describe it I end up in a knot.

Here's the practice, concretely. Someone posts something a lot of people find offensive. Within a day or two there's a pile-on: thousands of replies, people digging up things they said ten or fifteen years ago, someone finds out where they work and emails the employer, a conference un-invites them, maybe a platform suspends the account. There's no charge, no hearing, no moment where anyone announces what the sanction actually is, and no point at which it's officially over — people just keep bringing it up years later, and "they apologised and seem to have genuinely changed" is treated as irrelevant, or asevidence of cynicism. And the people doing it almost always describe it as not punishment at all: nobody is being sent anywhere, they say, we're just exercising our own freedom of speech and association, and the employer is just responding to its customers. Meanwhile a lot of the same people are extremely sceptical about prison and about retributive punishment generally, and if you ask them directly they'll say punishment is only ever justified by what it prevents.

That combination is what puzzles me. The practice has the shape of something retributive — permanent, insensitive to reform, with no real sense of proportion between what was said and what happens — while being defended in forward-looking language and denied to be punishment at all.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory. Concretely, I'd like you to take on these:

- What view of punishment makes "a thousand people each independently deciding not to associate with someone" not punishment? Does it presuppose that punishment essentially requires an authorised agent acting in the name of the community? Or can all those small refusals add up to something punitive even though nobody intends or designs the total outcome — and if so, what has to be true about action and responsibility for that to work?
- Most of the people doing the sanctioning aren't the ones who were wronged and have no relationship at all with the target — they encountered the remark as a screenshot, stripped of context, addressed to someone else. What does the practice presuppose about who may hold whom responsible, and about whether the stance you take up toward someone has to come from inside a relationship? Someone in our group mentioned an old paper about "reactive attitudes" versus taking the "objective" stance toward someone, which I haven't read; if that's relevant, say how, and be honest about whether the practice is coherent by its own lights.
- Norms move. Things that were unremarkable to say in 2008 aren't now, and people get sanctioned for the 2008 version. Does the practice presuppose that blameworthiness tracks the norms in force when you judge rather than when you acted, and if so, does it owe us an account of why ignorance of a norm isn't exculpating here when it usually is?
- Finally, separate the load-bearing commitments from the ones that just happen to fit. Could someone who really believed punishment is justified only by what it prevents run this practice consistently? Could a retributivist? Which parts of the practice are philosophically underdetermined, so several theories would license them? And what would concretely have to change — a proportionality ceiling, an end date, some standard of evidence, a rule about who gets to bring it — if you swapped the presupposed view for one of its rivals?

I want the implicit theory laid out, and I want to know which bits I'd be signing up for if I wrote any of this into a policy.
