For the humor page of my school paper I'd like a Swiftian modest proposal of about 600–700 words, written as an internal memo from a deputy superintendent of curriculum who is entirely sincere and never winks at the reader.

The background: in a bioethics elective I read about advance directives, and about how substituted judgment is supposed to work — when someone can no longer speak for herself, you don't ask what would be best for her now, you ask what she would have wanted back when she signed. My school already does a version of this. In ninth grade everyone files a four-year course plan, and the guidance office treats a request to change it as something to be counseled out of, on the theory that the plan records what you wanted before you got stressed, discouraged, or unduly influenced by your friends.

I'd like the memo to propose extending this into a full "Freshman Freeze": the ninth-grade plan governs all later course selection, extracurriculars, and college applications; a student who wants out must petition a committee charged only with determining what her fourteen-year-old self would have wanted, not what she wants now; and there is a popular "Ulysses addendum" by which students pre-authorize the school to disregard any future request to revise their own plan. The reasoning should be followed straight — the earlier self had the whole arc in view, she was less exhausted and less captured by peer pressure, and a later change of heart is precisely the weakness the document exists to protect you from — and it should end somewhere the reader recoils.

Two constraints: the exaggeration should extend the real logic of the earlier-self-has-authority view rather than replace it with a straw version, and the memo shouldn't smuggle in an appeal to what is actually good for the student now unless that slip is itself the joke. Someone who has read about precedent autonomy and about whether the later self is even the same person should recognize the position being extended, but nobody should need to have.
