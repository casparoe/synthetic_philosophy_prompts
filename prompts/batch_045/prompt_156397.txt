I am a retired observational cosmologist — thirty-odd years in the field, now auditing philosophy courses in my second retirement — and I have been trying to work out how much of one particular argument survives being stated carefully. I would be grateful for your help, and I would ask that you answer in American English.

At a lecture series on science and religion that I attended last spring, the speaker distributed a one-page summary of the fine-tuning argument. It ran roughly as follows:

"The constants of physics are exquisitely adjusted for life. The cosmological constant is balanced to roughly one part in ten to the hundred and twenty, the Higgs field's vacuum expectation value to something like one part in ten thousand, and the carbon-12 energy level that allows carbon to be assembled inside stars to a fraction of a percent. If you move any one of these by more than that margin while holding the others fixed, stars never ignite, chemistry is impossible, or the universe recollapses before anything could evolve. A life-permitting universe is therefore fantastically improbable on the hypothesis of chance. But it is exactly what a designer would want, so design is by far the better explanation. The standard rival reply — that there are vastly many universes with different constants, so it is no surprise that this one permits life — merely reruns the same improbable gamble billions of times over, which is just the gambler's fallacy; and since it predicts nothing we could observe, it is not science."

Something in this strikes me as unfair, but I cannot locate the unfairness precisely, and I suspect that at least one of my own suspicions is itself confused. Please give me a sentence-by-sentence audit, in the terms an advocate of the argument would recognize as their own position: what they would accept as stated, what they would accept as legitimate compression for a lay audience, and what they would regard as a misrepresentation they have explicitly disavowed.

Four matters I would like you to adjudicate specifically:

First, my main worry is that the summary slides from a claim about the width of the life-permitting interval to a claim about probability. It seems to me that "the interval is narrow compared with the range of values we take seriously" does not by itself yield "a life-permitting universe is improbable," unless one is also given a measure over the space of possible values — and the measure, I gather, is not supplied, with the further awkwardness that reparametrizing the space (writing the theory in terms of the square of a coupling, say) can change how large the window looks. Is that a fatal gap in the argument, a technicality that a defender can plug, or a misstatement on my part of what the defender is claiming?

Second, I suspect the summary misdescribes the many-universes reply. As I understand it, that reply does not consist in giving nature repeated tries at producing this universe; it rests on an observation selection effect — that we should expect to find ourselves in whatever universe permits observers, however rare such universes are. If that is the right statement of the reply, does the corrected version actually do the work its proponents claim, and is the "gambler's fallacy" charge simply aimed at a straw version of it?

Third, a vaguer worry: we knew that we exist, and that our universe permits life, long before we knew anything about the values of the constants. It is not obvious to me that a discovery made later can confirm a hypothesis that we should already have expected to accommodate the facts we knew earlier. Please tell me whether this worry has a standard name and standing, and whether it tells against the design argument in particular or against a wider class of arguments.

Fourth, on the physics itself: which of the tuning claims in the summary are robust, and which depend on conventions that a working physicist would want flagged — such as holding the other constants fixed rather than varying them jointly, the choice of parametrization, or the assumed range of "serious" values? If the illustrative numbers are inflated, or if some of these constants are not independent of one another, I would like that stated plainly.

Finally, once the summary has been corrected, does the conclusion it draws still follow from what survives? In particular, I would like to know whether the argument, stated carefully, supports anything as strong as theism, or only something considerably weaker — and whether the reply it dismisses as unscientific really is, in the form its defenders actually hold it. Please also flag any point at which my own framing above is mistaken.
