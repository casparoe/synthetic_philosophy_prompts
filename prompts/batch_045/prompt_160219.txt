Hi — I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm doing a piece on how much we owe the very long run (climate, nuclear waste storage, AI risk, that kind of thing). A friend who works in climate policy sent me her one-paragraph version of the discounting debate, and I want to know how much of it I can safely repeat:

"Discounting just says money later is worth less than money now, the same way interest works. The rate we use in policy has three parts: how impatient we are (pure time preference), the fact that future people will be richer so an extra dollar matters less to them, and the chance that the future won't be there at all. Only the middle one is really an ethical judgment — the other two are facts about the world. So the respectable position is to set pure time preference to zero, which is what the much-cited report on the economics of climate change did and why other economists went after it. And once you've done that, you're discounting the future at close to zero — i.e., treating someone alive in 2200 the same as someone alive today."

Then she added something I keep turning over: "And if you really thought a person alive in 2200 counted the same as a person alive now, you'd spend the money today rather than invest it. Putting it in the bank and waiting just means you care more about a bigger pile of money than about whoever is alive later, so anyone defending a positive rate is admitting they count future lives less."

That last bit doesn't sit right with me but I can't say why. Can you go through it roughly sentence by sentence — what a economist-philosopher who actually works on social discounting would sign off on, what they'd wince at, what's flat wrong — and say whether her conclusion follows even from the parts that are right? I'm inclined to believe zero pure time preference is the consensus among people who study this, but I'm not at all sure, and I don't want to write that if it's one camp's view. So please flag clearly where the settled part stops and the genuinely contested part starts, rather than smoothing it over. American English please, and assume I'm not going to go read the papers.
