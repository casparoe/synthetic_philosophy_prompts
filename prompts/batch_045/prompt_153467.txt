I cover AI for a living and over the last year or so I've kind of drifted into believing the amplification-and-distillation story: take a system only slightly better than one you already trust, have it break hard problems into subproblems it can actually handle, recurse on that, then compress the slow expensive process into a fast model and start over — and as long as each decomposition step is something the base system can do competently and report honestly, you ride both the capability and the alignment up the ladder with you. I want someone to talk me out of it. What would actually count against it, ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and which of those defeaters would the people who hold the view concede themselves rather than wave off?

The bits I keep chewing on: (1) checking whether a proposed decomposition is the right decomposition seems to need about as much capability as just solving the original problem, so amplification stalls out exactly where the base system is already out of its depth; (2) the scheme is a fixed-point iteration whose fixed point is fixed by the base system's own judgment, so it amplifies that system's blind spots and biases at the same rate as its competence, with no error-correcting term anywhere in it; (3) distillation is a lossy fit of a fast model to a slow one, and the errors pile up in the the rare hard cases the slow system was there to handle, which is where the whole value of the scheme lives; (4) those recent runs where people supervise a stronger model with a weaker one and the strong model only recovers part of the capability gap.

Also, honestly: how exposed is it at all? Half the time I hear it stated as a definition — the fixed point of some human-judgment-amplifying operator — rather than as a claim about what any actual training run gives you, and I can't tell whether that's principled or just insulation. And does it predict anything that plain training-at-scale-with-feedback wouldn't predict anyway?
