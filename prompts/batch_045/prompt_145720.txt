I spent my working life in comparative physiology — ion transport first, then the electrosensory system of weakly electric fish — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind in a slow, unsystematic way. I have run up against something in my own former subject that I cannot put a name to, and I would be grateful for help, not with a label as such but with the carving.

The argument I keep meeting runs roughly like this. Some capacity — seeing, remembering, digesting, echolocating — turns out to be carried out, in different organisms or different materials, by physically quite dissimilar machinery. Because the one capacity sits on top of heterogeneous hardware, the capacity cannot simply be identical with any one of the lower-level kinds that realize it; identifying it with a particular neural or chemical process would be like identifying a valve with the brass casting of the tap on my garden wall. So the higher-level kind keeps an explanatory life of its own, and reduction of the kind is blocked. I take it this is called the argument from multiple realizability, and that it is used well outside biology.

What puzzles me is how this argument behaves in the actual history of my field, where it seemed to work in the opposite direction and then quietly expire.

Three cases. Weakly electric fish: the African mormyrids and the South American gymnotiforms generate a field around the body and detect the distortions caused by objects in it, and their last common ancestor almost certainly did nothing of the sort. The electric organs are built out of different tissue — modified muscle in most lineages, and, in at least one group, modified nerve — and the receptor arrays are not the same structures. Yet what impressed my colleagues was not the heterogeneity but the recovery of a shared description underneath it: the same computational job, comparing the animal's own discharge with what arrives across the skin and responding to the discrepancy, stated in the same mathematics for both groups. Nobody concluded that electrolocation was autonomous and irreducible; they concluded that we had found the level at which the two cases are the same case. Second case: the camera-type eye of cephalopods and of vertebrates. Same optics, opposite wiring — our photoreceptors point away from the light and we have a blind spot; theirs point toward it and they do not. Again the discovery did not terminate in pluralism but in a search for what the two share, and I remember being shown, I hope correctly, that forcing the expression of a single regulatory gene in the wrong part of a fruit fly can set off the development of a complete eye, and that near relatives of that gene turn up in eye development across animals whose eyes are otherwise built on quite different plans. Someone at a seminar called that deep homology, which struck me as a claim about ancestry rather than about explanation, and I could not tell whether it was the same point. Third case, the one that undid my confidence in the argument: in my own system, the intermediate description that unified two lineages was often not a piece of anatomy at all but a computation, a set of equations, or an optimality claim — anything that correlates its own signal with the returning one, anything that solves this estimation problem. Such a description fits any hardware you like, by construction.

So the phenomenon I want located has two parts. First: multiple realizability functioning not as a verdict on a kind but as a standing instruction to keep looking, where the discovery of a shared description at some intermediate level is counted as the reduction after all — which makes "multiply realized" look like a remark about the state of our evidence at a time rather than about the kind itself. Second: unification achieved by a description that seems too abstract to be a mechanism, and the suspicion that some of these unifications are only redescriptions. And there is a connected puzzle I have never seen answered to my satisfaction: everyone agrees that writing down the list of actual realizers with "or" between them does not count as a reduction, but I have not seen a clean statement of what separates that disjunction from the intermediate-level descriptions that do count.

If there is an established concept here, I should like it placed precisely against its neighbours — multiple realizability itself, realization, reduction, the idea that a mechanism can be typed independently of what occupies it, functional characterization of the Cummins-like sort if that is the relevant thing — with a statement of how the described phenomenon differs from each and which fits it best. If there is not, please coin a term and define it, and give me the edges: a case plainly inside, a case plainly outside, and a case you would need more information to decide. Please also say whether it matters, for whether a unification counts, that the shared description reflects common descent rather than independent convergence.

A name alone will not settle anything for me; I am after what the thing is, what it is not, and where it stops. Two further requests. I have no university library and no access to journals, so please argue the distinctions out rather than point me to a reading list. And please mark clearly which parts of your answer are settled among people who know this material, which are genuinely contested, and which are your own proposal; I would rather be told that something is disputed than be handed one side of a dispute as though it were the received view.
