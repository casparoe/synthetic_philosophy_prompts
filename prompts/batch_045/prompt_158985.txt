Our hospital's patient-advocacy committee keeps a running list of encounters we've flagged as epistemic injustice, and I'm the one who has to write up the rulings so I'm trying to keep them consistent. Flagged so far: the Black patient whose pain report was discounted; the woman whose autoimmune symptoms got called anxiety for six years; the patient with a strong regional accent whose description was heard as vague. Not flagged: the patient with a documented history of false reports, where we said the credibility deficit tracked evidence rather than identity; the wealthy, articulate man whose self-diagnosis was accepted far too readily, where we said an excess of credibility is a clinical error but not the kind of injustice we track; and a patient whose symptoms had no established clinical description at the time, which we called a gap in shared concepts rather than anyone's fault.

New case: a patient brings orthostatic data she collected at home and says she thinks it's dysautonomia. The clinician discounts her for two reasons that seem separable — one, patients who turn up with a printed differential get typed as anxious/'Dr. Google', and two, her chart carries an auto-generated summary of an earlier visit saying 'health anxiety, likely somatization'; she contests that line in the room, and he trusts the summary over her even though the summary was built out of her own earlier words, just laundered through a system nobody gave her access to.

What should we rule if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I can see at least two rules that fit all six earlier verdicts: a narrow one requiring a deficit caused by identity prejudice, and a looser one about deficits that don't track the speaker's actual reliability whatever their source. They split here, since the Dr-Google typing attaches to an acquired affiliation rather than an identity, and the summary thing isn't really about a person's credibility at all. Give me your best guess at our ruling, tell me which precedent is genuinely the closest analog, say what principle we'd be committing to either way, and name the further case that would actually separate the two rules instead of just restating them.
