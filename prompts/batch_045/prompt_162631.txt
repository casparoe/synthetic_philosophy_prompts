Hey — quick one. I'm teaching the grad seminar on the socialist calculation debate this term, and I've got a first-year who came out of ML and keeps short-circuiting the whole thing. His line is that the market is just a computer. Prices are the protocol the nodes use to pass just enough information around to solve one enormous constrained optimization; entrepreneurs are the processors; profit and loss is the loss function; Lange's Central Planning Board was only ever a slow, hand-run version of the same algorithm, and the reason everyone thought it couldn't be done is that the hardware available in the 1930s was pathetic. Now we've got GPUs, differentiable simulators and economy-wide data, so the Mises-and-Hayek objection that you can't aggregate dispersed knowledge is a hardware complaint dressed up as a philosophical one. He uses this to say the calculation debate is settled, planning is an engineering problem now, and my assigning Hayek at all is nostalgia.

I want to push back on this properly rather than just assert the canon at him. So: lay the analogy out in the most charitable version you can, as an explicit table — what plays the role of the processor, the message, the protocol, the objective function, the training data, the update rule. Then audit it. Where does the mapping break, and seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for the inference he is drawing from the ones that are just atmosphere. Also flag which features of a real computer the analogy quietly imports that the market may not have — a fixed architecture, one objective function rather than many conflicting ones, a clean split between the hardware and the data, a defined stopping point.

Three things I keep circling and can't quite nail: (1) whether the optimization framing quietly assumes the objective function and the constraint set are already given, when part of Hayek's point is that the market is the process by which you find out what the ends and the constraints even are, so there may be no well-defined problem there to be solved faster; (2) whether the kind of knowledge Hayek cared about — the guy who just knows this particular machine is about to fail — is the sort of thing that can be a datum at all, or whether it only exists as a disposition to act and so cannot be uploaded into a training set; (3) whether profit in his picture is doing the work of a signal inside the system or the work of someone's actual motive, and what happens to the analogy if its the latter, since a loss function is our motive while prices are supposed to be theirs.

Then the bit I actually need: does the conclusion survive? Is the feasibility-of-planning claim carried by the analogy itself, so that once the disanalogies are on the table it collapses, or does some weaker version stand — e.g. the computer picture is fine for short-run allocation of a given stock of stuff and only breaks for the long-run discovery case? Also, does his picture even engage Mises's original argument about prices for factors of production, or is it only answering the Hayek knowledge version? And if the computer analogy is the wrong one, give me a better analogy to hand him: something that makes prices look more like a language or a discovery procedure than like numbers being shuttled between processors.

Don't just rerun the standard objections at me — he's read the digital socialism and big-data-can-plan stuff and will have heard them. Tell me which of them actually survive contact with the ML version of the claim, and be blunt about where I'm wrong too, because I've taught this the same way for years and I may just be attached to Hayek.
