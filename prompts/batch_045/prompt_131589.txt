I write novels — four out, fifth in copyedits — and at a dinner party last week I got into an argument with a friend of my husband's who does philosophy, and it's been rattling around my head ever since. He was arguing for the view that moral judgement has to depend on general principles, against the thing I'd have said I already believed: that there aren't really moral rules, because whether a feature of a situation counts in favour of an act depends on the rest of the situation, so the very same feature that's a reason one way in one case can be no reason at all, or a reason the other way, in another. He mentioned some names for all this that I've since forgotton. I believe the same thing about writing, which is maybe my whole problem.

His argument, as near as I can reconstruct it:

(1) People really do learn to judge new cases correctly. I can read a scene in a manuscript I've never seen and tell that it isn't working, and I aquired that by reading a lot of scenes and being corrected by editors. Likewise you can watch a kid learn that it's mean to exclude somebody from a game and then watch her apply it to a situation nothing like the one she learned it in.

(2) If you get new cases right, it can't be luck. The new case has to be like the old ones in the respects that mattered, and your judgement has to be keyed to those respects.

(3) But "the respects that matter" just is a general principle — something of the form: any case with features F is to be judged thus-and-so. If nothing general connects the old cases to the new one, then the old ones gave you no guidance at all and you were guessing and happened to be right.

(4) So anyone who has the ability in (1) must have picked up general principles. Maybe ones they can't state, but principles all the same, because that's the difference between having learned something and having just been drilled.

(5) Morality is no different in structure from craft. Someone who reliably judges that an act was cruel, or disloyal, or a betrayal, in situations she's never met before, has picked up general principles about cruelty, disloyalty, betrayal.

(6) So my original view is false. There are moral principles, at least tacit ones, and the idea that reasons can flip valence from case to case with nothing general holding them together is incoherent.

(7) At which point I said — and I thought I'd scored a hit, but he didn't look worried — that the exact same argument runs on craft. If (1) through (4) are right, then a line cook who knows by the sound when a sauce is about to break, a jazz bassist, me editing a paragraph: all of us are following tacit rules that could in principle be written down in advance, and if we can't produce them it's only because we haven't tried hard enough. Which is nuts. I've spent twenty years trying to write down what makes a scene work and what I actually have is a folder of examples and a shrug.

(8) He says (7) is just the price of denying (6): if I want to say craft is real knowledge without rules, I have to say the same about morality, and then I owe everybody an account of how anyone learns it, or argues about it, or gets corrected, or improves.

Every step from (1) to (4) looks like a truism to me and (5) looks fine, and yet (6) and (7) together are something I can't swallow. I keep going back and forth between thinking (3) is a bit of a scam and thinking (5) is where the sleight of hand is, and I can't settle it. If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1) and (7) than (6). So: which step do you give up, why did it look like a truism when it isn't, and what does giving it up actually cost me — does it commit me to saying I can never really explain to a student why her scene fails, or that two people disagreeing about something moral have nothing to reason with? I want to know what I'm paying, not just which line to cross out.
