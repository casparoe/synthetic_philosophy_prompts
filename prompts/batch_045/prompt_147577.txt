I host a podcast on the philosophy of technology, and next week's guest is Dr. Marisol Vega, whose new book *Who Has to Notice* argues that Langdon Winner's 1980 essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" asks the right question but looks for politics in the wrong place. Her summary of the target: Winner's two canonical senses — the artifact designed or arranged so as to settle a question in advance (the famously low overpasses), and the technology that requires, or is only strongly compatible with, one kind of social order (his centralised-energy case) — are both *design-time* politics, legible at the moment of construction and traceable to the purposes of whoever commissioned the thing. She proposes a third, *use-time* sense: an artifact has politics when it quietly allocates the labour of attention — who must anticipate, monitor, notice deviations, keep the thing working, and be the one who is asked why they didn't see it coming — and when that allocation then disappears from view by being experienced as a personal virtue of whoever carries it (conscientiousness, competence, care) rather than as a distribution that someone chose. Her paradigm case is the operator in a partly automated system who absorbs the blame for failures she had little real power to prevent, the arrangement that has been called a moral crumple zone. She is a good talker, and she has two reliable escape routes: when a case study is contested she says, "That's an objection to the example, not to the mechanism," and when pressed on whether she is a determinist she says the artifact is only strongly compatible with a distribution of attention, not productive of it.

Please draft me eight to ten questions, each answerable in three or four minutes and none of them generic — no questions about how she became interested in the topic, no requests for reading recommendations, and no question that requires her to cite or name another author. Across the set I want the following, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

One question on the standard constructivist reply — that the politics belongs to the social relations which produced and sustain the object rather than to the object — with a follow-up that meets her expected answer, which is that constructivism's story ends exactly where hers begins, once the designers have gone home.

One question on empirical fragility. Historians of technology have challenged the details of Winner's parkway example, and I want to know whether her use-time politics inherits that vulnerability; phrase it so that "objection to the example, not to the mechanism" cannot simply absorb it.

A pair of questions pressing on whether the allocation of attention is distinctively *political* rather than merely a division of labour or an unpriced cost. Her likely answer is that anticipatory attention is the precondition of agency and of blameworthiness, so allocating it is allocating agency itself; the follow-up should ask why that makes this a politics *of artifacts* rather than a chapter of distributive justice, and what the artifact contributes that a contract or a duty roster would not.

A short sequence of two or three questions that forces her to make the mechanism testable: ask for a design-level counterfactual — two machines or interfaces with identical informational and causal properties but different allocations of vigilance — and then ask what she would conclude if, in every real case, the difference in allocation could be traced to an employment contract, an insurance arrangement, or an institutional rule rather than to anything in the artifact. This sequence should leave the "not the mechanism" reply progressively harder to reach for.

One question that runs the view against a mundane, non-automated case: supermarket self-checkout, which converts shoppers into unpaid scanners while simultaneously arranging for them to be treated as suspects. The follow-up asks what a non-conscriptive self-checkout would look like, and whether it would still be self-checkout — that is, whether the politics is detachable from the function.

A closing question she cannot dodge: what would count as evidence against the view. Specifically, suppose a workplace adopts a system after an explicit, informed, uncoerced negotiation about who will monitor it and who will be answerable when it fails, with that negotiation periodically revisited. If she says the artifact thereby loses its politics, ask whether she has offered anything beyond the familiar complaint about absent consent; if she says it retains its politics regardless, ask why the negotiated arrangement is not the authoritative account of what the artifact is for.
