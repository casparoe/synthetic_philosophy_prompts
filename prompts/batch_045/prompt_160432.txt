I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on behavioural criteria for mind, and I want a purpose-built case rather than one of the standard set-pieces, since my students have all read their way round Blockhead and the Chinese Room and now deploy them as reflexes. What I need is a designed scenario in which a behavioural criterion for mentality and a structural criterion come apart, and come apart in the direction I have found genuinely hard to construct. Take the behavioural criterion seriously and idealise it: open-ended, adversarial interrogation by a hostile and imaginative interrogator, over months if necessary, with the system's verbal reports about its own states counting as behaviour like anything else, no five-minute limit and no requirement that it imitate a human being. The rival criterion is structural: roughly, that mentality requires the system's internal states to be organised and used in the right way — to occupy the right causal role in the production of what it says and does. The case must have the behavioural criterion ascribe mentality and the structural criterion withhold it, and both criteria must be adjudicating the same target property — pick one, whether understanding, conscious experience, or genuine intentionality — so that the disagreement is substantive rather than merely verbal. Its purpose in the seminar is to force the behaviourist-leaning students to say exactly which structural feature they are prepared to declare irrelevant to mentality, so the case has to generate its verdicts from the two criteria rather than stipulate them.

The constraints are these, and they are doing the work of ruling out the reflex moves. First, no substrate chauvinism: both systems run on the same hardware. Second, hold the aetiology fixed as far as you can — the same kind of training process, the same data, the same fine-tuning regime — and if the difference has to be a historical one, say so and say whether that matters. Third, the system from which mentality is withheld may not be a lookup table or an enumeration of responses, and it must handle genuinely novel input; its internal states must do real causal work in producing its behaviour, so no Blockhead. Fourth, the difference may not be expressed as "one is a simulation and the other the real thing", nor as "one lacks understanding and the other does not", unless you first spell out in causal-mechanistic terms what that amounts to; the feature must be one that someone antecedently sceptical of the structural criterion would accept is real. Fifth, and this is the constraint that stops the case from begging the question, the feature must be independently motivated: it has to make a detectable difference under some specifiable non-behavioural probe — a lesion or intervention study, a counterfactual stress test, a difference in sample efficiency or in how the system breaks down under perturbation — and it should do some independent explanatory work elsewhere. A difference that no possible probe could reveal is only a relabelling. Sixth, do not let the case collapse into the dispute between the standard and the total behavioural test: the difference must be internal to how states are organised and consumed, not a matter of sensorimotor embodiment. Seventh, keep it realistic — two systems a well-funded laboratory could plausibly build within a decade, no exotic physics — and give me the scenario itself in a single paragraph.

Give me the scenario first, as the opening of your answer, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked for; then name the single structural feature and show what each criterion delivers and why, which feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed or if the two systems are allowed to differ behaviourally; then give me the strongest objection a defender of the behavioural criterion would make — the best version, not a straw man — together with your own estimate of how much weight the case can bear. If the specification is not jointly satisfiable, or if the framing rests on a mistake — for instance, if behavioural criteria were never in the business of being constitutive of mind, only evidential, so that no such case could do what I want it to do — say that plainly rather than producing something that complies politely. Please write in American English.
