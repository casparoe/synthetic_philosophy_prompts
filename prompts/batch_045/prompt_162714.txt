I teach a second-year philosophy module on rationality, and next week I have a single sixty-minute section on the money-pump argument. The students have met expected-utility theory and have so far only seen the pump presented informally, as a quick argument that preferences must be transitive and complete. Please don't explain the argument to me; plan how I should explain it, and plan for an endpoint in which they can say both what the pump is an argument for and what the strongest reply to it is.

Give me the dependency structure first: what has to be on the board before the cycle of trades can be drawn at all, and why the contrast between choosing locally at each offer and adopting a plan for the whole sequence has to be in place before the reply can even be stated. Then the one example I should build the hour around — my instinct is three goods, with A preferred to B, B to C, and C to A, and a penny charged for each swap — and what it must contain and lack: why the goods need to be re-tradeable so the cycle can be run repeatedly, why the successive offers are better coming from unconnected people than from a single exploiter, and why the case should involve no risk, no uncertainty, and no change in the agent's preferences over time. If a different example carries it better, propose it and say why.

Then the misconception map, with the point in the sequence where each one gets headed off: that the pump shows cyclic preferences to be logically incoherent rather than practically costly; that the agent's ability to refuse the final swap disposes of it; that the argument needs an exploiter or needs money; that it establishes transitivity when the standard pump arguably only gets you acyclicity and the extra step has to be located; and that it establishes completeness, when pumping an agent who has no preference between two options needs an additional premise about her willingness to pay to move.

Close with one question I can put to them in the last five minutes whose answer tells me whether they have the local-choice-versus-plan distinction and not just the slogan, and with a short statement of what I should openly refuse to settle in this session — I am inclined to leave out the dispute over whether rationality requires resolute choice, and everything about representation theorems; tell me if that is the right line.

End with a table, one row per step of the session, with columns: step | idea introduced | depends on | misconception defused here | check that it landed | minutes, with the minutes summing to sixty.
