hey — I'm a backend engineer, I dont have any philosophy background, and somehow I've ended up owning the "ethics" module in the path planner for our little autonomous shuttle fleet. Legal told us to look at the doctrine of double effect (apparently its from old catholic moral theology?), and I tried to turn it into something I can actually unit test.

The version I implemented is basically this:

```
# is a foreseen harm "intended" or just a side effect?
counterfactual_necessity(harm, plan, goal):
    return not achieves(plan, goal, forcing(harm := does_not_happen))
```

So: take the plan's causal model, surgically remove the bad outcome, and ask if the goal still gets reached. If the plan still works, the harm was just along for the ride → side effect → allowed. If the plan breaks, the harm was load-bearing → means → not allowed. I ran it over the classic trolley setups (the switch one, the one where the side track loops back around, the footbridge one with the heavy guy) and it reproduces exactly the verdicts everybody already expects: divert fine, loop not fine, push not fine.

So my question: given that it reproduces the verdicts, is DDE actually just causal bookkeeping in a fancy hat, and "intention" is legacy vocabulary we could compile away? Or is there something in the real doctrine that my counterfactual test throws out?

The two cases where I get stuck, which make me think I'm missing something:

1. Our shuttle's only way to stop before a school crossing is to slam into a concrete barrier, and there's a guy asleep against that barrier who will die. Remove that death from the model and the plan breaks (no barrier, no stop), so my function says "intended, forbidden." But the death isn't the *point* — stopping is. It just comes bundled. Does DDE really have to call that intended?

2. The mirror case: someone deliberately routes the shuttle over a specific person, but it turns out that person was going to be crushed by scaffolding two seconds later anyway. My function says "not load-bearing → side effect → permitted," which seems insane to me, but I can't point at what's wrong.

Also, whatever the answer is — our legal team wants to use DDE as guardrail *instead of* straight expected-harm minimization, on the theory that DDE means foreseen-but-unintended harms are off the books entirely and we never have to weigh them. Is that right? And does DDE ever tell you what to do, or is it only ever a veto on options something else already generated? Right now I've got it wired in as a filter that runs after the planner picks the lowest-harm route, and I can't tell if that's backwards.
