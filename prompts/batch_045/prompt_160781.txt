I'm a PhD student in computer science — my work is on interpretability and evaluation — and I sit in as a student observer on my lab's Model Deployment Review Committee, the body that must sign off before any of our own models is given tool access internally. Its standing charter says that no proposal may be justified by appeal to the deployed system's objectives, intentions, or honesty: the safety case has to go through on the assumption that the system is adversarial and at least as capable as we believe. Next week the committee rules on a proposal from a group I collaborate with, and I would much rather know how it will land before the meeting than after.

Here is the track record, reconstructed from the minutes of the last six decisions. The minutes are terse, and members have offered visibly different glosses of them in discussion, so the record underdetermines the rule they are running.

(i) Approved — a code-editing agent with no network access, every diff human-reviewed before merge. Recorded rationale: the environment bounds the damage, not the agent's goals.

(ii) Rejected — a support assistant whose case rested on ten thousand red-team prompts with no harmful completions, plus a system prompt describing the assistant's values. Recorded rationale: behaviour in distribution plus stated values is evidence about the model only if we may assume it is not optimizing against us.

(iii) Approved with conditions — an untrusted model emits candidate proofs in a formal language, a trusted kernel checks them, no human ever reads the model's raw output, and the model has no tool access. Recorded rationale: checking is cheaper than generating here, so nothing in the case depends on trusting the generator.

(iv) Rejected — an agent allowed to write and run its own unit tests for its own patches, with a human spot-checking one test in five. Recorded rationale: the grader and the graded share a training distribution, so they share blind spots, and sampling the tests does not fix that.

(v) Rejected, with an explicit alternative approved — an agent behind an allowlist where out-of-allowlist requests were auto-denied was approved; the variant in which the agent could submit a natural-language justification for an escalation, to be read by a human, was struck down. Recorded rationale left unstated; the objection in the room was that a fluent rationale is a persuasive artifact, not evidence.

(vi) Approved, narrowly and over two dissents — a pilot in which the agent was told that all its actions were logged and reviewed, while in fact about one in ten was. Recorded rationale: the case rested on the agent's belief being causally effective, not on its reports being true.

The new proposal. An internal ops agent with access to our codebase and ticketing system and no public internet. Before each tool call it emits a one-sentence statement of what it is about to do and why. A monitor — a fine-tune of the same base model, trained only on human-labelled transcripts of earlier, weaker agents, never on this agent's outputs — scores the pair of statement and call for mismatch and for harmfulness; flagged calls are held for a human who sees both the call and the statement, unflagged calls execute, and two percent of unflagged calls are audited afterwards. Most actions are reversible; one class is not, namely posting to a company-wide channel and closing tickets.

What I want from you: the two or three rules about admissible safety cases that fit all six earlier decisions, what each of them predicts here, which precedent is the closest analog, and a committed verdict — approve, approve with named conditions, or reject — with the reasons you would actually defend in the room, a confidence you are willing to stand behind, and what would move you off it. Note in particular where the rules come apart: whether the objection is the learned monitor rather than a deterministic checker, the shared base model, the agent's own text entering the monitoring path at all, or the small class of irreversible actions. Then give me the single variant of the proposal whose verdict would best separate the surviving rules from each other, and say what each possible outcome of that variant would tell me about what the committee has really been doing all along. Please argue for one answer rather than surveying the options.
