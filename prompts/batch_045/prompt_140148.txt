ok so I think I've talked myself out of caring about the far future and I can't see where it breaks.

Setup: by *strong longtermism* I mean the deontic version, not just the axiological one — not merely "most of the value that will ever exist is in the future," but "what you should do is whatever has the best expected effects on the far future." I'm signed up for the stakes part. Pick whatever number you like for how many people could live over the coming millennia if we don't blow it; it's big enough that every near-term consideration is a rounding error by comparison.

The argument:

(1) An option's expected value is the sum of its expected effects at each time, and if one term in that sum is bigger than all the others combined by many orders of magnitude, then differences in that term fix the ranking of the options. So differences in expected far-future value determine which option is best.

(2) But we have no separate far-future lever. Everything we do now reaches the far future only through a long chain of intermediate effects — which institutions exist, which technologies and norms get locked in, who ends up alive, what gets rediscovered after a collapse. The near-term effects just are the early segments of those chains.

(3) For basically any pair of options actually available to me or to a funder, we cannot forecast the sign of the net far-future difference better than chance. The chains in (2) are long and chaotic, they run mostly through variables nobody has modeled, and the sign of the net effect flips under small changes in background assumptions. (This is the cluelessness point, and it feels stronger the more seriously you take (2).)

(4) If we can't tell the sign of the far-future difference better than chance, then our uncertainty about it is symmetric, so the good and bad long-run scenarios cancel and the expected far-future difference is zero. So all available options have approximately equal expected far-future value.

(5) If the far-future terms are equal, they can't decide anything, so by (1) the ranking is fixed by the near-term terms — i.e. do whatever has the best near-term consequences. That's exactly what a near-termist would tell you to do.

(6) So strong longtermism is self-effacing: take it seriously, take our actual ignorance seriously, and it instructs you to ignore the far future when you choose. Which is the one thing it exists to deny.

Two things make me suspicious rather than converted.

First, it obviously proves too much. Run the same moves on the near term: there's a slice of any option's near-term effects I can't forecast either (who meets whom, what happens decades out), so by (4) those cancel too, and by (5) I should maximize only the effects I can already see. Iterate and no consideration at any scale ever favors anything. A doctor reasoning this way would treat whichever symptom is currently in front of her and never think about the disease.

Second, I tried to test (4) with two stipulations. Call the first *clean cluelessness*: suppose we had excellent evidence that, for any pair of options, conditional on the far-future difference being nonzero, the sign is a coin flip and the magnitude distribution is symmetric around zero. Call the second *leaned cluelessness*: exactly the same, except there's a one percent excess chance of the good sign, and the tails are heavy. Under clean cluelessness I think I have to swallow (6). Under leaned cluelessness the argument collapses, because a one percent lean on an astronomically large magnitude dwarfs everything in the near term, and longtermism comes roaring back. But nothing in (3) — which is a claim about our inability to *predict* — seems to tell me which of the two worlds I'm in, and that makes me think (3) isn't delivering what (4) needs from it.

That's as far as I get. A friend says the argument is valid and (6) is a fine, interesting result rather than a refutation, and that I should just get comfortable with longtermism being a self-effacing view. I don't like that, but I don't have an argument against it.

So: which step do you reject? Tell me why that step looked like it was in good standing, what you have to pay for rejecting it, and what the second-best place to intervene is. I'd most like to keep (1) and the astronomical stakes behind it, and I'd rather not abandon expected-value reasoning wholesale since I don't know what would replace it; past that I don't care where the damage lands. Also: does (4) even get you (5)? Even if all the options come out equal in expected far-future value, aren't there still far-future-flavored reasons left — picking the option that keeps more doors open, or that shrinks the spread of outcomes — and if so does (2) cause trouble for using near-term effects as the tiebreaker, given that they're supposed to be the channel rather than a separate item?

Commit to something. Name the step, give me the reason, price the alternatives, and say how confident you are and what would move you. If you think the argument is valid and my friend is right, say that instead and tell me what self-effacement would actually cost a longtermist in practice.
