I'm the undergrad rep on my university's Commemoration Advisory Committee — the body that decides what happens to names on buildings, plaques, endowed lectureships, that sort of thing — and I'm also trying to turn our current case into my final paper for a seminar on the ethics of memory, so I want help with the reasoning rather than just a verdict. Please don't point me at the literature or tell me who to read; our prof wants us to work from the cases ourselves, and I have to hand this in on thursday.

Here's our track record, trimmed to the seven decisions that seem to be doing the real work:

1. Removed the name of Josiah Wrenham, a nineteenth-century benefactor whose gift built the original library and who published widely circulated tracts in favour of compulsory segregation. The views were public and central to his public life. The building got a purely descriptive name, no replacement honoree.

2. Kept the name on Ellersby Hall, named for a Classics professor who signed a 1938 petition against admitting Jewish refugees. One signature in a fifty-year teaching career, and the hall is named for his teaching. We added a wall panel about the petition.

3. Removed the name of the Vandermeer donor, a man whose personal life was, by every account we could find, unremarkable, but whose company's profits came largely from a wartime industry that used conscripted labour. Our own report said the name had become a standing insult to current students from the affected region.

4. Kept the name on the Boatwright Atrium, honouring a civil-rights-era alumnus convicted of fraud in his later business career. Nobody thinks the name celebrates the fraud. No panel, no statement.

5. Removed the name of the physiologist Aubrey Prynne, whose famous discovery was later shown to rest on data taken, without consent, from patients in a colonial hospital. Here the thing the name celebrates is the thing that is tainted.

6. Removed a name where the honoree himself was basically unproblematic: an 1890s building named for a small donor, Enoch Stoke, where the minutes show he was chosen specifically to kill a proposal to name it for Elias Toussaint, a Black mathematician who had been awarded a degree here in 1912 and then had it withdrawn. The committee's stated reason was that the name commemorated the choice as much as the man.

7. Declined to remove the name on the Ashby Room, named for one of the first women to lecture in the medical school here; the room is the last physical trace of the old Women's Annex, and the name is essentially the only commemoration of her anywhere. We found letters showing she had supported a campaign to keep the profession closed to immigrants. Instead of removing it, we took the name off the door and put it into a curated display with the letters, saying that deletion would finish an erasure the university had already mostly carried out.

Now the live case. The Aldiss Teaching Laboratory, in the chemistry building, is named for Dr Miriam Aldiss, who taught here from 1948 to 1957 and was denied tenure in a decision the university formally apologised for in 1994 — the naming was part of that apology. What the name honours is her work designing the undergraduate lab curriculum and her mentoring; she's one of very few women in science here from before 1970 with anything named after her. A batch of files released this year shows that in 1953 she gave a confidential statement to a government security board about two colleagues in her department, a technician and a junior lecturer; both were dismissed, one never worked in the field again. The two were being investigated for membership in a political association that was legal at the time and now looks completely ordinary. The files also show the dean told her her own renewal depended on cooperating.

The faculty union and the two families want the name down. Some of our women-in-STEM alumni say removing it would repeat the pattern: push her out, belatedly commemorate her, then delete her. Three further wrinkles. First, the archive opened in 1991 and the 1994 naming committee had access to it, but the minutes never mention any of this, so we genuinely don't know whether they knew. Second, whether the 1994 naming was partly reparative might matter, since taking the name back would undo an apology rather than just withdraw an honour. Third, the name has grown a second life: there's an Aldiss Prize, the first-generation STEM programme meets in that room, and a donor pledge is keyed to the name. Someone has floated co-naming the lab for the two dismissed colleagues alongside her, rather than either keeping or removing.

What I'd like: what rule or rules actually fit all seven precedents, and what does each of them predict about Aldiss? My best guesses are some kind of whole-life moral ledger, some kind of entanglement test about whether the flaw is in what the name actually celebrates or in the act of honouring itself, some kind of present-tense symbolic-harm test, and something like an anti-erasure or acquired-function principle — but several of those fit the seven equally well and they don't all point the same way here. So: what will the committee do, and what should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which precedent is the closest analogue — is it Ellersby, Vandermeer, Stoke, or Ashby — and what principle do we commit ourselves to whichever way we go, if the precedents really do underdetermine it? Is co-naming a genuinely distinct fourth option or does it quietly collapse into keep-with-context or into removal? And what single further fact, or what hypothetical case, would separate the surviving rules — I keep thinking the question of whether the 1994 committee knew is load-bearing, but maybe it isn't, and I can't tell which.
