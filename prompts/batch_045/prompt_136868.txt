I want to know what my case verdicts commit me to about whether death is bad for the person who dies. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I want my own verdicts taken seriously and either codified or corrected.

- Anna, 34, killed instantly by a falling tile. She would have had fifty years containing the ordinary run of goods. Her death is a great harm to her.
- Ben, 34, killed the same way, but the years ahead of him held only a small net surplus of good over bad. His death is bad for him, but much less bad than Anna's — roughly in proportion to what he loses.
- Cara, 34, killed the same way, with a future as good as Ben's, but she had reached a settled, well-informed indifference to living longer. Her death strikes me as barely bad for her, if bad at all.
- Dan, 34, killed the same way, whose future would have been net-negative — unremitting suffering, nothing to set against it — but who wanted very much to go on living. His death is not bad for him; if anything it is good for him.
- Cara and Dan are the pair I cannot reconcile: her attitude moved the verdict down, his did not move it up.
- There was, as the Epicureans emphasise, an indefinitely long stretch before I existed in which I was not there, and I do not feel that as bad for me. I do feel my own death as bad for me. I cannot give up the asymmetry.
- Frank, 34, is shot by a mugger sixty seconds before the meteorite that would have killed him in any case. The bullet seems to me barely bad for Frank — a minute's worth — even though it is what kills him.

The principle I keep reaching for is that death is bad for someone just when, and to the degree that, it removes a future they would have had reason to want, where the reason is fixed by the value of what lies ahead rather than by what they happen to want. That handles Anna, Ben and probably Dan, but it seems bound to call Cara's death bad, and I do not know what it says about Frank, or how it copes with the fact that there is no time at which I both exist and lack the goods my death removes.

Please state that principle — or a better one that fits the same data — precisely, clause by clause, naming the case that motivates each clause; give its verdicts on two cases I have not listed, namely the painless death of someone with advanced dementia who no longer forms any future-directed desires, and a death followed two centuries later by a technologically enabled reassembly of the very same person, who then lives the life she would have had; and say honestly which of my verdicts no single principle of this shape can preserve and which one you would overturn.
