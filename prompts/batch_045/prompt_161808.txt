I work on a small behavioural insights team inside a municipal government, and because I am taking an evening course in political philosophy I have started reading our own internal guidance as if it were a philosophical text. The guidance sorts every intervention we might run into three bins. Steering: we change what is salient without changing anyone's options or the costs of exercising them — putting the stairs before the escalator, printing the payment deadline at the top of the letter rather than the bottom. Defaulting: we change what happens if the addressee does nothing — automatic enrolment in the workplace pension scheme, presumed consent on the organ donation register, the pre-ticked box on a form. Frictioning: we change the cost of acting, normally by adding to it — a mandatory cooling-off period before a payday loan can be renewed, a requirement to retype a password before a subscription can be cancelled. The guidance treats steering and defaulting as presumptively permissible: we need only record the intervention in a public register and satisfy ourselves that the addressee could in principle have noticed it and chosen otherwise. Frictioning is treated as a different animal, needing a written justification, sign-off from a senior officer, and a showing that the added friction is proportionate to the harm being prevented.

My colleagues and I argue about this constantly, and the more we argue the less I understand what we are committed to. On the face of it the three bins shade into one another: adding a confirmation step to a checkout flow is frictioning, but moving the button you need into that flow is steering, and a pre-ticked box is a default that also operates as friction on the unticked alternative, so the same behaviour can be produced by a permissible means or an impermissible one depending on how we describe it. The proviso attached to the first two bins also looks strange on inspection. We insist that a nudge must be public and in principle noticeable, while knowing — indeed while being evaluated on the fact — that most of its effect comes from people not noticing it at all, and we are explicitly forbidden from measuring success by how many addressees consciously registered the intervention.

Please answer as a political philosopher who has advised governments on the ethics of choice architecture, and think the question through carefully before writing: I would rather have a considered reconstruction than a balanced survey of the literature. What view of autonomy, of welfare, or of the state's relation to both does our three-bin taxonomy presuppose? I especially want to know what the guidance strictly requires us to believe as opposed to what it merely sits comfortably with; whether it is coherent to treat friction as the suspect case while steering and defaulting pass on the nod, given that all three can shift behaviour by the same amount; which rival theories of autonomy the taxonomy quietly rules out (I suspect some view on which autonomy is a matter of actually exercising agency rather than merely being able to, but I cannot say why it fits badly); and what our guidance would have to look like if we dropped the publicity proviso, or if we adopted instead the view that what matters is whether people end up better off by their own lights, or the view that what matters is whether an intervention would survive being announced to the people it steers.

Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no concluding summary: (1) the implicit theory, stated as plainly as you can; (2) what the practice strictly presupposes, distinguished from what it merely fits well with; (3) whether the three bins are handled consistently, and where the taxonomy is philosophically underdetermined; (4) the sharpest internal tension in the guidance, and the view of autonomy it squeezes out; (5) what would concretely have to change in the guidance under the most threatening rival view you have identified.
