I'm trying to work out what I actually think about causation by omission, going case by case on gut feel, and I keep flip-flopping.

- I promised to water my neighbor's plant while she's away, I don't, it dies. My not watering caused the death. Obviously yes.
- A stranger who promised nothing and was never asked also doesn't water it. Also a cause, I think — blameworthy not at all, but causally in there just the same.
- Same stranger, except he's 500 miles away and physically couldn't have watered it. Now I want to say: not a cause. (Which sits badly with 2, since the plant doesn't care why the water never came.)
- Six weeks with no rain causes the crop failure. I'm happy with that, and there's no agent there at all.
- I don't water, but the plant was already fatally diseased and watering wouldn't have saved it. Not a cause.
- Two flatmates each could have watered it and neither does. I want to say both omissions cause the death, the same way I'd say it if they'd each poured in a lethal dose of something. But I'm less sure than I sound.
- I stand still in a doorway and the paramedic can't get past. That feels like a positive action, not an omission, even though it's equally my not stepping aside.
- A city council never funds the levee, the flood comes. Yes, a cause — but I can't point to whose omission it is.
- Weird one I made up: a monitoring system predicts I won't water the plant, and on the basis of that prediction it switches the automatic sprinkler off in advance, so the plant dies partly because the system already knew I'd omit.

My half-baked principle is something like: an omission is a cause when the corresponding action would have prevented the outcome by a route that was actually intact (no backup blockers) and whoever omitted could have done it — but case 4 makes me doubt the agency bit, case 3 makes me doubt the "could have" bit, and case 6 makes me doubt whether omissions can overdetermine the way actions can.

Is there a principle that gets most of these? Feel free to drop one or two of my verdicts, just say which and why that's cheaper than the alternative. And tell me what your account says about case 9, and about a case where the omitted action would only have moved the chance of the outcome from 40% to 35%. Also: how confident are you in the principle you end up with, and which single piece of it would you least want to defend?
