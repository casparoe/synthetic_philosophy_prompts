I'm writing a master's dissertation on the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism, and I've reached the point where I can no longer tell whether the literature is advancing or running on the spot, so I want a diagnosis rather than another survey. The shape of the standoff as I see it: everyone agrees on the data — that "stealing is wrong" contributes the same thing in unembedded and embedded occurrences, that arguments like modus ponens over moral conditionals are valid, and that the antecedent of such a conditional is not asserted — and both camps agree that the expressivist owes some story about complex sentences. The quasi-realist wing keeps producing stories of that kind (higher-order attitudes toward attitudes, planning-states over hyperplan-type contents, commitment-based logics, and so on) and concludes that the original challenge was a technical problem with a technical solution, so nothing of philosophical substance is left for the realist to press. The critics keep replying that any such story either covertly assigns moral sentences truth-conditions and representational contents, in which case it has conceded the interesting claim, or explains only why speakers are disposed to reason in the valid pattern without explaining why the pattern is valid, which they say is a confusion of a psychological prediction with a logical one. Each side accuses the other of having quietly redefined the task, and nothing I've read changes anyone's mind.

The particular place my group got stuck is what my supervisor has started calling the tower problem, and I'd like you to engage with it directly rather than with the standard one-level case. Take a construction in which a moral predicate sits inside an attitude report that is itself the subject of a further moral predication, and then iterate: from "stealing is wrong" to "Ben's believing that stealing is wrong is not itself wrong" to "Anna's believing that Ben's believing that stealing is wrong is not wrong is not wrong", and so on without bound. The two strategies I've seen for handling embedding seem to generalise very differently here. Call one attitude-inheritance: the complex sentence expresses an attitude of the same broad kind toward the attitudes expressed by its parts, so the tower is climbed by iterating that relation. Call the other content-inheritance: the embedded occurrence contributes a content that the whole sentence then takes some further attitude toward, so the tower is climbed by keeping the content fixed and composing attitudes around it. My sense is that attitude-inheritance makes the validity of deep inferences look like a lucky structural fact about the attitude hierarchy, while content-inheritance looks more and more like a truth-conditional semantics with the word "attitude" substituted for "truth", but I can't get either worry into a form the other side will grant.

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the stalled part of this dispute is not really about whether the inferences can be validated — I think both sides can produce a procedure, and the tower only sharpens the question — but about what an answer to a challenge like Geach's is supposed to be: whether a compositional account of the states expressed by complex sentences discharges the explanatory burden, or whether the burden is precisely to give truth-conditions and nothing weaker counts as meeting it. If that's right, some of the dispute may be verbal, about "semantics", "representation", and "explanation", and some of it may be a genuine second-order disagreement about what a theory of meaning is for. I'd like you to tell me which parts are which, using what each side actually commits itself to, and to say honestly whether the tower problem raises anything that the ordinary Frege–Geach problem doesn't. I don't want a literature survey, a reading list, or a guide to who said what when; I want the structure of the disagreement, the reasons for your diagnosis, and — given it — a concrete account of what would count as progress: a formal result, a distinction, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected. Please answer in British English.
