Bit of a weird one, but I'm a novelist with a working position I keep leaning on, and I'd rather have it stress-tested than validated.

I'm two books into a trilogy about a weaving family in the 1810s — the Tambora eruption in April 1815 and the wrecked summer of 1816 are doing a lot of the work in book three — and reviewers keep saying, approvingly or not, that I've turned the weather into a character. I've come to think that's not just fine but sometimes the honest thing to do, and that the usual warning about environmental determinism is aimed at a claim I'm not making.

My position, as precisely as I can put it: a germ, a drought or a volcano can be a genuine historical actor in a narrative without committing me to determinism, because actorhood for my purposes is a three-part test. One: discrimination — its effects track a man-made pattern of exposure (it kills the people in the bad housing, or the ones already weakened by enclosure), so the cause picks out something humans arranged rather than landing on everyone equally. Two: load-bearingness — hold the cause fixed and the human decisions stop explaining the outcome; swap it for an ordinary year and the same decisions produce something quite different. Three: answerability — somebody in a position to act could plausibly have foreseen it and didn't, or did and decided against. And the determinism worry, I think, is a thesis about what's sufficient across a range of cases, not a thesis about whether a cause mattered in this one. I can say the frost mattered enormously without saying it was sufficient, or inevitable, or that the humans were puppets.

What I want is what would count against that. Not the general case against environmental determinism, which I've read, but defeaters for my version, ranked from fatal to merely awkward.

Which of my three conditions is really doing the work, and can they come apart? My instinct is that the first two pull against each other: a plague that kills fairly indiscriminately across a whole region fails discrimination but is hugely load-bearing, whilst a hard frost that only kills the landless poor passes discrimination and might be pretty marginal to anything else that happens. Give me the case that splits them cleanest, tell me which condition I'd have to drop, and what I'd lose by dropping it.

What would someone who genuinely worries about determinism concede counts against me, as opposed to what they'd wave away as novelists mucking about with causality? I want the version of the objection I'd actually have to take seriously, not the strawman.

Whether I've insulated myself. I can always say of any awkward example that it was a cause but not an actor. Is the three-part test doing real work, or is it a rubber stamp I apply after I've already decided? And if it is a rubber stamp — be honest — does that matter for what I'm using it for, given that my claim is about what a narrative owes its reader rather than about how historians should allocate causal weight?

And what does my position predict that the rival view doesn't (the rival being: keep non-human causes off the stage, or at least out of the scenes)? I think it predicts something about which scenes I'm obliged to write — whether the volcano gets a chapter set inside a Javanese village, whether I owe the reader a scene of someone in a position to foresee the famine choosing not to — and about whether blame in a novel should track causation or something else. Tell me if any of that is a real prediction or if both views can swallow it equally.

No footnotes or reading lists, I can't be arsed with citations and I'll go and find my own. What would move me most is a concrete alternative novel I could imagine writing — same setting, same events — that my view can't describe and the rival handles better.
