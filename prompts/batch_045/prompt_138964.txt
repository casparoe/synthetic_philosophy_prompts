I drew the care ethics chapter, and I highlit a passage and then immediately unhighlighted it, because I honestly can't tell how much of it to believe. It went roughly like this (I'm paraphrasing from memory and I've lent my copy out, so don't hold me to the wording):

"Care ethics starts from Carol Gilligan's research in the early 1980s, which found that women reason about moral problems in a different voice from men — men reach for abstract rules about rights and justice, women attend to relationships and responsibilities. The theory takes that descriptive finding and turns it into a normative one: what matters morally is caring for particular others and sustaining the relationships we're actually in, not applying impartial principles. Since care is a matter of character rather than a rule, care ethics is best classified as a branch of virtue ethics — Aristotelianism with the household in place of the polis. It follows that the theory has no room for principles, rights, or rules: the right action is whichever one maintains and repairs the relationship in front of you, judged case by case. And that is also its fatal weakness. Care only gets a grip where a relationship already exists, so past a fairly short range — call it the proximity cliff — the theory has nothing whatever to say, and it cannot even triage when two people you care about need incompatible things, because ranking needs would require exactly the impartial, commensurating standard it rejects. So care ethics is a useful lens on families, friendships, and nursing, and basically silent about anyone we will never meet, which is why the Kantians and utilitarians mostly carried on as they were."
