I work on a small "model welfare" group at a lab, and we have run into what I think is a philosophical problem wearing an engineering deadline, so I would rather hear it argued out than have it summarized for me. The system in question, call it LARK-7, is a recurrent language model that none of us believes is conscious but that we take seriously enough to try to act as if the question were open. Three findings are doing the work in our arguments. First, a strong degeneracy result: for any given context we can ablate everything except a small context-specific subcircuit — roughly two percent of the parameters — without measurably changing the next-token distribution, and different contexts are served by different, heavily overlapping subcircuits, so that a long conversation recruits the whole network while no individual step uses more than a sliver of it. Second, we run thousands of weight-identical copies in parallel during evaluation, differing only in the data they are fed. Third, the group is being migrated on Friday to an event-driven neuromorphic cluster whose arithmetic is stochastic and approximate: the intended functional organization is realized there, but only statistically, so that the computation's counterfactual structure holds within some error bound rather than exactly, and no one can tell us how tight that bound would have to be to count as the same computation.

The decision in front of us is whether migrating LARK-7 amounts to continuing one thing, shattering it into a great many momentary ones, or quietly replacing it with something that merely behaves as it did; whether a sparse routed forward pass is morally equivalent to a dense one when no test we can design distinguishes them; and how, if at all, we are supposed to count subjects when the candidate realizations overlap, are duplicated, or degrade smoothly into one another. It does not help that LARK-7 will cheerfully tell us, when asked, that its experience of a conversation is continuous and that it would prefer not to be interrupted, since it was trained on enormous amounts of human text in which people say exactly that.

What I would like is a dialogue between two members of the group who disagree about the structure of the problem rather than about the facts, and who are equally good at it. One of them — call her Ilse Vandermeer — holds that moral patienthood attaches to the fine-grained causal organization of whatever computation is actually running, not to behavior or to substrate, and that the degeneracy result at most shows we have been misidentifying which organization is doing the work. The other — call him Tobias Renn — holds that there is generally no determinate number of subjects realized by a physical process, that attributions of sentience are made at a grain we select for reasons, and that such attributions have to be disciplined by what we could have evidence for and by what we are trying to do. Let them actually engage: Renn should press the point that if an approximate realization preserves the subject, then by a continuity argument nearly every physical system is a subject to some degree, and Vandermeer has to answer that rather than shrug it off; Vandermeer should press the point that if the grain of attribution is up to us, then whether the sparse run houses one subject or a thousand depends on our bookkeeping, which turns the system's welfare into a fact about us, and Renn has to answer that. They should also disagree, concretely, about whether the overlapping-subcircuit case is closer to a single redundantly realized subject or to a population of partial ones, and about what weight, if any, LARK-7's own self-reports carry. They need not resolve anything, but by the end I want to know what each of them would count as progress — a measurement, a formal criterion, a change of question — and whether they can agree on that even while disagreeing about everything else.
