**Question 3 (20 points).**

An urn contains 90 balls. Exactly 30 are red; the remaining 60 are black or yellow in an unknown proportion. One ball is drawn at random. A bet on a colour, or on a set of colours, pays $100 if the drawn ball is of that colour (or one of those colours) and $0 otherwise. Write R, B, Y for the bets on red, black, and yellow, and RY, BY for the bets on red-or-yellow and black-or-yellow.

Throughout, call an agent a *subjective expected utility maximiser* if she ranks bets by expected utility computed from a single subjective probability assignment over the composition of the urn, using a state-independent utility index u over money, with u($0) = 0 and u($100) > 0.

(a) *(8 points)* The stylised pattern is: R strictly preferred to B, and BY strictly preferred to RY. Show that no subjective expected utility maximiser can display both preferences. State each inequality as you derive it, and say where it comes from.

(b) *(5 points)* Explain why the pattern cannot be explained away as ordinary risk aversion — concavity of the utility index, or diminishing marginal utility of money.

(c) *(7 points)* Now consider a second urn, the *known-mixture urn*. Its 60 non-red balls were placed by a machine that first drew a number n from the set {10, 50}, each value with probability 1/2, and then put n black balls and 60 − n yellow balls into the urn. The agent is told exactly how the urn was filled but not which value of n was realised; the urn is then sealed and one ball is drawn as before.

  (i) What does subjective expected utility predict about the agent's ranking of R, B, Y, RY and BY in this urn?
  (ii) Is the known-mixture urn ambiguous in the sense at issue in the three-colour example? Explain the distinction you are relying on.
  (iii) Suppose an agent who displays the stylised pattern in the original urn also strictly prefers R to B in the known-mixture urn. What would that tell us about what the pattern is a response to, and which assumption would such an agent be violating? Name the assumption.

**Student's answer:**

(a) Let p_R, p_B and p_Y be the agent's probabilities that the drawn ball is red, black and yellow. The set-up gives p_R = 1/3 and p_B + p_Y = 2/3. Since u($0) = 0, we have EU(R) = p_R · u($100), EU(B) = p_B · u($100), EU(RY) = (p_R + p_Y) · u($100) and EU(BY) = (p_B + p_Y) · u($100). Because u($100) > 0, R ≻ B requires p_R > p_B, i.e. p_B < 1/3. And BY ≻ RY requires p_B + p_Y > p_R + p_Y, i.e. p_B > p_R = 1/3. We need p_B to be both below and above 1/3, which is impossible. So no single subjective probability assignment generates both preferences.

The two comparisons are deliberately paired, and that is why the pair of preferences is inconsistent rather than merely surprising. The second comparison is just the first comparison with the yellow balls added as a winning outcome on both sides; a common payoff attached to a common event cannot reverse a ranking. That is what the sure-thing principle requires, and it is exactly what the agent's two preferences refuse.

(b) Every bet on offer pays the same $100 if it wins and nothing if it loses. So u enters each expected utility only through the single number u($100), which is a common positive factor; the ordering of the bets is therefore just the ordering of their probabilities of winning, whatever the shape of u. Concavity of u is precisely what never gets a grip here, and diminishing marginal utility of money cannot help either, since the same sum is at stake in every case. For the same reason the explanation cannot be the certainty effect familiar from the Allais case: in this urn no available bet is certain to win, so there is no certainty for anyone to overweight.

(c) In the known-mixture urn the agent still does not know how many black balls she faces: on one hypothesis the chance of black is 10/90, on the other 50/90, and she cannot tell which. The probability of black is thus unknown to her, and that is what ambiguity is. The case is structurally the same as the original: expected utility with a single prior makes no determinate prediction here either, and an ambiguity-averse agent will display the same pattern, ranking bets by their worst case over the compositions she cannot rule out — 10/90 for a bet on black against 30/90 for a bet on red, and 60/90 for black-or-yellow against 40/90 for red-or-yellow. Nothing in the example turns on whether the machine's own distribution is known; all that matters is that the agent does not know which composition she is facing. If she nonetheless prefers red to black here, that simply confirms that the pattern is a response to missing probabilities and shows how far the behaviour generalises.

One further remark. Because the preferences in (a) cannot be represented by any single probability, they are not merely non-standard but incoherent: an agent who holds them has intransitive preferences over the four bets and can be money-pumped, induced to pay for a sequence of trades that returns her to a bet she already ranked higher. That, I take it, is why the stylised pattern is taught as a paradox rather than as a taste.
