I run a short course on decision-making for policy civil servants, and the last session of the year is on escalation of commitment. This time I ran the dollar auction live in the room: a £1 coin, bids climbing in ten-pence steps, and the rule that both the highest and the second-highest bidder hand over their final bid although only the highest takes the coin. It went to £4.60 with £4.50 underneath, so the room paid me £9.10 for a pound — and the detail nobody had anticipated is that the runner-up lost more than the winner. The group afterwards split almost exactly in half: one half said the two bidders had simply been irrational, the other half that each individual bid was the reasonable thing for whoever made it, since as long as the extra you must add in order to take the lead is less than the value of the prize, bidding once more beats walking away and paying your current bid for nothing. I want to close the course with a poem that honours the second half's case without making the first half look foolish.

The form I have in mind is a pantoum — quatrains rhyming ABAB in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, and in which the closing line returns to the opening one — because it seems to me to have the exact shape of the auction: you do not choose your first and third lines, they are handed to you by the other bidder, and you must build a fresh stanza around them before surrendering two of your own lines to be inherited in turn; and when the poem ends by repeating where it began, we are back at the opening bid, only much poorer. Please give me five or six stanzas in the voice of the runner-up, the one who pays and receives nothing, letting the inherited lines do the work of the other bidder's rising bids, and let the poem's turn be genuine rather than a restatement of the setup.

There is one further thought I would like the poem to carry if it fits. Somebody proposed the obvious safeguard — announce a hard ceiling in advance, say no bid above £5 — and it looks to me as though the ceiling cures nothing: run the same reasoning backwards from a known limit and the bidding still climbs almost to the top, so the ceiling merely tells you in advance how deep the hole will be, and worse, anyone who could credibly commit to going to the ceiling would take the coin for ten pence, which turns a safeguard into a threat. Please render that, if at all, as the moment the speaker realises the way out being offered is another move in the same game. Write in British English and keep the money in pounds; avoid the jargon we used in the seminar — no "sunk cost fallacy", no "escalation of commitment", no "expected value" — so that the poem has to earn its ideas instead of labelling them. It should be accurate enough in its reasoning and its arithmetic that a decision theorist would not wince, and still read as a poem to someone who has never heard of the game. Since I will be reading it aloud, give me the poem only, with no preface, notes, or afterword.
