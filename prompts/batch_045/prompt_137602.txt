I teach an MA seminar on truth and epistemic modality, and I'm writing this year's take-home final (48 hours, 1,200 word cap, 25 marks; they've had eight weeks of it so they can be pushed). Last year I set a straight question on Fitch's result and got eleven near-identical papers that reproduced teh derivation and concluded "so much for verificationism." I want something where the derivation is the starting point, not the answer — I want to see whether they can tell which bit of the machinery is actually load-bearing once you start moving the principle around.

Here's the set-up I'd hand out (tidy it, improve it, change the notation if you like):

Operators: K for "it is known" (for the first two parts, don't worry about whose), L for possibility. Rules: (A) Kp → p; (B) K(p ∧ q) → (Kp ∧ Kq); (C) p → LKp; (D) if ¬p is provable outright, then ¬Lp. The derivation: suppose K(p ∧ ¬Kp); by (B) get Kp and K¬Kp; (A) on the second gives ¬Kp, contradicting Kp; so ¬K(p ∧ ¬Kp); by (D), ¬LK(p ∧ ¬Kp); then (C) with p := (p ∧ ¬Kp) forces ¬(p ∧ ¬Kp), i.e. p → Kp.

Then two principles I've made up so there's nowhere to crib from:

(SK) Situated Knowability: for every truth p there is an accessible situation in which p is known, where both "known" and the "¬K" in any ignorance claim are evaluated at that same situation.

(EK) Estranged Knowability: for every proposition p true at the actual situation there is some possible situation — it may contain agents who don't actually exist, with evidence and cognitive powers we don't have — and some agent in it who knows p there. The anti-realist who goes this route adds that her principle only has to cover the truth p itself, and that any residual "and nobody knows it" clause is to be read as anchored to the actual situation: the relevant instance is p ∧ ¬K@p, where K@ is knowledge at the actual situation by actual agents.

And a hard case to place: N = "no cognitive agents ever exist," supposing for the sake of argument that it's true. It contains no knowledge operator. It is (I take it) unknowable, since any situation in which someone knows it is a situation with a knower, hence one in which it's false.

The question, three compulsory parts:

(a) Run the derivation against EK instead of SK. Say exactly which step fails and why. If your answer is that nothing fails, say what has to be true of the anchoring for that to work.

(b) Someone says the way out is obvious: keep the knowability thesis but restrict it to propositions that don't contain K — or, more charitably, to empirical propositions — since the troublemaking instance p ∧ ¬Kp is precisely a proposition about knowledge. Use N to test that. Does the restriction help? What would a restriction have to look like in order to help?

(c) So what should the moderate anti-realist say instead? Whatever you propose, say what it costs her — in particular whether it still gives her whatever she wanted from the knowability thesis in the first place, and whether your fix is a principled distinction or a "whatever it takes to stop the proof" move.

Deliverables: the question sheet as I'd actually hand it out; a model answer of about 800 words; and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points at 5 marks each (total 25), with a one-line note on each about how candidates typically blow it. I'd like one of the five points to be about the difference between narrowing the modality (making L nomological, or evidential, or feasible-for-us) and restricting which propositions the thesis quantifies over, because in my experience that's exactly where the good students separate from the ones who've memorised the labels.

Things I care about: no names in the answer key — no "so-and-so restricts to Cartesian propositions," no "Williamson says it's an embarassment" — I want the moves, not the literature, and half of them will name-drop to pad. Where the literature genuinely hasn't settled something, say so in the model answer instead of pretending there's a clean line; I'd rather award a mark for a well-argued "this is where I think it bottoms out" than for a fake consensus. And please flag anywhere my two principles are ill-defined or where I've smuggled in an assumption — I suspect EK has one, something to do with how the domain of agents is allowed to vary across situations, but I want to see whether you find the same one. If a strong student could reasonably refuse to answer on the grounds that my set-up is broken, I need to know that before I send it out.
