I am writing a paper on whether the thesis that species taxa are concrete individuals rather than classes can be reconciled with the explanatory role that species-level generalizations play in organismal biology. A referee's argument — reconstructed as faithfully as I can below — concludes that it cannot, and I have come to think the argument proves too much. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where it fails and of what the repair costs.

The argument:

(1) Species taxa are the units of evolution: it is species that originate in speciation, that carry macroevolutionary trends, and that go extinct.

(2) Whatever originates in time, persists through change of its constituents, and goes extinct must be a concrete particular. A class or set neither comes into being with its members nor perishes with them, and it cannot literally be the object of selection.

(3) Hence a species is an individual: a bounded spatiotemporal particular whose organisms are parts of it, not members of it.

(4) If organisms are parts of a species rather than members of it, then what places a given organism inside a given species is not its instantiating some qualitative profile; part–whole relations are settled by causal–historical connectedness, not by intrinsic properties.

(5) So for no species S is there any nontrivial property — or conjunction, or cluster of properties — that all and only the organisms making up S actually share. There is no qualitative "what it is to be an S" to be found in the organisms themselves.

(6) A generalization whose subject is a species — "the Arctic tern migrates between polar regions," "the platypus lays eggs," "E. coli metabolizes lactose by way of the lac operon" — is a genuine kind-generalization, the sort that explains its instances and licenses induction to unexamined ones, only if those instances have some common explanatory basis. Where the instances share nothing beyond a label, the generalization is an accidental summary and supports no projection.

(7) Therefore, if (3) is true, the generalizations of comparative anatomy, physiology and behaviour that constitute most of organismal biology are not genuine kind-generalizations. They explain nothing and entitle us to expect nothing of the next observed member of the species.

(8) But they plainly do explain, and they plainly do entitle us. Organismal biology is not a catalogue of coincidences.

(9) Therefore species are not individuals, and (1) or (2) must be abandoned.

Two things make me suspicious.

First, the reasoning seems to run past its own conclusion. Consider any paradigm individual with parts: an organism, a language, a legal system, a cell line, a lineage of copied manuscripts. By (4), an organism's cells are its parts rather than its members; by parity of (5) there is no property shared by all and only the cells of a given organism; by (6) it follows that "human hepatocytes synthesize albumin" is not a genuine generalization and licenses no induction. The same would hold for every claim about the parts of every individual. That cannot be right, and I read it as evidence that the fault lies somewhere in the middle of the chain rather than at (3).

Second, I have tried to escape by distinguishing two ways species-level induction might work. On projection by resemblance, we extrapolate to unexamined organisms because they are similar to examined ones; on projection by transmission, we extrapolate because unexamined organisms descend from examined ones and inherit a common developmental programme from them. If only the first requires a shared qualitative profile, then (6) is true of it and false of the second, and the argument stalls at (7). But I cannot tell whether this distinction does real work or merely redescribes the difficulty: if transmission supplies the common explanatory basis that (6) demands, then (6) is satisfied by any causally unified collection whatsoever, and I am no longer sure it was ever a substantive constraint on anything.

I would much rather keep (1), (2), (3) and (8) than accept (9); abandoning the individuals thesis would cost me more of the paper than I want to lose. So, concretely:

(i) Which step do you reject? Please name it precisely and explain what makes it look innocent — I have reread (4) through (6) many times and each still seems to me to follow from what precedes it. If you think no single step is invalid and the trouble is instead that some premise is false, say which one.

(ii) What does rejecting it cost? Does the repair leave me with a notion of a genuine generalization too weak to do the work I need in (8)? Does it force a re-reading of (1) or (2) — and if I have to give up (2), what becomes of the argument for (3) in the first place? Does it commit me to claims about shared properties that hard cases such as hybrid speciation or extensive interspecific gene flow make awkward?

(iii) Is the resemblance/transmission distinction the standard way out, or is it a distraction that I should drop?
