I sit on the eleven-member governing board of the Association of American Novelists, and the drafting of our decision memo on the Kiln Head petition has fallen to me. Before I write anything, I want an outside document I can argue with, and I'd like you to produce it. You are a political philosopher who has also spent years on arts-funding panels and prize juries: you know the liberal and communitarian arguments at the level of real practice, not just as positions in a survey, and you know how a board like mine actually behaves. Take a page first to brainstorm the candidate principles you could build a recommendation on — including at least one that would make my board wince — and discard them in a line each; then write the memo. Don't give me a tour of the liberalism-versus-communitarianism debate. The board will not read it.

The case. One of our members published a novel, *The Drownings*, eighteen months ago. It is set in an identifiable former mining village of about nine hundred people, Kiln Head, in a county I'll leave unnamed. She made two visits totalling eleven days, recorded conversations in pubs without saying she was a novelist, photographed the cemetery, and built her plot around a real unsolved disappearance of a teenage girl in 1974. She changed the names but not the surname of the family, and the book's movement is toward the village having closed ranks around one of its own. The novel won an award, and it has drawn true-crime tourism: a café now runs a walking route, podcasters have doorstepped the dead girl's brother, and a shopfront has been bricked. Some residents say the book broke a sixty-year silence; a local history society says it gets the pit closure badly wrong; two of the girl's cousins have said publicly that they are glad someone finally wrote it down.

The petition, from the town council, asks for three things: a censure; a guaranteed short response, written by a delegate the village chooses, carried unedited in any future edition and any licensed adaptation at no cost to the village; and an amendment to our code making that standard practice for fiction set in identifiable living communities. The author refuses all three. Her position is that the village is a setting and not a claimant, that communities as such have no beliefs and hold no rights, that a rule requiring her to publish someone else's prose inside her book converts her novel into a public forum, and that any resident who is genuinely identifiable has remedies elsewhere.

Three motions are on the table. A: dismiss the petition, on the ground that the Association has no standing over what members imagine. B: censure the author for failing to engage the place she used, and refer the code question to a committee. C: decline the censure, adopt the reply mechanism for this case and as a standing rule, and pay for it ourselves.

The distinction I've been circling, and which I can't get precise, is this. Sometimes a depiction merely represents a community; sometimes, for a small and weakly mediated place, it becomes part of the material out of which its members understand themselves and out of which outsiders treat them — there is no local paper left, no local publisher, and the novels and podcasts are most of what exists. Call the trigger thin mediation. If a depiction is in that sense constitutive rather than merely damaging, then the stake looks less like injury and more like contested authorship, and the remedy that fits is a say over the paratext rather than either damages or a veto. My own interest is not neutral: I've published two novels set in the valley I grew up in and never asked anyone's permission, and I don't want a rule that would have made me. Two further things bear on consistency. Our code already obliges members to credit and pay sources in nonfiction. And two years ago we declined, without much discussion, to act on a complaint from a police benevolent association about a novel's depiction of officers, on the stated ground that an occupational body has institutional channels for answering back.

Write me the memo I can circulate: roughly a thousand words, addressed to the board, recommending one of the three motions or a fourth I haven't thought of, with the operative principle drafted as one or two sentences of amendable language the board could actually vote on, and with the strongest version of the motion you are rejecting stated as strongly as you can state it. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasons; no preamble, no restatement of what I've asked you. Assume American law roughly as it stands and do not rest anything on legal claims you can't verify — if a point would need counsel, flag it as such rather than asserting it. Close with a short section on confidence: where your recommendation turns on a substantive moral claim and where it merely cashes out a stipulation, and how much you'd move if the board rejected your framing.

And one thing I'd rather have than politeness. If the task as I've posed it is confused, say so plainly rather than producing a competent memo around it. I take seriously the possibility that 'the community' is not the sort of thing that can hold a stake at all, that my constitutive-depiction idea collapses into either ordinary reputational harm or a cultural-property claim wearing better clothes, that the reply mechanism is a veto with the serial numbers filed off, or that a professional association of novelists is simply the wrong body to be doing any of this. If one of those is what you think, tell me, and tell me what the board should do instead.
