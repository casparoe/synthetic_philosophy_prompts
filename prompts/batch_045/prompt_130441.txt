I write literary fiction, and in three weeks I am giving a ninety-minute masterclass at a writers' centre to a room of working novelists. They are intelligent adults with no formal logic and no probability theory, and almost all of them currently think of disagreement between characters as a function of temperament, or of who happens to have more facts, or of how much the author needs the scene to last. My aim for the session is that they leave able to open a draft of their own, find a scene in which two competent characters fail to persuade one another, and say exactly why that failure is earned rather than manufactured — and, when it is manufactured, what would have to be true of the characters for it to be earned.

The idea I want to build the session around is Aumann's agreement theorem: roughly, that agents who start from a common prior and whose current posteriors are common knowledge between them cannot persist in disagreeing. I am drawn to it because it is a theorem about the structure of a conversation rather than about the content of one, which is exactly what a novelist has control over. But it is also fragile to teach: stated too early it sounds either obvious or false, and my writers will reach for it as a rule about how people behave when it is really a constraint on what a scene can coherently contain.

What I want from you is the plan for the explanation, not the explanation. Give me: the order in which the components must arrive and what depends on what — the shared prior, the posterior, the difference between both parties knowing something and its being common knowledge between them, the way each round in which a character declines to move is itself a piece of evidence the other must absorb, and the fact that the result is about beliefs and not about preferences, so that characters who agree completely about the facts can still argue forever about what to do. Tell me where in the sequence each of these belongs and why moving it earlier or later would break something.

Then give me the one example that has to carry the whole thing. I would rather it be a scene between two people than an urn of balls, since my writers will only half-attend to anything abstract — but tell me what such a scene must contain and, more importantly, what it must lack: no hidden evidence, no factual asymmetry, no stubbornness or self-deception or bad faith in either character, at least until we are ready to let those in deliberately.

Give me also the misconceptions, in the order they will actually form in the room, and the exact point at which each should be headed off. The ones I anticipate are: that the theorem recommends splitting the difference or meeting in the middle; that it assumes the characters have the same evidence rather than the same prior; that convergence should happen within a single exchange when in fact it takes iterated rounds; and that it says something about how real people behave. Name any I have missed.

Two further constraints. First, I want the session to end with what I call the leak test: for any disagreement the writers want to keep, they should be able to point to the specific condition that fails — a prior not shared, a posterior not common knowledge, an inference too costly for a character to perform, a value difference masquerading as a factual one — and if they cannot name the leak, the scene is contrived. Tell me at what point in the sequence the leak test can be introduced without turning the theorem into a checklist of excuses, and what check for understanding I should use at the end to tell whether they have the structure rather than the slogan. Second, tell me what to refuse to explain at this level and say openly that I am refusing it.

Commit to one plan: one order, one carrying example, one placement for each misconception. Where you think my framing is wrong — including my insistence on the leak test — say so in a single sentence each time and then give me the plan as you would have it, with your reasons.
