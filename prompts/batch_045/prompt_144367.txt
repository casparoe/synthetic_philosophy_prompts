Quick context so you know where I'm coming from: I write novels — three out, I teach the occasional workshop, and I took exactly one philosphy class in college and dropped it. I want to actually understand George Berkeley's idealism, and every time I try to read about it I get lost in vocabulary that isn't mine. So do me a favor: translate it into the vocabulary I work in every day, and then tell me where the translation breaks down.

The version I think I'm meant to be translating, so you can see where I'm starting from: things like tables and trees are collections of ideas; ideas exist only in minds; so for a thing to be is to be perceived. The famous move is the master argument — try to think of a tree existing with nobody perceiving it and you can't get it done, because the moment you try, the tree is in your mind, so you've failed to think the thing you were trying to think. God is in the system because without a perceiver who never blinks, the world winks out every time you leave the room. And minds themselves aren't ideas: we don't get an idea of a mind, we get something else, some kind of notion. Correct me where I've got that wrong, but do the correcting in my terms.

My terms: scene and summary, point of view, focalization, free indirect style, the implied author, voice, backstory, the world bible (everything I know about these people that never made it onto a page), published text versus draft, and what readers decide happened between chapters (they definately do this at Q&As).

Four things I want you to get to:

1. Summary looks like a counterexample to him. In summary I assert events nobody in the book perceives — no character is in the room, and the narrator isn't a witness either, it's just a voice. Whose mind is holding that tree up? If the answer is the reader's, then does my novel blink out of existence at 3am when nobody's reading it? Does the implied author get drafted in to do God's job, and is that a cheat or the same move Berkeley made?

2. The sense-versus-imagination business. He separates ideas we take in from ideas we make up, and says the made-up ones are the faint ones. But the master argument is an argument about conceiving, and conceiving is what I'd call storyboarding a scene in my head — not the scene actually being on the page. So is the master argument quietly trading on that distinction? Does my on-the-page/in-your-head split map onto his so cleanly that it rescues the argument, or does it expose a slide he's making?

3. Free indirect style. When I write a whole chapter inside a character's head, every sentence is made of that character's impressions, but the character herself is never one of the things depicted. Is that his notion of a spirit, finally cashed out in something I can point at? Or is it a grammatical trick that lets me fake a mind out of ideas — in which case craft has the same hole in it that his system does.

4. What won't translate at all: drafts and cut material. I genuinely don't know what my protagonist had for breakfast if it never came up; there's no fact of the matter sitting in a drawer somewhere. Does that make my world more idealist than his — unwritten means genuinely indeterminate — or does it ruin the analogy, because he has an actual perceiver and I've only got an empty chair?

Then give me the residue report: what carried over clean, what you had to fudge to make it fit, what has no expression in craft vocabulary at all, and whether that residue is my framework being thin or Berkeley being less clear than he sounds. And then the part I actually care about: I'm stuck on a chapter set in a house after everyone has left it, and I can't decide whether to narrate it or cut it. Tell me what I'd be doing in each case, and what a novel can do here that the argument can't. I think there's a workshop essay in this for the spring, so be honest about where the reading won't hold.
