I serve as the student representative — I'm in eleventh grade — on the committee drafting my school's new statement on civil discourse, and I have to vote on the current draft next week. Most of the committee are teachers and two parents.

The draft contains a clause I'll paraphrase rather than quote, since I'm working from memory of the meeting: it says that moral positions are matters of preference rather than of fact, that they are therefore relevantly like tastes — the example one teacher used was preferring chocolate ice cream to vanilla — and that because nobody's taste can be wrong, nothing a student says on a moral question can be more or less reasonable than anything else anyone else says. This clause is doing real work in the document. It is the stated reason the school will decline to adjudicate moral disputes, will give every position equal classroom time, and will settle disagreements about school policy by polling the student body rather than by argument.

What I can't work out is how much of that work the ice-cream comparison is actually doing. Two things bother me. First, some things we call tastes plainly do admit reasons, expertise, disagreement and improvement over time — film criticism, or comedy — so the force of the comparison seems to depend on which features of taste one helps oneself to. Second, a friend on the committee who has read a little metaethics says the clause isn't a consequence of moral anti-realism at all, and that plenty of people who deny that there are moral facts still think moral questions have answers that argument can reach. I'd like that checked rather than assumed, because if she's right then the clause is resting on the analogy alone.

Please audit the comparison as it is being used here, in these sections:

1. Correspondences (about 150 words): set out explicitly what maps onto what — the person with the taste, the taste itself, the statement that the thing is good, the disagreement between two people, the impossibility of being mistaken — and flag any element of the moral case that has no counterpart on the taste side.
2. Where the mapping holds (about 150 words).
3. Disanalogies that matter for this clause (about 350 words): which of the features imported from taste are doing the work in each of the three consequences above, and would each consequence survive if that feature were dropped.
4. Disanalogies I can ignore (about 150 words): differences that are genuine but don't bear on the use being made of the comparison here.
5. Verdict on the clause (about 250 words): does the comparison actually support it? Is the clause better supported by moral realism or by anti-realism, or by neither? If by neither, say so plainly and explain what is really motivating it.
6. A better comparison (about 150 words): if one is available, give it; if the honest answer is that no comparison is doing the work, say that instead.

Please keep the whole thing under roughly 1,200 words. I don't want a survey of the realism debate or a list of things to read — I want to know whether the analogy is carrying the clause or merely illustrating something the committee had already decided.
