hey — I'm in lower sixth and I've been trying to write down what I actually think about cancel culture / deplatforming, partly for my EPQ and partly because I've agreed to speak at the politics society thing next week, and my mate reckons my views don't fit together. I'd like a proper check before I stand up and say them out loud. Roughly where I've landed:

1. Social sanction — petitions to get someone sacked, being dropped from a line-up, pile-ons, platforms booting people — is punishment. Not "consequences", not just other people exercising their own free speech: actual punishment, deliberate and aimed at someone for something they did.
2. So it has to follow the normal rules punishment follows. Mainly proportionality — the size of the hit has to fit what they actually did — and some minimal process, i.e. they get told what they're accused of and get to say something.
3. But I don't want the law anywhere near it. No regulator, no new speech offences, and I think a publisher or platform should be free to drop whoever they like, including for dumb reasons.
4. The point of punishment is to say something — censure, a community marking a line — not to deter or reform anybody.
5. Two cases. Case A: a historian gets uninvited from a museum lecture series after backing a view that basically everyone now finds grim — I'm fine with that. Case B: a lad in my year got his uni offer pulled over a group chat message he sent when he was fifteen — that was wildly disproportionate and wrong.
6. The bit I made up, which my supervisor called "interesting": I don't think the severity of this stuff should be measured by how much audience someone loses full stop, but by whether they can get an audience back. Call it the Audience Replacement Test. Dropped from one panel but twelve others would still have you? Small punishment, whatever the headlines say. Blacklisted everywhere with no route back? That's something like civil death, and hardly anything deserves it.
7. And the thing I keep coming back to: usually the people doing the sanctioning aren't the people who were wronged. Someone says something now, gets punished in thirty years by people who weren't there, on behalf of a norm that shifted. I call it the inheritance problem, and I don't think you can punish like that — except (4) says punishment is the community speaking, so maybe a later community is allowed to speak? Idk, that one might just collapse into ordinary changing-your-mind stuff.

My mate says (3) contradicts (1) and (2), and separately that (4) contradicts (2). I think he's wrong about at least one of those but can't say why.

So: does any of this hang together? I want the real tensions ranked worst first, and for each, which bit has to go and what that costs me. Don't waste words on the ones that only look like tensions — just flag them and say why they're fine. And if (6) can't actually deliver the answers I want in (5), say so plainly. Under 450 words, short headed sections rather than one big blob, cheers.
