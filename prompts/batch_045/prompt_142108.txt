I have been reading around the paradox of tragedy, and I think my confusion is narrower than the standard statement of it, so I would rather have an explanation of the narrow thing than a survey of the positions.

Set the puzzle up in its strict form. The loose form asks why we seek out and value artworks that make us feel bad, and that version is fairly cheap to answer: the distress is real, but it is offset or followed by some separable positive payoff, whether that be release, the satisfaction of closure, the pleasure of craft, or moral reassurance. The strict form rejects this move. It insists that what needs explaining is that the painful response itself contributes to the value of the experience, that the work is better because it moves us to grief or pity and not merely although it does. Call this the in-virtue-of constraint.

Once that constraint is in force, I want to understand where the positive element can be located, because I think there are three candidate sites and I cannot tell whether they are genuinely distinct.

The first is the first-order response itself: perhaps, under the conditions of art, the response is not wholly negative to begin with, because it is quarantined or held at aesthetic distance, so that what we feel is a shadow or analogue of grief rather than grief, and the analogue is partly pleasant in its own right. The second is a second-order response whose object is the first-order response: what we enjoy is not the fate of the character but our own capacity to be moved by it, our own pity, fear, or tenderness. The third is neither the object nor the response but the relation between them: what pleases is the sense that the sorrow is fitting, or earned, or that one has got the work right, so that the positive valence attaches to a kind of correctness rather than to anything's being pleasant.

Please explain how these three differ in what they actually claim, and then test them against three cases.

First, wordless instrumental music. There is no state of affairs represented and no one to pity, so it is unclear what the first-order negative response is even directed at, and unclear what a second-order response would be responding to. Does the paradox for music turn out to be the same puzzle at all, or does it collapse into the separate question of how music can express emotion?

Second, repetition. The hundredth hearing of a piece one knows completely seems, if anything, to deepen the response rather than flatten it, even though there is no residual uncertainty and no new information. That looks awkward both for accounts that tie the payoff to suspense or novelty and for any simple hedonic bookkeeping.

Third, a case I want you to take as stipulated. Suppose a device could induce, without any artwork, an emotional episode subjectively indiscriminable from the one the adagio produces, with no content taken in and nothing that the emotion is about. Nearly everyone I put this to says it is not what they want, that being moved by the music and being put into that state are not interchangeable. If the second option above is right, why not? And what does that refusal tell us about where the value really sits?

So: which of the three sites survives these cases, and is the third genuinely a third option or only a redescription of one of the others? If it is genuine, does accepting it dissolve the paradox rather than solve it? And finally, which of the three, if correct, would generalise beyond art to funerals, memorials and ordinary grief, where the quarantine the first option relies on is unavailable?
