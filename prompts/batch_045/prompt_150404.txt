Need a thought experiment built to order, on Aumann's agreement theorem.

What it's for: I'm putting together a short module for a hospital tumor board on second opinions. The point I want the case to make is that two equally competent, equally honest colleagues can look at the same patient, trade their credences as plain numbers, and rationally stay apart — that persistent disagreement isn't by itself evidence that somebody is biased, lazy, or bad at bayes. So build me that case.

Specs:

- Realistic setting, two people who are genuine epistemic peers: same training, same published base rates, same access to the literature. One paragraph of scenario max. No demons, no ideal reasoners, no exotic setup.
- They have a genuinely common prior — same priors over the hypotheses and over how reliable the relevant tests and observations are.
- Each updates only by conditioning on their own private observations. Both are careful bayesians about that: no motivated reasoning, no forgetting, no gamesmanship, and each knows all of this about the other.
- They exchange their actual credences for one single event, truthfully, and each reliably recieves the other's number — and knows they did, and knows that they know, and so on. Don't let the disagreement ride on a lost email or a dropped phone call.
- And yet: they keep disagreeing. Show their numbers across several rounds and show me they don't collapse onto a common value.

Anti-cheating clause: if both posteriors really were common knowledge, a common prior would force them equal — that combination isn't merely unlikely, it's impossible. So your case has to put its finger on exactly where common knowledge breaks down, without making either doctor irrational or dishonest.

Then give me, in this order: your bottom line in the first sentence (which condition of the theorem fails and why it's that one rather than a boring one); the walkthrough; the verdicts under (a) the one-shot common-knowledge formulation and (b) the iterated-exchange version where convergence is supposed to be forced by repeated announcements instead — that one needs more than a common prior, so say what; which single structural feature of the case is doing the work; what happens to the numbers if you remove that feature; what someone who thinks rational disagreement is basically impossible would say to defuse your example; and, if it fits, whether the case causes trouble for a conciliationist about peer disagreement too. No preamble — open straight into the scenario, and keep the whole thing under a page.
