ok so im a junior and this year my teacher let me do an independent thing in philosophy instead of the normal unit, and I've gotten kind of stuck / obsessed with one thing and I'd really like someone to help me figure out what kind of problem it even is.

Here's the setup. Fair lottery, 1000 tickets, exactly one winner. It seems rational to accept that ticket 1 will lose (it's 99.9% likely), and rational to accept that ticket 2 will lose, and so on for all 1000. But if you're allowed to lump all those together, you get "no ticket will win", which contradicts "exactly one ticket will win", which you also know. Kyburg published that in 1961 in a book called Probability and the Logic of Rational Belief. Then a few years later David Makinson had the preface paradox, in a 1965 paper in Analysis: an author who checked every single claim in her book carefully has reason to think each one is true, but she also knows from experience that a book that size almost always has at least one error somewhere, so she has reason to believe something in it is false. Same shape: each item is individually acceptable, the whole bunch isn't.

What confuses me is how little this seems to have moved. From what I can gather there are like three basic moves and everyone just picks one: (1) the probability people say there is no seperate thing called acceptance at all, there are just degrees of confidence, so there's nothing to conjoin and no contradiction to derive; (2) the Kyburg-ish people say keep acceptance but block aggregation — you can rationally accept each ticket-loses without it being rational to accept the big conjunction; (3) the logic people weaken all the principles a bit, paraconsistent logics, non-monotonic logics, various ways of letting you hold inconsistent acceptances without everything exploding. And that's basically still where it is, sixty years later. Nobody seems to convince anybody.

My theory, which I have no idea whether real philosophers say, is that part of why it's frozen is that belief is being asked to do three different jobs at once and no single thing can do all three: being the state you act on when the stakes are ordinary, being the thing you can flat-out say out loud without hedging, and being the thing you're allowed to help yourself to as a premise in more reasoning. So the two sides aren't really disagreeing about the lottery, they're each picking the job that they think defines the word. I also suspect the two paradoxes arent actually the same argument — in the lottery your evidence is statistical and about one draw, in the preface your reason for expecting an error is a base rate about people like you, and each camp quietly uses whichever case makes their own fix look obvious.

So: is this a substantive disagreement or is it verbal, about what "believe" or "accept" should mean? Is it a fight about which intuitions count as data — like, is "I don't believe my ticket will lose" really a report about belief, or just about what I'd be willing to assert without hedging? Is there some hidden question, formal or maybe even empirical about how people actually use these words, that's doing all the real work? Are the lottery and the preface even the same problem, or has treating them as one thing been the mistake? And whatever you think the diagnosis is, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, some formal result, actually going and testing something, or nothing, because the question is just badly posed? I'm not looking for a summary of who argued what, I want to understand the structure of the disagreement and why it won't budge.
