hey — I'm a science-and-ideas reporter working on a feature about self-binding: why people, firms and states voluntarily give up options they'd rather keep. The philosophers' version of it keeps surfacing in my reporting, so I want to pressure-test one particular line on it before I write. The case: a driver who will only pick you up if he can already tell that you're the sort who'll pay once you're safely in town — so the only way to get to town is to be someone who'll pay at the point where paying does you no further good. I've become half-convinced by the view that what this shows is that rationality can require you to be irrational: what's rational is to have the disposition, while the act it produces is, at the moment of acting, irrational. So here's the setup: you play me. That is, argue my position as well as it can possibly be argued — not a strawman, willing to concede small points, but you don't fold on the main claim, and you push back when my objections are lazy. Rules: stay in it until I say stop; a paragraph or two per reply, plain English, no citations and no named theorists (if you need a theory, describe it rather than naming it); it's off the record, I'm not quoting you. If at any point you decide my position is just wrong, say so plainly and then tell me what my position should have been instead.

Two things I'll keep dragging us back to, so you know the terrain. First, my real case rather than the desert one: a source who'll only hand over documents if I promise not to name them, and then, after the piece runs, naming them would definately get me a better follow-up and a friendlier editor, and almost nobody outside the newsroom would ever find out. I don't do it. Nobody I respect does it. I don't want you to explain this with 'reputation' — I want the case where the leak is genuinely untraceable. Second, a wrinkle I invented and can't stop thinking about: suppose a cheap, reliable and *selective* commitment device exists — you can switch it on just for the promises you want to be bound by, and it's invisible to everyone else. Once that's on the table, my view seems to collapse into: use the device when you've got one, and when you haven't, become the kind of person who pays. Which makes the interesting half of it sound less like a claim about what to do and more like advice about who to be.

My opening, then: I can't choose my dispositions. I can choose what I do, and I can sometimes rig what I'll do later, but 'be the kind of person who keeps promises nobody can check' isn't something I can just decide at will — and where I can shape it at all, it happens through a long indirect process of habit and association that doesn't obviously answer to reasons at all. So a theory whose central recommendation is 'have this disposition' is a theory telling me to do something I can't do, and telling me to do it by a route that isn't responsive to the very reasons it says I have. Why isn't that fatal?
