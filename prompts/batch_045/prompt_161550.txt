I sit on the allocations committee of a foundation that funds two very different things: programs for people who already exist, and work aimed at lowering the chance that humanity goes extinct this century. Every time we argue about the second bucket we talk past each other, and I've decided that the real disagreement is about how to value populations rather than about probabilities. So I've written down what I actually believe. I'd like an audit before I take it back to the committee.

Here is the set:

(1) The sum of well-being is not the thing to maximize once the number of people is itself up for grabs. A world of 120,000 lives that are only barely worth living is not better than a world of 1,000 excellent lives, and I don't accept arguments of the form "each step is harmless, therefore the endpoint is fine."

(2) Quality dominates quantity, and not merely at the margin: there is no number of barely-worth-living lives, however enormous, that compensates for the loss of a single excellent life among a disjoint population.

(3) Adding people whose lives are clearly worth living, when nobody already there is affected, cannot make an outcome worse. Whether it makes it better I want to leave open.

(4) Distribution matters independently of the sum: for a fixed population and a fixed total I prefer the more even spread, and I weight gains to worse-off people more heavily.

(5) There is a strong reason not to bring someone into existence whose life would be bad for them, but no matching reason to bring someone into existence whose life would be good for them. Creating a happy person is permissible but not an improvement; not creating one is not a loss.

(6) And yet: a world of a billion flourishing people is better than an empty world and better than a world of a thousand flourishing people, and our going extinct this century would be a catastrophe rather than a matter of indifference. I can feel (5) and (6) rubbing against each other and I don't know which to bend.

(7) No difference between outcomes without a difference to someone: an outcome is not worse than another unless it is worse for someone, and not better unless it is better for someone.

(8) Two case verdicts I'm confident about. First: a woman who can either conceive now and have a child with a serious impairment whose life would still be worth living, or wait three months and conceive a different child who would be healthy, has a decisive reason to wait — and this holds even though conceiving now makes no child worse off, and even though neither child would have existed on the other option. Second: it is a genuine loss when a flourishing person dies at forty, which is part of why I fund the interventions I do; but there was no loss in my not having a third child.

Methodologically I'm also committed to: (9) within a fixed population, rank outcomes by the unequally weighted sum of well-being; and (10) under uncertainty, if one policy is at least as good for every person who might exist and better for at least one, take it — even if, once the uncertainty resolves, nobody turns out to be better off than they would have been.

The concrete case I'll call the Terrace. Lifetime well-being on a scale where 0 is neutral and 100 is the best human life; populations are disjoint:
— A: 1,000 people at 80.
— B: 21,000 people at 32.
— C: 120,000 people at 2.
— D: 1,000 people at 80 plus 20,000 people at 30.
— E: 1,000 people at 80 plus 120,000 people at 2.
My verdicts: D is not worse than A; B is better than D; A is better than B; C is worse than both A and B; E is worse than A. What actually worries me is the staircase version: a hundred policies, each adding 1,200 people at slightly lower well-being than the last while nudging everyone already alive up by 0.3, where every individual step looks like D over A and the endpoint looks like C.

What I want from you: which of these commitments really collide, ordered by how severe the collision is; for each one, exactly what has to give and what giving it up costs me elsewhere on the list; which apparent collisions dissolve on inspection and why; whether (7) can be read in a way that survives (5) and (8) or whether it simply has to go; the single revision that repairs the most; and finally, what ranking of A through E the repaired package delivers, including its verdict on B versus A and on the staircase. I'm not asking for a survey of positions or for citations. Audit the list, and be blunt — I would rather surrender one item than keep a view that only produces verdicts case by case.
