My seminar on anatta and personal identity has me half convinced and half annoyed. We did the standard reductionist line — no further fact of identity over and above the causally connected stream of psychophysical aggregates, the person is a designation laid over the stream rather than something the stream contains — and I basically buy it. But a freind in the class who thinks the whole Buddhist picture is unstable handed me this argument after section and I can't locate the bad step, even though I'm sure the conclusion is bunk.

(1) Anatta: there is no enduring self, and there is no further fact about personal identity beyond the causally connected stream of aggregates. (2) "Person" is a useful designation imposed on that stream; where one stream ends and another begins is drawn rather than found, so there is no fact of the matter how many persons a given human life contains. (3) If there's no further fact, then no way of drawing the boundaries is correct or incorrect in itself: the coarse convention (one person per human life) and the fine-grained one (a new person whenever psychological continuity drops below whatever threshold you like) are both just conventions. (4) Conventions get adopted for purposes, and different questions can legitimately be answered using different ones — "person" in criminal law, in medicine and in ethics needn't coincide, and we already live with that. (5) So a competent agent can do what he calls convention shopping: use the coarse convention when there's credit, a legacy or an inheritance to claim ("I wrote those novels"), and the fine-grained one when blame, a debt or a prison sentence is on the table ("that stage is gone, this is somebody else, no continuity worth the name"). (6) Nothing in (1)–(4) marks convention shopping as a mistake: by hypothesis it doesn't get anything wrong, and it's useful to the shopper. (7) But convention shopping is obviously bogus — it's special pleading, the man who wants both the royalties and the exemption is up to something, and every one of us would say so. (8) So (1)–(4) can't all be true: either anatta is false and identity is a real further fact after all, or conventionalism about where persons begin and end is false and some ways of cutting the stream are just wrong.

He also had a second version of the same worry about counting: if you go fine-grained, the number of persons who have ever lived is some huge multiple of the number of human bodies, so every population-ethics or aggregate-welfare calculation is hostage to a stipulation that nobody could be wrong about — which he says is on its own a reductio of (2). I'd rather give up almost anything than give up (1), and I defintely don't want to end up saying that identity is a brute further fact, since that feels like the ātman sneaking in through the side door — but I'd also rather not have to say that the demented eighty-year-old and the twenty-year-old who made the promise are "really" the same person in some deep sense. So: give me a committed answer. Which single step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you — what do you have to accept instead, and what new problem does that acceptance create? Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence (name the premise you're dropping and the cost), then give me the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate my argument back at me. And be straight about your confidence: if you think two of the steps are each independently defensible and the real issue is which price to pay, say that — but still pick one and defend it rather than handing me a menu.
