I'm writing an essay for a philosophy of mind module and I want to spend most of it reading Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through higher-order theories of consciousness — the idea that what makes a mental state conscious is that the subject is, in some way, aware of being in it, whether via a higher-order thought or via something more like an inner sense.

The version I actually want to try out on the novel is one I've been toying with myself, so you won't find it in the literature. Call it the classified-affect view: an emotional state is consciously felt only if the subject is in a higher-order state that represents it under some concept, and for the thick emotions the concept has to be a thick one — you have to be aware of yourself as grieving, or as having been humiliated, or as in love, not just as being in some generic stirred-up condition. That gives you two ways to fail. You can lack the concept altogether, or you can possess it and withhold it from your own case, deflecting or re-describing what you're in. My question is whether Stevens is definately a case of the second, and whether that's supposed to mean his grief and his love aren't conscious at all.

Scenes I want you to work with: the night his father dies upstairs while he keeps serving the gentlemen downstairs; the moment Miss Kenton catches him reading what he pretends is a book about household management; the occasions where she more or less tells him how she feels and he lets it slide; and the end of the book, on the pier at Weymouth, talking to the stranger about evening being the best part of the day.

Two wrinkles I want you to take seriously. First, the whole thing is narrated years later by an older Stevens who hedges and corrects himself constantly — so does the narration supply higher-order thoughts after the fact, and can a higher-order thought make a state conscious decades after it happened, or does that just show the theory needs a diachronic clause it doesn't have? Second, Miss Kenton spends the novel reading his emotions for him; the novel makes you want to say her awareness does some of the work his own won't, which every standard version of the view says is impossible.

Give me a committed reading: what the theory predicts about Stevens, whether that prediction is a point for the theory or against it, and where you'd say the novel adds something the argument alone can't. Also tell me where my classified-affect version is doing real work and where it's just gerrymandered to fit. I'd rather you took a position and defended it than listed the options, and flag the places where you're going out on a limb about either the novel or the theory. Oh, and please write in British English.
