I am preparing a short presentation on psychiatric classification for a mixed audience of clinicians and philosophers, and I want to be sure I have Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects right. A chapter in a recent popular book on the history of diagnosis summarizes the view like this:

"Psychiatric categories are unlike the categories of the natural sciences, because the people being classified react to the classification. Tell someone they are depressed and they begin to behave in ways that fit the label, so the label makes itself true: that is the looping effect, and it is essentially the old point from labelling theory that a diagnosis is a self-fulfilling prophecy. Hacking's conclusion is that psychiatric kinds are therefore not real — they are social constructions, produced by our classificatory practices rather than discovered in nature, and there is no fixed nature that research could ever pin down. This is why the diagnostic manuals are revised every few years: the kinds themselves keep shifting under the pressure of being classified. It also puts psychiatric research in permanent crisis, since a category can never be validated: by the time you have studied it, the people it described have moved on."

The chapter then draws a practical conclusion: because of all this we should stop regarding diagnostic categories as discoveries about how things are and treat them simply as instruments we adopt for our purposes, and it presents this as something that follows from Hacking's argument.

I am suspicious of at least part of this. The claim that the kinds are not real sounds cruder than I remember Hacking being, and I have also seen him described as some sort of nominalist, which makes me unsure whether the chapter has his view backwards. But I cannot tell which sentences are doing the damage, and I would rather not overcorrect by assuming the whole thing is wrong.

Could you audit the summary for me?

1. Please go through it and separate what a careful reader of Hacking would accept as fair, what is a defensible simplification for a popular audience, and what is straightforwardly wrong. Where a sentence goes wrong, I would like to know what distinction it is collapsing — for example, whether the looping effect is being run together with a different phenomenon it should be kept apart from, whether a claim about our classifications is being read as a claim about what exists, whether one of several possible directions of causal influence is being presented as the only one, or whether a technical term is being used in its ordinary sense.

2. Tell me whether the practical conclusion about treating categories as mere instruments follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate, and what someone working in Hacking's framework would actually allow to follow from the looping effect.

3. Help me with a case that does not seem to fit the chapter's description at all. Suppose a category exists only as a proposal — say a draft diagnosis of compulsive digital engagement in adolescents — and that the draft criteria circulate publicly: they are reported in the press, taken up by schools and parents, and used by adolescents themselves to describe and regulate their own habits. The behaviour that clinicians later count as symptomatic has been shaped by awareness of a classification that has not officially been applied to anyone. Is this a looping effect, or does it fall outside the account because the classification is not yet in force, because the people who changed have not themselves been classified, or because what moves them is a public description rather than a diagnosis? Does the account require that the classification actually be applied to the people classified, that they know of it, or that their reaction be a reaction to the classification as such rather than to something merely correlated with it? If it is not looping, say what it is instead and how much of Hacking's machinery it borrows.

4. Finally, a practical question. If I wanted to argue that some actual change in a diagnostic category over recent decades was due to looping rather than to revised committee judgments, professional or commercial interests, improved case-finding, or genuine change in the underlying phenomenon, what sort of evidence would distinguish the looping explanation from those alternatives? I would like to know what a looping explanation would have to show that the others would not, and whether the chapter's version of the view would lead me to expect evidence of a kind I should not expect.
