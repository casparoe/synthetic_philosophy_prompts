hi — I've built an argument for a seminar and now I don't trust it, because it goes through far too easily and the conclusion is one I don't believe. I need someone to find the joint.

Context in case you don't know the setup. Three people on a university panel have to decide whether to renew a teaching fellow's contract. The rule they've been handed: renew if and only if both (P) her teaching evaluations are above the threshold and (Q) the teaching load was actually delivered as timetabled. Their individual views:

- A: P yes, Q no, so no renewal
- B: P no, Q yes, so no renewal
- C: P yes, Q yes, so renewal

Majority on P: yes. Majority on Q: yes. So if you aggregate on the premises, she's renewed. But two of the three think she shouldn't be, so if you aggregate on the conclusion, she isn't. This much is a known thing with a name in the literature and I'm not worried about it.

What I wanted to show is that it isn't a weird pathology of contrived agendas, it's everywhere. My argument:

(1) Both procedures are just majority rule. The only difference between them is which propositions happen to be on the agenda.
(2) Nothing in the pattern of votes above selects one agenda over the other. Aggregating on the premises and aggregating on the conclusion are equally faithful to what the three panel members actually think.
(3) So which verdict the panel reaches is settled by something over and above the members' views: namely, whoever drew up the agenda.
(4) And there's no privileged way to draw up an agenda. Any question can be split into sub-questions and any sub-question folded back into a bigger one, and a member's answer to a narrower question is no less genuinely her opinion than her answer to the broad one.
(5) So for any collective decision whatsoever, the outcome is fixed by framing rather than by the views being aggregated. I've been calling a verdict framing-proof when it survives every re-framing that leaves each member's own opinion untouched, and by (3) and (4) almost nothing is framing-proof.
(6) But if the outcome isn't fixed by the members' views, then majority rule isn't tracking anything. It's a readout of the votes, and a coin toss, or just letting the chair decide, is an equally good readout of the same votes.
(7) Which is absurd. I really don't want to be committed to saying that a jury's 10–2 verdict is no more the jury's view of the evidence than a coin toss is.

So which step do I cut? I'd rather hold on to (1), which looks like a plain description of the case, and (7), which is the bit I actually care about — but tell me if that instinct is wrong and I should be giving up one of those instead.

Three things I'd like you to press on while you're in there. First: someone in the seminar said the dilemma only shows up because the agenda is incomplete, and that if you asked each member every logical consequence of what they say, consistency would force the two procedures to agree. I don't know whether that's an answer to (4) or just a restatement of it, and if it's an answer it seems to give the absurd result that the fix is always to ask more questions, which can't be right.

Second, the version that worries me most: run the same pattern across one person over time. On Monday I believe P and not-Q, on Tuesday not-P and Q, on Wednesday P and Q. Nothing in (1) through (6) seems to care whether the three vote-sets come from three people or from me at three times, so the argument appears to show that I have no judgment about P and Q either. I'm fairly sure that's a reductio of the argument rather than a discovery about me, but I can't say what the relevant difference is supposed to be.

Third, the room is mostly political theorists, plus one public-law person and one economist, so if there's a way of cutting this that only the economist would reach for, or an objection the law person would put in terms of what courts actually do with reasoned verdicts, I want to hear that too.

What I want back: name the step, say why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives — what each candidate rejection commits me to elsewhere, including the bits about agenda-setting I wanted to build the conclusion on. Please write in British English. Don't write the paper for me, just tell me where it breaks and what it costs to repair.
