For three hours last night my oldest friend — he teaches early modern European history — and I went round and round about whether the Spanish Armada mattered, and I came away convinced that we had been disagreeing about something for which neither of us had a word. I would like your help finding that word, or, if there isn't one, making one.

Here is the thing I kept noticing. When he takes a counterfactual seriously — suppose the invasion of 1588 had put Parma's army ashore and London had fallen — his account is wonderfully specific for about a year: who would have been killed, who would have fled to Scotland, which council would have split. Then, without any announcement, the detail thins and the actual seventeenth century starts showing up again. A parliament that is awkward about taxes. An Atlantic trade. A church whose habits look strangely unreformed for a reconquered country. He is not being sloppy; he would say he is being disciplined. But the effect is that the further you get from the moment of intervention, the more the counterfactual world is built out of material imported from the world that really happened — and so the intervention is always, in the end, a local disturbance that gets absorbed.

I spent thirty years building scenario trees for a reinsurance company, and in our work the branches fan out and never rejoin. We had a rule of thumb: borrow nothing from the base case beyond about two years out, because once you do, you are not modelling anything, you are relabelling. So I kept saying: your branches go back into the trunk. He kept saying: of course they do, that is what plausibility means.

I do not think plausibility is the same thing as what I am describing. Plausibility as he uses it is a constraint on the size of the departure at the point of intervention: no comets, no one behaving wildly out of character. But the movement I noticed is downstream of the intervention and looks independent of it. You can make an enormous change at the point of departure and still tell the story so that everything is back to normal within a generation; you can make a tiny change and tell it so that nothing is ever recognisable again. He did both in one evening and did not notice. So there seem to be two knobs, not one — how big a departure you permit, and how fast the world in the story is permitted to drift away from, or is made to return to, the real one — and he only has a word for the first.

The part I really cannot settle is whether what I am describing is a claim about history or an artefact of the method. Sometimes he has a mechanism: gunpowder warfare is expensive, so whatever else happened, something like a fiscal-military state was going to appear in western Europe; a society under that kind of pressure has an equilibrium, and the story snaps back to it. That is a substantive claim about the past and I can argue with it. Other times the snapping back is pure exhaustion: he has no way to generate a genuinely different 1650, so he fills the gap with the 1650 he already knows. On the page those two are indistinguishable, and I suspect that most arguments about whether some outcome was inevitable are arguments in which one person is making the first claim and the other is accusing them of the second.

It happens small, too. When he talks about his own past — if I had not taken that job in Lyon, something very like it would have come along — the same move seems to be doing a third job: not a theory about states but a way of deciding how much of his life he takes himself to have chosen. I do not know whether that is the same phenomenon in a smaller costume or a different one that merely resembles it.

Three words came up last night and none of them did any work: inevitability, determinism, chaos. The first two say the future was fixed, which is not what I am asking about — I am asking about the shape of the stories we tell, not about the furniture of the world. The third says small differences grow, and my puzzle is mostly about the opposite movement. So: does the thing I am describing have an established name, either as a feature of historical reasoning or as a failure of it? If it sits near concepts that already exist, tell me which is closest, exactly where my thing departs from each, and which one you think it really is. If nothing fits, coin a term, define it tightly, and give me boundary cases — one clear instance and one clear non-instance. Tell me whether the mechanism version and the exhaustion version are one phenomenon or two, and if two, give me a test that separates them. And tell me whether the personal case belongs with the historical one.

Please commit. Do not lay out the options and leave me to choose: take a definite position on what this is, on which existing concept if any it falls under, and on the one-or-two question, with your reasons. I would also like you to mark plainly which parts of your answer are established usage, if any, and which are your own proposal or judgement — and if you think my description bundles together things that should be pulled apart, say so and say which distinction I should be drawing instead. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I have no way to chase them up and my friend would only argue with them. The carving is the point, not the label.
