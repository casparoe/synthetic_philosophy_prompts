I help coach a university debating society, and last term we ran a mock-trial-style debate on the motion that the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals were exercises in victors' justice. The closing speaker for the proposition finished with the line "victors' justice is no justice at all," and it landed as though it had settled the debate, even though nobody had said what it was supposed to mean. Since then it has become our stock conversation-ender: it was used to wave away the suggestion that the tribunals also produced something doctrinally new, and to wave away the further suggestion that a tribunal hand-picked by one side can still reach true verdicts about what the defendants actually did. I would like to take the sentence apart instead of continuing to trade on it.

For what it's worth, here is the sorting I have been attempting, so that you can correct it rather than start from scratch. The line might be any of: a claim about how the defendants were selected (the prosecuting powers decided who sat in the dock and were never going to select their own); a claim about the adjudicators (judges and prosecutors drawn from the winning coalitions could not have been impartial, or at least could not have been seen to be); a claim about the law applied (that crimes against peace and crimes against humanity were, insofar as they were new, retroactive, so that nothing the defendants did was a crime when they did it — though people sometimes reply that the interwar pacts renouncing war were already on the books, which would make this less about novelty than about vagueness and about whether a treaty between states can ground individual criminal liability); a claim about selective enforcement (that comparably culpable conduct by the victors' own forces went uncharged, and that this selectivity does not merely make things unfair but undermines the very norm being enforced); a claim about standing or clean hands (that a party with blood on its own hands lacks the standing to punish, or forfeits the right to); a causal-explanatory claim (that the verdicts are better explained by the victors' interests than by the evidence and the law); and an epistemic claim of the "history is written by the victors" kind (that the victors controlled the archives, the categories, and the record, so that much of what we now take to be established about these trials is partly their artifact). My worry is that these come apart badly: some look trivially true, some look straightforwardly false, and only some of them, if true, would support the conclusions the line was being used to carry.

So here is what I want. Set out the precise theses the sentence could be expressing, as a menu ordered from the thinnest to the most radical, and for each one say three things: whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or false; whether it is a normative claim about the trial's legitimacy or an epistemic claim about what we can now know; and what it would actually establish if true — that the trials should not have happened, that the convictions were wrongful, that the punishments were undeserved, that the precedent is worthless for later tribunals, or only that we should discount the tribunals' own account of themselves. Then tell me which reading or readings the debater needed in order for the line to do the work it was doing, and whether any reading that is actually defensible is strong enough for that job. Two things I would especially like tested. First, my "mirror tribunal": suppose the Axis had won and had tried Allied political and military leaders before judges drawn only from the Axis powers, under a charter drafted in identical terms, charging the area bombing of cities and the use of atomic weapons as crimes against humanity — which readings of the line convict that tribunal too, and does convicting it show anything about Nuremberg, or only that the shape of the argument is symmetrical? Second, a test I half-invented and cannot evaluate, call it the symmetrical imposition condition: a norm may be applied to a defeated party only if the party applying it would in principle have accepted its application to its own personnel — is that condition already smuggled into any of the readings above, is it plausible on its own, and does the line need it in order to get anywhere? Where my sorting is confused or my distinctions are fake, say so plainly.
