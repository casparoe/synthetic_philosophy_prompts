I write the wall texts and the audio guide at a mid-sized municipal gallery — started last September, six shows a year, about a fortnight per show to get from a loan list to print-ready, with the director signing off at the end and the learning team wanting their own versions for schools groups. I did a ten-week evening course in aesthetics last spring and got into a long argument with a mate who's writing a PhD on it: she reckons you can't come to a justified aesthetic judgement about a work you haven't experienced first-hand — she called it the aquaintance principle, I think, and said something Kant-ish backs it up — and I said that can't be right, because basically my entire job is telling people things about objects they're standing in front of but haven't actually looked at yet. I've also started noticing my own bad habits: I open with critics have called this one of the great paintings of the decade, I tell visitors they'll find this devastating, and last month someone emailed to complain that our texts do the feeling for you.

So: can you turn that debate into an actual procedure? I want something I can run per object in the twenty minutes I actually have before the director sees it, for both formats — 90 words of wall text, roughly 200 words of audio per stop. It should sort the sentences I write into kinds — ones that direct attention, ones that give non-aesthetic background like materials or what the artist was responding to, ones that report someone else's verdict, ones that predict what the visitor will feel — and then give a rule for each kind about when it's allowed, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where it stops with a no. I've been using a rough two-key rule of my own, no evaluative claim without a paired instruction to look at something the visitor can check for themselves, and I'd like you to keep it if it survives and tell me honestly if it doesn't.

Hard cases I need it to handle: a fresco destroyed in a fire that we're showing as documentation, so nobody alive can experience the thing itself; our audio description for blind and partially sighted visitors, which is testimony given to people who can't have the visual experience at all; the marketing team's poster line, if you loved the Hockney you'll love this, which is testimony about taste-matching rather than about the work; a catalogue essay by a scholar who has written about the painting for years but never seen it in the flesh; the learning team wanting a content note saying this work is disturbing; and verdicts where the critics definately disagree. Then run the whole thing on three objects — the embroidered textile piece I want to call exquisite, the destroyed fresco, and the poster line — and include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like. Mark clearly the points where it has to hand the decision back to a person because it's a values call rather than a facts call, and say what the procedure can't do at all.
