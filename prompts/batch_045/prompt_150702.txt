I am presenting a paper at a comparative historical social science workshop in three weeks, and I need a question-period preparation sheet built for the specific room I will be standing in rather than for the sympathetic version of that room.

The thesis I am defending deflates the strong path-dependence claim. The literature standardly states path dependence in a strong form: but for some small, early, contingent event — a technical standard adopted in one town, a committee vote, a battle, a colonial administration's filing choice — the outcome would not merely have been delayed or reached by a different route and with different incumbents, but would have been of a different kind, a different equilibrium with different consequences, perhaps never reached at all. My claim is that what the canonical cases actually support is what I call arrival-time path dependence: the contingent sequence fixes when the outcome arrives, by what route, and with which particular actors, firms, or places holding it, but not whether the outcome-type arrives. The strong claim is habitually asserted on evidence that could only establish the weak one, and the two have opposed practical implications — under the strong claim a reformed institution cannot be expected to re-converge on the old form, whereas under arrival-time dependence it should be expected to re-derive the same functional solution within some relevant horizon.

My argument runs in three moves. First, a taxonomy: I separate routing effects (fragility of the trajectory), arrival-time effects (same outcome-type, different date, route and holder), and lock-in proper (counterfactual irreversibility of the outcome-class), and I insist that any path-dependence claim name its estimand — the outcome under a stated grain of description — before it can be tested. Second, an evidential argument: the only evidence that discriminates lock-in from arrival-time is what I call the erasure-and-replay test, cases in which a lineage is severed or a system is isolated before the outcome, so that we can watch whether the outcome-type reappears — techniques lost and independently re-derived, solutions reached separately in systems with no contact, traditions destroyed and rebuilt from surviving conditions. My contention is that such replays, where we have them, tend to show the outcome-type recurring, and that where we lack them the strong claim is untested rather than supported; that asymmetry should move the burden of proof onto anyone asserting lock-in. Third, a concession and a limit: durable differences in welfare properties between equilibria, and durable distributional legacies — who industrialised first, who was dispossessed, whose costs financed the transition — survive my deflation intact, and I want to keep them as genuine path dependence while giving up only the claim about the outcome-class.

The room is about twelve people. Two are quantitatively trained economic historians who think a causal claim about history is credible only when it comes with an identification strategy and who will read the erasure-and-replay test as a research design, not as a metaphor. Two are historically oriented institutional economists working in the increasing-returns and critical-juncture tradition, who regard lock-in as a consequence of the formal structure of the model — non-ergodicity, multiple equilibria, small events selecting among absorbing states — rather than as an empirical hypothesis awaiting evidence, and who use the QWERTY-style anecdotes as illustrative rather than load-bearing. One is a philosopher of science who works on counterfactuals and single-case causation, and who will press on modality: what makes my outcome-classes the right grain of description, and whether a historical replay is ever a controlled replay. Two are comparative-historical political scientists and a historical sociologist whose own work is about durable legacies — colonial administration, post-communist trajectories, racialised dispossession — and who will hear any deflation of path dependence as a political claim about whether historical injustices persist or wash out. The remainder are graduate students in the same traditions.

What I want from you is a prep sheet of eight or so objections, ordered by expected damage rather than by the order in which they will be asked, each attributed to the kind of person above who would raise it and phrased the way that person would actually phrase it — in the vocabulary of identification and research design, of equilibrium selection and non-ergodicity, of counterfactual semantics and levels of description, or of durable inequality and legacy, as appropriate. For each objection give me what I should concede, what I should hold, a reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes without notes, and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Then tell me, explicitly: what I should concede in the first minute of the session before anyone attacks; the single reply that has to land if the paper is to survive the hour; the weakest joint in my own three-move argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope the room misses; and the question I am hoping nobody asks. Where a reply would be stronger with a figure, a date, or a concrete case in my pocket, name it and say frankly whether it is one I could assert from memory in front of this audience or one I should refuse to guess at. Please target the argument as I have sketched it — generic scepticism about counterfactual history, or about whether history is a science, is not useful to me, since I have no intention of defending those.
