One of the first things I internalized from years of reading rationalist blogs was the rule about sunk costs: once an expenditure is unrecoverable it drops out of the decision entirely, and any choice that stays sensitive to it is worse for that sensitivity. Escalation of commitment, throwing good money after bad, continuing a failing project because of what has already gone into it, finishing a meal you dislike because you paid for it — all treated as one failure with one diagnosis. I had come to read the rule as a claim about which facts can be reasons at all: no bare fact about what has already happened can be reason-giving; only facts about what a candidate action would bring about can be.

Then I noticed that contract law runs, systematically and unapologetically, in the opposite direction, and I would like to know what it presupposes.

As I understand the doctrines: a promisee who has spent money in reliance on a promise that is then broken can often recover that wasted expenditure outright, as an alternative to being put in the position performance would have achieved; a promise with no bargain attached becomes enforceable, or partially enforceable, once the promisee has reasonably relied on it to their detriment and it would be unjust not to hold the promisor to it; and in some land cases partial performance by the promisee is itself what lifts an otherwise unenforceable informal agreement into enforceability. In each case it is precisely the fact that the promisee has already incurred a cost that generates the claim. Someone who had been promised the same thing and had not yet relied has, generally, nothing. The law does not merely permit attention to sunk costs; it makes sunk expenditure the trigger of an entitlement.

Two details make it less crude than that: reliance recovery is normally capped so that a claimant cannot use it to escape a bargain that would have lost money anyway, and reliance can take the form of an opportunity forgone rather than money spent, so what matters seems to be worsened position rather than expenditure as such.

So: what theory of reasons, or of rationality, does this pattern presuppose? I would like the implicit theory reconstructed, and I would like the following separated out for me.

First, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Does it require genuine retrospective reasons — reasons whose force derives from what has already happened rather than from what they produce — or is it fully compatible with a purely forward-looking view on which past expenditure is only a convenient measure of damage and a way of pricing the incentive to rely? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license the same rules?

Second, I have been using a distinction of my own: call a practice cost-honoring if the bare fact that a cost has been incurred counts as a reason to continue, and cost-regarding if the fact of incurred cost operates only as evidence, as a trigger of a rule, or as a measure. Which is contract law, and does the answer differ across the three doctrines?

Third, there is a familiar move I want tested rather than assumed: shift the unit of assessment from the individual act to the policy or disposition over time, and honoring sunk costs becomes forward-lookingly rational after all, because an agent who is visibly disposed to continue is able to make commitments, invite reliance, and secure cooperation that a purely consequentialist agent cannot. If that move works, does the law presuppose anything my original reading of the sunk-cost rule actually denied, or does it show the rule was always a claim about a different unit of assessment?

Fourth, if we took the strict version seriously and rebuilt the doctrine around it — expectation-based recovery only, no claim generated by reliance as such — what would have to change? Would promissory estoppel collapse into incoherence, or would informal promising simply stop being usable, and is either of those a cost a forward-looking theorist should care about?

Finally, is there a defensible core in honoring sunk costs that the legal pattern reveals and the blog-post version flattens — the thought that some goods, such as projects, relationships, and promises kept, are partly constituted by having been costly to sustain, so that bracketing the expenditure is not neutrality but a way of destroying the thing — or is that just the fallacy in a better costume? I would like that last claim argued both ways and then adjudicated.
