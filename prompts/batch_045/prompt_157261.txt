Background: I've been turning over the idea of karma recently — not as religious doctrine, but as the claim that people undergo good and bad things because they deserve them, i.e. as a theory of moral desert. Two friends of mine got into this on a long weekend walk and I couldn't referee it. Elena grew up around the idea and is inclined to defend it; Yusuf thinks it falls apart under pressure. Both are smart non-specialists and neither was being obstinate, so I'd like the arguments taken seriously rather than dismissed.

(1) Yusuf opened by saying karma isn't a theory of desert at all but causation wearing a moral label. His reason: desert is a three-place relation — someone deserves something from someone, in virtue of something — whereas karma is a two-place relation, act followed by outcome, operating like a mechanism. On his view "you got what you deserved" in a karmic framework really just means "that outcome was caused by your earlier act," and labelling the causation desert is a category mistake.

(2) Elena answered that plenty of ordinary desert claims are two-place. People say a wrongdoer deserves to suffer, full stop, without naming anyone who owes it to him, and retributive intuitions don't seem to need a claimant or a vindicating victim. She also said the karmic link isn't impersonal in the cold mechanical sense Yusuf is implying: the outcome is tied to the agent's own will and character, which is the opposite of an indifferent mechanism.

(3) Yusuf conceded that but switched attacks: desert, he said, requires proportionality — there has to be a fit between what was done and what comes back, and karma supplies no exchange rate. How much suffering does a lie merit? Or a cruel impulse never acted on? Without a scale, "deserved" floats free and simply re-describes whatever happens. He compared it to a criminal code with no sentencing range.

(4) Elena replied that moral desert is ordinal, not cardinal: we can rank worse acts as meriting worse outcomes without being able to say how much worse, and our ordinary judgments — and even actual sentencing practice — work that way too. So the missing exchange rate isn't fatal; demanding one is asking for a precision that desert talk never had.

(5) Yusuf then made what I thought was his strongest move, about time and identity. Suppose the payoff lands in a later life. Then either the later person remembers the act, or doesn't. If not, there is nobody for whom the suffering is their punishment — it is indistinguishable from misfortune, and recognition seems to matter to punishment in a way it doesn't to mere causation. Worse, the karmic process is supposed to shape what the next person is like: temperament, circumstances, perhaps even which person comes to exist at all. But then you can't say that person would have been better off without it; there is no independently identifiable individual who got harmed or rewarded. So, he said, karma either loses the connection to the original act, in which case there's no desert, or it loses an independent recipient, in which case there's no one for the desert to attach to.

(6) Elena answered both halves. On memory: someone who commits an offence in a blackout and wakes with no recollection of it can still, she thinks, deserve punishment; what amnesia blocks is reform and self-recognition, not desert as such. On identity: the theory is free to define the bearer by the karmic link itself — the continuity across lives just is the causal and moral thread running through them — so the subject of desert is the ongoing stream rather than a pre-given individual who gets landed with something.

(7) Yusuf called that a rigged move. If the moral link is what constitutes who the subject is, then "you get what you deserve" can't fail: whatever happens is by definition your outcome, and so by definition your desert. That, he said, is just the habit of seeing someone suffer and concluding they must have earned it — a theory that cannot distinguish desert from coincidence isn't a theory of desert.

(8) Elena said the view does allow that some suffering isn't karmic — not everything that happens is payback — but Yusuf seized on that: if so, you can never tell which is which, and the theory has no predictive or explanatory content at all. Elena granted the point but turned it, suggesting karma may never have been a prediction about who suffers; it may be a claim about character. Acts shape dispositions, dispositions shape perception and choice, so outcomes express what you have become rather than being a wage paid out. On that reading the payback picture was always a teaching device.

(9) Yusuf replied that if that's all it is, the desert vocabulary should be dropped, because it's a causal psychological claim in moral costume — and a harmful costume, since it licenses blaming people for their misfortunes. But he agreed the psychological core is partly true: some vices really are self-defeating, and that's worth saying. Elena's last word was that the moralized framing earns its keep precisely where the causal one goes quiet: it answers the feeling that the world ought to be answerable to how we act, which a bare causal story leaves untouched.

My own half-formed thought, which they didn't get to, is about audience. It seems to me that desert may need someone who can hold you to it — someone to answer to — and that in a karmic scheme there is no such audience: not a victim, not a community, and if the recipient doesn't recognize the suffering as theirs, not even the recipient. That would make karma something like desert with nobody to answer to. But then I wonder whether the audience could be the agent's own earlier self, so that karma is best read as a demand that you be accountable to the person you were, which is neither retribution nor education but a kind of integrity across time. I can't tell whether that reframing clarifies the dispute or just changes the subject.

Please tell me who is right about what, move by move, where each of them is arguing past the other, what both of them are missing, and whether my "audience" idea helps or is a dead end. If you think one of them is overall more right, say so and say why.
