I've somehow agreed to give the speech at the final assembly of Marlowe Grammar — the school in the town where I grew up, which is being shut down at the end of term and merged into a bigger comprehensive in September — and it's in three weeks. The audience is about as mixed as it gets: a couple of hundred alumni, some in their eighties, who are basically there to say goodbye to a building; the current students, maybe half of whom are quietly relieved because the school's streaming system sorted them into a bottom set at eleven and never let them out; the staff, four of whom are losing their jobs; and the local historical society, which has raised money for a plaque in the entrance hall and would like the building preserved as a small museum of itself. The site is being turned into a clinic that the town definately needs. My sister, who went here and hated every minute of it, is coming specifically to hear me say something honest for once.

I've been reading Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life on the commute, in a reading group with two of the teachers who are losing their jobs, and it's made this harder rather than easier: as I understand it, each of the three ways of relating to the past he distinguishes comes with its own structural vice, and every stock move I'd normally reach for in a speech like this is one of the vices. The monumental version — the great dead alumni, the chain of exemplars, be worthy of them — he seems to think essentially falsifies by analogy, flattening the past into models and setting the dead to compete with the living. The antiquarian version — the plaque, the little museum, the school living on in our memories — is the one where everything becomes equally venerable, so nothing can be told apart from anything and you preserve a thing by killing it. And the critical version — putting the school on trial, which part of me badly wants to do, because we only just found out the original endowment came from a sugar factor's estate — is the one he says is necessary but that you can only do if you're strong enough to build something afterwards, since you are yourself a product of exactly what you're condemning. My reading group keeps making the point that you can't put a school on trial while standing in its ruins without it sounding like you're exempting yourself, and a friend of mine who does heritage work has a name for the version of this I'm most worried about: she calls it the vitrine problem, the fact that anything you successfully preserve stops being something anybody can live inside.

So: write me the six-or-seven-minute speech. I don't want a graceful generic goodbye. I want it to deliberately take up one of those three postures and make its cost visible — I want the room to feel, without being lectured at, why the obvious consoling lines ('the school lives on in each of you', 'we honour the past best by learning from its mistakes', 'the spirit of Marlowe will endure') are precisely the moves the taxonomy is built to block, and why that isn't just contrarianism for its own sake. It has to work for eighty-year-olds who've never heard of Nietzsche and will walk out if it sounds like I'm trashing their youth, and for the four teachers losing their jobs who will catch every move. It has to be a speech, not a lecture, so the idea has to do its work in what I can and can't bear to say rather than in anything I explain. And it has to end somewhere — I don't want to deconstruct the plaque and then walk off the stage. There's something in the essay about a capacity to transform and incorporate the past, plastic power I think it's called, that I haven't really got my head around; if that's the way out of the trap, use it, but I'd need it to be doing something in the speech rather than being named.
