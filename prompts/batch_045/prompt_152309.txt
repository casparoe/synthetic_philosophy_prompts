Hi — I need a dialogue for a training set I'm putting together on how people argue about what probability claims even mean. Here's the setup: two forecasters at a national geohazard agency. A submarine volcano was discovered eight months ago about 60km upwind of a big international airport, nobody has ever monitored this one before, and the immediate question is whether to write the number into the risk register that triggers the civil-aviation ash protocol. So they have to produce one sentence — roughly, "the probability that this volcano produces an ash cloud that closes the airport in the next twelve months is 0.2" — and the two of them completely agree on 0.2 and completely disagree about what they just wrote.

One of them is a frequentist: hypothetical relative frequencies in a properly specified reference class, sophisticated about the reference-class problem, and its not the naive "just repeat the experiment forever" version. The other is a subjectivist in the de Finetti tradition: betting rates, coherence, exchangeability, proper scoring rules. Neither should be a strawman — the frequentist needs an actual story about single cases, the subjectivist needs an actual story about calibration and shouldn't come off as saying "whatever I feel like."

Things I want them to chew on rather than announce:

- They both agree that if you could somehow make a hundred clones of this volcano-in-this-situation, about twenty of them would blow. The frequentist says that is all the number means; the subjectivist says that just restates the number in a mirror and explains nothing. Who has the better of that exchange?
- There's exactly one sort-of analog in the record: a broadly similar submarine volcano in a different tectonic setting that erupted in its twenty-fifth month of monitoring. In or out of the reference class? And why does that question bite harder for one of them?
- The same 0.2 has to do three jobs: price a reinsurance contract, sit above or below the evacuation trigger written into the protocol, and get graded by the agency's auditor with a Brier score at the end of the year. I suspect the frequentist is happier about the scoring rule than you'd expect and the subjectivist is less happy about the trigger than you'd expect, but you decide.
- The auditor's actual question, which is the one I care about: what makes your sentence true or false, and how would I check?

Give me exactly five numbered exchanges, each two or three turns of dialogue, no intro and no wrap-up, no narration beyond a one-line scene heading if you want one. Real back-and-forth — each of them has to answer the actual last point the other made, and in at least one exchange one of them has to give up something that costs them.
