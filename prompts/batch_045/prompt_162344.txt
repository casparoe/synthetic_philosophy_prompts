Two people whose work I follow have been sniping at each other about predictive processing for about three years, and I want the document they should have produced instead of the sniping. Call them the Unifier — the brain really is, at the computational level of description, a hierarchical generative model minimizing precision-weighted prediction error; perception is inference on the causes of sensory input; attention is precision weighting; action is the fulfilment of predictions; active inference is the right way to write perception and action in one currency — and the Redescriptionist — the free energy principle is close to a tautology for anything that manages to hang together over time, and at the level of actual circuits and task-specific models the prediction-error framing either adds nothing a plain mechanistic or RL-flavoured model wouldn't have predicted, or survives only because the generative model and the precision parameters get hand-set after the data are in.

Write the joint statement. Concretely:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. I have candidates I want dealt with, and I want you to add the ones I've missed. (a) The layer cake: FEP as a claim about any system that maintains a boundary and stays out of equilibrium; predictive coding as a circuit-level process story; predictive processing as an architectural/cognitive claim; active inference as a control scheme. Both should be able to sign that nothing proven at the top layer settles anything below it, and that scoring the view at the wrong level is where most of the last three years went. (b) The triviality point: surprise minimization is satisfiable by an enormous range of systems, so success at that level is not evidence for the cortical-process claim — the Unifier should be able to sign this without giving anything up. (c) The dark room point: both should agree that surprise minimization alone doesn't pick out an interesting agent, that the content has to come from the generative model and its prior preferences, and then say plainly whether that's a virtue of the framework or the moment the principle stops doing work. (d) Both should agree PP has to be scored against a comparably resourced alternative rather than against nothing.

2. A disagreements table, columns roughly: the issue; the wording the Unifier would sign for their own side; the wording the Redescriptionist would sign for theirs; whether the disagreement is genuine or verbal; and which crux below it hangs on. I want formulations that are neither empty ("we disagree about how much of the brain's activity is prediction error") nor slanted.

3. The cruxes, each with a case or test both sides accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. Here is the test I want used for at least one crux, so the two of them stop arguing about retrofitted explanations. Design: a stream of near-regular tones; occasional omissions at a predictable time; two blocks differing in how volatile the inter-tone interval is; reward value of the omitted event manipulated orthogonally. Three readouts: whether there is an evoked response at the expected time of the omitted tone at all; how its amplitude scales — with an estimate of recent volatility on the one hand, or with reward value on the other; and in a macaque arm with laminar electrodes, whether the response has the laminar profile of a prediction-error-like signal rather than of release from adaptation, plus what happens to it when top-down feedback is suppressed. Assume a pilot already ran the human arm and came out like this: a clear response at the right time, amplitude tracking the fitted volatility estimate, and no detectable relation to the reward manipulation. For each of the three readouts, write down what each side would have predicted before seeing anything; then say what each side makes of the pilot, whether it actually discriminates or is confounded (attention, arousal, recent violation count, a too-weak reward manipulation), and what each would count as evidence against their own view here.

4. One more crux I want in there: the overfitting/transfer question. Fit a PP model on one task, predict behaviour and neural data on a second task with no new parameters, and compare held-out performance to a task-specific model with a comparable hand-set budget. Each side should predict how this goes and say what result would move them.

5. At the end, flag which disputes you think are verbal and why. My guess is at least one hangs on what "inference" means — Bayesian updating over a learned generative model versus any state change describable as reducing surprise — and another on what "prediction" means, a distribution over hidden causes versus a merely anticipatory or entrained state, where PP and entrainment-style accounts might not be rivals at all. And say whether there is anything both would agree would actually move the debate, or whether the honest answer is no.

Don't cite or name any papers, I know the literature; and if you're unsure about some empirical detail, mark it as unsure rather than producing a result.
