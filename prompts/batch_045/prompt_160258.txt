Me and my flatmate (he's doing a PhD in maths, so he's annoyingly hard to corner in an argument) got into this after my epistemology seminar and I've been going in circles for about two days. The seminar covered the lottery paradox and the preface paradox — 1000 tickets, one winner, for each ticket you seem to know it loses, conjoin them and you get something you know is false; and the author who believes every sentence she wrote but also believes there's an error somewhere in the book. Fine, I get the setup. What I can't shake is an argument I built afterwards that seems to show any exit from it proves way too much, and I can't find which step to blame.

First the wrinkle I added, because the argument leans on it. Everyone's favourite fix for the lottery is to say you never knew each ticket loses in the first place: your evidence is a bare base rate, nothing that discriminates ticket 7 from ticket 800, and purely statistical evidence about a single event doesn't amount to knowledge. So I tried to build a version where that escape is closed off. Suppose that instead of reading the odds off the poster, I hire an inspector for each ticket separately. Each one examines her own ticket and reports back "this one loses", and each inspection is 99.9% reliable and independent of the others. Now for each ticket I've got a specific, discriminating, causal source — somebody actually looked at that ticket — not a base rate. But the paradox walks straight back in: I know the rules, so I know exactly one ticket wins, so I know exactly one of those thousand reports is wrong, I just can't tell which. For each ticket I still have excellent evidence specific to it, and I still can't put them together. Here's the argument, then:

(1) Fallibilism: you can know p even when your evidence doesn't entail p. Nothing about knowledge requires certainty.
(2) In the plain lottery, and again in my inspector version, I know of each ticket that it loses — 99.9% is past any sane threshold, and in the inspector version it comes from a real investigation of that ticket.
(3) Knowledge is closed under putting things together: if I know p and I know q, I know p-and-q. Run it 999 times and I know that ticket 1 loses and ticket 2 loses and ... and ticket 1000 loses.
(4) But I also know the rules, so I know that conjunction is false. Contradiction.
(5) So (1), (2) or (3) has to go — and (6) below is meant to show it isn't (2).
(6) The inspector version reproduces the paradox with everything the "merely statistical" story demands, so rejecting (2) doesn't save us. That leaves (3): knowledge isn't closed under conjunction.
(7) But (3) is load bearing everywhere. If known truths can't be conjoined, then my flatmate who checks a thousand-step proof step by step and is confident of each step doesn't know the theorem; a lab that runs a thousand careful experiments each individually convincing doesn't know the result; the author of the book is fine, but so is anyone who reasons at all, since transmitting knowledge across steps is basically the only thing deduction does.
(8) So either mathematicians and experimental scientists know nothing (come on), or the failure to aggregate in the lottery has to come from somewhere other than (3) — and I've argued in (6) that it can't come from (2) — which leaves (1), infallibilism, i.e. skepticism about everything. I don't want that either.

One thing I've been toying with as a way out: in the lottery I know for certain that one of the conjuncts I believe is false, whereas in the thousand-step proof I only have some vague risk that one is, and maybe that's the whole difference. But I don't find it satisfying, because the preface author only has a probabilistic reason to expect an error — books like hers usually have one — and the intuition that she shouldn't conjoin still fires pretty hard. So which step do you reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost you? I'd rather keep (1) than anything else and I'm fine with blaming (2), I just don't see how (2) survives my inspector case. If the answer is that the inspector case is subtly different from the base-rate case after all, tell me exactly where the difference is and what it predicts about the proof and the preface.
