so I've got two friends whove been arguing about the Fermi paradox for something like three years now and I want to make them actually produce something instead of doing this over drinks again. One of them thinks the silence is a genuinely strong Bayesian update: the galaxy is old enough that a single settlement wave at even modest speed should have crossed it many times over, we see no artifacts, no waste heat, no probes, so the great filter is probably ahead of us and work on existential risk is the most important thing anyone could be doing. The other thinks the silence is close to uninformative: the uncertainty on the biological factors in the Drake-style calculation is spread over many orders of magnitude, we've only surveyed a laughable fraction of the search space, and on top of that there's a selection effect — if technological species show up at random times and the loud expanding ones set the boundary of what's left, then anybody at all who appears early should expect to look around and find an empty neighborhood, which is exactly where we'd find ourselves whether or not we're doomed. (I think that last bit is basically the grabby-aliens picture but I might be mangling it badly.)

Can you write the joint statement they'd sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration? Here's what I want in it:

- a numbered section of claims both of them can sign, including about what the silence actually consists in, what present-day surveys do and don't rule out, what the crossing-time calculations assume, and that they're both setting aside the whole ufology thing and any claim about relics in our own solar system
- a disagreements table: each side restated in wording that side would accept for itself, not the other side's version of it
- the cruxes, with for each one a concrete case or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance

My guesses at the cruxes, tell me if theyre the right ones: (1) whether the observation-selection point fully screens off the update or only weakens it, and what it predicts about how early we should find ourselves; (2) whether putting wide, roughly log-uniform priors on all the early factors is licensed, or whether some of those steps are constrained enough that the expected number stays informative; (3) how much a settlement wave really costs, and whether colonizers fill everything within reach or stall out — and whether that's an empirical question or purely a modelling one.

Two things I specifically want built in, because they come up every time and I've never seen either handled properly:

- Call it the Low-Dissipation Thesis: the idea that any civilization that gets very good at computation has overwhelming reason to move toward thermodynamically efficient, low-temperature, low-waste-heat infrastructure, so that the natural end state of an advanced civilization is to be nearly invisible in exactly the bands our searches are most sensitive to. Both of them half believe this and neither has ever said clearly what it does to their own position.
- Call it the FIRMOS case. Stipulate that an all-sky far-infrared survey of around ten million galaxies in the local supercluster comes back in the 2040s with a hard upper bound on Type III style waste heat of fewer than one galaxy in a hundred thousand, with well-characterized completeness and a published catalog of the warm-dust confounders. They both agree in advance that this result bears on the disagreement. What does each predict it would find, and more importantly what would each say afterward if it came back exactly that way — would either of them actually move, and on which crux?

Keep it tight enough that neither of them can wriggle out of it, and flag any place where you think the dispute is really about words rather than about the world.
