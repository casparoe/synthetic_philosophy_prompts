I am a senior at a large public high school, and I write for our school's literary magazine. Last month I turned in a history essay on why Reconstruction ended, and it came back with an A and a comment about my command of the material. A week later my teacher stopped me in the hallway and asked, casually, why 1877 was really the endpoint when the same pressures had been building for years. I could not answer. Not because I had forgotten — it felt, standing there, as though it had never been anywhere in me to begin with. What I could do, instantly and without effort, was get it back again.

That gap is what I want the poem to be about, and I would like it as a villanelle: nineteen lines, two refrains alternating through the tercets and meeting in the final quatrain. Refrain A should be a sentence of the sort that came out of the model I worked from — balanced, confident, the kind of clause that arrives shaped like a conclusion about the subject. Refrain B should be a sentence in my own first person about standing in that hallway with nothing to say. I want the two to be close enough in length and rhythm that, as they trade places stanza by stanza, they begin to sound as if they came out of the same mouth; by the closing lines, where the form sets them side by side, a careful reader should genuinely not be able to tell which is which, and the poem should not tell them.

The thought I want it to carry, and to carry accurately, is this: I am not interested in whether I cheated, and the poem should not be about cheating, academic integrity, or whether technology is ruining anything. I am interested in where the understanding went. It is no longer in me; that much is plain. But it is not in the model either — it has no view about Reconstruction, no sense of why the claim would be true, nothing in it that could be called understanding at all. So there is a piece of knowledge that used to exist and now has no location: not in the writer, not in the tool, and not anywhere else. Please let the returning lines do that argument rather than state it — the borrowed sentence is the thing that survives and keeps coming back, and the sentence about not knowing is carried along by it, not the other way around.

Some constraints. No product or company names, no robot or soul vocabulary, nothing about the future of education or of work. Keep it in concrete physical detail — the hallway, a phone, the printed essay with the grade on it — and in a metre a reader can hear, so that the refrains land as lines rather than as slogans. Do not resolve it: no deleting anything, no moral, no last stanza that decides whether what happened was loss or relief. And it has to work as a poem on its own terms: my faculty advisor reads the magazine, knows nothing about any of this, and should simply find a good villanelle that is about something.
