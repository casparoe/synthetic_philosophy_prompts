I host a small, slightly caotic podcast on political theory — long-form interviews, about 70 minutes, audience is mostly grad students and people who read too much — and next week's guest is Prof. Elias Vandermeer, a theorist who's just published a book defending Simone Weil's case for abolishing all political parties and pushing it further than she did.

His position, as I understand it: Weil's three diagnostic marks of a party — that it is a machine for manufacturing collective passion, that it is organised to put pressure on the thought of each of its own members, and that its only real end is its own growth without limit — aren't really about elections at all. They describe a mechanism that can show up anywhere, and on his telling it shows up most purely today in engagement-maximising ranking and recommendation systems, whose survival depends on exactly that loop. His positive proposal is what he calls the "dissolving body" rule: no standing organisation may make a collective political decision; any body convened to decide something must dissolve once it has answered its single question. He's also said in print that the argument doesn't need Weil's theology — swap her notion of truth for something like epistemic reliability and it survives intact.

Draft me 8-10 questions, in the order you'd ask them, written the way people actually talk (short, no "can you unpack that for our listeners"). Skip anything biographical. I want at least one question each on:

- whether the three marks are supposed to be a definition of a party or an empirical tendency of groups that compete for power — he'll almost certainly say "a tendency, an ideal type", so give me the follow-up that asks what a non-pathological party would look like and whether one has ever existed, and press on whether "none has" makes the claim unfalsifiable;
- the pressure-on-thought mark in particular, given that juries, hiring commitees, editorial boards and scientific collaborations all shape what their members believe — his likely move is that science has reality as an outside check while politics doesn't, so hit that with the fact that politics does have outcomes (elections, failed policies, blown budgets);
- where the critique stops: Weil herself applied a milder version of it to trade unions and churches, and treated the state as an idol too, so ask whether universities, professional associations, newspapers with editorial lines and unions are all condemned, and what the stopping principle is if not;
- the feed-curator extension, including the obvious objection that a recommender system is answering demand rather than manufacturing it, and — importantly — what empirical result would make him give the extension up;
- the "dissolving body" rule as a way of actually governing: how a budget gets passed or a ten-year policy sustained by single-question assemblies, followed by the standard worry that ad hoc coalitions are just factions minus the accountability and legibility a branded party gives voters;
- Weil's distinction between parties and mere currents of opinion, and whether a movement that endorses, funds and slates candidates is really different in kind.

For each question, add a line on the answer you expect him to give and the follow-up that keyed to that answer. End with one question you think he definately won't have a good reply to, and say why it's hard for him specifically.
