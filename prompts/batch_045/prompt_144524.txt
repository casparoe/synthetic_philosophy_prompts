I'm in a small interdisciplinary reading group this term — half of us are in a conflict-mediation certificate program, the rest are philosophy and religion students — and last week we went through a chapter on classical Indian philosophy that struck me as unusually readable, so I didn't expect to come away unsure of it. The chapter is on the Jain view of many-sided truth, and our instructor wants to build a whole session on pluralism out of it, so I'd like to know how much of the chapter is actually the view before I repeat it to anyone.

Here is roughly what it says, paraphrased and in the original order. Every object, the Jains argued, is inexhaustibly complex: it has infinitely many properties and relations, and it is simultaneously coming into being, passing away, and persisting. So no single statement — "the pot is in the room" — can capture the whole of what it describes. Anekāntavāda, the doctrine of non-one-sidedness, is the name for this fact about objects. Built on top of it is syādvāda, the method of qualified assertion: every claim should be prefixed by the particle syāt, "in some respect," because the claim holds from one standpoint (naya) and fails from another. The formal version is the sevenfold scheme, which lists the available predications of any claim — it is, it is not, it is and is not, it is inexpressible, plus three further combinations of those with inexpressibility. The moral is epistemic humility: since nobody occupies a standpoint that can be shown to be better than anyone else's, no worldview can claim the whole truth, which is why Jain thinkers have been so strikingly willing to grant that other traditions are right too — an ancient version of what we would now call relativism.

Two things make me suspicious. First, the Jains I've read about elsewhere seem extremely confident about their own commitments — karma, liberation, the soul — which doesn't sit well with the idea that no statement is true outright. Second, at the end of the session our instructor said the "inexpressible" option is basically the same move as the modern position that some contradictions are true, and when I said that seemed to turn a claim about religious tolerance into a claim about logic, nobody could tell me which of us was right. I also don't know whether the summary's "true from one standpoint" means a standpoint in the sense of a person's opinion or a standpoint in the sense of a respect in which the object is being described — and that matters a lot for me, because I've been asked to run the exercise our instructor wants to base on this: each disputant states their claim, then each is asked to acknowledge the other's as "true from a standpoint," and the group moves on. I'd rather find out now whether the philosophy supports that or whether I'd be teaching something the Jains wouldn't recognize.

Could you audit the chapter for me? Please use these sections, with roughly these lengths:

1. **Bottom line** — about 40 words: broadly fair, broadly misleading, or fair with one serious problem.
2. **What the chapter gets right** — about 120 words: what a specialist would accept without wincing, including what anekāntavāda, syādvāda, and the sevenfold scheme each actually do in the view.
3. **Where it distorts the view** — about 300 words, one labelled paragraph per problem, ordered by how much damage each does.
4. **The inexpressible option and true contradictions** — about 120 words: is treating "inexpressible" as the claim that some contradictions are true a clarification or a misreading?
5. **Standpoints: opinions or respects?** — about 100 words: which reading the texts support, and what that means for the two-disputants exercise.
6. **What a defender of the view would insist on** — about 120 words.
7. **Does the conclusion survive?** — about 100 words: once the distortions are corrected, does the view still underwrite pluralism, religious toleration, and my mediation exercise, or only some weaker version of them?
