hey — I'm writting a paper for a law-and-humanities seminar about what legal categories for atrocity actually *do*, and I want to open it with a counterfactual: Raphael Lemkin — the Polish-Jewish lawyer who coined "genocide" and pushed the 1948 convention through — gets dropped into 2029 and is asked to advise on two things that didn't exist when he died in 1959. I want the answer to actually be his, built out of what he argued, not just "he'd be horrified and say yes."

The background I'm working from, which I'd like checked: he introduced the word in his 1944 book on Axis occupation; he lost 49 family members in the Holocaust; he worked with Jackson's team at Nuremberg; in the drafting he pushed for "cultural genocide" — destroying a group's language, religion, institutions — to count on its own, and lost that vote (I've seen the figure 25 to 16 with 4 abstentions, but check me). The convention that came out covers killing members, serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to destroy, preventing births, and transferring children, and it needs that special intent to destroy a group *as such*; political and social groups got dropped too. I also half-remember that in the 1930s, before he had the word, he floated two separate crimes with names like "barbarism" and "vandalism" at some international-law meeting — is that real or am I making it up?

The passage of his I have open in front of me is this: "It is intended rather to signify a coordinated plan of different actions aiming at the destruction of essential foundations of the life of national groups, with the aim of annihilating the groups themselves."

The two things I want him on:

**One.** A draft protocol that's supposedly circulating (I'm inventing it, play along) adding a standalone crime of "structural erasure": deliberately dismantling a group's schools, religious bodies, professional associations, land registries and legal personality — no killing, no expulsion — when it's done with the purpose that the group should stop existing as a distinct group within two generations. Two wrinkles: it deliberately does *not* use the word genocide, on the theory that the g-word is what sank the cultural clause in 1948 and that a fresh name can actually get ratified; and its protected-groups list is wider than the convention's four, adding groups defined by language or shared way of life. Supporters say this is Lemkin's lost clause finally restored. Critics say it converts a crime of extermination into a grievance machine that any majority can use to claim it's being erased. Would he take the renamed, widened version as a victory or a betrayal? Is cultural destruction for him the *same kind* of thing as physical destruction, or the leading edge of it, or something he always kept seperate? And does he think the name itself is doing work that a new label can't do?

**Two.** A case nobody has charged before. A state agency licenses and tunes the ranking system of a big social platform, optimised for engagement; over about eighteen months the feed reliably pushes material describing a minority as vermin; a militia with no official status then massacres a couple of thousand people from that group. No orders were given. Internal memos show officials mostly cared about engagement numbers and about "owning the information space," though a few mid-level people wrote things like "it's the only language they understand." Prosecutors want a genocide charge against the state. Where does his intent requirement bite here, when the thing doing the destroying is a system whose objective function was something else entirely? And given that he defined genocide as an attack on the foundations of a group's life rather than only on bodies, is a recommendation engine reshaping the conditions of group life the kind of mechanism he'd want the concept to reach — or would he think stretching it this far cheapens the word exactly when it's finally working?

My own hunch is that he'd hate the renamed protocol, because his whole complaint was that atrocities go unnamed, but I can also see him taking the deal to get something ratified, so argue me out of whichever version of that you think is wrong.
