I volunteer at a benefits advice centre and I also sit on a small reading group that drafts responses to consultations on welfare reform, and there's one move that comes up at literally every meeting: someone says "but National Insurance is insurance — people have paid in, it isn't charity," and uses that to argue that conditionality, sanctions and means-testing are an insult to contributors. Then last week someone from a think tank ran the same comparison the opposite way: insurers don't just hand over money, they require you to mitigate your loss, they require full disclosure of anything material (contracts of utmost good faith, apparently), and they investigate suspicious claims — so conditionality isn't a betrayal of the contributory principle, it's exactly what being insured looks like. Both sides seem completely certain the analogy is on their side, which makes me think it's carrying the whole argument and nobody has checked the mapping.

So: can you audit it? Roughly what I'm after:

- A correspondence table. What plays the role of the premium, the insured event, the claim, the insurer, the policy, the excess, the payout, the duty to mitigate. Be explicit where there is no candidate in the target. My hunch is that it comes apart on job-search effort, because unlike a fire or a theft you can't observe whether it happened, and I want to know whether that makes conditionality look like ordinary claims-screening, or like something insurance has no analogue for at all (moral supervision of the claimant?).
- Whether unemployment can be pooled in the first place. Insurance works because risks are roughly independent — not everyone's house burns down in the same year — but unemployment risk is correlated, it all lands at once in a recession. If nothing is really being pooled, what is the premium buying? And note that a premium doesn't build up a pot you own: unused cover isn't refunded. So maybe the "I've paid in" argument dies inside the analogy before you even get as far as conditionality.
- What the analogy quietly imports. Insurance individualises risk and prices it, careful drivers pay less, so importing it may build in the assumption that unemployment is caused by the claimant's choices rather than by the labour market. I want that made explicit, and I want your view on whether the "undeserving poor" part actually follows from the comparison or was smuggled in from outside it.
- Fraud. The insurance analogue is claiming for a loss that never happened. Does benefit fraud and overpayment map onto that, or onto something else (misreported earnings feels like a different beast)? There's also a distinction I've been playing with: moral hazard (cover changes how you behave once you have it) versus adverse selection (the pool itself is skewed). My group conflates them constantly and uses both as a synonym for scrounger. Does the analogy give a clean way to seperate them, and does either one really support workfare?
- If the comparison can't carry either conclusion, is there a better one? My own candidate: unemployment benefit is closer to the way governments backstop risks the private market won't cover at all, like flood or terrorism cover — a public backstop, not a policy. Or maybe it's closer to smoothing earnings across a working life. Tell me whether either does better, or whether the honest answer is that no analogy in this neighbourhood bears any normative weight and we should just argue about it directly.

Please write in British English, keep it concrete rather than programmatic, don't cite anything and don't give me a reading list — I'm boiling this down into a two-page briefing note for people who won't read one anyway.
