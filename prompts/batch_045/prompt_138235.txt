I'm putting together the background paper for our hospital's ethics committee, which has been asked to update the policy on determination of death — specifically the relation between the neurological standard, in both its whole-brain and brainstem formulations, and the circulatory standard, because our organ procurement organization wants to expand donation after circulatory determination of death. I argued with a neurology colleague last week about whether we should even be asking for a single account, so before we look at the standard positions I want the specification drawn up. Give me the desiderata: what an account of death has to deliver in order to be good enough for our purpose, where the purpose is explicitly a hospital policy — a rule a clinician can apply at the bedside within a few hours, that can be explained honestly to a family at the worst moment of their lives, that gives our attorneys and our procurement partners a determinate time to write down, and that does not require the staff to say one thing to relatives and believe another.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and be brutal about which are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudices of one camp. I have some candidates to start from, which you should sort rather than simply accept: the cases the account must classify correctly (decapitation; a body declared dead by neurological criteria whose somatic functions — wound healing, temperature control, in reported cases gestation — continue for weeks with support; circulatory arrest a few minutes in, where autoresuscitation is possible and where restarting the heart is what the transplant team needs to do; the permanently unconscious patient with an intact brainstem; anencephalic newborns; the hypothermic drowning victim who revives after prolonged arrest); the distinctions it must not collapse (the concept of death from the criteria and tests we use to diagnose it; dying from dead; the death of the human organism from the death of the person; irreversible from merely permanent — won't reverse versus we won't try; death as an event from death as a process; legal death from biological death); and the biological results it must not contradict, such as the brain's role as the integrator of the organism and the fact that the organism is not just a pile of functioning organs.

I also want two cases that I don't think anyone has a settled answer to. First, stipulate that our hospital acquires a perfusion technology that can restore oxygenated circulation to the brain hours after the heart has stopped and restore some cellular and electrophysiological activity with it; does the account then owe us a reading of irreversibility that survives, and what happens to a circulatory determination made before the technology existed? Second, stipulate a gradual neural prosthesis that replaces neurons with functionally equivalent hardware over years, with no break in consciousness or behavior and no biological neural tissue at the end; what does the account owe us about whether, and when, that patient died? Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for our purpose and which are the preferences of particular camps — I have in mind the demand that death be a biologically unified state, the dead donor rule, the demand that the criterion not obstruct organ procurement, and the demand that it track ordinary or religious intuitions about when someone is gone. If some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect that a single unified biological state, a determinate time of death, and continued use of both the circulatory and neurological criteria as we actually apply them cannot all be had together — say so plainly, since that tension is the part I most want; flag which single requirement excludes the most candidate accounts, and tell us where we are being forced to choose between desiderata rather than between theories. Please keep clear what is settled — the descriptive physiology, the content of the statute most US states have adopted — and what is contested among people who work on this; do not present a contested position as the consensus, and say where you think the disagreement is merely verbal, about what "death" should mean, and where it is substantive, about what we should be permitted to do. Skip the survey of the standard positions and don't cite anything: I want the specification first, and we can score the whole-brain, brainstem, higher-brain, and circulatory views against it in committee ourselves.
