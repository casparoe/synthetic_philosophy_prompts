I am preparing a graduate seminar on Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public, and I want to use a line that has become something of a slogan in my department: in The Present Age (1846), Kierkegaard writes, 'A public, on the contrary, is an abstraction.' At a recent departmental colloquium, a colleague deployed this line to argue that the comment section of a newspaper website—or the readership of a daily paper—cannot be a bearer of anything like public opinion, and therefore that editors owe no distinctive responsibility to 'the public' but only to the individual subscribers they can actually identify. I am not convinced the line does that work, but I also do not want to dismiss it as mere obscurity.

I would like you to take that one sentence and lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise claims it could be making, from the trivial to the radical. Please proceed in three steps.

First, separate the readings. Is the line merely the conceptual point that the word 'public' picks out an abstract object? Is it the empirical-sounding claim that readers of the press are never really simultaneous with one another, so that the press only produces what I call aggregate uptake—the mere fact that many real individuals consume the same text—and never a genuine assembly? Is it the normative claim that an abstraction cannot be the subject of responsibility, agency, or accountability? Or is it the stronger metaphysical or existential claim that a public is not merely an abstraction we construct but an illusion that corrupts the individual's relation to truth? Add any further precise thesis you think the sentence could be used to express.

Second, for each reading, give a verdict: is it trivial, substantively defensible, or false? Say what kind of argument would be needed to defend it. As a test, incorporate two distinctions I use in my lectures. The first is between aggregate uptake and phantom simultaneity: the illusion that private acts of reading become a collective verdict because they are coordinated by the publication schedule of the press. The second is between representative publicity—a group that can act through identifiable delegates or spokespersons—and anonymous abstraction: a putative collective with no decision procedure, no fixed membership conditions, and no way to revise its judgments. For each reading, say whether it turns on aggregate uptake or phantom simultaneity, and whether it turns on the contrast between representative publicity and anonymous abstraction.

Third, tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually support my colleague's conclusion that a newspaper's readership or an online comment section cannot be a public in the sense relevant to editorial obligation. Be explicit about where the argument succeeds only on the trivial reading, where it needs a substantive premise my colleague has not supplied, and where it would prove too much. In particular, say whether any reading would threaten to dissolve the real majorities or minorities that Kierkegaard, in the same discussion, says are real people and assist the individual by being adhered to, and whether that consequence is avoidable.

Please present the result as a handout-style menu of readings with verdicts, not as a single settled exegesis. I want to bring it to the colloquium so we can see exactly which premise is being smuggled in when the line is used as a conversation-stopper.
