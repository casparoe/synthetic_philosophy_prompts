My day job is quantitative research — most of my week is filtrations, changes of measure and Radon–Nikodym derivatives, exchangeability arguments, large-deviation / Sanov-type estimates, proper scoring rules and calibration, and the odd Savage- or de Finetti-style representation theorem — and I've been poking at the Everettian probability problem on and off since a grad-school reading group, mostly out of stubborness. A friend in quantum information keeps telling me it's "basically just a change of measure once you accept branching," which I'm fairly sure is glib, but I don't have the vocabulary to say why. So here's the ask: translate the Born-rule problem — why an agent who fully accepts that every outcome occurs should nonetheless apportion credence or concern by the squared amplitudes, and by the squared amplitudes specifically — into the machinery I actually use, together with the main families of responses, and then give me a residue report. The families I have in mind: (1) the decision-theoretic derivations, where an agent who knows the universal state is supposedly forced by preference axioms that look like plain rationality constraints to bet as if the weights were chances; (2) the envariance / symmetry derivations, which get equal amplitudes to equal probabilities via an entanglement-assisted invariance and then handle unequal amplitudes by a fine-graining step; (3) the self-locating-credence arguments, which grant that you're uncertain which branch you're on and then constrain those credences to factorize sensibly across subsystems; (4) the typicality or frequentist route, on which the overwhelming bulk of branches see relative frequencies matching the weights; and (5) the skeptical and the shrugging responses — that no coherent notion of probability is available here at all, or that the Born rule is simply an extra postulate bolted onto the dynamics. I don't want references or a reading list, I want the argumentative structure. For each of the five: what does it look like in my terms? Is (1) genuinely a representation theorem of the Savage kind, and if so what is playing the role of the fact that normally only one payoff is realized? Is (2) an exchangeability or invariance argument plus an infinite-divisibility / atomlessness assumption, and how much of the work is that second bit doing? Is (3) a conditional-independence assumption of the sort I'd normally treat as a free modeling choice rather than a constraint? Is (4) just a law of large numbers, and is the gap between an LLN and a decision rule the reason I find it unsatisfying? Is (5) a claim of the form "the parameter isn't identified by the data" or "the loss function isn't pinned down"? Please mark explicitly where the translation only goes through if you fudge something, and please don't quietly put amplitude-squared in as a premise and then present the result as a translation.

Two things of my own are really why I'm writing, because I can't tell whether they're original, already in the literature, or confused. First: I tried to get the weights out of pure bookkeeping, by requiring that the weight attached to a branch be invariant under refinement — splitting a branch further shouldn't change its weight, and the weight of a branch should be the sum of its sub-branches' weights. That definately kills the obvious rival rule I came up with (count the branches whose amplitude exceeds some threshold and renormalize), since that rule is sensitive to the threshold and to which basis you do the counting in. But refinement invariance looks to me like it assumes the very thing at issue: that what matters is an additive *amount* of something, rather than a number of distinct successors. It also feels like it's one step away from Gleason's theorem, in which case the load-bearing assumption is non-contextuality. Am I right about where it bottoms out, and is an Everettian entitled to that assumption, or does granting it concede the interesting part of the debate? Second: I can't work out who the forecast is scored against. In my world a probability forecast is a measure and it gets graded by a proper scoring rule against the outcome that actually happens; here every outcome happens to some descendant, so the tempting move is to aggregate the scores incurred by all the descendants — but the aggregation functional (Born-weighted average? unweighted average over copies? worst case?) seems to just encode whichever answer you wanted, and nothing in my scoring-rule framework says which aggregator is correct. A case that makes this vivid for me: a branch of Born weight around 10^-9 that, a few minutes later, branches far more prolifically than the fat branches, so it ends up containing vastly more observers by count — measure says ignore it, counting says worry about it. Does anything settle that case, or is it stipulated? In the residue report I'd like your view on whether the aggregation question is the whole problem in disguise, and whether my vocabulary is missing something real (some notion of how much a future copy matters, the thing people gesture at when they talk about caring or about a measure of existence) or whether that philosophers' talk is less contentful than it sounds.
