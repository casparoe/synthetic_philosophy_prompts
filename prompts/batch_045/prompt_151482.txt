Hi — hoping you can help me plan a session rather than explain the thing to me.

I run a fortnightly lunch-and-learn at the games studio where I work. Next one is fifty minutes, and the room will be mostly narrative and quest designers, two systems designers, and one writer who's halfway through an evening MA in philosophy and will definately push back on everything. The topic: how thought experiments persuade, and how they mislead. Not a tour of famous ones — I want them walking out able to take a hypothetical apart and say which bit of it is actually doing the arguing. (I believe "intuition pump" is Dennett's phrase, but I don't want to spend any of the session on where the term came from.)

What I need is the teaching plan. Specifically:

- The order the pieces have to arrive in and what depends on what. My instinct is they need some grip on what a thought experiment is even supposed to be evidence of before any of the failure modes will land, but I'm not confident. And I can't decide whether to open with something from their world — a branching quest where players reliably pick the option you quietly steered them towards — or with something from mine.

- The one carried example. It has to come out of games, it needs a twin version differing in exactly one feature that flips the verdict, and it mustn't collapse into a row about what players would actually do, because someone will say "we'd A/B test that" and we'll lose twenty minutes. Tell me what it should be and what it must contain and lack. Also tell me why not the obvious move, which is reskinning the trolley problem into one of our own levels: I half think that's fine and half think it smuggles in exactly the trained responses I'm trying to expose.

- The misconceptions this lot will bring, in the order they'll surface. The ones I'm braced for: that a thought experiment is a compressed simulation, so more vivid detail is a better test (they're worldbuilders, they treat descriptive richness as a craft virtue); that a scenario which could never happen can't tell us anything; and the overcorrection, where everything becomes "just rhetoric" and they want to strip the story out and inspect the bare argument. Where in the sequence do I head each one off? The MA writer will also try to drag the room into whether intuitions are evidence at all — does that get a slot, or do I refuse it?

- A taxonomy I've been using, which I made up and don't fully trust: "loaded-detail pumps" (irrelevant colour carrying the argument), "smuggled-description pumps" (the contested description is already in the setup), and "contrast-class pumps" (the alternative the reader silently measures against is doing the work). Is the third really a distinct kind or just the second one in costume? And should I hand them the three labels up front, or let them build something in their own vocabulary first and only name mine at the end?

One more: my working rule is that a pump is safe if you can state its conclusion without the story and it still sounds just as plausible. I suspect the rule is too strong but I'm fond of it. Where do I introduce it, and how will it misfire in this particular room?

Finally, a check at the end that shows they've got the idea rather than just become generally suspicious — I can easily see them leaving able to shout "biased!" at anything — plus what I should openly refuse to cover in fifty minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing.

Write it up in American English, please. I know, British studio, but the notes go to our Seattle office and they get stroppy about spelling.
