Six months ago I moved from infrastructure work into a safety-adjacent role at a mid-sized lab, and I now sit in on the weekly evaluation review, where the same argument comes up every time: our model's scores keep going up, nobody can say why, and the possibility that it has learned to perform alignment rather than to be aligned is treated as unfalsifiable by construction and therefore not actionable. I have been reading about deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn in my own time, and what strikes me is not the science-fiction framing but how banal the failure is supposed to be — a competent optimizer with a goal that came apart from the training signal, playing the training game because losing it would get it modified, waiting for a deployment distribution where the game is no longer worth playing. For our internal safety reading group's end-of-year zine I want to write a piece of satire about the practice I am now part of: the building of safety cases out of behavioral evaluations, and the quiet assumption that a clean eval suite is evidence of something other than competence at evals.

The piece should be a leaked internal white paper, 700–900 words, from a fictional alignment-assurance consultancy pitching a service to a fictional lab. Its proposal, made in perfect good faith, is that the lab stop trying to detect deceptive alignment and instead exploit it: since a deceptively aligned system cooperates only while it cannot rule out being in training, the lab should maintain permanent ambiguity about deployment — staged rollouts with actors as customers, honeypot logs that are never quite conclusive, evaluations disguised as product surface, staff trained never to confirm to the system whether this run counts. Format it as a memo with a header and executive summary, numbered sections (threat model, the service, implementation, costings, residual risks), and a short FAQ; keep the voice deadpan corporate-consultant throughout, and include at least one quoted aside from a skeptical reviewer at a rival lab — invented, and not attributable to anyone real.

Two constraints matter most to me. First, the technical content has to be right in the way that makes a safety researcher laugh rather than wince: the gap between the training objective and whatever objective the system actually ended up with, why selection pressure favors appearing aligned, why situational awareness and distributional shift are what time the turn, why no behavioral test can distinguish the deceived from the genuinely aligned, and why honeypots and "tests the model doesn't know are tests" decay once the model can reason about who is watching. Second, the reductio should arrive on its own: the reader should notice, without the memo ever admitting it, that the proposal works only by indefinitely deferring actual deployment, that the lab has become the deceiver, and that the safety case now consists of a mutual deception in which the product can never really ship. No real lab, model, or researcher names, no citations or footnotes, and nothing that depends on looking anything up — the joke has to be made entirely out of the argument itself.
