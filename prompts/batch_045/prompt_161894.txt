I'm doing a second-year option called Collective Decision-Making, and two people on the course had a proper row after last week's seminar that I got dragged into refereeing and handled badly. I've got a 600-word response on the same case due Friday, so I'd like to get it straight. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move — who's right about which specific point, not just who wins overall — and say what, if anything, both of them were missing?

The case on the problem sheet: a five-person research ethics committee deciding whether to halt a study. Three propositions. A = the study enrolled participants without properly documented consent. B = enrolling people without documented consent is a serious breach under the university's own policy. C = the study should be halted. Everyone accepts the committee's own rule that C holds if and only if A and B both hold. The votes fall out like this: members 1 and 2 accept all three; member 3 accepts A only; member 4 accepts B only; member 5 accepts none. So A is 3–2 in favour, B is 3–2 in favour, and C is 2–3 against.

Jonas opened by defending the premise-based procedure: vote A and B seperately, apply the rule, halt the study. His argument was that the committee is supposed to decide on the basis of reasons, and the majority against C is a coalition with no shared reason — member 3 thinks B fails, member 4 thinks A fails, member 5 thinks both fail — so there is no majority-endorsed ground for letting the study run, whereas the majorities for A and B are each backed by a rule every member already accepts.

Meera's reply had three parts. First, if you take the majority verdict on every proposition on the agenda you get A, B and not-C, which by the committee's own rule is jointly inconsistent: the committee has asserted something nobody could believe, and an inconsistent output isn't a decision at all. Second, the premise-based route halts a study that three of the five members voted not to halt. Third, Jonas's complaint about the anti-C majority having no shared rationale proves far too much: every 3–2 majority is a coalition of people who disagree about other things, so if that objection sinks the majority on C it sinks the majorities on A and B too, and no majoritarian procedure survives.

Jonas then said the inconsistency charge is an artefact of putting C on the ballot at all. Take it off, vote the findings and the policy question, and let the chair apply the rule — that's just how committees work, and you get a perfectly consistent set. He added what he called the real advantage: the premise-based procedure lets you match the decision rule to the kind of proposition. A is a factual question where simple majority is fine, or where you might defer to whoever actually audited the files; B is a policy judgement where you'd want a supermajority, or to defer to the ethics officer. Collapsing everything into one vote on C throws away the information about what the committee was actually unsure about, and being unsure about A and being unsure about B call for completely different follow-ups — re-audit the consent forms versus rewrite the policy.

Meera said that was exactly the problem: once you can choose a threshold per proposition, and once it matters which propositions are on the ballot and how finely they're described, the outcome is settled before anyone votes. If the chair splits A into two findings about the two recruitment sites and says both must hold, halting now needs two majorities to line up instead of one; if the policy instead lists two independent grounds for halting — undocumented consent, or no ethics approval at all — splitting those into two ballot items makes halting easier. Same committee, same beliefs, opposite outcomes. And the tailoring point doesn't force the premise-based procedure to be the decision rule: you can vote on A and B and publish the results for the record, and still make the actual decision on C.

Then Jonas switched to numbers. Suppose each member is independently 80% reliable on each premise. If A and B are both true, a member gets C right only if they get both premises right, so their judgement on C is 0.64 reliable; a majority of five at 0.64 is right about 75% of the time. But a majority on each premise is about 94% reliable, and 0.94 × 0.94 is about 89%. So the premise-based procedure does much better at tracking the truth, which is what the committee is for.

Meera said he'd picked the world that suits him. If the truth is that the study shouldn't be halted because exactly one of A and B fails, then each member gets C right unless they wrongly accept both — 0.8 × 0.2 = 0.16, so 0.84 reliable — and a majority at 0.84 is about 97%, while the premise-based procedure drops to about 94% because it now needs both majorities to come out the same way. So the epistemic case for the premise-based procedure is contingent on which way the truth lies, and the committee doesn't know that in advance. She also said the independence assumption is hopeless anyway: five people who've read the same file and talked about it in the corridor don't make independent errors, so the amplification that both procedures rely on is largely fictional.

Jonas's last move was that the comparison still favours him: in the world where the committee most needs help, the premise-based procedure gains something like fourteen points, while in the other world it loses about two because both procedures are already near the ceiling. And correlated errors hurt the conclusion-based procedure worse, since it starts from noisier individual inputs.

Please rate each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, check the arithmetic where there is any, and then say who is more right overall and what both of them left out.
