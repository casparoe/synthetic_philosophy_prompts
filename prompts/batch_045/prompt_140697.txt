Hi — I teach epistemology (upper-level seminar, a couple of grad students auditing) and next term I'm doing a short unit on the epistemic paradoxes, and a colleague over in the law school has talked me into pairing the lottery and preface paradoxes with a screening of the 1957 Lumet version of 12 Angry Men. I've seen it maybe a dozen times and never once thought about it in these terms, and I'm genuinely of two minds about whether it's a good fit. So: give me the reading you'd give my students. Which of the two paradoxes, if either, does the film actually dramatize, and where? My naive first pass is this: the state's case is a conjunction of maybe six or seven independent claims — the switchblade that was supposed to be unique, the threat heard by a neighbour while an elevated train was going past, the disabled man downstairs who says he got to his door in fifteen seconds and saw the boy running, the woman across the way who claims to have watched the stabbing through the windows of that train, the boy's alibi that he can't fill in — and juror 8 never argues that any single one of them is certainly false. He argues, item by item, that each is shakier than it looked, and the case comes apart without anyone ever locating a flat contradiction. That looks to me like the preface paradox run in reverse: the prosecution is the author who beleives every sentence of the book but who, if honest, should concede that somwhere in it there's a mistake, and the twelve men in that room are proofreaders hunting for it. I've been calling this the errata model of the prosecution's case, and I want to know whether it holds up or whether I'm smuggling in a moral point about the asymmetry between hanging an innocent man and freeing a guilty one and calling it an epistemic point.

Two more things I want you to get into, because they're where I suspect the film might actually contribute something to the paradox literature rather than just illustrate it. First: I'm no longer sure the film is doing lottery-style reasoning at all, in the way I first assumed. What juror 8 seems to be insisting on is a no-pooling norm — every element has to clear the beyond-reasonable-doubt threshold on its own, and you don't get to add six shaky items together into one strong case — and that looks different from the failure of closure under conjunction that the lottery is supposed to demonstrate. Or maybe it isn't different and I've drawn a distinction without a difference; tell me which, and what turns on it. Second, and this is the bit I keep going back and forth on: the film treats unanimity as the epistemic gold standard, but a lottery-flavoured argument seems to cut straight against it. If each juror is individually reliable — right nineteen times out of twenty, say — then a jury required to be unanimous is right a bit better than half the time; push it to ninety-nine per cent per juror and twelve of them together are still only at roughly eighty-eight. Unanimity is conjunction all the way down. And yet the emotional climax of the film is the last holdout folding so the vote can go twelve-nil, and we are obviously meant to cheer. Is the movie cheering for the one institution the paradox says we should be most suspicious of?

Please be honest about where the reading breaks down, because I think it does in at least two places: juror 8's doubt seems to start as a refusal to take a life on the cheap rather than as a verdict on the evidence, which makes the item-by-item demolition look like post hoc rationalization; and the final holdout isn't argued out of his position at all, he's undone by a photograph of his son, which is psychology and not epistemology. So maybe the film's real subject is the social mechanics of dissent — one man refusing to vote is what forces the other eleven to think — and the paradoxes are just scenery. I'd still like a reading that takes both sides seriously: what does the film get right about corrigible, fallible, high-but-not-certain belief that the paradox papers don't, whether it's something about institutions as a response to closure failure or something about the gap between not accepting a claim and accepting its negation. Don't give me quotations from the film or a reading list — I know the movie, and I can find the literature myself. Just tell me which scenes you'd put on the syllabus and what you'd say about them.
