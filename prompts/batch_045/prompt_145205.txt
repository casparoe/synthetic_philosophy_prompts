Two theorists have been arguing about the doomsday argument for years without convergence: one is a committed defender of the Carter–Leslie argument who reasons from the self-sampling assumption with a reference class of terrestrial humans; the other is a committed self-indication-assumption theorist, who holds that hypotheses get a prior boost proportional to the number of observers they contain and that this boost cancels the doomsday shift. They have agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint document they should produce.

Stipulations both parties accept for the exercise (do not look anything up; the numbers are given so that both sides reason from the same case): about 1.1 × 10¹¹ humans have lived before you and about 8 × 10⁹ are alive now; the two competing hypotheses are H_soon, on which the total number of humans who will ever live is about 2 × 10¹¹, and H_vast, on which it is about 10¹⁶; both parties agree to report their anthropic reasoning as a likelihood ratio and to state their prior odds over the two hypotheses separately, rather than pretending to agree on them.

Deliverable, in four parts:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. This should include the exact Bayesian shape of the argument; the size of the shift the birth-rank evidence produces under the stipulated hypotheses; what the argument would not establish even if sound (it names no mechanism, and it does not by itself say when the end comes); the point that both parties accept the likelihood of your birth rank conditional on a total population and therefore locate their dispute elsewhere; a shared statement of why the choice of reference class is load-bearing and why neither side claims a principled derivation of it; and an admission of any case in which both frameworks fail, rather than a silent omission of it.

2. A disagreements table with columns: issue; the formulation the SSA-based doomsday arguer would sign; the formulation the self-indication theorist would sign; the crux beneath the disagreement; a test, case, or argument both would accept as bearing on that crux; and each side's prediction about how it would come out. Treat at least these five cruxes:

(a) Whether the prior over hypotheses should be weighted by the number of observers each contains — and, if so, whether what is counted is persons or observer-moments. Both sides should state the formula they accept.

(b) The reference class, tested against a stipulated discovery: in the year 2089 we verify a signal establishing that a civilization elsewhere in our galaxy persisted for about 10⁷ years and contained about 10²⁵ observers. Both camps record in advance how this redistributes their credences about H_soon versus H_vast, and what becomes of the maneuver of treating such observers as outside the reference class.

(c) The cheap-minds case: suppose the observers counted by H_vast are almost entirely artificially instantiated minds, about 10⁶ times as numerous as biological humans and running about 10⁶ times faster. Does counting observer-moments rather than persons strengthen, weaken, or reverse each side's conclusion, and why?

(d) Infinity: suppose the cosmology turns out to contain infinitely many observers of the relevant kind. Each side states which limiting or regularization procedure it trusts and whether any doomsday-style shift survives it.

(e) Practical upshot: does any of the above change what it would be reasonable to do — how much to spend on reducing extinction risk, or how fast to pursue settlement beyond Earth? If a framework yields a number, give it; if it falls silent, say so and say why.

3. A short section sorting the disagreements into substantive and merely verbal. For each one you call verbal, give the re-description that dissolves it and state what, if anything, survives the re-description.

4. Two closing paragraphs, one for each camp, each written in that camp's own voice and containing the strongest claim it still holds plus an explicit statement of what would make it abandon that claim.

Constraints: no citations, no literature survey, no appeals to authority, and no exposition of what various authors have said — the document should stand on its own arguments. Keep the justification for any contested claim to about two sentences. Where a crux admits no test either side would accept, flag that plainly instead of inventing one.
