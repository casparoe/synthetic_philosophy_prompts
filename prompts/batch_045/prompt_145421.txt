I'm TAing for an applied ethics course and marking a problem set on the meat-eating / wild-animal-suffering material. There's one answer I'm not sure how to place, and I'd rather not have my own take on it colour the comments, so here's the question and the answer as submitted.

THE QUESTION

A familiar argument in the "which meat is worse" debate runs as follows. Producing a kilogram of beef takes several times more crop feed than producing a kilogram of chicken. Crop cultivation kills wild animals — small mammals through ploughing and harvesting, invertebrates through pesticides — and it also sustains populations of animals whose lives are, on the whole, short and nasty. So, other things being equal, eating beef is worse than eating chicken.

(a) Say what quantity the comparison between beef and chicken should actually be made in terms of, and explain why the more obvious quantities (per animal killed, per kilogram of meat) mislead.
(b) Give the strongest consideration pushing in the other direction, and say how much weight you think it has.
(c) Identify one factual or evaluative question that your verdict would turn on and that you cannot settle from the armchair.

500 words max.

THE ANSWER

Start by fixing the unit of comparison. What matters for me as a consumer is not harm "per kilo of beef" considered on its own but the expected difference my order makes: if I take the chicken instead of the beef, beef production falls by about one beef-serving and chicken production rises by about one chicken-serving, so the relevant figure is harm per typical serving, not harm per kilo and not harm per animal. This matters because the animals differ hugely in size — one steer yields hundreds of kilos of meat, a broiler a couple — so per-animal figures mislead in one direction and per-kilo figures mislead in the other, since a kilo of chicken is several birds.

The feed-conversion argument has real force. Cattle need much more crop feed per kilo of meat than broilers do, and cultivation kills field animals. It is also not rescued by the thought that pasture is wildlife-friendly: grazing land is fenced, often fertilised and reseeded, grazed by animals that compete with and prey on natives, and in any case most beef cattle are grain-finished.

But it doesn't settle things. First, the argument is stated in harm per kilo of feed, whereas the cultivation deaths occur per hectare per year. Pasture is cultivated far less intensively than cropland, and a hectare of grazed grass probably kills fewer animals per year than a hectare of maize; and cattle spend a large part of their lives on pasture. Beef therefore does better on the only comparison that tracks the cultivation deaths directly. Second, and more importantly, deaths are the wrong currency. What matters is suffering, and here chicken is worse: broilers are bred for rapid growth and killed after a few weeks, and there are vastly more of them per kilo of meat, so a chicken-heavy diet involves many more lives of suffering per kilo than a beef-heavy one.

What I can't settle from the armchair: whether the lives of the field animals whose deaths cultivation causes are good or bad on the whole. Ploughing kills, but it also prevents the existence of animals that would have lived short, brutal lives; if those lives are net negative, cultivation may prevent more suffering than it causes, and the feed-conversion argument loses its force. A second unknown is how much of a beef animal's life is really spent on pasture rather than in a feedlot — but that only scales the argument, it doesn't reverse it.

Conclusion: the two considerations roughly cancel. I don't know which is worse, and the beef-versus-chicken question is probably less important than the gap between animal foods and plant foods.
