Next Thursday I'm giving the last seminar of term to the Climate and Economy modelling group at my institute, and I'd like a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defence of the view. The talk is twenty minutes plus twenty for questions, and the room will not be philosophers: it is four integrated-assessment and climate-economy modellers (one senior, who has spent years building and defending these models and whose reflexive response to methodological criticism is that all models are false and the only question is whether one is useful), two philosophers of science from a neighbouring department (one sympathetic to the idea that models are autonomous objects, answerable to neither theory nor data in the simple way, and therefore suspicious of any norm that grades models by the truth of their assumptions), a statistician who works on calibration, validation and uncertainty quantification, and half a dozen graduate students who will mostly follow the lead of the senior people. I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, this is my first talk outside my own department, and I know I over-concede the moment someone says that decisions have to be made under uncertainty anyway.

My thesis is a limited one about Friedman's F-twist — the methodological claim, from his essay on the methodology of positive economics, that the descriptive realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to its worth, and that unrealistic assumptions are if anything a sign of significance, because the theory's job is prediction. I want to argue that the F-twist can only do work for a model used inside the regime in which it was tuned and tested, and delivers nothing at all for the uses these models are actually built for: policy counterfactuals and extrapolation into regimes for which there is no data. To make the point I introduce a distinction I need the room to grant me, at least provisionally. Call an idealization a surrogate when the modeller can say what real feature the distortion stands in for and can sketch how the model's outputs move as the distortion is relaxed — a frictionless surface as the limit of small viscosity, a representative agent as the special case of a heterogeneous population whose dispersion can be dialled up. Call it a placeholder when there is neither a de-idealization story nor any data that would discipline the de-idealized version: a damage function fitted on observed variation and then extrapolated several degrees beyond it, a single social discount rate held fixed over centuries, a climate module that carries global mean temperature and nothing else. I call the resulting norm the surrogacy requirement: an assumption may be unrealistic only if the modeller has some account of what it replaces and what happens as it is removed. My argument is roughly four steps — that the F-twist's own defence of unrealistic assumptions is a defence of predictive success, that predictive success is always success within a tested regime, that the counterfactual and extrapolative uses of these models are precisely uses outside that regime, and that the surrogacy requirement is what does the justifying work there, so that a placeholder cannot borrow the F-twist's cover. I end by sorting a handful of idealizations actually in use in these models into the two categories and conceding that some of my verdicts will be unpopular.

What I want from you is the prep sheet I should carry into the room. Give me the seven or eight objections I will actually get, ordered roughly by how likely they are to come and how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, and phrase each one the way that person would phrase it — in the modeller's vocabulary of validation, calibration, scenario ensembles and decision-relevance when it comes from the practitioners, in the vocabulary of representation, confirmation and the autonomy of models when it comes from the philosophers, and in the vocabulary of out-of-sample testing, identifiability and cross-validation when it comes from the statistician. For each, give me a reply I could genuinely deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft or evasive, and a one-line statement of what I should concede up front about it before I'm pushed. Tell me which single concession I must make in the first two minutes if I want the rest of the room to stay with me, and which reply has to land for the paper to survive at all. Then give me the two places where my own position is weakest and that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices: I think one of them is whether surrogacy can be checked at all in the cases where we lack the data that would discipline the de-idealized model, and the other is whether my requirement is action-guiding for someone who has to produce numbers for a policy process next quarter. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it comes from the senior modeller, who has read more of the philosophy of science on idealization than I have and may simply say that my distinction between surrogates and placeholders has been made before under other names, and that my paper is a redescription rather than an argument.
