I'm in a political philosophy reading group this term and we spent a session on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem a few weeks ago, and I keep going back and forth on whether it has any real institutional bite or whether it's just a tidy representation result that gets wheeled out in footnotes. So I want to test it on an actual case: Oregon's Medicaid reform of around 1989–1993. My understanding of the facts (please just take them as given — I rember them loosely and don't care about the fine historical detail): Oregon wanted to expand Medicaid eligibility to everyone below the poverty line, and to pay for the expansion by capping the benefit package instead — i.e. by funding only a prioritized list of condition–treatment pairs, with everything below a cutoff line left unfunded. The first version of the list was generated by ranking treatments by cost-effectiveness, basically cost per quality-adjusted life year, and it threw up some famously embarassing orderings; after public outrage the list was substantially re-sorted by hand by a commission using other considerations. The plan needed a federal waiver, and the Department of Health and Human Services pushed back in 1992 on the grounds that the ranking violated the Americans with Disabilities Act, on the argument that it valued the life of a disabled person less than the life of a non-disabled person; after revision it was approved in 1993. Please don't cite anything or reconstruct the legislative history — I want the conceptual analysis, using the case as I've summarised it.

What I want is a disciplined mapping run in both directions. First, set it up properly in the theorem's terms: who the "individuals" are, what the social alternatives are (is the object of choice a benefit package, a cutoff line, an eligibility rule?), what the individual utility functions are supposed to be here (health-state utility? QALYs? welfare more broadly?), and whether Oregon's procedure is even of the form the theorem describes. Two things I specifically want you to be honest about: the theorem says a Pareto-satisfying, vNM-rational social ordering is a weighted sum, but Oregon wasn't choosing among lotteries over social states — it was choosing under certainty, subject to a budget — so tell me what the vNM assumptions on the social side are doing, and how much of the theorem's conclusion survives if all you ever observe are choices over sure outcomes (my suspicion is that a lot of different social orderings over lotteries extend the same ranking over sure things, so the theorem constrains less than it looks like it does). Second, take the mechanics seriously: maximizing a weighted sum of QALYs subject to a budget is not the same thing as sorting by cost-per-QALY ratio and drawing a line, greedy-algorithm style, when treatments are lumpy and interact — so say whether the league table is actually implementing the social welfare function or approximating it badly, and whether that's the theorem's problem or Oregon's. And third, the weights: the theorem doesn't deliver equal weights, and QALY league tables weight a gained QALY the same whoever gets it and whatever their baseline is; walk me through whether the ADA objection is an objection to the weights, to the intrapersonal utility scale (lower attainable ceilings for disabled patients), or to something the theorem has no vocabulary for, and whether Harsanyi's veil-of-ignorance route to equal weights even applies to a decision made ex post about an already-identifiable population.

Then turn it around: does this case make the theorem look more or less useful as a guide to institutional design? Does it show that once you commit to cost-effectiveness analysis you're already committed to utilitarian aggregation, so the theorem just makes explicit what the practice smuggles in? Or does it show the opposite — that the hard choices (who's in the sum, where the budget comes from, what to do about people at their ceiling) are all made before the theorem has anything to say, so the theorem is decorative? I'd also like a short comparison: if Oregon had instead ranked by something Rawlsian, or by a sufficientarian floor, or by treating eligibility breadth and benefit depth as a single tradeoff within one fixed population, would it have avoided the objection, and would it have avoided it for a reason the aggregation framework can represent? Where the theorem is silent, just say it's silent — I'd rather have that than a forced reading.
