I am convening a two-day adversarial collaboration on whether philosophy makes progress, and I need the joint statement that the two delegations could sign at the end of it, together with the residual-disagreement record that would accompany it.

The two delegations are as follows. Camp C (the convergence camp) holds that philosophy makes genuine cognitive progress, but that progress has to be measured by what happens to the *space of live positions and their supporting reasons* — positions eliminated, arguments made explicit and shared, known error-types no longer re-run, distinctions drawn that later work can inhabit — rather than by the settlement of verdicts. Camp C's standing complaint is that the pessimist's metric is borrowed from inquiries whose objects admit of accumulation, and then applied without argument to an inquiry whose product is largely the articulation of a dialectical space. Camp E (the endurance camp) grants that philosophical writing becomes more sophisticated, more careful and better informed, but denies that this is progress in the sense that matters: on its view, progress requires verdicts that get settled, stay settled, and can thereafter be taken over by a later inquirer as a premise without re-running the arguments that established them, and philosophy characteristically fails this test — the same small set of questions, including the free-will question and the skepticism question, are disputed now in forms recognizably continuous with those of many centuries ago. Camp E adds that a field can improve its manners without improving its epistemic position.

Both delegations have already agreed that the word "progress" is doing them no favors, so the statement should avoid the unglossed term and should define whatever successor notions it uses.

Deliverable, in four parts.

1. A numbered agreed-claims section: the theses both camps can sign, stated so that neither side would want to revise the wording. It should cover at least: what an account of progress has to be an account *of* (a question? a research programme? a field? the discipline?) and what fixing that unit does to the dispute; what would count as the field being no better off than it was; and whether there is progress that is not truth-directed in the usual sense.

2. A disagreements table with these columns: the crux; Camp C's own position on it in wording Camp C would accept; Camp E's own position in wording Camp E would accept; the test, case, or stipulated finding both accept as genuinely bearing on the crux; Camp C's prediction about that test's outcome, written down in advance; Camp E's prediction, likewise; and what each side would count as a result that moves it. The cruxes to be covered, at minimum:

   (a) *Verdict-persistence with reason-drift.* Suppose a Position-Space Audit: for a given question Q, an idealized archive records at two times the set of live positions, the strongest known formulation of each, the standing objections, and the distribution of specialist credence. Case: the credence distribution and the verdict set are the same at both times, but the argument-sets are entirely different and strictly stronger, and three argument-forms once treated as decisive are now known to be invalid. Is Q's history progress? Which feature of the audit is the progress-making one?

   (b) *The rediscovery test.* A position P is argued for and adopted in the present; it then emerges that a structurally identical argument for P was made, lost, and forgotten long ago, and that the field had in the interim occupied every position now regarded as a rival to P. Has the field progressed, regressed, or returned? Does it matter whether the present authors could have cited the earlier argument?

   (c) *Downloadability.* Define the premise-reuse rate: the proportion of an inquiry's established results that a competent later inquirer can invoke as a premise without re-arguing them. Suppose philosophy's rate is low and stable while a mature science's is high. Is the gap a defect in philosophy, a category error about what philosophy produces, or evidence only about the kind of question philosophy retains?

   (d) *Stabilization without settlement.* Suppose a dispute on which every live move, every reply, and every known dead end is mapped and universally known, but on which credence remains dispersed and no position has been eliminated. Does a fully mapped and unsettled dispute constitute an achievement, and if so what kind?

   (e) *Secession and selection.* Suppose an audit were to show that a large share of the questions treated as philosophy at some earlier date are now handled in departments using empirical methods, while syllabi and histories over-represent the questions that stayed. Is the apparent persistence of philosophy's questions evidence of stasis, or an artifact of what a field exports and what it remembers? What would distinguish genuine progress from depletion of the question stock?

   (f) *Dissolution versus abandonment.* Some questions stop being asked because they were answered, some because they were dissolved as confused, and some because attention drifted. How can the three be told apart in a given case, and how should each be scored by the two accounts?

3. A short verbal-dispute appendix: identify which components of the disagreement, if any, are disputes about words, about the unit of assessment, or about how much weight to give a shared description of the facts, and say what disagreement survives once those are set aside. If you conclude that some part of the dispute is not verbal but is also not evidentially tractable, say which part and why.

4. A closing note: one claim each camp wants in the statement and the other refuses to sign, in one sentence each, with the refusal given in the refusing camp's own voice.

Constraints. Do not cite, name, or attribute anything to any author, school, or historical period beyond what the cruxes themselves require; this is a conceptual document, not a survey. Where a crux needs a finding, stipulate it as a hypothetical audit result and make clear that it is stipulated. Every prediction must be written so that a stipulated finding could in principle come out against the side making it. If a crux admits no test both camps would accept as bearing on it, say so and diagnose the obstacle rather than inventing a test. The document will be read by the graduate students attached to each delegation and by a funder deciding whether to renew the project, so it must be intelligible to a non-specialist without being vague.
