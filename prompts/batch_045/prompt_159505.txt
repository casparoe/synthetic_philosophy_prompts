I'm a reinforcement learning engineer, and until recently the absent-minded driver problem was just something I vaguely remembered hearing about in a philosophy elective. That changed last month when a memory bug in one of our warehouse delivery robots produced exactly the kind of situation the problem describes. Our robot has to navigate a simple route with two identical intersections: if it exits at the first intersection it reaches the wrong dock and gets a payoff we can treat as 0; if it continues past the first and exits at the second, it makes the correct delivery and gets a payoff of 4; and if it misses both and keeps going it ends up at a charging station and gets a payoff of 1. Because of the bug, the robot cannot tell whether a given intersection is the first or the second: its observation at the two intersections is exactly the same, and it has no recurrent memory of having already passed one. My teammate and I got into a dispute about what the policy should do. She wants to hard-code a stochastic policy that continues with probability two-thirds at every intersection, on the grounds that this maximizes the expected return from the start of the episode. I pointed out that, when the robot is actually at an intersection, the on-policy belief that it is at the first versus the second intersection is not one-half but is skewed toward the first, because the second is only reached if the robot already chose to continue once; depending on how one does the self-locating update, continuing and exiting can look equally good, or exiting can look strictly better, and I'm not sure whether our training setup (on-policy policy gradients with a memoryless policy and no way to condition on the timestep) is even asking the same question as the philosophical one.
