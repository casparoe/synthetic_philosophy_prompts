I work as a backend engineer, mostly on distributed systems, and over the past year I've done some consulting for a medical device company that builds cortical implants. They are now planning what they call a substrate migration procedure: replacing small patches of a patient's brain tissue, a few cubic millimetres at a time, with silicon units individually calibrated to reproduce that patch's behaviour — same inputs in, same outputs out, down to the timing of individual spikes. A full course takes roughly fifteen years. I've been pulled into an internal review to help draft the consent language and a written go/no-go rule, which has forced me to notice that I have confident reactions to particular scenarios and no idea what general thing I believe. I've never studied philosophy and don't have the vocabulary for any of this, so I'd like the general thing stated precisely enough that I could defend it to a colleague who disagrees.

Here are the cases I've worked through, with the verdicts I'd give today.

1. The standard course: one patch per month, patient awake and talking throughout, no measurable change in behaviour or memory. Clearly the same person at every point; nothing here needs consent language beyond ordinary surgical risk.

2. The same total replacement performed in one operation under general anaesthesia, every patch swapped at once. Still the same person. I feel less comfortable than in case 1, but I can't identify what the gradualness was doing, and I don't think my discomfort should change the answer.

3. A shadow-mode variant: each new unit runs alongside the tissue it will replace for a month, receiving identical inputs with its output discarded, and only then is the original tissue switched out. Fine — and, importantly, I do not think that during the overlap month there are two of that patient. There's one patient and an unused component.

4. Scan and replace: the tissue is mapped at fine resolution, an emulation is built from the map, and the biological tissue is then removed. The end state is indistinguishable from case 2's end state. My reaction is completely different: the original patient has died, and the emulation is a different person who thinks he is the patient and has his memories. I'd treat this as a killing, and I'd block it.

5. Rollback: an emulation has been running for years, a bug corrupts its stored state, and the team restores the previous night's checkpoint and replays that day's recorded inputs. Same person; fine; at worst a day is relived or lost. I have no objection.

6. Branch and prune: two copies are started from the same checkpoint, run for a month, diverge slightly, and then one is deleted on a coin flip. Somehow worse than case 5, even though in both a running instance is destroyed and one continues. I can't settle on whether to describe it as a coin flip I survive half the time or as a case where neither copy is me after they diverge — but I am sure it isn't as benign as the rollback, and I'd want it prohibited.

7. Dark substitutes: the replacement units reproduce the input-output behaviour perfectly, but nothing is experienced in the replaced tissue — the lights are off inside. My reaction is that this does not preserve the patient even though every observable test passes. I also don't know whether this case is even coherent: if the behaviour matches perfectly, what work is the phrase the lights are off doing? If it's incoherent, I want to be told, and told what that does to my other verdicts.

8. Order and geometry: replacing contiguously, region by region, versus scattering the swaps randomly through the brain. Doesn't matter to me at all, which makes me suspect the physical layout isn't what I'm tracking.

9. Drift: the substitutes are only approximately equivalent, and over fifteen years the patient's behaviour changes about as much as anyone's does over fifteen years. Same person. But I don't know where the line is, and my job requires me to write one down.

The tentative rule I keep coming back to is something like: you get the same patient out as long as no step in the process ever produces two equally good candidates for being that patient. That handles 4 and 6, but it seems to give the wrong answer in 3, where the standby unit is running for a month, and it says nothing about 7.

So: is there a single principle that delivers all nine verdicts, and if not, a small set of candidates? For each candidate I'd like the clauses stated separately with the case that motivates each one, its verdict on three cases I haven't listed — the full procedure carried out while the patient is in continuous dreamless sleep and wakes at the end; the original tissue kept alive but disconnected in a vat after the emulation starts; and two copies that diverge and are later merged back into one — and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would require me to overturn.
