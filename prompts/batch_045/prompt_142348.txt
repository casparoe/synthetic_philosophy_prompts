I should be grateful for your help in preparing for the question period after a paper I am giving in a fortnight's time.

The occasion is a one-day seminar on invention in historical fiction, held at a county record office and jointly convened with a university department. I have written a novel, now in development as a three-part television drama, drawn from the life of my great-aunt, who died in 1981. After my mother's death last year I inherited a box of her letters; they revealed that in 1963 she was convicted of obtaining money by false pretences — a small, shabby fraud connected with a burial club — served nine months, and that the family never afterwards mentioned it. The letters also suggest she had been covering debts run up by her younger brother. Roughly nine months of a long life, chosen by me as the spine of the drama. Amongst the papers there is also an envelope dated 1978, marked to be burnt unread, in which she asks that the box be destroyed. I opened it. The script I have written composites three of her friends into a single character, compresses about a decade into one year, and — the part I am least comfortable about — includes extracts, shown on screen in her handwriting, from a diary she never in fact kept.

The position I am defending has three parts. First, I concede at the outset that the dead cannot be harmed, and I therefore do not claim that a false portrait wrongs her by making her worse off. What I claim instead is that we owe the real dead *answerability* rather than accuracy: a duty to keep the portrait contestable, grounded in the thought that in dramatising someone we borrow a voice that was theirs, and borrowings should in principle be returnable. Second, the duty has two teeth. The *counterfeit-evidence prohibition*: a work must never present invented material as record — no fabricated letters, diaries, transcripts or documents shown as if archival — because a counterfeit record makes the evidence itself lie in her voice, and she cannot issue a retraction. The *revisability condition*: the work must carry inside itself, in its own texture rather than in a disclaimer, the means by which a viewer could revise or reject the portrait — which I argue is satisfied by visibly marking invented and composite material, and not satisfied by compressing events silently or by balancing a distorted scene with a flattering one elsewhere. Third, against the family's objection that she would have hated it, I argue that a wish to be forgotten is itself part of the material to be interpreted, not a veto over the work; but I also argue that the nine-months-versus-a-lifetime complaint has real force, because selection distorts even where no fact is false, and my answer is that the remedy is not fairer proportions but a visible selection.

The room will contain four constituencies. Two published biographers and the county archivist, all committed to source criticism and to provenance, who are likely to think my fact/invention line is unsteady and my marking device either naive or a gimmick. A media lawyer, who will want to know what any of this costs given that the operative constraints are the reputations of identifiable living relatives, copyright in the letters, and malicious falsehood. My great-uncle, her surviving son, seventy-nine, a retired engineer who thinks the whole enterprise disloyal and who will say that I never knew her; and his daughter, who wants the story told and who will say that "answerability" is a polite word for letting the family decide. And a moral philosopher who works on posthumous harm, who will press whether answerability is coherent when the answerer does not exist, whether I am smuggling interests in by the back door, and whether my duty is really owed to the living — in which case, she will say, my framing is misleading and I should own it.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defence. Give me the eight or so objections I shall actually get, in the order they are likely to arrive and ranked by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that constituency would really put it rather than in my vocabulary: an archivist's objection in an archivist's terms, a lawyer's in a lawyer's, a grieving son's in his. For each, a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds, a note of what I should concede on the spot, and a note of where the reply is softest. Tell me what I should give away in the first two minutes to buy myself the room, which of the four constituencies has the objection with genuine force against me, and which single reply has to land if the position is to survive the session. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks; from what I have said above you should be able to work out what it is.
