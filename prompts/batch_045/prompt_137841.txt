I write a column for a popular science magazine, and I've now done three pieces that leaned on the word collapse — one on the Late Bronze Age in the eastern Mediterranean, one on the Classic Maya, one on Easter Island. Going back over them I keep noticing something I don't have a word for.

In all three, the evidence cited for collapse is about the same narrow slice of life: palaces stop being built, monumental inscriptions stop, long-distance trade in luxuries dries up, literacy drops or vanishes. Meanwhile the things most people spent their days on — growing food, making pots, burying their dead — either carry on with barely a blip, or we just don't have the kind of evidence that would tell us. And when the pattern runs the other way — the monuments keep coming but a lot of people die, move away, or get poorer — the same writers reach for transformation or continuity instead of collapse. So the word seems to be tracking something much narrower than the failure of a whole society, while being presented as if it tracked the whole thing.

Two made-up cases I keep testing it against. First: a polity loses its writing system, its palaces and its standing army, population halves over a century, and the people who remain live longer, eat better and are less likely to die violently. Second: population and monumental building stay put, the ruling house changes four times in a generation by what the inscriptions politely call a change of ruler, and the average household gets markedly worse off. Everything I've read would call the first one a collapse and the second something milder — and I genuinely can't tell whether that's because the first is the bigger failure, or just because its the one that stops producing the kind of stuff we can read.

Is there an established concept here? Please don't give me a reading list or names attached to theories, I really just want the carving. People gesture at collapse versus transformation, and at definitions built on lost complexity, but neither quite does it: the first just relabels the asymmetry, and the second seems to bake the asymmetry in on purpose, since palaces and literacy are the things that are easy to count. What I think I'm after is a term for a verdict about a whole society that's driven by what happened to a small, unusually well-documented part of it, plus maybe a companion term for the second case, where things get worse for nearly everyone without anything that looks like a collapse. If nothing established covers it, coin and define it, say where its edges are, which of my two cases it counts as a collapse and which it doesn't, and be honest if you think my two cases are less different than I think. A bare label won't do me any good.
