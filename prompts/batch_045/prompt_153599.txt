Thought I'd run this past you before I make a fool of myself. I'm 68, retired — thirty-odd years doing protein NMR, so I was never a mathematician, I just used the maths as a tool. Recently I've been going to a philosophy of science reading group and I'm writting up a short thing on where I've landed on philosophy of mathematics, and I'd like a consistency check, because I suspect I'm holding several things at once that don't fit, but I can't see where the seam is.

Here's the pile, roughly in the order I acquired them:

(1) I'm a scientific realist and I find the indispensability argument convincing: if our best physical theories quantify over mathematical objects then we're committed to their existence, and it's the only argument for mathematical ontology I find respectable. Discovering, not inventing.

(2) But I've also become convinced by constructivism: to assert that something exists is to be able to produce it, and the law of excluded middle is illegitimate once you go infinite. Over finite domains you can in principle check, so excluded middle is fine there; the trouble begins with the infinite.

(3) What actually appeals to me about constructivism is the algorithm business — a proof of an existential should hand you a procedure. And I like the modern formal versions, type theory and machine-checked proofs and proof assistants. That strikes me as the grown-up form of the whole programme.

(4) Separately I'm drawn to Brouwer's picture of mathematics as an activity of the individual mind, something we create rather than discover, prior to and independent of language and of formal rules. That's the bit that makes Platonism feel like a superstition to me.

(5) This worries me, because it looks like constructivism slides into finitism: if you can only assert what you can exhibit, you can't have real analysis, and I need the reals for physics. So either constructivism is false or my physics is in trouble.

(6) And concretely: I think the intermediate value theorem is non-negotiable. Continuous function, negative at one end, positive at the other, it crosses zero. Anyone who denies that has stopped doing mathematics. I used it constantly.

Two smaller things I've been assuming: that intuitionistic logic is a rival to classical logic in the sense that it flatly contradicts it (intuitionists asserting the negation of classical results), and that the cute argument about irrational a and b with a to the power b rational shows there are places where classical reasoning simply cannot be avoided.

So: which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest way out of each? Please rank them by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, say for each what would have to give, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Be specific about which numbered item has to go — I'm not attached to any of them, I just can't tell which is doing the damage.
