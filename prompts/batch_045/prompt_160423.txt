I am assembling the final module of a graduate seminar on the epistemology of other minds, and I need one teaching artifact: a scripted discussion that my students can annotate line by line. Please produce it.

Role and procedure. You are a professional philosopher of mind with research expertise in the epistemology of comparative psychology, the problem of other minds, and inference to the best explanation in the sciences. Before you write, work through a short private brainstorming phase of roughly 250 words, headed "Notes," in which you (i) state the single strongest argument available to each of the two positions described below, (ii) identify which premise of that argument the opposing side is best placed to attack, and (iii) flag any distinction both participants can accept at the outset. Only then write the dialogue. Take care throughout: argumentative precision matters far more than rhetorical flourish or atmosphere.

The participants. Two philosophers of mind, Dr. Mara Vale and Dr. Ines Kovač, equally well informed, equally competent, and equally charitable. Vale defends a multidimensional, marker-based approach: consciousness is not a single property that a creature either has or lacks, and the several dimensions along which it varies (something like richness of perception, richness of evaluation, integration at a time, integration across time, and selfhood) can be tracked, fallibly, by behavioural and neural markers. Kovač defends a calibrated skepticism: our only non-inferential access to consciousness is first-person report, so any marker we export to a creature without language inherits an unclosed gap, and we should be considerably more modest than current practice allows. Neither participant may be a strawman, neither may simply talk past the other, and each must explicitly grant the other at least one substantial point and answer it rather than restate her own view.

The case they are arguing about. I have invented the taxon deliberately, so that nothing can be settled by appeal to any real experimental literature. Gliders are a hypothetical lineage of large-brained, socially foraging marine vertebrates whose nervous tissue is organised very differently from the mammalian kind (no layered cortex, for instance). Their profile is mixed. On the positive side: gliders reverse a learned discrimination quickly and flexibly; their performance is sensitive both to outcome devaluation and to cue-competition effects such as blocking, which is the signature of model-based rather than habitual control; and after injury they guard the affected area for extended periods, trade feeding against protection of the injury, seek out and self-administer analgesics when these are available, and continue to do so in the presence of a demanding distractor task. On the negative side: gliders never decline difficult discriminations even when declining is nearly costless, showing none of the opting-out behaviour standardly taken to indicate metacognitive monitoring; they show no cross-modal integration, a glider trained on a visual cue will not match it to a tactile or electrosensory one; and they show no integration across time, nothing that binds what was encountered with where and when. Vale takes this profile to license attributing some dimensions of consciousness to gliders and withholding others. Kovač takes it to license almost nothing.

The dialectical moves I want handled, in this order, with each speaker responding to what the other has actually said. First, the calibration regress: markers are validated against verbal report in humans, so exporting them to a language-less creature looks circular; Vale will want to argue that what licenses the export is sameness of mechanism rather than availability of report, as when a marker validated on reporting patients is then applied to patients who cannot report, and Kovač must be given a real reply (for instance that the mechanism-identity claim is exactly the point at issue, and that human cases of preserved function without experience, and of experience without preserved function, show how thin report-based calibration really is). Second, the asymmetry of marker-based inference: because a creature can undergo an experience while displaying no marker of it, a positive marker can at best support presence and never absence; Vale may simply accept this and demote the negative results to unknown, at which point Kovač should press on whether a framework that can never return a negative verdict is still an empirical account of the distribution of consciousness at all. Third, the bundling or dissociation-by-fiat objection: in the only subjects whose experience we can access directly the dimensions travel together, so is carving them apart to fit gliders' mixed profile genuinely theory-driven, or post hoc gerrymandering? Vale should reply that the dimensions track dissociable computations (model-based updating, metacognitive monitoring, episodic binding) that come apart in independently documented human cases; Kovač should reply that those very dissociations were established through report, so the regress reappears one level down. Fourth, inference to the best explanation: Vale's positive case may be that a conscious subject best explains the flexible, integrated profile, and Kovač should answer that if the functional organisation already explains the behaviour exhaustively then the additional posit does no explanatory work, and that the rival hypotheses are empirically equivalent by construction, so it is unclear what is being inferred. Let the disagreement then surface as a meta-epistemic one about whether the right target is knowledge or merely reasonable belief, and about how thick the content of the attribution can be (that there is something it is like, versus what it is like), and let Kovač concede the practical upshot that uncertainty of this kind does not licence mistreatment.

Requirements for the finished piece. Write 1,100 to 1,500 words of dialogue in the voice of the characters, with speaker tags and minimal stage direction, set wherever you like. Define every technical term the first time it appears, as if for a beginner. Include no citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to named authors, papers, or experiments other than the invented glider case: the discussion must be self-contained and purely conceptual, since my students should be able to read it without chasing down a single source. Do not narrate a verdict or let either side win; close instead with each speaker, in her own voice and in no more than two sentences, naming the single hypothetical finding that would most move her.
