I've been trying to say precisely what is wrong with being in an echo chamber, and the argument I've built has talked me into a conclusion I do not accept. I am working with the distinction now standard in the recent literature between an epistemic bubble, which merely omits opposing sources, and an echo chamber, which also actively discredits them, so that a member who does encounter an outsider has positive evidence, from sources they trust, that the outsider is corrupt or duped.

(1) A belief is epistemically defective if it is held in a way that is insensitive to counter-evidence, in the sense that its holder would dismiss contrary sources without examining them.
(2) The echo-chamber member's dismissal of outside sources is insensitive in exactly this way: it is produced by the chamber's discrediting mechanism rather than by any assessment of what the outsiders actually say.
(3) So the echo-chamber member's beliefs are epistemically defective.
(4) But the same description fits anyone who relies on others to identify which sources are worth attending to: the epidemiologist who sets aside a tobacco-industry report without reading it, the historian who ignores a Holocaust-denial pamphlet because of its provenance, the patient who takes the oncologist's word over the wellness blogger's. Each dismisses contrary sources unexamined, on the basis of trusted testimony from insiders.
(5) It is absurd to call all of them defective; declining to engage some sources is a requirement of epistemic hygiene rather than a failing.
(6) So there is nothing epistemically defective about echo-chamber membership as such, and the notion does no work that the bare claim about a member's sources being unreliable does not already do.

I would rather give up (1) than (5), but I want to know the price before I pay it, and I am not convinced the equivocation is in (1) rather than in how (2) applies it or in (4). A colleague has proposed replacing (1) with a different test: ask not whether an agent dismisses outsiders, but whether their trust is invariant under swapping contents, that is, whether they would trust the insiders and dismiss the outsiders just the same if the two groups exchanged the claims they make. The epidemiologist, the historian and the patient all fail that test, while the echo-chamber member passes it. I cannot tell whether this rescues the argument or quietly convicts people I do not want to convict, such as a child who defers to her parents on nearly everything.

Which step should I reject, and what does each alternative cost me? Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
