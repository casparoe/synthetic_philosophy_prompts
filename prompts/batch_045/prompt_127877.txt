I work as a software engineer on reinforcement learning infrastructure — offline RL, reward modelling, offline policy evaluation — and I have no formal philosophy background, only what I have picked up from blog posts and the odd argument with colleagues. I recently came across the experience machine as a way of testing theories of well-being, and the discussions of it I can find seem to turn on distinctions I have no grip on. I would like the whole thing restated in the vocabulary I actually reason in, together with an honest account of what does not survive the translation.

Here are the variations I keep coming back to, since the bare version (lifelong stream of maximally pleasant experience, you do not know you are in there) feels too easy to answer:

1. The standard machine: subjectively indistinguishable from a very good life, nothing outside improves.
2. The same, but you know you are in it and can unplug at any time, no deception involved.
3. The effort machine: it simulates not passive bliss but a life in which you struggle and accomplish difficult things — finishing a difficult piece of work, sustaining a long relationship — where the struggle and the accomplishment are real inside the simulation and real nowhere else.
4. The authored machine: you write the script soberly beforehand and cannot alter it once inside, versus an adaptive machine that gives you exactly what you want moment by moment.
5. The relational machine: the world is simulated but the other minds in it are other real plugged-in people, so the relationships are with real agents even though nothing you do together has effects outside.
6. A machine whose model of what is rewarding for you was trained on your own past behaviour, versus one with a hand-written hedonic target.

My gut says I would refuse all six, and I am suspicious of that gut: it feels like the same shape as refusing to move to a nicer city on the grounds that it would be too pleasant, which I do not take seriously. So I would rather have the structure than the verdict.

What I am asking for:

First, translate the debate as faithfully as you can. Give me the mapping for each major family of answer about well-being — the felt-quality or pleasure view, the desire-satisfaction view in both its actual and its informed-desire forms, the objective-list or perfectionist views, and the family of views which says that what matters includes whether your life is actually answerable to something, not merely how it seems from the inside — and tell me what each translated view predicts about plugging into each of the six variants. Use whatever fits from my working vocabulary: reward functions, learned reward models, policies and trajectories, return, the distinction between observation and intervention, on-policy versus off-policy evaluation, sim-to-real gap and distributional shift, wireheading, proxy metrics and Goodhart-style failure, model-based rollouts, exploration. Correct my vocabulary where it is too thin for the job, but in my terms first.

Second, the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which claims translate cleanly? Which translate only if you distort them — and say precisely what the distortion is, rather than quietly importing a philosophical assumption and calling it a translation? Which have no expression in my vocabulary at all? In particular: is the question of whether to plug in a question about the expected return of a policy under a fixed reward, or a question about which reward to install, and can my vocabulary even mark that difference? Can the gap between pleasure as something felt and reward as a training signal be stated in it at all, or does stating it already answer the question? Does variant 5 have a counterpart in multi-agent RL, and if it does, does that counterpart help or does it beg the question?

Finally, your view on the residue: where something will not translate, is that a limitation of the engineering vocabulary — something RL practice really cannot say and perhaps should be able to — or a sign that the philosophical claim was doing less work than it looked like it was doing? If it turns out that the whole literature is only a fight about proxy objectives, say so plainly and tell me what that would mean for someone deciding whether to plug in.
