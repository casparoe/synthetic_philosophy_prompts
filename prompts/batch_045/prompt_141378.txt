I'm trying to work out what I actually think about the man-of-his-time defence, and I'd rather start from my verdicts on particular cases than from an argument. Three things I want kept separate throughout: whether the act was wrong, how much blame the agent deserves, and whether it is fitting to commemorate him.

Jefferson, who owned slaves while writing that all men are created equal, at a time when contemporaries were arguing that slavery was wrong: not excused. But I still don't think it was wrong to name things after him, and I can't say why that verdict doesn't follow from the first.

Columbus, whose treatment of the Taíno was, as I understand it, objected to by people on his own side: not excused, and less excused than Jefferson.

Churchill, whose views on race and empire seem to me roughly those of his class and time, though held with unusual conviction: I blame him, but I blame him less than I blame Jefferson for slavery. That ranking feels wrong to me and I keep it anyway.

A Roman slaveholder in a world where I can name nobody who questioned the institution: I withhold blame, but I still want to say that what he did was wrong, and I don't find the gap between those two verdicts embarrassing.

A man enslaved as a child who, once free, earned his living catching other people's slaves: I blame him far less than the planter who once owned him, and I can't tell whether that is about his options, his formation, or simple pity.

A mid-level bureaucrat who processed the paperwork of a genocidal policy and would have been shot for refusing: I blame him, but barely. I seem to be pricing in what refusal would have cost.

Two agents in one system, with the same beliefs and the same acts, one of whom enforced them with relish and unusual cruelty: I blame the second much more, and I don't think the difference is in the acts.

My grandfather, who used racial slurs as a matter of course: I blame him less than a contemporary who does the same. But I also expect people in 2200 to excuse me for things I cannot currently see, and I don't want to be excused for those. That pair is the one I trust least.

Finally, of two men who profited from the same practice, I blame the one who privately doubted it more than the sincere believer. Yet if the excuse is for ignorance, the sincere believer is the one who needs it — and he is also the one whose character strikes me as worse.

My tentative principle: I excuse when the true moral view was neither available to someone in that position nor cheap to act on — availability doing the epistemic work, cost the practical work.

Can you state a principle, or two or three candidates, that captures these verdicts or nearly does so, motivating each clause by the case that forces it? Apply it to two cases I haven't listed: whether people alive now should be blamed for participating in practices whose wrongness is publicly argued but genuinely contested, and whether commemoration should track blame, achievement, or something else. Tell me which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which you would overturn. And say what would change your mind about the principle you give: which case or argument would make you abandon it, and where you think its reasoning is most likely to be failing.
