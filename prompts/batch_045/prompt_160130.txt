I lead a small engineering group that runs automated negotiations: a few dozen software agents, each acting for a different principal, bidding against one another in continuous double auctions for compute and logistics capacity. Before we let them commit real money, we want to be able to say something principled about what each agent can be assumed to know about the others. My own vocabulary is that of distributed systems and formal methods: crash and Byzantine fault models, failure detectors, synchronous versus asynchronous rounds, safety versus liveness, shared specifications versus local implementations, unreachable states, model checking a finite-state system against a temporal or epistemic formula, and the standard facts about common knowledge in a message-passing system — that simultaneous common knowledge of a non-trivial event cannot be attained when message delivery is unbounded or lossy, and that the usual workarounds are synchronized rounds, timestamps, or some version of eventual common knowledge. I can read formulas built from individual knowledge operators, everyone-knows operators, the common-knowledge operator, and p-belief operators. I have never read the game-theory literature that uses them.

What I want is a translation of that literature into my vocabulary, and then — this is the part I actually care about — an honest report on what the translation fails to carry.

The source material is the epistemic program in game theory: the project of reconstructing solution concepts as theorems about what players know or believe. Take rationalizability and iterated strict dominance, Nash equilibrium, correlated equilibrium, and a sequential refinement such as sequential or perfect Bayesian equilibrium, and restate the epistemic hypotheses that purport to characterize each of them in terms I use daily. Treat a game form as a specification; strategies as local programs; information sets as the indistinguishability of local states; a strategy profile as a run; higher-order mutual belief as the fixed point of an operator over the product of local state spaces; and each solution concept as a property to be model-checked against a system whose processes are the players. Be explicit, concept by concept, about which hypothesis is doing the load-bearing work — common knowledge versus common p-belief, belief with probability one versus truth in all epistemically accessible states, a shared prior versus merely a shared specification — and about what kinds of systems would satisfy each hypothesis. If some widely used hypothesis has no protocol-like reading at all, say so rather than stretching one.

Then the residue report. I have three guesses about where the translation breaks, and I would like you to test them rather than confirm them:

First, that the common-prior assumption — and the familiar style of argument for treating it as the default, associated with Harsanyi — has no real counterpart in my world, where the nearest objects are a shared initial state or an adversary that fixes the environment once and for all. If that is right, then results of the agree-to-disagree shape do not translate into claims about bargaining or trade but into something closer to a consensus result: agents who start from one prior and whose posteriors become commonly known cannot end up knowingly disagreeing. Tell me whether that is the right shape for the translated claim, or whether the analogy to consensus distorts it.

Second, that the demandingness of simultaneous common knowledge puts the solution concepts requiring common knowledge of rationality in the same position as protocols requiring coordinated attack: correct as mathematics, unimplementable in the asynchronous systems I actually run. If so, I want to know whether the literature's retreat to common belief, to p-belief, or to lexicographic hierarchies of beliefs is genuinely doing the kind of work that weakening a synchrony assumption does in protocol design — trading exactness for implementability — or whether the resemblance is a pun on the word common.

Third, that the worst failure is off the path of play: the requirement, in sequential refinements, that players hold beliefs conditional on events the equilibrium assigns probability zero. In verification, unreachable states are don't-care states, and no specification grades a process on what it would have done in a state the protocol cannot reach. Explain why game theorists insist on grading it anyway, and what that requirement becomes in my vocabulary — if it becomes anything.

For each of the three, say whether the suspicion survives contact with the material, correct what I have gotten wrong, and then answer the question I am really asking: is the residue a blind spot in my framework, something a protocol designer ought to be able to express and cannot, or is it evidence that the game-theoretic claim is less determinate than its formalism suggests?

One further wrinkle, which I would like handled inside the translation rather than set aside: my agents have no utility function anyone wrote down. They have prompts, fine-tuning, and a mandate from a principal, and whether they maximize anything is an empirical question about a learned system. Say what becomes of common knowledge of rationality when rationality is an empirical regularity rather than a specification, and whether the epistemic characterizations survive being read that way.

Please argue for your claims directly; I am not looking for a survey of the field or for anything to go read. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, with short labeled sections, and end with a single machine-readable line in this form:

\answer{prior} or \answer{common-knowledge} or \answer{off-path} or \answer{substitute: <your own label in eight words or fewer>}

naming which of the three sources of residue you judged to be the deepest — or, if you think the deepest one is somewhere I did not point, the substitute you would put in its place.
