I sit on my town's planning board (small New England town, basically all single-family zoning) and "housing is a human right" gets said at nearly every meeting — the genuinely annoying part is that it gets said by people arguing for opposite things. Last month a neighbors' group opposing a 12-unit building used it: housing is a human right, so the town has a duty to make sure whatever gets built is permanently affordable, and until it is we shouldn't approve market-rate units that'll just bid up rents around them. Then a YIMBY guy used the same seven words to argue we have to legalize apartments everywhere, and that requiring affordability covenants is itself a rights violation, since it makes new homes illegal unless somebody subsidizes them. I'm sure at least one of them is leaning on the slogan for something it doesn't say, but I can't say which, and that's a problem because I have to vote on this stuff.

So: lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making. Not "different people mean different things" hand-waving — an actual menu of readings I can line up next to each other, and for each one, whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. The axes I keep getting tangled on:

- Is it a right against the state, against whoever owns land, or against my town specifically? Does anything change if the duty-bearer is a legislature rather than a court?
- Is it a right to be handed a unit, a right that nobody arbitrarily deprive you of shelter, or a right to have a say in how the place you live gets produced — I think that last one (the "right to the city" version) is a different animal entirely and shouldn't be lumped in, but tell me if I'm wrong.
- Who holds it: people already living here, anyone who might want to move here, or households in general? And is it a right against being displaced or against being excluded, because those come apart.
- Does it come with a remedy, and does it specify a level — enough to not be homeless, enough to live in this particular neighborhood, enough to stay put?

The part I actually need: for each reading, would it support the neighbors' conclusion, the YIMBY's, both, or neither? My suspicion is that most readings are compatible with basically any policy and so aren't doing the work either speaker thinks they're doing, and I want to know which readings would have to be true for the sentence to actually settle something. Also tell me which reading each of my two speakers probably had in mind, and if it helps, say in the old claim-right / privilege / immunity vocabulary what kind of entitlement each reading is positing — I think "right" is doing three different jobs in that one sentence and that's where the confusion lives.

No citations or reading lists, I've read plenty of op-eds. Just clean up the distinctions.
