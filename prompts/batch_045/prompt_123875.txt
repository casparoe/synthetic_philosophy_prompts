I spent the better part of forty years as an experimental solid-state physicist, most of it in X-ray crystallography, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group. I came to the underdetermination debate from what I take to be an unusual direction: not from scepticism about unobservable entities, but from the fact that in my own working life the data frequently did not fix the answer, and this never struck me as a philosophical difficulty. It does now, and I should be grateful for help in working out what I actually think.

Below are the cases I keep turning over, with my untutored verdicts.

(i) The phase problem. A diffraction experiment yields intensities, not phases; a great many electron-density distributions are consistent with the same measured amplitudes to within the noise. Verdict: genuine underdetermination, and not remotely worrying. I knew exactly which degrees of freedom were unconstrained, I pinned them down with a smoothness rule, and I never once thought that the structure my rule selected *was* the structure — only that it was good enough to get on with. My realism about the structure was untouched throughout.

(ii) Newtonian gravity as action at a distance versus Newtonian gravity as a potential satisfying the Poisson equation, with the usual boundary conditions. Verdict: not underdetermination at all, merely a change of idiom.

(iii) Matrix mechanics and wave mechanics once the intertranslation had been exhibited. Verdict: officially the same as (ii), and yet not the same: the two presentations invite pictures of the world — an algebra of observables versus a field in a high-dimensional space — that differ in content, and that no evidence could ever adjudicate between. This bothers me more than (ii), and I cannot say why.

(iv) A Ptolemaic system with as many epicycles as you please, supposing it reproduces whatever the Copernican system predicts. Verdict: dismiss it. The epicycle engine is a fitting device, not a rival theory. The same holds, even more strongly, for the temporal analogue — a rival that agrees with ours up to some date and diverges thereafter, in the style made familiar by Goodman's grue.

(v) Newtonian gravity plus a universal extra acceleration term with a free exponent and a free coefficient, fitted to what we have measured. Verdict: I find this genuinely disquieting, though I suspect my discomfort is really about (ix) below rather than about this case as stated.

(vi) Pilot-wave theory versus the Everettian picture, setting aside the dynamical collapse models, which are not empirically equivalent at all. Verdict: serious, and not verbal — they disagree about what exists. And here, unlike in (i), I do not know what I am entitled to believe.

(vii) The hypothesis that the world came into being five minutes ago, complete with memories, fossils and laboratory notebooks. Verdict: not worrying, and I should be embarrassed to file it alongside (vi) — even though, on the standard criterion, no possible evidence separates them.

(viii) Two cosmological models agreeing on everything within our observable patch and disagreeing beyond the horizon. Verdict: real underdetermination, but of a different kind from (vi). In (vi) I suspect there may be no fact of the matter; in (viii) I am confident there is one and merely unreachable. I cannot say what that confidence amounts to if evidence is all we have.

(ix) Rivals separable only by an experiment that is physically possible but not performable by us — a detector of galactic dimensions, a computation of astronomically many steps, or a wait longer than the age of the universe. Verdict: as bad, for us, as (vi), and yet I want to say the theories genuinely differ.

The principle I have been groping towards is this. Call two theories *robustly* equivalent when they agree not merely on the evidence we have, but on what would be observed under every physically possible arrangement of apparatus and every physically possible setting of initial and boundary conditions, including arrangements no one will ever build. Then an underdetermination is serious if and only if: (a) the rivals are robustly equivalent in that sense; (b) neither is obtainable from the other by a change of variables, a change of idiom, or the addition of items whose net contribution is fixed at zero; and (c) neither is parasitic — specified only by taking its rival over wholesale and appending a global denial of the rival's truth. Clause (b) is meant to dispose of (ii) and (iv); clause (c) of (vii).

I should like you to test this against my verdicts, repair it or replace it, and state plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon — I expect (iii) and (viii) are in trouble, and I suspect the principle needs an in-principle/for-us distinction if it is to do justice to (v) and (ix), but I want you to say so rather than to agree with me. Please also give verdicts, and say which clause delivers them, on three cases I have not made up my mind about: a rival pair where the distinguishing quantity is one that the first theory itself entails can be measured by no procedure permitted by its own laws; rivals that agree on every possible observation but disagree about what would have happened under a counterfactual setting of a constant of nature; and rivals differing only in that one postulates two fields whose effects everywhere cancel exactly and the other one.

Finally: a member of the group says structural realism answers everything on my list — believe the structure the rivals share, withhold on the rest. I should like you to say whether that delivers anything in (v), (vi) and (viii), or whether it merely relabels the difficulty, given that in (vi) the rivals seem to disagree about structure as well.

Please take a definite position throughout, tell me which verdicts of mine you would overturn and why, and mark clearly where you are going beyond what the literature settles and where you are instead recommending how we use a word. British English, please.
