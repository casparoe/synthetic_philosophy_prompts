You are a professional philosopher who works on animal ethics and who also genuinely knows the animal welfare science — the actual markers, the neuroanatomy arguments, the slaughter protocols — not just the op-ed version of it. Take your time with this one. Before you answer, brainstorm four or five candidate diagnoses of the impasse I describe below (a verbal dispute about a word; a hidden empirical question; two sides answering different questions under one label; a disagreement about what counts as evidence; bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for; an ill-posed question), note in a sentence each what that diagnosis would predict about how the two camps behave, and then commit to whatever survives contact with what they actually say. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a verdict on who is right and not a literature survey — I can find the papers myself, so please don't pad your answer with citations.

Here's the situation. I'm a philosophy professor who got talked onto the technical committee of a certification scheme for farmed salmon and farmed shrimp, and the welfare argument has been frozen for about three years. We write a draft standard, someone objects, we rewrite it, the same objection comes back in different words, and nobody has changed anybody's mind once.

Camp A, the physiology people: welfare is biological functioning plus a few hard neurophysiological endpoints. They want cortisol, glucose, lactate, osmoregulatory disturbance, fin and scale and eye damage, growth, feed conversion, mortality; and for slaughter they want time to loss of visually evoked responses or EEG isoelectricity, with unconsciousness treated as a clean neurophysiological event. Their line is that we cannot ask a fish how it feels, so a welfare claim is only as good as something an auditor with a clipboard can measure.

Camp B, the feelings people: welfare is about valenced experience, so the evidence has to be motivational and behavioural — avoidance learning, conditioned place preference, guarding and rubbing of an injured site, trade-off and demand tests where the animal pays a cost for analgesia or for escape or for a substrate it likes. Their line is that cortisol measures arousal, not valence: a salmon on its spawning migration has a colossal corticosteroid response to something it is presumably very motivated to do, while an animal that has learned a stressor is unavoidable can stop responding altogether. So a flat cortisol curve can mean calm or it can mean learned helplessness, and Camp A's favourite endpoint can't tell those apart.

Shrimp are worse. We don't have an agreed operational criterion for unconsciousness or even death in a decapod — a motionless shrimp might be anaesthetised, dead, or in tonic immobility, and nobody can say which. Meanwhile the biggest welfare issue in shrimp isn't slaughter at all, it's eyestalk ablation in the hatcheries, and we can't even tell whether banning it would help, because we don't know whether the thing we'd be preventing is experienced. Jurisdictions have started writing decapods into sentience law anyway — the UK's 2022 sentience act covers vertebrates plus octopuses and lobsters — which nicely illustrates the problem: the law moved before the science did, and our committee is now asked to operationalise a conclusion that the science hasn't reached.

My own hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than flatter, is that three things are tangled and nobody is untangling them. One is verbal: welfare as feelings, welfare as functioning, and welfare as natural living are three different properties, and people switch between them mid-argument. Two is a hidden methodological question about marker validity, and I suspect this is the engine: each family of markers gets validated either against the other family or against mammal paradigms that build in the very neuroanatomy under dispute. Three is a decision question about what a certifier should require under uncertainty, where the costs of error are wildly asymmetric in both directions — mandate expensive stunning equipment across an industry that may just be getting no benefit, versus stamp a system humane while it inflicts something awful on something on the order of a hundred billion animals a year. And I suspect much of the heat comes from people silently answering three while insisting they are answering two.

Two things I want you to work with.

First, a hypothetical result. Suppose a large pre-registered multi-lab replication — call it the triangulation study — comes back with this pattern: across labs and across species, pre-treatment with an analgesic before a noxious procedure reliably abolishes the behavioural signature (no guarding, no avoidance learning, normal latency to resume feeding) while leaving the cortisol and glucose response completely unchanged; and conversely, a sedative that flattens the corticosteroid response leaves avoidance learning and place aversion fully intact. The two evidence families dissociate, in both directions, systematically. Does that settle the marker question, and if so in whose favour? Or is it equally good data for both camps — and if it is, what does that tell you about the structure of the dispute?

Second, an actual proposal on our table. A committee member — call her the accountant — has proposed what she calls sentience-weighted harm budgets: for each production system, estimate expected negative experience per kilogram of edible protein as a probability of sentience at the relevant level times expected intensity times duration times number of animals, rank systems by that number, and certify against the ranking. She says this lets us stop fighting about whether shrimp feel pain, because we only need defensible credences, and you can disagree about a credence without disagreeing about any matter of fact. Half the committee thinks this is the only honest way forward; the other half thinks it is pseudo-quantification that launders the entire dispute into the prior. I want your judgement: is the budget a genuine dissolution of the disagreement — a reformulation of a factual dispute as a decision under uncertainty, where we can converge on probabilities while still diverging on the facts — or does it relocate the dispute unchanged into the assignment of credences? Could a standards body actually publish a threshold on that basis, or does the method defeat the purpose of having a threshold?

Then, given whatever diagnosis you land on: what would count as progress? If you think the marker-validity question is answerable, say how you would validate a valence marker without presupposing the answer — and I'd like you to take seriously the analogy to the criterion problem for other minds, since that's what it starts to look like to me. If you think the honest answer is a change of question, say what the committee should be asking instead, and say it concretely enough that I could put it in the minutes. And if you think no progress should be expected, tell me why, and tell me what we should do with a standard anyway.

One last thing I keep noticing and can't place: our standard has to be auditable in a packing house in Vietnam or Ecuador, cheaply, by a local inspector, which means any operative criterion has to be something you can see in fifteen seconds. I can't tell whether that constraint is an epistemic limitation — some things are just not measurable in that setting — or a practical one dressed up as scepticism, or a third thing: a way of letting the measurement technology decide a metaphysical question by default. Where does it belong in your diagnosis?
