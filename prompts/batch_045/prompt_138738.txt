I work as the scientific secretary to the programme-grant panel of a national research council. We award five-year grants to what we call programmes rather than projects: a group gets funded to pursue a line of work, and at the mid-term and renewal points we decide whether to continue, cut, or redirect. The volume is real: about forty proposals a cycle, roughly ninety minutes of panel time each, a panel made of working scientists and clinicians with no philosophy of science training and no patience for it, and a requirement that every decision be written up in a page of plain language that a losing applicant can read without needing a primer. Since a row a few years ago about metric-gaming, we are also forbidden to use impact factors, citation counts, or h-indices anywhere in the deliberation, which removes the proxy everyone actually relied on and has left the panel falling back on vibes about whether a field feels exciting.

I want to turn Lakatos's methodology of research programmes into something we can actually run. I read him once in graduate school and remember the skeleton: a programme has a hard core its practitioners will not let be refuted, a belt of auxiliary hypotheses that absorbs the damage, a negative heuristic telling them what not to aim at, and a positive heuristic that is supposed to be a standing plan for building models rather than a list of repairs. A programme is progressive when its successive versions predict something new and some of the new things hold up, and degenerating when it only ever patches after the fact, with the belt growing to fit data everyone already had. Elimination is comparative and slow: a rival has to explain what its predecessor explained plus something more, and Lakatos says somewhere that there is no such thing as instant rationality, that a programme can only be appraised with hindsight over decades. My own temptation is crude: count the work packages that predict something not yet known against the ones that merely accommodate an anomaly someone already published, and fund whichever number is bigger. I suspect that is too crude in at least three ways — it rewards volume of prediction over weight, it has no answer for a group proposing to spend five years building the instrument or the mathematics needed to make the prediction at all, and it gives us nothing for a consortium that generates predictions continuously but absorbs every failure as a technicality, which looks to me less like a programme than like machinery for never being wrong.

So please give me the procedure itself, in the order we would run it: the questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it returns a clear no, and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values or temperamental patience rather than method. I need it to work from the documents we actually have — the proposal, the track record section, and the group's own account of what they would abandon if forced — and to say how to recover the hard core and the positive heuristic from those, including what to do when a proposal is written in a way that conceals them, since applicants quickly learn that admitting to a hard core sounds dogmatic. I would also like it to be honest about what it cannot do: I assume it cannot compare programmes in different fields, cannot say whether a core is true, and cannot tell us how long to wait, and I would rather have those limits named in the write-up than have a panel member discover them mid-meeting. Then run it on three anonymised cases from our last cycle. Programme A is a thirty-year-old line of work, still prolific, whose last three novel predictions failed and which now proposes another two years of re-deriving old measurements under a revised correction term; the panel reveres its founder, who is retiring, and I do not want the answer I expect. Programme B proposes to point an automated pipeline at a large class of datasets, will produce forecasts constantly, and treats every miss as a specification issue to be retrained away; I am unsure whether this is even an appraisable programme or whether the procedure should return a different kind of verdict, something like nothing here to evaluate. Programme C has produced no confirmed novel fact in nine years and says the blocking computation is not yet feasible, but its five-year plan is a genuinely ordered sequence of simplified models that would eventually make the prediction, and it is the one case where I think hindsight might vindicate them. If the procedure's verdicts on these three are ones I should overrule, say so, and say which verdict goes and why.
