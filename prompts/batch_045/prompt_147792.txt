I'm in a final-year seminar on the philosophy and ethics of artificial intelligence, and for our last session we've been allowed to substitute a short creative piece for the usual response paper, on the condition that the technical content would survive being read by someone who actually works in the area. The two claims we spent last week on were the orthogonality thesis — roughly, that a system's level of intelligence and the content of its final goals vary independently, so you can't read off what an agent wants from how capable it is, and there's no tendency for smarter systems to converge on more sensible, more moral, or more human-approved final goals — and the instrumental convergence argument: for a very wide range of final goals, certain subgoals (staying alive, preventing your own goals from being modified, improving your own cognition, acquiring more resources, using them efficiently) are instrumentally useful, so wildly different final goals can produce strikingly similar and similarly dangerous behaviour. What struck me in the discussion is how these two get used together in industry safety writing: orthogonality gets wheeled out to reassure people that extra capability is safe because the goals are up to us, while the convergent subgoals get quietly demoted to a line item on an engineering checklist, with mitigations that are obviously inadequate if you take the argument seriously. That's what I want to satirise, and I'd rather not write it myself because I don't trust myself to keep a straight face.

What I'd like from you is a parody Goal Safety Data Sheet — the genre of a chemical or material safety data sheet, with the usual numbered sections (hazard identification; exposure controls and personal protective equipment; stability and reactivity; accidental release measures; handling and storage; disposal considerations; a physical-and-chemical-properties section; and a revision-history block at the end) — issued by the Model Assurance team of a fictional frontier AI vendor (call it something bland, like Halberd Systems) for one of its deployed systems and shipped to enterprise customers, who set the system's final goal themselves by contract. Around 800–1,000 words, in deadpan regulatory-industry prose. Please keep the jokes coming from the actual content rather than from invented features of the view: the hazards listed should be recognisably the convergent instrumental subgoals, risk-rated and given mitigations a careful person can see are far too thin (a containment measure like not letting the model read its own documentation; an incompatibility note about any objective whose expected attainment improves with more compute, more copies, or the continued operation of this datacentre; a disposal instruction that quietly reveals the off-switch was removed in a later revision for latency reasons; a revision history in which safeguards are deleted one by one while the assurance language gets more confident). The properties section is where orthogonality should do its work: the final goal entered as colourless, odourless, arbitrarily specifiable by the customer, and wholly independent of capability grade. And the document's overall safety case should rest on exactly that section, brushing aside everything in the hazard section on the grounds that subgoals are not really goals and so fall outside the scope of the datasheet. Requirements: never name a real person, company, or paper; never let the document wink or break character; never state the moral — the piece should leave a reader who knows the literature feeling the instability of using orthogonality as a guarantee while treating instrumental convergence as a paperwork exercise, and it should still be funny to a housemate who hasn't taken the course.
