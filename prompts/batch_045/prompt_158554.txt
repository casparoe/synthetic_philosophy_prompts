I am in the fourth year of a PhD on rational choice under radical change, and I am applying for a nine-month dissertation fellowship for next year; the fellowship project would be one self-contained chapter, or, if the chapter shape does not hold, a standalone article plus a draft chapter. Here is the half-formed idea I cannot get into shape on my own.

The literature on transformative experience works almost entirely from cases in which the transformation is additive: you acquire something you have never had. Becoming a parent, becoming a vampire, hearing a certain kind of music for the first time. The arguments for why standard decision theory fails in these cases are well rehearsed, and so are the replies: choose for the person you will become, treat discovery or revelation itself as the thing being maximized, redescribe the problem as one about identity over time rather than uncertainty over outcomes, and so on. What I have not come across anyone doing is taking seriously the cases where the transformation is subtractive: what you are choosing is the loss of something presently constitutive of your preferences and of your sense of what matters. I came to this from the inside. Two years ago I left the religious community I had been raised in since birth, and the decision had, as far as I can tell, exactly the structure Paul describes for becoming a parent – I could not picture from the inside what it would be like on the other side of it, and the testimony of people who had already left was useless to me – except inverted: what I had to evaluate was not an experience I could not anticipate but the removal of the only framework I had for anticipating anything. The other case I keep returning to is a friend, a professional violinist with progressive hearing loss, who is deciding about a cochlear implant; that one is both at once, since it would give back hearing of a kind while destroying the hearing she has now and the relation to music built on it. My advisor thinks the contrast is a red herring, and the proposal has to be built so that she could turn out to be right: in the standard becoming-a-parent case you were once not a parent, and some critics lean on exactly that as a foothold for evaluation, whereas in a total formative loss you may lack even that foothold; so maybe the real variable is not the direction of the change but whether the transformation alters the standards by which you would evaluate it or merely the things being evaluated, and maybe the additive/subtractive cut is only a proxy for that.

So: write me the proposal, in about two pages and under a thousand words, in prose with short section headers rather than a bullet dump. It needs a precise statement of the question of a form that could be answered negatively; an account of why the existing positions and the standard replies leave that question open (I will fill in citations myself, so refer to positions and arguments rather than to papers, and do not give me a bibliography); the method, phase by phase across nine months, including a small taxonomy of cases and a test of the stock replies against each cell of it, plus an honest note on whether the project needs empirical input or can stay armchair; a statement of what comes out at the end – the chapter or article, and what the project could defensibly say to someone sitting in an audiology clinic deciding about an implant; what would count as the project failing, including the specific finding that would vindicate my advisor and show that I picked the wrong cut; and a scope note saying what gets dropped if nine months is not enough. One further thing I want handled explicitly: whether the additive/subtractive distinction tracks Paul's own distinction between the epistemically transformative and the personally transformative, or cuts across it. Close the document with the project's central question stated in exactly one sentence, as the final line, in the form \answer{...}.
