Hope you can help me think something through before our department meeting on Tuesday — I'm a philosophy professor (early modern, mostly), and this is one of those situations where I can feel that there's a real argument in there somewhere but I keep talking in circles.

We're a tiny philosophy department, seven people. Teaching assignments rotate according to a schedule drawn up in the mid-nineties by the founding chair, who — everyone agrees on this, and there's someone who was there and swears its true — set the original order by pulling names out of a softball hat. It's been patched since then as people arrived and left and one person swapped a year with another, but the bones of it are still the hat.

A new colleague, very sharp and not shy, has said publicly that the rotation has no authority over him. His argument: the original allocation was pure chance, so there's no reason it should bind anyone now; he should teach the courses he's best at and so should the rest of us. When someone said "but it's the system we've got," he said that's only a polite way of saying the chair can make life difficult for whoever opts out — that people comply out of convenience or low-grade fear, and that dressing that up as obligation is a kind of fraud. He now wants us to add a line to the department handbook recording that the rotation's origin was a random draw, for the sake of transparency.

A third colleague has proposed something she calls "open conventions": every standing arrangement gets re-argued from scratch each year with no presumption in favour of whatever we did last year, and any decision has to carry a written rationale that anyone can challenge. She thinks this answers the new guy's complaint, since nothing would bind us merely by being old.

I have two reactions I can't reconcile. First: the new colleague seems both right about the history and wrong to conclude anything from it — surely nearly every arrangement anywhere has some accident or somebody's muscle at its origin, and if that dissolved obligation we'd have nothing left. Second: "open conventions" makes me nervous in a way I can't put my finger on. It sounds fairer than the hat, but I suspect it would actually be worse.

So: what is going on here, and what should I say at the meeting? Concretely — is there a defensible reason to keep the rotation that isn't just "it's old"? Is the new guy's objection more or less on target than he thinks, and is the transparency line in the handbook a good idea or a bad one? And should I back or fight the open-conventions proposal, and on what grounds? Please skip the generic mediation advice.

One more thing: whatever you tell me, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to break, and what argument or consideration would make you change your mind on each of those three questions.
