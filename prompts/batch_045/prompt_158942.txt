I convene a fortnightly reading group on the metaphysics of consciousness — a few graduate students, a neuroscientist, and some people from adjacent departments — and last session we spent the whole two hours on the combination problem for constitutive micropsychist views (the family of positions on which macroscopic conscious subjects are wholly made up of microscopic ones) without converging on anything. What stuck with me afterwards was an offhand remark from a friend who works in the civil service: she said the research programme reminded her of her own department, where every structural objection is answered by creating a new unit whose entire function is to have authority over the objection, and where the two options no one is permitted to name out loud are admitting that the merger really did produce something new, and quietly deciding that the merged entity was never there in the first place. I would like to see whether that joke survives being written out at length.

Write it as an in-universe document: a set of numbered caseworker guidance notes, eight to ten items and 700-900 words, issued by the Office for Phenomenal Integration under a title of your choosing, in a deadpan civil-service register — the voice of an agency that has been administering this for decades and finds the whole subject faintly tedious. Assume that constitutive micropsychism is official doctrine in this world and that the Office exists to make it workable. The humour has to come from the actual structure of the problem, so the notes should work through: the difference between composing qualities and composing subjects, with the second quietly treated as the harder file; the Ministry's standing ruling that the bonding relation is not itself experience-bearing and does not itself stand in any need of bonding, so that applications for a second-order bond are summarily refused; why citizens cannot introspect the micro-level qualities, and how petitions about what resolution of experience is even funded are disposed of; boundary disputes over which collections of micro-subjects are entitled to a macro-subject at all; reports of intrusive experience from citizens claiming to undergo experiences that are not theirs; and decombination — the citizen who, after callosal surgery, applies to be registered as two subjects, together with the symmetrical difficulty for the minority view that takes the basic subject to be the cosmic one and must explain how it came to be many. The target is not any person but a practice: the way each structural objection is met with a fresh fundamental posit, and the way each of the two escape hatches — brute emergence of the macro-subject, or eliminativism about it — cancels the original motive for the theory. The Office should never say this and should never wink; a reader who knows the literature should be able to tell that the author does, and a reader who does not should still be able to follow it. Please do not cite or name any real philosopher, and do not include anything a writer without internet access would have to look up.

When the document is finished, add a note in your own voice of at most 150 words on where the piece leans on a contested reading of the dialectic or exaggerates for effect, and — the nearest equivalent here to saying what would change its mind — which reader would be entitled to say the satire misses them, and why. My own guess is that the two entitled readers are the cosmopsychist who takes the macro-subject as basic and therefore inherits only the decombination version of the problem, and the micropsychist who simply bites the eliminativist bullet about macroscopic subjects; tell me if you think I have picked the wrong ones, or missed a better candidate.
