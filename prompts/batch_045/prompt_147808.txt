I direct the appellate unit of a legal-aid office. Four staff attorneys file roughly two motions or briefs a week each, on deadlines the court sets; I sign and file everything, and I have about half an hour per draft. Over the past year they have all begun drafting with an AI assistant, and my problem is not that the work is wrong. It is that I increasingly cannot tell whether the person who produced a draft could defend it. I receive correct motions whose author cannot say which holding is load-bearing, cannot say what would have to be different for the motion to fail, and did not notice that the assistant quietly omitted the one contrary decision in our jurisdiction. I also notice the problem in myself: I am now tempted to check drafts by asking the same assistant, so neither of us is positioned to catch the failures that matter, since the loud ones I can catch unaided and the silent ones look exactly like everything else.

I have been working with a rough three-way distinction — trusting the answer, trusting the route by which it was reached, and trusting one's own ability to tell when it is wrong — and I suspect the third is the one that erodes first and the only one my signature actually rests on. Sharpen this distinction or replace it with a better one, then turn it into a procedure my office can run on every assisted draft before I sign it.

The procedure should give me:

- Ordered questions, each with what counts as a sufficient answer, and a time budget that fits inside half an hour.
- A way to separate, in language an overworked attorney can apply without any theory, between getting the right answer and grasping why it is right — while being explicit that the procedure only tests proxies for the second, and saying where those proxies break.
- An explicit treatment of the asymmetry between failures the author can detect unaided and failures that are invisible to someone who no longer has the understanding the tool replaced, with each question labelled by which kind it targets.
- The points where the procedure stops with a no, meaning it goes back to the attorney or is filed with something disclosed, and the points where it hands the decision to me because it turns on values rather than fact.
- What to do when the honest answer to a question is that I cannot tell and the filing deadline is tomorrow.
- What the procedure cannot do, including anything it can do about my own dependence on the tool when I review.

Then run it on three cases of the shapes I actually see: a motion to suppress that is very likely to win, produced in twenty minutes, whose author cannot articulate why the controlling case applies; a procedural-history section that is boilerplate, machine-drafted, and spot-checked against the docket; and a novel argument the attorney found with the assistant, then verified against the reporters and can state what would defeat it. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like — keep it that way, and tell me what I am supposed to do with that result. Finally, flag anywhere the procedure will systematically misfire by manufacturing the appearance of understanding rather than the thing itself.
