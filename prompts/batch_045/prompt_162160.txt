I'm on my college's academic-policy committee and we vote Thursday on this. A friend of mine who directs the writing center and I spent about two hours going in circles about it after the last meeting, so I'd rather circulate something than keep arguing with her.

The proposal: every first-year writing seminar would have to break the final 15-page paper into four graded checkpoints (thesis paragraph, source list, rough draft, revision) instead of assigning the paper in week 1 and collecting it in week 15. The case for it is that students reliably plan against themselves: in week 1 they fully intend to start early, in week 14 they wish they had, and the odd part is that the students who most need the structure are also the ones most certain in week 1 that they don't.

Her objection, which I couldn't shake off: staged deadlines don't fix weakness of will, they remove the occasion for it. A student who never has to face an unbroken 15-page assignment never builds whatever it is that lets you begin one, and we'd be graduating people who function fine as long as somebody else installs the guardrails. I said that by that standard using a calendar is a crutch. She said there's a real difference between binding yourself and being bound by an authority, and I think she's got something there.

Two things I want the memo to deal with. First, pilot data from a nearby college: staged deadlines improved completion and final grades, but the effect was much bigger for students who opted into the structure than for students it was imposed on. Both of us claimed that as evidence for our own side and I dont think either of us had earned it. Second, student evaluations of the pilot were brutal, and I can't tell whether to discount them — the complaints come from the same present-stage self whose judgment the whole proposal treats as unreliable — or to treat them as the only real data we have about what the policy is like from the inside.

Please draft the decision memo I'll send out before the vote: a definite recommendation (adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions, and if conditions, say which), reasoning the writing-center people could actually respond to rather than just restate committee values, a note on what we should look at again in two years, and the strongest version of her position stated fairly enough that she'd recognize it. Don't hand me the menu of options — pick one and defend it, and tell me which part of your recommendation is most fragile.
