I am trying to settle a dispute with a colleague about what the no-free-lunch theorems for search and supervised learning actually establish, and I would like a careful answer that engages with the formalism rather than with authorities.

Set up the problem this way. An agent must choose, before seeing any data, a predictive procedure together with a weighting over the set of candidate data-generating processes it takes seriously; call this pair the agent's stance. Define the stance's leverage as the difference between two numbers: the agent's expected accuracy on future observations when the expectation is taken with respect to its own weighting, and its expected accuracy when the expectation is taken with respect to the uniform weighting over the same candidate set. Where the candidate set is infinite, read the uniform weighting as the limiting maximum-entropy weighting, assuming one exists.

My colleague's argument, which I have been unable to refute, runs as follows. The no-free-lunch theorems show that when performance is averaged uniformly over all possible objective functions, every algorithm does exactly as well as every other one. Averaging over all possible objective functions is simply the special case of computing leverage in which the candidate set is the set of all possible worlds and the weighting is uniform. Uniformity is the only weighting that is neutral among hypotheses, and an agent with no evidence ought to be neutral. Therefore every stance has leverage zero; the choice of prior in Bayesian inference is bookkeeping with no expected predictive payoff; and the problem of induction is not merely unsolved but settled in the negative, so that the rational response to any prediction problem is indifference among the outcomes.

I find the argument compelling and the conclusion unacceptable, so I want to know which step fails.

1. Is the averaging result a claim about the world, or a claim about a choice of measure? If the latter, does the symmetry it establishes survive restricting the candidate class to a proper subset, or imposing a nonuniform weighting on it, or does it hold only for a class that is closed under the relevant permutations and weighted uniformly?

2. Is there, in fact, a canonical neutral weighting over the space of all possible data-generating processes? If the answer depends on how that space is parametrized or encoded, or if no limiting maximum-entropy weighting exists in the cases of interest, what does that do to the argument above?

3. If every stance has zero leverage, then a Solomonoff-style weighting that concentrates on simple worlds has zero leverage, and so does the uniform weighting. Yet it is often said that universal priors carry convergence guarantees that a uniform weighting lacks, and that these guarantees hold regardless of which world is actual. Is that compatible with no-free-lunch, or is it a sign that leverage as I have defined it is not the quantity I want?

Please answer in short prose under each numbered question, then add a brief closing paragraph stating what you take the single weakest step in my colleague's argument to be.

Conclude with a machine-readable verdict on its own final line, chosen from exactly these three options: \answer{symmetry holds} if the argument is correct as stated, \answer{symmetry fails} if the theorems do not deliver leverage zero for the stances actually in question, or \answer{ill-posed} if the argument cannot be evaluated as stated because one of its steps is not well defined.
