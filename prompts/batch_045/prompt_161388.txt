I teach a short professional-ethics module to trainee solicitors, and over the last couple of years I have been collecting cases out of their placement reports that all seem to me to have the same shape, without my being able to find a label that fits them. The shape is this: a question is asked and is answered with something true, sincerely believed, and literally responsive to the question as asked — no playing on an ambiguity, no quietly narrowing the question into a different one, and no withholding of anything the hearer was in a position to demand, since every question they actually asked received a complete answer — and yet the hearer ends up believing something false, and the speaker chose those particular words because they foresaw exactly that and wanted it. What I cannot get past is that the deception seems to run on the speaker's truthfulness rather than in spite of it. One trainee described asking a senior partner, who has an entirely deserved reputation for never asserting anything she cannot document, whether a client meeting had gone badly; the partner said, accurately, that the client had signed the engagement letter without asking for changes. The trainee had been in the room, knew the partner knew what had happened there, and also knew that the partner's habit of saying only what she could prove was precisely what made the answer land as reassurance. Another trainee, on a hospital placement, described a consultant asked by a family whether there was any hope; he said, truthfully, that some patients with this pattern on the scan do go on to recover consciousness, knowing that this family, who had watched him answer hard questions bluntly for two weeks, would hear it as good news. In both cases nothing false was said, nothing would have to be walked back, and the speaker's credibility came out of the exchange not spent but earned — it was the mechanism. And if either hearer later spelled out what they had concluded, the speaker could deny ever having suggested it, and the denial would be true.

The feature I keep circling is that the practice is self-sealing. With an ordinary lie there is a false statement on the record, something that can be pointed at, retracted, and used as evidence about the speaker's trustworthiness; with the more familiar kind of misleading-by-selecting-true-facts there is at least the awkwardness of the omission to be called out. Here there is nothing to point at: every sentence survives being checked, the generalisation the hearer relied on is true, and the speaker's reputation for truthfulness is not eroded by the exchange but is what made it work, so it can be repeated indefinitely and the usual defence of discounting an unreliable source is unavailable. It even seems to me that the hearer is left worse off than if they had simply been lied to, because a discovered lie gives you a reason to go back and check everything else, whereas this gives you nothing to check and nobody to blame.

So my question is whether there is an established concept for this, or a literature that treats it, and I should say that a name on its own would not satisfy me. I have come across the term paltering for producing a false impression out of literally true statements, a little about false implicature, and the view that the distinctive vice of the spin-doctor is not falsity but indifference to how things are; but none of these seems to me to describe the mechanism, since in my cases the speaker is tracking the truth fastidiously and the hearer's error is not so much an implicature the speaker invites as an inference the speaker foresees and depends on, drawn from a generalisation about the speaker that is perfectly correct. I would like to know what the nearest concepts are, exactly how the thing I have described differs from each of them, and which of them fits best; and if nothing fits, I would like a term coined for it, with a definition and clear boundary cases — in particular an account of what separates it from paltering, from withholding, and from mere plausible deniability. The reason I care is that the standard arguments for treating misleading as a lesser fault than lying seem to me to come apart here: it is supposed to leave the hearer a route to the truth, to respect their agency by making them a party to the inference, and to leave the speaker's own relation to the truth intact, and in these cases all three look true and yet none of them seems to count for anything.
