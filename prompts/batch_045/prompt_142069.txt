I've been edging toward a Gödelian conclusion about time and I cant tell how much skin it has in the game, so: tell me what would count against it. The version I hold (picked up from a visiting talk in my phil-physics seminar, then argued over with a friend who does cosmology) goes like this. The interesting moral of Godel's 1949 rotating-universe paper isn't the suggestion that our own cosmos rotates — obviously it doesn't: the model needs a negative cosmological constant tuned against the matter density, the dust isn't expanding, and there's no observed global rotation. The moral is supposed to be modal. General relativity admits a perfectly regular, singularity-free, geodesically complete model in which the matter is at rest while the local inertial frames rotate with respect to it, and in which there is no way to slice the whole spacetime into one ordered succession of nows — no global time coordinate that increases along every future-directed path — because the light cones tip far enough that some future-directed trips come back to where they started. If the theory has room for a world like that, then an objective, mind-independent lapse of time isn't something the theory requires or even represents; passage must be something we bring to the world rather than something we find in it, and the intuition that time flows becomes something to be explained away rather than respected. My friend says this only shows that passage isn't a global, observer-independent structure, which no one who takes relativity seriously should have wanted anyway, and that what the model really lacks is a shared present, not becoming as such. I dont know how to settle that, and part of what I want from you is a verdict on which of the several things the model lacks is actually load-bearing for the argument: a global time function, a preferred foliation, global hyperbolicity, an ordering of past and future that every observer agrees on, or something else — and why that one, and not the others, is supposed to matter to the A-theorist.

Three cases I made up while failing to sleep, which you should use or discard as you like. (1) The bubble model: a spacetime that matches every observation we've actually made — expansion, the microwave background, all of it — inside our past light cone, but which is smoothly joined to a Godelian region far outside it, so the ugly part is real and yet permanently beyond our reach (nobody can get there, nobody can come back from it). Does the modal argument still get its conclusion? Does the premise need the bad region to be admitted by the laws, or admitted and relevantly accessible? And if the region is unreachable, is it even a claim about time to say that time is ideal there? (2) The near-miss model: a spacetime with no closed timelike curves at all and yet no global time function either — light cones that tip further and further, so that with enough fuel an observer can return arbitrarily close to her own past without ever landing in it. If that model is worse for the A-theorist than the Godel one, then time travel was a red herring and the load-bearing feature is something else entirely; if it's no worse, I want to know why not. (3) The rival: someone hands you a theory that reproduces every test general relativity has passed but adds a preferred foliation, and it's arguably simpler or handles some other problem better (quantising gravity, say). Does that count against the Godelian conclusion, or can the Godelian just reply that the extra structure is unobservable surplus?
