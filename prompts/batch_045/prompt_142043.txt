I retired four years ago from a state water agency — thirty-odd years of flood hydrology, mostly spillway sizing — and since then I've been going to a Tuesday morning reading group that a philosophy grad student named Nadia runs out of the public library. We've drifted onto AI risk, and one of the other regulars, Doug (retired control engineer, and a considerably harder case than me), has an argument that I'm pretty sure proves far too much, but I can't locate the bad step. I've written it out below in what I think is his best form, numbered, because he himself never numbers anything.

Setup: the question is whether to deploy a system S that's general enough that its own outputs change which situations come up later — reservoir releases that change what farmers plant downstream, which changes what a safe release is next season. Call that the closed-loop feature. Our group calls the following Doug's argument.

(1) We may deploy S only if we have a safety case that is a *guarantee*: a justification whose force doesn't come from an empirical guess about how S will behave in situations unlike any we've tested or enumerated.

(2) Guarantees come in exactly two kinds and no more: worst-case (holds in every situation in the relevant class) and average-case (holds with high probability under some distribution over situations).

(3) Worst-case guarantees are unavailable for any system whose situation space is open-ended, since there will always be situations nobody enumerated — and for a system with the closed-loop feature, the system's own policy partly determines which situations those are.

(4) So only the average-case kind is on the table for S.

(5) An average-case guarantee is a claim about a probability, and a probability is defined only relative to a distribution over situations.

(6) For S the distribution over situations isn't given in advance of the decision: it depends on S's own policy and on what other agents do in response to S.

(7) A decision can't be justified by a number that the decision itself fixes. So the average-case probability of catastrophe for S has no determinate value at the point of decision, and can't be used to justify deploying.

(8) So (by 3, 4, 7) no guarantee is available for S, and by (1) we may not deploy.

(9) But nothing in (3)–(7) is special to S. Take "don't deploy", or "pause", or "ship only narrow systems". That choice also changes what other labs build and how fast, and so it too fixes a distribution over situations; the probability of catastrophe under not-deploying is equally indeterminate.

(10) So no option whatever has a usable safety case, and the whole worst-case/average-case apparatus can't rank any two options against each other.

That last bit is what makes me suspicious: on my reading it says my old agency's rules were nonsense, and it says "pause" is unjustifiable exactly as loudly as it says "deploy" is. I tried the obvious fix — conditional guarantees ("S is safe provided the envelope monitor works and the sandbox holds") — and Doug says that runs into what he calls the second-guarantee problem: the monitor is itself a thing that can fail in ways nobody enumerated, so its guarantee is either another conditional, which just pushes the problem down one level, or it bottoms out in an assumption nobody warranted, and (1) said the force can't come from an empirical guess. If he's right, (1) plus the second-guarantee problem eats every conditional guarantee there is. I also floated the envelope gambit — worst case *within a validated envelope*, with the envelope's integrity monitored — which he says is just the second-guarantee problem wearing a hat.

Where I stand: if I have to pick, I'd rather keep (3) than anything else, because in thirty years I never once had a true worst case, only a design envelope with load factors and a story about why the tails were unreachable; and I'd rather keep the endogeneity point in (6). I'm most suspicious of (2), but (2) looks like a taxonomy and I can't tell whether giving it up costs me anything at all. Nadia thinks it all turns on (1). Neither of us can say why, and Doug just smiles.
