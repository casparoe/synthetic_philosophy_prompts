I teach a short module on collective action to policy masters students, and I am trying to work out what I actually mean by "Moloch" — the loose label for a multipolar trap or a race to the bottom — by testing it against cases where my gut reaction is already settled. I don't want references or a literature survey, and I don't care about the provenance of the term; I want the principle, if there is one, in a form I could hand to students. Please take the stipulated facts in each case as given rather than querying them.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Two coffee shops at opposite ends of a high street. Both owners would rather charge £4.20 for a flat white than £2.90; if either raises prices alone it loses most of its trade; both end up with thin margins, worse coffee and seventy-hour weeks. My verdict: not Moloch. That is competition, and the customers are better off.

2. Two neighbouring states. Each spends about 4% of GDP on the military; each would be equally secure and substantially richer at 2%; neither can disarm alone without inviting coercion. My verdict: Moloch, the paradigm case.

3. Tutoring for selective secondary entry. The places are fixed at around 1,200 and the examination functions essentially as a ranking device; families spend several thousand pounds a year for two years; the same children get the same places. My verdict: Moloch. I note that the tutors are paid, so the money is not literally destroyed — and that consideration does not move me.

4. Commercial litigation. Two companies in a contract dispute each spend roughly £2m on lawyers and experts; the spending roughly cancels out in its effect on who wins. But I believe the adversarial process is what forces the documents into the open, and that a capped inquisitorial alternative would produce worse judgments. My verdict: not Moloch — or, if it is one, then a trap we should keep. I hold this uneasily.

5. The latency race. Trading firms spend hundreds of millions on microwave towers, colocation and engineers; being fastest is purely positional, since if all of them slowed down the same trades would occur at the same prices. But the byproduct is tighter spreads and deeper order books, which every pension fund in the country benefits from. My verdict: Moloch, but genuinely less bad than case 2, and possibly worth keeping.

6. The off-ramp pair — the one I cannot reconcile. (a) A beef farm declines to use a routine additive that all its competitors use; it survives and keeps its customers, but is permanently about a quarter smaller than it would otherwise have been. My verdict: not a trap, just a costly choice the farmer can afford to make. (b) A mid-sized consumer brand stops buying targeted online advertising while its competitors continue; it loses most of its customer acquisition and ceases trading within two years, although its product is as good as ever. My verdict: Moloch. My worry: (a) and (b) differ only in how much unilateral exit costs — shrink by a quarter versus cease to exist — and I have no non-arbitrary place to put the line. What about a farm that shrinks by 70%, or a brand that limps on for five years?

7. A security dilemma inside a firm. Two divisions each build an overlapping internal data platform because each believes the other is about to encircle it; given accurate figures, both would stop tomorrow; the waste is real and large. My verdict: I want to say Moloch, but the remedy here is not a binding contract, it is a shared spreadsheet — both sides already want to stop, they simply do not know it. And on a literal reading of my own first clause this is not Moloch at all: given what each division believes, each prefers to build.

8. The guardians' race. Issuers of a certain class of debt choose and pay for their own credit assessor, and assessors compete to be the most accommodating; the credibility of the assessment is a shared asset that each act of leniency depletes. The usual remedy for a trap is a binding rule with an enforcer, but here the enforcer is appointed by, funded by and recruited from the same industry. My verdict: the worst case on my list, and it is the recursion that makes it bad rather than the amount of money involved. I also suspect this one may really be a commons problem wearing a trap's coat.

My tentative formulation is this: a situation exhibits Moloch when (i) there is an alternative outcome that every participant prefers to the one they are in, (ii) no participant can move to it unilaterally without becoming significantly worse off than the others, and (iii) the competitive activity yields no benefit to anyone outside the group that compensates for its cost. Clause (iii) is what lets me call the coffee shops fine and the arms race not. But I notice that clause (iii) makes the whole notion hostage to contested empirical questions — whether tutoring teaches anything, whether microwave towers really tighten spreads, whether lawyers' fees really surface the truth — and that "significantly" in clause (ii) has no non-arbitrary setting.

What I would like back is the following, in British English:

- Test my formulation against all eight cases, say which clause fails where, and either repair it or replace it. If no single principle covers them, tell me how many distinct phenomena I am conflating and where the fault lines run.
- For each principle you offer, state it precisely, note which of my cases motivates each clause, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate — and why I should give up that verdict rather than another.
- Give its verdict on a case I have not settled: in a firm of management consultants everyone answers client email within the hour because everyone else does, each would prefer a norm of next-day replies, whichever firm slows down unilaterally loses clients, and the cost is burnout and shattered work. No money is spent, no resource is consumed, and there is no one with whom to strike a bargain. Moloch, or not?
- Give its verdict on one further case of your own construction that I have not listed, ideally one where the principle returns an answer you would expect me to resist.
- Say whether the presence of a third party who profits from the continuation of the race — the ranker, the advertising platform, the rating agency's own parent — changes the verdict or only the remedies available.
- Say whether the concept can be given a version that does not require me to settle, case by case, whether the competitive activity is productive; if it cannot, tell me whether that is a defect in the concept or a fact about the world.
