I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and I've been chewing on something a friend from grad school mentioned once — Good's theorem on the value of information, the result (I.J. Good, 1960s something-or-other, I never read the original) that free information can't have negative expected value for someone who maximizes expected utility coherently, and, more usefully for me, that a piece of information is worth nothing unless it could change what you'd actually do. I want to turn that into something I can run, because my real problem isn't "should I believe this study," it's "should I wait for one more thing before I file." This comes up maybe twice a week: I have a draft, a deadline six to twenty hours out, a standards editor who has to sign off on anything carrying a health or risk claim, and one or two calls I could still make — the study's statistician, a second lab that isn't in the press release, the agency press office. Everyone in my newsroom has an opinion and they're all vibes ("it can't hurt to check one more source"), and I'd like an actual procedure with a fixed order of questions and defined stopping points instead of that.

Here's the wrinkle I keep getting stuck on, and I want it handled head-on rather than smoothed over. When I do the extra reporting, half the time what changes isn't the claim but the verb: "shows" becomes "suggests," "doubles the risk" becomes "is associated with a higher risk." I've started calling this the hedge sink — information whose entire effect is to soften the hedging language rather than the content. Taken naively, Good's theorem says that information was worthless, since my publish/don't-publish decision was never going to flip on it. But the hedge is an action I take, and what readers do (do they stop taking the thing, does the agency open a review) turns on it, and once you let hedges count as actions it feels like any scrap of information can change some action and the theorem stops biting. So should the procedure count a change of verb as a change of action? If it does, what's left of the discipline? My half-baked thought is a two-decision rule — value the information against my filing decision and separately against the downstream reader's or regulator's decision, and treat it as the interesting case when those two disagree — but I don't trust it and I'd rather have something I can defend to my editor.

So: give me a decision procedure, in words, that a reporter with no stats training can run in under an hour, on the back of an envelope if need be. It should spell out what to write down first (I'm guessing: name the decision the information is supposed to change before you go get it), what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where it stops with "file now," where it stops with "wait," where it hands the decision to my editor because it's a values question rather than a factual one (how bad a false alarm is relative to a missed harm is not mine to settle), and where it simply doesn't work. Two things it definately has to handle: scoop risk, meaning if I wait for the replication a competitor may file first and my information becomes worth approximately nothing because the public conversation already moved without me; and the fact that my "second source" usually isn't independent — same lab, same funder, same press release feeding four outlets — which I suspect makes the value of that extra call much smaller than it feels. Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) a preprint, n=23, linking a common sleep supplement to elevated liver enzymes, with a rumor that a bigger outlet already has it; (b) a leaked draft of a government risk assessment for an industrial solvent, where the final version is due in nine days and might well come out softer; and (c) a piece I've spent three weeks on and love, where I'm dying to make one more call to the lead author — I want to see the procedure tell me something I don't want to hear, so if it concludes that call is theater, say so plainly. Finally, be straight with me about what the procedure can't do: is this just expected-value bookkeeping with a reporting-shaped paint job, or does it catch something my newsroom intuition actually misses?
