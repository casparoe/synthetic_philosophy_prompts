I'm a retired field ecologist — forty years of population biology, mostly insect-plant systems, later some disease ecology — and about a year ago I joined a reading group that's been grinding through the AI safety literature. I think I half understand the argument about safety costs, but I want to find out whether I actually do, so here's the ask: restate it in the vocabulary I spent my career in, and then tell me honestly what didn't survive the trip.

The argument as I recieved it: making a very capable AI system safer costs something — extra testing, slower or narrower releases, refusal behaviour that makes the product less useful, compute spent on things other than capability — call it the alignment tax. But anyone who pays it is at a disadvantage against anyone who doesn't: the fast movers ship first, get the users and the revenue and the talent, set what counts as normal practice, and at the extreme end get to a system that can stop anyone else from defecting later. So competition drives the tax down toward zero, everyone can see this coming, and no single lab or firm or country can afford to be the one that pays it. Against this: the tax is smaller and shorter-lived than it sounds, capability and safety aren't really in tension the way it's drawn, and coordination, standards or regulation could make everyone pay it at once, which is a different situation entirely. I may be caricaturing one or both sides; fix that if so.

Now translate. The vocabulary I actually reach for: individual versus group (or multi-level) selection; the Price-equation way of splitting a change into selection and transmission; fitness as realised reproductive output rather than as health or vigour; ESS and hawk-dove style games; Red Queen arms races; the tragedy of the commons; the evolution-of-virulence tradeoff, replicate fast inside a host versus keep the host patch alive long enough to transmit; the enforcement mechanisms that hold mutualisms together, partner choice and sanctions and policing of cheaters; niche construction; and alternative stable states with hysteresis plus the early-warning signals that precede a regime shift. I'm fluent in all of that, don't explain it back to me.

Two things of my own that I want tested rather than flattered. First, I've been using a distinction I made up: a handicap-cost, which lowers your rank in a race you're still running and still want to win, versus a substrate-cost, which changes the race so that winning it stops being worth anything, the way a pathogen can evolve a virulence that burns out its own host patch. My suspicion is that the dispute quietly slides between the two, that the interesting version is the second, and that most of the standard replies are aimed at the first. If that distinction is doing real work, say so and sharpen it; if it's muddled, say where. Second, a worry about the whole exercise: every tool I listed assumes variation is blind, inheritance is slow relative to selection, and nobody gets to redesign the game they're in. Here the traits are designed on purpose, copies are made deliberately, a design that works can spread through the whole population in months, and the players can try to rewrite the rules. That smells Lamarckian to me, and I don't know how much of my vocabulary still bites once that's true.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which claims in the argument map cleanly onto something I already have? Which ones only went across because you fudged them — and specifically, where did you smuggle in an assumption of mine and call it a translation, the way fitness-talk can make "the unsafe actor wins" come out true by definition? And what has no expression at all in population biology or ecology: I'd guess the ought part, and the part were the agents can see the trap coming and choose the population structure they're selected in, but you tell me. For each leftover, is it a blind spot in the vocabulary I've got, or a sign that the original argument was less clear than it sounds? Don't leave me with "it's a tragedy of the commons" — I got that far in the first week.
