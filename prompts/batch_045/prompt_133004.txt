I spent about thirty-five years as an atmospheric chemist — a lot of it messing about with error propagation, instrument drift, and the question of when to stop believing your own numbers — and since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university. We've been going through consequentialism recently, and I keep coming back to something from my own world that I think is a real, existing practice with an implicit theory buried inside it. I'd like help digging the theory out.

The practice is the randomised controlled trial, as actually run. Four features of it:

- You are only supposed to enrol patients when the expert community is genuinely uncertain which arm is better. If people already knew, running the trial wouldn't just be pointless, it would be unethical.
- Given that uncertainty, you don't give people the arm with the best point estimate. You randomise.
- The protocol pre-registers its primary endpoints at a fixed horizon — 90-day mortality, say — and the downstream stuff nobody can track (effects on the patients' families and careers, twenty-year health effects, the way the published result shifts prescribing habits for a decade) simply isn't counted. It's off the protocol, so it's off the books.
- And the trial doesn't run to its planned end if a data monitoring committee sees one arm pulling clearly ahead. At some point uncertainty stops being decision-relevant and you stop flipping coins.

So uncertainty is a precondition of the practice being permissible at all; under uncertainty the practice deliberately refuses to maximise; it has a threshold past which uncertainty stops mattering; and it cuts the causal chain off at the protocol horizon.

That strikes me as interesting because everything else I've seen in the policy and charity world does the opposite: rank the options by best estimate, hand the money to whatever came out top, and treat anyone who suggested tossing a coin as having abandoned the job. Medicine tosses coins with people's actual treatment; a government or a foundation wouldn't dream of it.

So here's what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory of rational choice under uncertainty that this practice only makes sense under. In particular: does it presuppose that cluelessness about downstream effects is a reason to stop trying to maximise and hand the decision to a procedure instead? Does the early-stopping rule presuppose that there is a sharp point at which ignorance becomes decision-irrelevant — and if so, is that compatible with the standard worry that our long-run effects are not just unknown but in some sense unmanageable, with error bars that don't shrink? And what about the truncation: is the practice presupposing that untracked effects are genuinely worth zero, or just that they're unusable, and is there a difference that matters?

Some things I'd specifically like you to deal with. First, my reading group's obvious objection: randomisation in trials is justified by the knowledge the trial produces, by the expected benefit to future patients, not by any view about what ignorance does to choice. If that's right the practice may presuppose much less than I think, and I'd like you to say how much less. Second, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits nicely with — I suspect several quite different theories would license it, and I want to know where it's genuinely underdetermined. Third, take the thing I've started calling the equipoise rule — when the spread of your outcome estimates across the live options is smaller than your uncertainty about the estimates themselves, don't maximise, defer to a chance procedure — and tell me what a consequentialist would actually have to change in day-to-day decision making if they adopted it for, say, long-termist or high-uncertainty giving. Would it collapse into maximising under some descriptions? Would it end up licensing things no one would accept?

Please don't give me a reading list or point me at papers — I can't get at half of them and I'd rather you just reasoned it out.
