A friend of mine who took an Indian philosophy class summed up Nyāya for me over coffee, and I can't tell which bits are solid and which are just gloss. He said somthing like: Nyāya holds that there are four pramāṇas—perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Perception requires a sense faculty to be in contact with an object and yields a determinate awareness of that object rather than a guess. Inference works by noticing a mark that is pervaded by what you want to establish—smoke on a hill, say, because smoke is invariably accompanied by fire—and in formal debate Nyāya often lays this out in a five-step form. But, on his telling, the other two are not really seperate: testimony is just inference from a speaker's honesty, since you accept what a trustworthy person says by inferring that their reliability makes the claim true; and comparison is just applying a remembered description, as when someone told that a gavaya is like a cow later sees a similar animal and thinks 'that's a gavaya,' which is really memory plus inference, not a distinct source. He added that memory itself can be a fifth pramāṇa—his example was truly remembering where you left your keys—and called this the 'reliability reading' of pramāṇa: since Nyāya is basically a reliabilist theory, any reliable belief-forming process counts, so the famous four-pramāṇa list is only a teaching device. I'm inclined to trust the bit about reliabilism, but the memory claim and the reduction of testimony/analogy both sound off to me. Is this a fair summary? Go through it and tell me what a Nyāya defender would accept as accurate, what is subtly wrong or a reduction the school would reject, and whether the conclusion that the list is merely pedagogical follows from the accurate parts.
