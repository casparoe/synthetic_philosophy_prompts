I am working through the bank-style cases in contemporary epistemology and have arrived at what looks like a forced choice. I would be grateful for help with it. I am not asking for references or reading suggestions — only for the mechanics of the views.

Set-up. In the familiar kind of case, two subjects have exactly the same evidence for the same proposition p; one of them has a great deal riding on whether p, the other has nothing riding on it; and the intuitive verdict is that the second knows and the first does not. Let "pragmatic encroachment" name any view that explains this by letting what is at stake for the *subject* help determine whether she knows, and let "contextualism" name any view that explains it by letting the *attributor's* conversational context determine how strong an epistemic position the word "knows" demands.

The case I have been testing them against. Nura and Pell both work at a night shelter that runs a van. Each has the same evidence that the van's road-safety certificate is still valid: a shared calendar entry saying it was renewed in early spring and runs for twelve months, plus a somewhat foggy recollection of the mechanic's email. Neither is certain; on that evidence each would put the chance that the certificate has lapsed at roughly one in twenty. Nura is about to drive twenty residents across the county tonight: if the certificate has in fact lapsed, the insurer will not cover the trip and the shelter will lose its licence. Pell intends to mention the certificate in passing to a colleague, and nothing whatever turns on it. Now add Sable. She is in Nura's exact practical position — same drive, same residents, same objective consequences — but nobody has told her that a lapsed certificate would cost the shelter its licence. She reasonably believes the worst case is a modest fine, which she would pay without a second thought.

My argument.

1. If stakes are to make a difference to knowledge, they must do so by changing how strong an epistemic position one needs. But the strength of an epistemic position is a matter of how well the evidence supports the proposition — that is, of the credence the evidence warrants. So a stakes-sensitive view must say: the required credence goes up when the stakes go up.

2. Yet the credence a body of evidence warrants is a function of that evidence alone; no practical consideration can make evidence that warrants 0.95 warrant 0.99 instead. So the only thing that could rise with the stakes is not the warranted credence but the credence it is *prudent* or *reasonable for the agent to act on* — a practical matter, not an epistemic one. Pragmatic encroachment would then not be a thesis about knowledge at all, but advice about caution dressed up as epistemology.

3. And if that is what a rising standard comes to, contextualism is the very same thesis. "Standards rise when much is at stake" and "standards rise when the conversation raises them" are one claim, since in these cases what raises the standards in the conversation just is the mention of high stakes. I cannot see what would distinguish them: if I say in one breath, with no shift in conversational setting, "Pell knows the certificate is valid but Nura does not," the contextualist should have to hear that as incoherent — yet that is exactly what the pragmatic encroacher wants to say, and if the contextualist accommodates the sentence by letting standards vary from subject to subject within a single context, she has simply become a pragmatic encroacher. The two views look like notational variants, and the debate between them looks merely verbal.

4. Sable makes it worse. Knowledge is supposed to be the thing that guides action and practical reasoning, so the stakes that matter must be stakes the subject can reason from. But if they must be accessible to her, knowledge depends on her *take* on her situation — on how risky things seem to her — which is just the collapse into confidence described in (2). And if instead we say that Sable's stakes are high whether or not she recognises them, then her knowledge is destroyed by a fact she has no way of discovering, and knowledge has been severed from anything she could act on. Objective stakes sever knowledge from action; accessible stakes reduce knowledge to a feeling.

So my questions: (i) is there a version of pragmatic encroachment that escapes this dilemma, or must one take a horn? (ii) Am I right that contextualism and pragmatic encroachment are the same view under two descriptions? And (iii) on the view you favour, what is the verdict about Sable? If my argument goes wrong somewhere, please say exactly where.

Nothing should follow that line.
