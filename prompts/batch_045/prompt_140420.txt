THE QUESTION

Question 4 (20 marks).

(a) In Putnam's Twin Earth case, Oscar and his counterpart on Twin Earth are molecule-for-molecule identical and in identical psychological states, yet the liquid Oscar calls "water" is H2O while the liquid Twin Oscar calls "water" is XYZ. Explain how this case is supposed to support semantic externalism — the claim that "meanings ain't in the head". Your answer should say clearly what the case is supposed to rule out, and why the speaker's "stereotype" of water cannot do the work an internalist needs it to do. [8 marks]

(b)(i) Now consider a variant I'll call Two-Source Twin Earth. Everything is as before, except that on this planet there are two watery liquids: the liquid in the lakes and rivers is XYZ, while the liquid that falls as rain and fills the wells is H2O. Both are called "water" in Twin English; both are clear, odourless, drinkable, and behave the same way in cooking and washing; and no one on Twin Earth has ever compared them chemically or noticed any difference. So roughly half of what they call "water" is H2O and half is XYZ. Does the Twin English word "water" refer to H2O, to XYZ, to both disjunctively, or to neither? Argue for the answer you take to be most defensible, and explain what your answer shows about how reference is fixed on an externalist picture. [6 marks]

(b)(ii) Compare this with a different case from the literature. A man with a rheumatic condition in his thigh goes to his doctor and says, sincerely, "My arthritis has spread to my thigh." In his linguistic community the word "arthritis" is used only of inflammation of the joints, and the doctor corrects him. Is this the same kind of case as Putnam's? In particular: should we say that the man's word "arthritis" refers to the condition in his thigh, so that his belief is true and the doctor is the one who is wrong? [6 marks]

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) Putnam's argument works by holding everything internal to the speaker fixed while varying the extension of his words. Oscar and Twin Oscar are molecular duplicates, so any theory that identifies the meaning of a word with something in the speaker's head — an idea, a mental description, a conceptual role, or even the stereotype the speaker associates with the word — has to say that "water" means the same thing in both of their mouths. But it doesn't: Oscar's word picks out H2O and Twin Oscar's picks out XYZ, which is a different substance altogether. Since meaning determines extension, and the extension comes apart while the internal states are held constant, meaning cannot be fixed by what is in the head. Hence "meanings ain't in the head". The stereotype — clear, drinkable, odourless, found in lakes — doesn't rescue the internalist either, because the whole point is that XYZ satisfies the stereotype on Twin Earth just as well as H2O does here: the stereotype is the same in both cases while the extension differs, so it can't be what settles reference. What settles it is the speaker's causal relation to the actual stuff in his environment: his word refers to whatever bears the same-liquid relation to the samples around him. And for terms whose stereotype doesn't discriminate between the candidates — Putnam's own examples are "elm" and "beech", and "aluminium" and "molybdenum" — reference is fixed by the experts, via the division of linguistic labour; ordinary speakers defer to them, so in effect it is the specialists who determine what our natural-kind terms refer to.

(b)(i) On Two-Source Twin Earth the speakers are causally related to two quite different liquids, and they use one word for both. It seems to me that the word therefore fails to refer. For reference to be determinate there has to be a single kind of stuff for the term to latch onto, and here there are two, with nothing in the case to choose between them; the stereotype is no help, since both liquids fit it perfectly. So the best we can say is that the extension is disjunctive (H2O-or-XYZ), and arguably the word has no extension at all. This shows that externalism is in worse shape than it first appears: the mechanism Putnam offers — the same-liquid relation to local samples — only delivers a unique reference when the environment happens to contain a single relevant kind. When it doesn't, externalism has nothing to say, and we are left with indeterminacy or a disjunctive extension, neither of which is what externalists advertised.

(b)(ii) The arthritis case is the same in all the relevant respects, and it's really just Putnam's point in another setting. The man's word "arthritis" is causally related to the condition he actually has, in his thigh. Externally individuated content is fixed by what the speaker is related to rather than by what is in his head, so his word refers to the thigh condition; his belief is therefore true; and the doctor is simply mistaken if she thinks he has a false belief about his joints. The structure is exactly parallel to Twin Earth, with the man's rheumatic condition playing the role that XYZ plays for Twin Oscar. Putnam's general moral — that reference depends on the environment and not on the speaker's internal states — covers both cases, and this is why the Burge-style version of the argument is really only a special case of Putnam's.

One further consequence is worth spelling out. If externalism is true, then what I think is partly constituted by features of my environment that I may know nothing about. It follows that I can be massively and systematically mistaken about the contents of my own thoughts, and that introspection gives me no authority whatsoever about my own mind; to find out what I really think I would have to go and investigate the world empirically, in just the way I would to find out anything else about my surroundings.
