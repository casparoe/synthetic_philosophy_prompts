I've been reading on my own, with no formal background in philosophy or psychology, about whether what a person believes, expects, wants or has been trained to notice can change what they actually see — the controversy over the cognitive penetrability of perception. I have roughly ten hours a week for the next year, no supervisor, no laboratory and no library beyond what I can find free online, and I would like to spend that time on the two or three questions in this area that most deserve it, ending with a long essay that advances a position rather than one that summarises the state of play.

To make the question concrete, here is the map I have ended up with after a few months of reading, and I would be glad to be told it is the wrong map. It seems to me that the question "is perception penetrated?" is really at least three claims that get run together:

(i) *Which-one* — what you believe or expect settles which candidate object, grouping or Gestalt you see: how a scene is segmented and bound, what is figure and what is ground, whether the ambiguous shape is a duck or a rabbit.
(ii) *What-it's-like* — the phenomenal character shifts, so that colours look warmer or colder, distances look longer, a shape looks more regular, without any change in which object you are seeing.
(iii) *Whether-at-all* — you come to see something that is not in the stimulus at all, or stop seeing something that plainly is, as when a degraded or camouflaged image suddenly resolves once you are told what to look for.

There are also two worries that seem to me to sit at a different level from those three. The *readout worry*: nearly all the evidence here is verbal report or forced choice, and if judgement and report are themselves belief-sensitive, the evidence base may be structurally incapable of separating penetration of seeing from penetration of saying. And the *granularity worry*: penetration might show up only in fine detail — precision, saturation, the exact placement of a boundary — while every experiment asks for a coarse category and therefore keeps finding nothing.

What I want from you is an agenda, defended rather than surveyed. Which of these should I spend my year on, and in what order? Please be explicit about the criteria you are using and about how you weigh them against one another: how much other work depends on the answer, whether it is tractable at all for someone working alone with no data of their own, how neglected it is, and how much it would actually change what I think at the end of the year. In particular I want to know whether the two methodological worries are genuinely upstream of the three substantive claims — so that settling one of them first would save me from wasted effort — or whether they are a distraction I should set aside and revisit later. And tell me what would move things up or down the list: what kind of result, from whatever quarter, ought to change the ranking over the next couple of years.

One last thing, since it is why I got interested: there is a stock list of consequences that gets attached to this debate. That if perception is penetrable it cannot be a neutral tribunal for belief, or a source of non-inferential justification. That scientific observation is theory-laden all the way down. That trained expertise — the radiologist's scan, the birdwatcher's silhouette — is itself a case of penetration. That the boundary between perception and imagination goes soft. Which two or three of these actually follow, and which are overrated or do not follow at all? Rank them, and say why the ones at the bottom are there. I am not asking for a reading list or for references; I can gather those. I want the shape of the argument and a reason to care about one part of it more than another.
