THE QUESTION (20 marks, ~600 words)

(a) Distinguish naturalism from normativism about health and disease, illustrating the contrast with Boorse's biostatistical theory and Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis. [6]

(b) "The Tyndall variant" is a genetic variant carried by roughly 55–60% of people alive today. In carriers, the body converts thyroid hormone T4 into T3 at about 70% of the usual efficiency. The variant causes no symptoms anyone notices. In populations with iodine-poor diets, carriers score on average about three points lower on childhood IQ tests and have slightly fewer children over a lifetime; in iodine-sufficient populations it makes no measurable difference to anything at all. On current dietary trends, carriers will be a minority within a century. Say what each of the accounts in (a) implies about whether the Tyndall variant is a disease, and flag any point at which an account's answer depends on a choice that the account hasn't told you how to make. [8]

(c) Vasquez has recently proposed a "counterfactual-benefit" account: a condition is a disorder if and only if, given the bearer's actual environment and given the bearer's own ends, the bearer would be better off without the condition. Is Vasquez's account naturalist or normativist? Which of the three accounts does best on the Tyndall variant, and why? [6]

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

Naturalists and normativists disagree about whether "disease" is a descriptive or an evaluative concept. Naturalists (Boorse is the canonical example) hold that disease is a matter of biological fact: a condition is a disease in virtue of descriptive facts about how the organism is functioning, and two people who disagree about what is valuable can still agree about what is diseased. Normativists deny this. On their view, calling something a disease necessarily involves a value judgement — typically a judgement about what is bad for the person, or about what the person's society disvalues. This does not mean normativists think anything goes; most of them are not social constructivists about the biology, they just think the biology is not enough to settle the classification.

Boorse's biostatistical theory (BST) is the standard naturalist account. On BST, a condition is a disease just in case some functional ability of the organism falls below the statistically normal level for the appropriate reference class — same species, same sex, same age group. Health is therefore just statistical normality, and that is what makes the theory value-free: normality is something you measure, not something you approve or disapprove of.

Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis (HDA) is the standard normativist alternative. It says a disorder requires two things: a dysfunction, i.e. a failure of a mechanism to do what it was selected by evolution to do, and harm, where harm is judged by the values of the bearer's society. The dysfunction clause is what stops HDA collapsing into "whatever people happen to disapprove of"; the harm clause is what draws the line between a genuine disorder and a merely unusual but harmless bit of variation.

Applied to the Tyndall variant: BST has to say it is not a disease. Since 55–60% of people are carriers, the variant is statistically normal in the species, and in any case it produces no symptoms. This shows how badly BST is tied to prevalence: if everyone had the variant, the theory would have to call everyone healthy. HDA does better. Whether it is a disorder depends on whether it is harmful, and it does lower IQ and fertility slightly in iodine-poor populations, so there it is a disorder, while in iodine-sufficient populations it is not. But that is also definately a bit absurd — it makes whether you have a disease depend on what the people around you eat.

Vasquez's counterfactual-benefit account is a naturalist account, and a better one than BST. It appeals only to counterfactual causal facts about what would benefit the organism — not to anyone's values, not to social disapproval, not to what clinicians think — so it is value-free in exactly the way Boorse wanted. It also gets the intuitive answer on the Tyndall variant without the prevalence problem: in iodine-poor environments, removing the variant would benefit the bearer, so it is a disorder; in iodine-sufficient ones there is no benefit to be had from removing it, so it is not. That is the same answer HDA gives, but reached without smuggling in values, so Vasquez's account is the one that does best here.
