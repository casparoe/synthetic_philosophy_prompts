I have recently joined the acquisitions committee of a museum of modern and contemporary art, and next week we vote on a proposal that I describe below. Before I decide how to vote, I want to know what our own record commits us to. For roughly thirty years our senior curator has set the committee's practice, and the minutes record her votes, each with a line of reasoning, on the following ten matters. In each case the question was whether the object was a work of art; she has always insisted that this is a separate question both from whether it is good and from whether we should buy it.

1. A factory-made urinal, bought from a plumbing supplier, turned through ninety degrees, signed with a pseudonym and submitted to an open-submission exhibition; the jury refused to hang it. — Art. (We later acquired a reconstruction of it.)
2. Plywood boxes, silkscreened to look like a supermarket soap-pad carton and shown in a commercial gallery. — Art. The actual cartons on the supermarket shelf. — Not art.
3. A processional banner made in the fifteenth century by an anonymous workshop at the commission of a religious confraternity, by makers who were never called artists and never called what they made art. — Art.
4. An eighteenth-century ship's figurehead, carved to the shipwright's specification, and beautiful. — Not art. Her note: "decoration in the service of a ship."
5. An oil painting made by a zoo chimpanzee in an enrichment programme, sold at a charity auction and hung in a law firm's lobby. — Not art.
6. A painting made in the 1960s to be passed off as a seventeenth-century original, since exposed. — Art as to status; but she voted against acquiring it, on grounds of institutional integrity, and recorded that the two questions are independent.
7. A commercially published video game with a named creative director. — Art. Her note: "a work of art that is also a product."
8. A screenprinted placard made by an activist collective and carried in a march. — Not art. Her note: "its point was to move people to act, not to be regarded."
9. A drawing produced by a plotter running code the artist wrote herself, in an edition of one, which she never saw until it was hanging; the code was written with artistic aims and the particular output was unforeseen. — Art.
10. An unedited field recording of a bird's song, issued on a nature label in the 1970s. — Not art. The same recording, reissued in 2019 on a sound-art label in a small edition with an essay by a composer known for that kind of work. — Art. Her note: "the reissue made it a candidate for a kind of regard the recording could not have had."

The proposal now before us is this. Our own digitised catalogue, some forty thousand works, was licensed to a university laboratory and used as training data for a generative image model. The laboratory produced two hundred thousand images and scored each one for dissimilarity to the training set. Our director took the thirty highest-scoring images without looking at them, had them printed at the scale of the paintings in our collection, framed, and hung in the main galleries for three months under the title *Samples*, with wall text stating that they are not works of art but specimens of what the model does with our collection. Nobody chose the forms. Nobody had any aesthetic or artistic aim regarding these thirty images in particular; the engineers had no aims about them either; the artists whose works were in the training data stand in no relation to them beyond that. The director's stated purpose is documentary and critical: to show visitors what our collection looks like when it is computationally reprocessed, and the wall text and press release both deny that the prints are art. By construction the images are as unlike anything in the collection as the scoring could make them, and visitors have in fact spent as long in front of them as in front of the paintings. A donor has now offered us a sum on condition that we accession one print into the art collection. The director supports accepting, as a document of the episode rather than as a work of art. The vote is on whether we accept, which requires us to say whether the print is art.

What I want from you:

1. The rules that fit the whole record. Set out the two or three principles about art-status that are jointly consistent with all ten votes, stated sharply enough to be applied to a new case. Where a rule needs an auxiliary assumption to swallow a particular verdict, say which verdict and what the auxiliary is. I do not want a survey of positions in the literature or any names attached to them; I want the rules themselves.
2. What each rule predicts about this proposal. If a rule divides into a strict and a loose version that disagree here, say so. I suspect the crux is whether a status can be conferred by someone who is expressly refusing to confer it, and whether an algorithmic selection counts as the right kind of selection.
3. Your best guess at her vote, the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives, and an explicit statement of which single feature of the proposal the guess turns on. I would also like two sub-questions answered: whether the requirement that art be an artifact is even met, given that the sheet of paper is made while the image is not anyone's making; and whether a causal chain that runs through a training set rather than through a practice of making and responding to works is the right sort of historical connection for a rule that needs one.
4. The closest precedent, and how the proposal differs from it in the respect that matters. My own sense is that item 9 and item 10 pull in opposite directions and I cannot tell which is nearer.
5. If the record genuinely underdetermines the vote, what principle we would be adopting with each choice, put plainly enough that the committee can see what it is voting for.
6. One further case, constructed but cheap enough that we could actually stage or at least argue it, that would separate the surviving rules, together with what each possible verdict on it would show.

Close with a single paragraph of advice to the committee. Please do not settle this by observing that art is an essentially contested or family-resemblance term and leaving it there; I know that much, and I still have to cast a vote.
