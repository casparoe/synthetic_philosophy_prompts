I joined the clinical ethics committee of my hospital three months ago, a year after sitting with my family through my father's final admission to its intensive care unit. The committee is about to rewrite its guidance on two matters in particular: withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, and what clinicians should do when a family asks the team not to tell a patient their prognosis. I find that views I had previously held only as private opinions now have to be turned into something written down, and I would be grateful for a consistency check before the drafting meeting. My sympathies in the generalism/particularism dispute lie with the particularist side, though I arrived there informally and by no careful route. Here is roughly where I stand:

(1) I do not think there are exceptionless moral principles, nor that there is a manageable set of defeasible ones that captures what matters. Whether a feature of a case is a reason at all, and which way it points, seems to me to depend on what else is going on; a feature that counts in favour here can be silent, or count against, there.

(2) I am not a relativist. Some verdicts on a case are simply wrong, and what makes them wrong is fixed entirely by the descriptive facts of the case. I take the supervenience of the moral on the descriptive for granted, and I have never seen how to give it up.

(3) In every case I have actually encountered, the fact that a course of action would leave a patient in severe untreated pain has been a reason against it, and I expect our case mix to stay like this. I have never had to treat it as otherwise and I do not expect to.

(4) When a clinician does what our guidance says partly because the guidance says so, I want to say that this gives her some genuine moral support and not merely institutional cover: the guidance is a consideration in favour of acting, and not just a fact about what the hospital will later accept.

(5) I think the guidance has to pass what we have started calling the corridor test. A reviewer who was not in the room, who does not share our judgment, and who has only the notes must be able to determine afterwards whether the clinician complied. Guidance that cannot be audited in that way is not guidance at all. This I regard as non-negotiable.

(6) I think clinical moral judgment is a trained perceptual skill, that novices acquire it partly by being handed rules, and that the point of the rules is that they should eventually be outgrown. At the same time I want to say that a clinician who does the right thing only by applying a rule whose cited features were not the reasons actually present in the case has got it right without seeing it, and that this is a real defect even when the outcome is identical.

(7) I think several people on the committee are better at this than I am and that I should frequently defer to them. But I do not accept that anyone's verdict is right because she reached it, or that one person can have authority over another's moral judgment.

(8) On the case that led to me being asked to join: my father's team told him his prognosis after my mother and I had asked them not to, and I remain convinced that telling him was right. Yet I also want to say that in every case what ought to be done is what the balance of reasons in that case favours, and that this is the whole story about what makes a verdict right. In that case the reasons I am able to articulate seem to me to point the other way, and what I am ultimately relying on is that the senior nurse saw it and I did not.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious; for each, say what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me; and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If one item is doing more damage than the rest, say so plainly. I do not know where the trouble is, and I would rather be told than reassured.
