Hey — I run a little fortnightly reading group on distributive ethics (mostly grad students, plus two philosophers who drop in when they feel like it), and last session we spent the entire time going in circles about the levelling-down objection. Everyone agreed we don't want a view that says we should blind the sighted so that everyone is equally sighted, but nobody could say exactly which step of the argument they were rejecting, and my friend who is a hardcore prioritarian kept insisting that his view simply dissolves the objection while I think it only relocates it. So instead of another round of arguments I want an actual diagnostic instrument: a decision tree / taxonomy that starts from someone's answers to concrete cases and lands them on a named position, with branches that nobody occupies marked as empty and briefly diagnosed.

What I'd like concretely: build it from the logical space first — the branching questions and the combinations of answers they allow — and only then locate the real positions in that space. The distinctions I care about (add your own, but these need to be in there): whether the relational fact that some are worse off than others can be bad even when it is bad for no one; whether that relational fact is something to promote as a goal or only a constraint on how we may act and what justification we owe people; whether the extra moral weight the worse off carry is a weight on *benefits* (a function of how badly off they are absolutely) or a weight on their *claim* when claims compete, which is where numbers and lotteries come in; whether the numbers of people on each side change the answer; whether what matters is only being below a threshold or is continuous; whether the badness of inequality depends on how it was produced (choice, responsibility, luck) or on what it does to social standing and relations; and whether the verdict changes when the choice is under uncertainty rather than between sure outcomes. Required leaves: telic egalitarianism that bites the bullet on levelling down, egalitarianism construed purely as a deontic constraint, plain prioritarianism, prioritarianism *plus* a seperate equality term, sufficientarianism, relational egalitarianism, a competing-claims / weighted-lottery view, and an unweighted beneficence view, plus at least one leaf you label "unnamed" if the space has a coherent spot nobody defends. Aim for eight to ten branch nodes and eight to ten leaves.

Two of the cases need to be ones I made up, because the textbook ones let people recite: (i) *top-addition* — handing a pure bonus to someone already at the top, or bringing an extra well-off person into existence, makes the distribution more unequal while making someone better off and nobody worse off; what does each view say about that, and does any of them end up having to decline to create a happy person or withhold a free benefit, which looks like levelling down by omission; (ii) *intra-personal levelling down* — a single life with great years and terrible years, where smoothing would mean removing goods from the good years; if a view says yes there and no in the interpersonal case, or vice versa, say what that reveals about whether it is really tracking relations between people. I'd also like a variant where nobody at the top or bottom knows where they stand (does the objection lose its force when no one can complain?) and one where the choice is risky rather than sure, since that's the only way I can see to pull the weighted-lottery leaf apart from plain prioritarianism. Every node should be phrased as a question someone can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to. Please flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate rather than inventing a question that pretends to, and for the empty branches say why you think nobody is there — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. No citations and no author names, please; half of us can't get to journals anyway and I want the structure, not the reading list. If it's easy, add a ten-question quiz version with a scoring key at the end that I could actually hand out.
