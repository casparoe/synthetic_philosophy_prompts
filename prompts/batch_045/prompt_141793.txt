"Assisted reproduction now lets prospective parents choose which child to bring into existence, and the principle of procreative beneficence says they are morally required, not merely permitted, to select the child expected to have the best life — the most welfare, the most happiness, the best odds. The argument for this is just the non-identity problem: whichever child we pick wouldn't otherwise have existed, so no one is made worse off by our picking them, and the only thing left for morality to care about is maximizing the wellbeing of whoever does end up existing. Procreative beneficence is thus a new eugenics — and it has been refuted. Selecting against an embryo because it would be disabled expresses the judgment that lives like that one are not worth living; since we all reject that judgment, the principle collapses. Nor does its logic stop anywhere: if you should pick the healthiest of your own embryos, you should also shop around for the healthiest available gamete donors instead of using your own, and you should wait to conceive until better screening technology exists, since that gets you an even better child."

I'm suspicious of the bit where the non-identity reasoning is doing the arguing, because I had the impression that kind of point is normally used against procreative beneficence rather than for it, but I can't quite say why. Could you go through the passage and tell me what a defender of the principle would accept, what's a tolerable simplification, and what's just wrong or question-begging? And seperately: do the two extensions at the end (donor shopping, delaying conception until screening improves) actually follow from a careful version of the principle, or do they change the case in a way the chapter is missing?
