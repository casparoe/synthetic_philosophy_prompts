I run a graduate reading group on moral progress, and one of the regulars — a political theorist with a taste for sceptical arguments — produced the following over dinner. I've spent a fortnight on it and cannot locate the mistake, but the conclusion is one I won't accept. I'd be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a survey.

Her argument, as nearly as I can reconstruct it:

(1) The circle of moral concern has expanded. At each stage of the history we can reconstruct, some class previously treated as outside the circle — outsiders, slaves, women, animals, future generations — comes to be counted, and the earlier arrangement now looks to us defective in exactly that respect.

(2) The best explanation of this pattern is not that earlier people were unusually stupid or unusually cruel. It is that moral conviction is socially situated: people reliably fail to register considerations that their social world gives them no occasion to register, and they are most confident precisely where they are blind.

(3) If (2) is right, then we are in the same position. There are considerations bearing on our moral judgements that our situation prevents us from seeing, and so some of our confident moral judgements are false in ways we cannot now detect.

(4) And we cannot say which. Every test we might run — coherence, reflective equilibrium, appeal to our firmest principles — is run using the judgements that are themselves in question, since the blindness described in (2) is a blindness to exactly that.

(5) When you know that an unidentified subset of your confident judgements in some domain is false, and you cannot identify which, you should lower your confidence in each of them by the same proportion. Call this the flat-rate defeater.

(6) The judgement that the earlier stages were defective — the thing that makes (1) more than a demographic fact about who counted whom — is itself one of our confident moral judgements, and (5) applies to it as much as to any other.

(7) And the consequence generalises: the argument gives us no more reason to trust the abolitionists' confidence than the slaveholders', since both were socially situated in the sense of (2). On this telling, abolitionism was one more local conviction.

(8) But (7) is absurd — the abolitionist judgement is precisely what the expanding-circle story was supposed to vindicate — and (1) is about as well-confirmed as anything in this neighbourhood. So something in the chain is false, and I cannot see what.

Two wrinkles. First, she says it does not matter whether a given expansion is additive (an already accepted principle extended to a new class of subjects) or revisory (a new principle displacing an old one); the argument, she claims, runs on either assumption. I suspect it does matter, and that the difference bears on which step to reject, but I cannot work out which way round. Second, she offers an illustration of (5): imagine a reliable oracle that tells us, truly, that exactly one of our ten firmest moral judgements is false, and tells us nothing more. She thinks the flat-rate defeater plainly applies and we should dock all ten equally. I find that I want to rank the ten and dock the shakier ones harder — while also suspecting that ranking is exactly what (4) rules out, because the only rankings available to me are built out of the ten judgements themselves. So I go round in circles.

If I have to choose what to keep, I would keep (1) and (2): the historical claim and the social-situation explanation strike me as the best-supported parts of the story, and I would rather give up almost anything else. But (5) strikes me as close to a platitude about evidence, and (4) strikes me as little more than what (2) already says, so I do not see how to surrender either of those either.

Please don't send me a reading list or a summary of the literature on moral progress, on the argument from disagreement, or on genealogical debunking — I've read a good deal of it and it will not settle this. What I want is the diagnosis. Please write in American English, in under 1,000 words, in four named sections in this order:

- "Diagnosis" (250–300 words): which step or premise fails, and precisely what the mistake is — an equivocation, a scope or quantifier shift, a slide between two claims that the premises don't support, or whatever it turns out to be. Include what the oracle case does and does not show.
- "Why it looked sound" (about 150 words): what makes the faulty step attractive, and why a careful reader wouldn't notice the shift.
- "The alternatives and their price" (about 350 words): for each of the other steps I might reject instead — at least (3), (4), (5), (6) and the move to (7), plus the additive/revisory distinction — what I would be committed to if I rejected it, and how much that costs me.
- "Bottom line" (about 80 words): the single sentence I should say to my friend.
