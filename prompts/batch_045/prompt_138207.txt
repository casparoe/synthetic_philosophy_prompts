I'm on a tiny team building an office helper thing — it watches what people actually do during their day (which doors they take, what they click, when they stop) and tries to work out what they're after, so it can start doing bits of it for them. I keep tripping over the same three things, and I want a word for them, or for whatever they have in common, but I don't want just a word — I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, whether it's one thing or three, and where its edges are, because my friend and I have been going round in circles about it on walks and getting nowhere.

First: there's a stairwell in our building that's permanently cluttered, so everyone takes the long way round. Our system watches this for a few weeks and concludes that people prefer the long way. Fine so far, except that once it's concluded that it stops suggesting the stairwell route, so from then on the only thing it ever observes is people taking the long way, and its estimate gets tighter and tighter. The error isn't random — it always points the same direction, toward reading the shape of the building as the shape of the person — and it seems to quietly eat the very observations that could have corrected it. Second, and I suspect this is a different thing but I can't tell: the people our model is most confident about are the people with the least room to manoeuvre. Someone on a tightly scripted shift does the same thing at the same time every day and comes out looking like a model of crisp, stable preference; someone with a loose schedule looks noisy and inconsistent, so we trust them less and lean on the scripted person's data. That feels backwards. The confident read is coming from the situation that told us least about what anyone wants, and I've started wondering whether the noisy person's messiness is actually where all the information is. Third, smallest one: a colleague always declines the window seat, and we'd have written down "prefers aisle", but she's avoiding the sun in her eyes rather than wanting the aisle at all.

I know economists have some phrase for reading desires off of choices, and I know there's philosophy about people who stop wanting what they can't get — the fox and the grapes sort of thing — but neither of those feels right, because in both of those the person's wants really do shift or really are the thing you infer, whereas in my cases nobody's wants moved at all and only my picture of them did. So: is there an established name for this, or for any of the three? If not, I'd want it carved rather than labelled — how each piece differs from the two neighbours I've half-remembered and from the usual warnings about feedback loops in general, which of the three are really the same failure wearing different clothes, and what kind of observation would actually settle whether I'm looking at a preference or at a wall. My friend says you can never tell the difference from the outside, which seems too quick to me, but I can't say why.
