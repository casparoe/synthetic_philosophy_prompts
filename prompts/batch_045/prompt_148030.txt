Hey — I write novels (one published, one I'm currently mangling) and I've been talked into running a 90-minute workshop at a writers' conference this autumn. About 14 people, mostly adults, mostly novelists, no philosophy background, but the sort who will argue about a semicolon for forty minutes. I really don't want to stand at the front and lecture.

Here's the thing I want them to feel. My new book is set maybe forty years on, and there's a treatment in it — a course ofsomething, four weeks, that raises what everyone in the book calls "stamina": the ability to keep your mind on one hard thing for hours without it fraying. It's scarce. There's a committee that decides who gets it, and the committee's charter says it can only go to people who need it back, people being restored to something they've lost — not to people who want to be more than they ever were. That distinction is the whole moral architecture of the book, and I need to know whether it can hold weight before I write four hundred pages on top of it.

So: design me an activity where the participants ARE that committee, and where they hit the crack in that rule themselves, before anyone names it. Nobody should hear the words "therapy" or "enhancement" until the debrief.

What I think I want, and change it if you've got better: they get a stack of maybe 15-20 applicant files — a paragraph each — and something like six doses. Small groups, they have to allocate, and they have to write a one-line justification for every yes and every no. Then round two: I hand each group back its own justifications from round one with the names, ages, occupations and pronouns swapped around between files, and they have to apply their own stated rule consistently. Then round three: a handful of files built so the rule gives no answer at all — I'm thinking especially of applicants whose stamina was fine until something in the world changed (a new working regime, a contaminant, whatever you like) and who only want back what they had ten years ago; plus a couple who were born after the change and never had it; plus maybe one who's clearly above any baseline and wants it for a reason that would sound like an obvious need in somebody else's mouth. Invent your own cases too, you'll probably do it better than me.

And the bit I care about most and can't work out how to stage: I want them to notice on their own that "restore" is doing all the work while nobody ever picked what it restores TO. Past self? Typical human? Typical person around here, right now? The person they'd have been if the world hadn't shifted under them? However you do it, I want them to catch themselves having already chosen one.

Give me: materials and prep, the files themselves (write them out), a timeline, what I as facilitator am allowed to say and when, the debrief that maps specific moments onto specific hinges in the idea, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I handle it, and an honest note on which part of the problem the game just can't generate and I'll have to say out loud. Small stakes are fine, no real money.

Oh, and selfishly: if the activity also hands me characters, arguments and lines I can steal for the book, so much the better.

Lead with the bottom line — one sentence, what the participants are physically doing at the ten-minute mark that makes everything after it inevitable. Then the rest. No preamble, and don't repeat my question back at me.
