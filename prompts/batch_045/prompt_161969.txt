I should be grateful for your help with an argument I was unable to settle, and which I should prefer to understand rather than to have settled for me.

Since retiring from a career in protein physical chemistry I have been attending a philosophy of science reading group at the university. At our last meeting two members of it — Dr Halloran, who teaches philosophy of science there, and Dr Biswas, a historian of science — disagreed about incommensurability and neither would give ground. I was the only person in the room who had lived through the case under discussion, so I was asked to referee; I could not, and I should like to know what I ought to have said. I have tried to report both sides fairly. I take no view myself: I was persuaded, and then unpersuaded, by each of them within the same hour.

The case was one I had supplied. The working hypothesis of my field in the 1960s was that a protein's native conformation is the conformation of lowest Gibbs free energy available to the chain and is therefore fixed by the amino acid sequence alone; the classical demonstration was that a small enzyme, unfolded in urea with its disulphide bonds reduced, would recover both its structure and its activity when the denaturant was removed, and would do so unaided. What has replaced this is not a hypothesis but a picture: a funnel-shaped energy landscape with many routes down it, kinetic traps and metastable intermediates, folding in the cell usually assisted by chaperones, a large class of proteins with no single native fold at all, some of which fold only upon binding a partner, and — most awkwardly — prions, in which one sequence supports two stable self-propagating folds, so that what the chain becomes depends in part on what it was last alongside rather than on the sequence alone. The sentence *the native conformation is determined by the sequence* is therefore now, depending on how charitably one reads it, false, or trivially true because it has been emptied of content, or not the sentence anybody was asserting in 1962. The group's question was whether that sentence was refuted or merely abandoned.

Halloran opened with the strong reading. The reference of a theoretical term, he said, is fixed by the whole network of laws in which it appears; when the network is replaced the terms do not keep their referents, so there is no proposition both theories assert of which one says yes and the other no. What looks like refutation is replacement. It follows that no neutral procedure could have adjudicated the transition, that the shift was a conversion rather than an inference, and that *the better theory won* is true only retrospectively, told in the winner's vocabulary.

Biswas granted that meanings shift but denied that reference is controlled by the whole theory. Some terms are anchored from outside it, by instruments, samples and measurement routines that survive the change, and it is precisely in that shared vocabulary that the old theory made numerical predictions which then failed. There is therefore a genuine contradiction, hence a genuine refutation, hence no need to invoke conversion. She added that if the strong thesis were true we could not even say that the new picture handles the old cases better, which is a visible fact about the recent history of the field and not a piece of ideology.

Halloran conceded the shared measurement vocabulary but said it does not reach far enough. The proposition that failed was stated in terms of a quantity — the free energy of *the* native conformation — whose identity conditions were supplied by the old theory, which allowed exactly one such conformation per sequence. Once the successor makes the native state an ensemble, allows chaperone-dependence and allows two stable folds for one chain, the old quantity has no successor quantity, and there is nothing both parties assert about it. He proposed a diagnosis of his own: successor lexicons are *one-way expressible* in the predecessor's — the new vocabulary can state the old claim and say where it fails, while the old vocabulary cannot so much as formulate the new one — and, he said, one-way expressibility gives you replacement, not refutation. The appearance of refutation is produced by doing the translation in the winner's language and then forgetting that one did it.

Biswas replied, first, that one-way expressibility is enough for refutation in the only sense that matters: to refute a claim is to show, in a vocabulary one accepts, that the claim, on its best reconstruction, is false; the asymmetry is a fact about who is in a position to see this, not a semantic obstacle to its being so. Second, she said the asymmetry is itself a reason for preferring the successor, and offered it as a standard both sides could apply from outside either theory: *expressive span*, the capacity to state more of what the rival can state. Halloran's own concession, she said, supplies the common measure he claimed did not exist.

Halloran answered that expressive span is not neutral. Counting what a lexicon can state requires a prior decision about which states of affairs are worth stating — whether the ability to talk about chaperones and disordered ensembles counts as gain depends on whether those things matter — and that decision is exactly what changes in a transition. He offered a different account of the rationality of a shift, which needs no comparison at all: a practice is entitled to supersede its predecessor if it dissolves the problems its predecessor generated, where *problem* is fixed internally to the predecessor. Progress is inheriting a problem list and shortening it, not outscoring a rival on a shared scale.

Biswas said this smuggles comparability back in. To say that the successor solves the *old* problems one must re-identify those problems across the lexical break, and re-identification requires translating at least the problem statements; so Halloran owes a criterion for problem re-identification, and any criterion strong enough to re-identify a problem is strong enough to carry a contradiction. If problem inheritance can survive the break, why cannot failure also survive it?

At this point I ventured a case from my own métier, and each of them then used it against the other. When the kilogram was recently redefined by fixing the numerical value of the Planck constant, the *definition* of the unit changed while the quantity was held fixed by an explicit continuity stipulation, checked against theInternational prototype to within the available uncertainty. Halloran said this proves his point: continuity had to be imposed by a convention adopted for practical reasons, which shows that reference is not preserved automatically, and in a scientific revolution there is no body with the authority to impose it — indeed the disputes just are about whether the old term and the new term pick out the same stuff. Biswas said it proves hers: meaning change plainly does not entail reference change, which is Halloran's premise; and the continuity in such cases is not secured by a description at all but by ostension, by our continuing to handle the same samples in the same vessels. In our case, she said, the anchor is a thing and not a sentence: the same polypeptide chain, the same preparation, the same calorimeter. Halloran replied that ostension individuates objects and not quantities or properties, and that the disagreement is about which quantities the chain bears — whether there exists a property of the kind the old theory ascribed — which pointing at the chain cannot settle.

Would you go through the exchange and adjudicate it move by move? I should like each move rated from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification, rather than a verdict on the whole; then an overall judgement as to who is more right; then what, if anything, both of them are missing. Four things in particular puzzle me. Whether *refuted* survives at all if Halloran's one-way expressibility is granted. Whether expressive span is a neutral standard or merely the successor's own verdict about what is worth saying, restated in comparative form. Whether my metrological case is a genuine model for a paradigm shift or a misleading one, and on which side of the dispute it finally falls. And whether either of them has actually supplied a criterion for re-identifying a quantity, a problem, or a sample across a lexical break, or whether that is the load-bearing omission in both positions. If you think the argument turns on a distinction neither of them drew, say what it is.

One request: please adjudicate the arguments as they were actually made, and do not simply report what the secondary literature says about incommensurability. It was the secondary literature that got them into this, and I can read it myself.
