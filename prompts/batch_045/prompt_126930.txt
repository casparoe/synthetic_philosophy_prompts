Draft the joint statement that two philosophers could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the symbol grounding problem: one holds that a symbol is anchored only when the system using it stands in the right kind of non-linguistic causal and sensorimotor contact with what it is about, so that a system trained on text alone has at best meaning borrowed from the interpreters who read its output; the other holds that meaning is fixed by a symbol's role in inference and use, and that a purely linguistic system can satisfy whatever that role demands.

Format it as a numbered section of claims both can sign, followed by a disagreements table.

In the agreed section I want them to settle at least this much: that a text-only system's tokens can nonetheless refer, by whatever causal-historical route allows speakers to refer to things they have never perceived, so that their dispute is not about reference but about something else — and to say precisely what else.

The case I want both to confront: two systems indistinguishable in linguistic behaviour, one trained on text alone, the other on the same text plus a synchronous, unlabelled stream of sensorimotor data from a body it controls. Is the second system's vocabulary any better anchored? Note that each camp has its own reason to answer no, and say whether that agreement is deep or merely verbal.

For each crux in the table, record one test or argument both accept as bearing on it. I suggest this one: give each system live, unlabelled access to a genuinely novel sensory channel, let it coin a token, and check whether its subsequent use of that token tracks the channel. Record each side's prediction in advance. Include as a crux how much of a competent adult's own vocabulary would count as ungrounded by these standards, and whether that is a reductio or a bullet to be bitten. Argue from cases and argument alone; no appeal to the literature.
