I'm in a final-year philosophy seminar that runs Hume's problem of induction and Goodman's riddle alongside formal learning theory, and we covered the no-free-lunch theorems a couple of weeks ago. I've been trying to use them to settle an argument with a classmate, and the more I try to make the argument precise, the less I can tell where it goes wrong.

The claim I came in with: the NFL theorems vindicate Hume. If you average over all possible worlds, every learner's expected off-training-set error is identical, so no inductive bias is better supported than any other; the only thing separating the working scientist's bias from the conspiracy theorist's is the brute, unknowable fact of which world is actual. On that picture our preference for simple, smooth, projectible hypotheses is arbitrary, and the best we can say for it is that it has paid off so far — which is just the circular justification Hume already diagnosed.

I want to sharpen this rather than assert it, so here is the repair I've been working on. The NFL average looks rigged: on a uniform distribution over target functions, almost all the mass sits on incompressible, structureless targets where no generalization is possible at all, so of course every bias comes out equal. The natural move is to restrict the space — say, to computable targets, or more aggressively to targets specifiable in under 5,000 bits — and then universal Solomonoff-style prediction seems to break the tie, since it dominates any computable competitor up to an additive constant. But I can't tell whether that move repairs my argument or quietly abandons it. Either the restriction just is the simplicity prior again, in which case the whole thing is circular and Hume is untouched; or it is a substantive, contingent claim about what the world is like, in which case the "arbitrariness" conclusion I started from seems too strong and I don't know what remains of the argument.

I don't want readings or a bibliography — I want the argument located. Where exactly does it break: in my setup, in the step from the NFL theorems to Hume, or in the move to a restricted target space? And if the dominance result is real, does the fact that it holds only up to a machine-dependent constant matter for what I was trying to conclude, given that the constant stops being negligible when your data are finite?
