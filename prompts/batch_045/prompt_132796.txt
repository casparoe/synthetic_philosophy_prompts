In my statutory interpretation study group we keep falling back on the same picture: a statute is like the firmware shipped with a device — written at one moment by people who couldn't have anticipated the hardware you'd later plug into it, updatable only through a slow formal process, and read afterward by technicians who just want the thing to work.

Two people used it for opposite conclusions. One: firmware is written against a spec, so you read the code in light of what it's for rather than the literal opcodes — so purposivism. The other: the device does exactly what the code says and nothing else, so a judge appealing to purpose is shipping a feature the manufacturer never wrote — so textualism.

Take a hypothetical we argued over: a 1994 landlord-tenant law requiring "written notice posted on the door of the dwelling unit" 24 hours before entry, and a tenant who only ever got an email with a scanned notice attached.

Can you audit the analogy? Start with the correspondences — what plays the legislature, the text, the judge, the agency, the amendment process, the bug report — then where it fails. Devices have one author and an outside standard of "working"; statutes have hundreds of authors and nobody agrees what they're for. Nobody litigates about what a router is *for*. And firmware imports a lot I suspect statutes don't have: a manufacturer who patches errors, a spec that exists apart from the code, and technicians cooperating with the author instead of adversaries arguing past him.

Does either conclusion survive once the disanalogies are out, or was firmware carrying all of it? And is there a better comparison — a recipe, a contract, a translation, a will?
