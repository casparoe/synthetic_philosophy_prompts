Hey — I'm putting together the final for my third-year phil of language seminar and I need one essay question on rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori. The problem I keep having is that every version of it gets answered by a student writing down the definition of a rigid designator, announcing that the Hesperus/Phosphorus case is necessary but only knowable empirically, and stopping. That's worth maybe a C and it tells me nothing about whether they can actually work the machinery.

The angle I've been pushing in lectures, and the thing I want the question to turn on: I split rigidity into what I've been calling thin and thick. Thin: the term picks out the same object in every world where that object exists, and in worlds where it doesn't, it either fails to refer or latches onto whatever else happens to satisfy the reference-fixing description. Thick (some people call it obstinate): it goes on designating that very object even into worlds where the object doesn't exist, at the cost of designating something that isn't there.

So build the question around a case where the object exists only contingently — say two names introduced for the same individual animal, one by ostension at a lab where it was cloned and one by a description in a newspaper, where the animal might very easily never have existed at all. Get them to say whether the move from both terms rigidly designating the same thing to the identity being necessary still goes through once the object is contingent, what exactly has to be added to make it go through, and whether whatever they add costs them the claim that the truth is a posteriori — including the epistemic-route story where you supposedly know a priori that if a = b then necessarily a = b, and only then find out empirically that a = b. And whether the two-dimensional way of talking (primary vs secondary intension) genuinely dissolves the category or just redescribes it.

What I want from you: the question as I'd actually hand it out (one essay, about 45 minutes), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each has to contain — not vibes. Add one line per mark-point on how students usually blow it.

Two constraints: it shouldn't reward knowing who said what, so no name-dropping of the usual suspects, and if the model answer needs a distinction like primary vs secondary intension it has to define it rather than assume they've read it; and I want it self-contained enough that someone who's only got the lectures can do it. Last thing: at the end of the model answer, give me a short note on where that verdict is most likely to be wrong — the strongest attack on the line the model answer takes, what would change its mind, and whether a student who raises that attack should be credited for it or marked down for going off-script.
