Next month I am presenting my position on knowledge and practical factors to a graduate reading group, and before I commit to it in writing I would like an audit of whether it hangs together. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Contextualism about "knows": the truth-conditions of a knowledge attribution shift with the context of the attributor, so that a relaxed speaker and a demanding speaker can both say something true while attributing knowledge to the same subject on the same evidence.

(2) Pragmatic encroachment on the subject's side: what is at stake for the subject herself partly fixes whether she knows. So in the asymmetric case — where a bystander with nothing riding on it says of someone facing a serious loss, "she knows the bank will be open tomorrow, but she had better check" — I want to say the bystander says something false, because it is the subject's stakes that matter here and not the bystander's.

(3) Our evidence in this debate is intuitive verdicts on cases; that is how questions about the semantics of "knows" get settled.

(4) Knowledge is the norm of practical reasoning: one may treat p as a reason for acting only if one knows p. This is my main reason for holding (2).

(5) Fallibilism: knowledge requires neither certainty nor entailment by the evidence, and I do not want to concede anything to the sceptic.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, and which only look as though they are. For each real tension, say precisely what would have to be given up and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. One thing I especially want settled: can (3) really deliver both (1) and (2), or does the case that supports one tend to starve the other?
