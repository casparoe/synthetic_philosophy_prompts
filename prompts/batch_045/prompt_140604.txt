I have been trying to work out what, exactly, bothers me about holding moral views on other people's say-so, and I cannot find a principle that reproduces my reactions case by case. Here are the cases with my gut verdicts.

(1) My daughter comes home from school certain that slavery was wrong, entirely on her teacher's authority, unable to say why it was wrong. Fine. It would be absurd to object, and there is nothing second-rate about her belief.

(2) I ask a friend whether it was rude to leave the party without saying goodbye. She says yes. I believe her on her say-so alone and send a note. Fine.

(3) A colleague I hold in low regard — self-serving, usually wrong about people — tells me that the joke I made in the meeting was sexist. I look again, believe him, and stop telling it. Fine, and I feel no deficit at all; if anything I owe him more than I would owe a friend for the same remark.

(4) My partner tells me that going through her phone was a betrayal. I believe her entirely on her say-so; I still cannot see it. I apologize. Something is off here: I feel I had no real right to that apology, that it was not quite mine to give. Uneasy.

(5) The same situation, except I never come to believe it. I do not see it, I trust her judgment, and I simply stop doing it. This feels fine, or very nearly.

(6) There is a service that will pair me with a trained stranger who returns a written verdict on any personal moral question within a day, for a fee. I use it to settle whether I should report a colleague's minor expense-account fraud. The verdict is yes. I believe it and act on it. Uneasy — and, oddly, more uneasy than in (2), even though the belief and the action have the same shape.

(7) I read a careful summary of what most people who have thought long and hard about some question believe, do not follow the arguments, and come to hold the verdict on that basis. My verdict: fine as a guide to action, but second-rate as a thing to believe. I would be embarrassed to say why I hold it, and I would change the subject if pressed. Second-rate, but not uneasy in the way (4) and (6) are.

(8) A woman who grew up on the housing estate tells me the redevelopment plan is cruel. She gives no argument; she says it in a certain tone. I believe her, and I feel better grounded than I would after reading three policy papers. Fine, and then some.

(9) I come to believe, on my friend's say-so alone, that I may keep the extra change the cashier handed me. I am suspicious of that belief in a way I am not when she confirms me in the belief that I must return it. Uneasy in one direction only.

The pairs that trouble me: (2) and (4) look alike — my own conduct, someone close, pure say-so — but I treat them differently; and (6) and (2) differ although the belief and the action are the same shape. My own tentative principle is that testimony is fine when what I need from another person is a verdict, and deficient when the belief is supposed to be a standpoint from which I relate to someone, as in apologizing or standing with them; and further, that it is objectionable when I have arranged things so that I never had to judge at all but only to purchase a verdict. But I suspect this gets (3) wrong and cannot explain why (6) is worse than (7), let alone why (5) is fine when (4) is not.

So: can you state a principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which case, tell me what it predicts about a case where a reliable medical intervention simply gives me the true moral belief with no testifier at all, about a careful letter I wrote to myself at twenty-five and read at forty-five after losing the thread of my own argument, and about a whole community testifying unanimously against what I believe — and then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have me give up? I would also like to know whether the residue I am calling unease is really an epistemic failing at all, or something else entirely. Please write in continuous prose, the way you would write an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
