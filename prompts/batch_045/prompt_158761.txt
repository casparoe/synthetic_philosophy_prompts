I had a long argument with a friend last night about whether the growth work my team does is manipulative, and I came away unable to say why her position seemed too clean. Her claim was that an influence counts as legitimate persuasion whenever it leaves the person's options unchanged, says nothing false, and works by way of her own deliberation — on that view manipulation is just the residue case: bypassing deliberation, or feeding in falsehoods. That test exonerates things I am fairly sure are manipulative. A notification whose timing is chosen by a model that has learned which minute of the user's day finds her most depleted, sent with no false claim, no blocked option, and no interference with her reasoning, still strikes me as the paradigm case; and a signal structure designed so that a fully rational, correctly updating person ends up more likely to buy, purely because of which true facts were packaged together and in what proportions, strikes me as manipulative even though every step of it runs through her own deliberation.

I'm an economist, and that is the vocabulary I would like this translated into. I do information design and the experimental side of a large consumer app: signal structures and Bayes-plausibility, a sender who can commit, receivers who update, posterior means and obedience constraints on one side; uplift and incrementality, heterogeneous treatment effects, intent-to-treat versus treated, short-run proxies against long-run guardrails, and the "would it have happened anyway" counterfactual on the other. Philosophy I have essentially none — please don't assume I know any of the standard labels. So take three candidate accounts of what separates manipulation from persuasion and restate each of them in that vocabulary as precisely as you can: (i) the bypass view, on which manipulation works around the target's capacity to weigh reasons rather than addressing it; (ii) the counterfactual-information view, on which an influence is manipulative if its force would evaporate once the target knew the relevant facts, including the influencer's purposes; and (iii) the self-endorsement view, on which the test is whether the target can, on reflection, avow the influence as tracking her own ends rather than merely producing the behaviour. My own candidate for a fourth, which I floated to my friend and could not defend, is a transparency counterfactual: call it audit-incrementality — an influence is manipulative to the extent that its measured uplift would collapse if the target could inspect the model the influencer is using of her, including the objective that model is optimising. I suspect that test is either unidentifiable or vacuous, and I would like to know which.

Then I want the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. For each account say what translates cleanly into committed-sender/updating-receiver and uplift terms, what had to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression in my framework at all — I have a particular suspicion that "reasons" has no counterpart, since in my models the receiver's preferences are simply given and nothing distinguishes addressing someone's reasons from rearranging the information she conditions on. Say also whether my framework can represent the difference between influencing what someone believes and influencing which considerations are live for her, deception about the world versus deception about one's own intentions, and the thought that someone is being used as a mere instrument. And then give me your verdict on where the residue points: is it that decision-theoretic and experimental vocabulary is built so as to be blind to something real, or is it that the philosophers' dispute is thinner than it looks, with the three accounts differing mainly in which counterfactual they privilege? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question, and write in British English.
