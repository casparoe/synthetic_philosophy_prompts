Not sure whether this is a real thing or whether I've invented it. Reading about rent control and minimum wages side by side I keep seeing the same shape of argument, and its not the usual one.

With rent control the loudest defenders are the sitting tenants. But that's partly because rent control gives them a reason never to move — the longer you stay, the deeper your discount — so the rule selects for a population of long-stayers who then turn up at hearings and tell pollsters that things are working fine. The people it screened out, like the person who wanted to move to that city and didn't, arent in the room, arent in the survey, and mostly never learn they were affected. Minimum wage looks similar: the visible winners have the job, the loser is someone who never got hired and doesn't know there was ever a vacancy. And it seems to get worse over time — a rule that's been in place for thirty years has a far more lopsided constituency than the same rule passed last year, even when the economics is identical.

Is there an established concept for this? It's not concentrated benefits and diffuse costs, which I read as a claim about how easy it is to organise; and it's not survivorship bias, which is about your sample being unrepresentative. Here the population itself has been reshaped by the policy, so the missing people are missing from the city, not just from the data. If nothing quite fits, give me a name plus a definition with boundary cases — does it still count when the winners drift away too, and is this the same as what I think political scientists call policy feedback, or is that about something else entirely? A bare label won't do much for me.
