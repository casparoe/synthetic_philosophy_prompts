I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background, and I arrived at this question through a side project. A few of us at work built a small tournament harness for a variant of the centipede game: two agents alternate, each either taking (which grabs the larger share of a pot and ends the game) or passing (which grows the pot and hands the choice to the other agent), for a hundred rounds. We mostly run prompt-based LLM agents against each other, and nearly all of them pass for the first ten to twenty rounds before someone finally takes. My own solver is just backward induction over the game tree, and it returns take at the root. As far as I can tell that argument is nothing more than mathematical induction over a finite object, so it should be as reliable as anything I trust in my day job — which is why I have been treating every observed pass as a defect: either the model cannot carry the recursion deep enough, or it is pattern-matching on cooperation norms in its training data. Either way it is noise, and the engineering conclusion I drew was to hard-code take-at-node-one and stop spending money on tournaments.

What I cannot square is the self-play case. If I ship two instances of the same binary against each other, the induction looks stronger rather than weaker, since each copy knows exactly what the other will do, so take-at-node-one ought to be completely uncontroversial there — and yet it feels like throwing away ninety-nine rounds of free pot growth for no reason at all. A friend suggested what she called a program-equilibrium trick: let each agent inspect and verify the other's source before the first move, and pass conditionally on what it finds. I do not see how that can matter. If the induction is a valid proof, then no pre-game handshake changes what is rational once play has actually begun, and a handshake that changes your move is just a way of being irrational on purpose.

So my questions. Is there any defensible sense in which passing here is rational, or is the honest answer that the proof wins and my agents are simply broken? If it is the latter, why does anyone treat this as a paradox rather than as a psychological finding about people — isn't the fact that taking at the first node is the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium the entire content of the result? And does the particular structure I chose matter at all? In my version the pot roughly doubles on every pass, and I added a one percent chance per round that the game simply terminates with the current player receiving nothing. My intuition is that backward induction washes out details like that, the way a compiler folds away constants, but I would like to know whether the payoff schedule or the termination rule can genuinely change what the induction delivers. One last thing: someone told me this is connected to the surprise-exam paradox, which I cannot see at all — that is a puzzle about self-reference, whereas my game has concrete payoffs and a machine-checkable solution.
