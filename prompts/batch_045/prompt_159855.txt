So I've been reading pop-science stuff about the prisoner's dilemma and the evolution of cooperation on and off for a couple of years now — no philosophy or biology background at all, I got into it after an argument with my brother in law about whether it ever pays to be nice when nobody's watching — and I've ended up sitting on a pile of views that each feel pretty sane on their own but which I can't quite get to line up. Could you audit the pile?

Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. In a genuine one-shot prisoner's dilemma — anonymous stranger, no reputations, no repeat encounters, no-one finds out — I defect, and I think defecting is what rationality requires. The move where someone says 'but what if everybody reasoned that way' has always struck me as a mistake, because nobody else's choice changes what my choice brings about.

2. If we both know up front that we're playing exactly 100 rounds and then stopping, I think the argument that unravels it from the last round backwards is just correct, and the right play is to defect from round one. I know loads of people find that result repulsive but I've never actually seen what's supposed to be wrong with it.

3. In real relationships and long-running working relationships, though, I cooperate, I think that's rational, and I think roughly tit-for-tat is the right way to play. I also think this is basically where morality comes from: it's the reciprocity machinery plus the feelings that stop you from defecting when defecting would pay.

4. I'm fairly convinced group selection is a confusion. Every 'they do it for the good of the group' or 'for the good of the species' story I've run into seems to me to be better told as kin selection, or as plain gene-level self interest.

5. But I also think the genuinely distinctive thing about humans is that we cooperate at huge scale with strangers who aren't relatives and who we'll never meet again — big cities, offices, online. And I think the same reciprocity story from (3) is enough to explain that once you scale it up. I don't think we need to drag in group selection or culture-level selection or anything like that; that always looks to me like people reaching for a big explanation when a small one will do.

6. And related to that: I think it's rational, not just noble, to train yourself into being a cooperative person. I've deliberately worked on becoming the kind of person who cooperates more or less automatically, because nearly all the interactions in my life are open-ended, and I think that's straightforwardly self-interested over the long run. I specifically would not want to be someone who sits down and calculates each case. (Though I'll admit I don't tip in restaurants in cities I'm just passing through, and I don't lose any sleep over it.)

So: does that hang together? Some of these feel like they're obviously in tension with each other and some feel like they only look like they are, and I honestly can't tell which are which, so please don't just flag everything as 'in tension' — I want you to discriminate. Where there's a real problem, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Rank the problems by how bad they actually are rather than listing them flat, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. And one more thing: I have a suspicion that at least one item on my list is way more contested among people who actually work on this than the pop books let on, but I can't tell which. So please keep separate what's basically settled and agreed from what's a live fight, and don't dress up a contested view as the consensus one — and say plainly where you're not sure either.
