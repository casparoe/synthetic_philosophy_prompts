I've been asked to do forty minutes on the units-of-selection business — genes vs individuals vs groups — at a Wildlife Trust volunteer training morning, in a village hall, no handouts, flipchart if I'm lucky. About twenty-five adults, mostly serious amateur naturalists: they can pick a warbler out by call, they know what a territory is, maybe half did GCSE biology and have forgotten it, and none of them have ever met a population-genetics model. They will arrive being thoroughly group-selectionist without knowing the word — behaviour is for the good of the colony, for the species, for the balance of nature. I want to dislodge that, but not by replacing it with 'everything is really selfish and nasty'.

I dont need the explanation — I need a plan for giving it. So:

1. What has to be in place first, and why. My instinct is that before any of the levels stuff I need them to have whatever-it-is that makes 'selection needs heritable variation in what gets passed on' feel like common sense, and that without that the whole thing degrades into 'survival of the fittest means the strongest wins'. But I'm not sure what the minimum version of that is, or in what order the other pieces have to land: the replicator vs. whatever the right word is for the thing selection acts on (should I even use those words with this lot?), 'selected for' as opposed to merely selected, and the point that one single process can be carved up at several levels while the different carvings have to agree. Don't give me a running order — tell me what actually depends on what, and what happens in the room if I do it the other way round.

2. The carrying example. I've been toying with an invented bird: colonies of about six pairs per tussock in a fen, each pair fledges more young if it feeds further out into the neighbouring tussocks' feeding ground, but the colony's total output drops as more pairs do it, and failing tussocks get recolonised from elsewhere. So within a tussock doing it pays, and between tussocks the tussocks full of birds doing it send out fewer colonists. Is that the right example, or is it quietly broken? What does it have to have, and what does it have to not have? I don't want ants or bees — the room will turn it into a conversation about sterility and slavery — and I don't want anything where I'd first have to explain what DNA does. Also: should I say upfront that I made the bird up, and how do I stop the whole thing sounding like a just-so story?

3. The misconceptions, and roughly where in the forty minutes to head each one off:
- 'so the group-selection lot were right all along' — a couple of them will have read somewhere that group selection has had a comeback, or that it's all the same maths partitioned differently, and I need that to be neither a win for what they walked in with nor an obvious walk-back of what I just told them.
- 'if it's genes then animals are selfish', the slide from a claim about what gets passed on into a claim about motives.
- 'the gene for X' talk nudging them into one-gene-one-trait determinism.
- and anything else you'd actually predict from this particular audience — over-produce here rather than trim.

For each one, say whether I should meet it head-on before it's voiced, or let someone say it out loud and then use it.

4. How I'll know it's landed. No show of hands on 'does that make sense'. Give me one thing to ask for at the end that they have to apply rather than repeat, where a right answer shows the idea took and a specific wrong answer tells me which bit of the sequence failed.

5. What to leave out and openly say I'm leaving out. My guesses are relatedness coefficients, the Price equation, and the replicator/vehicle vocabulary — but tell me if dropping the vocabulary costs me something I can't do without.

One last thing I genuinely can't settle: whether to say the word 'gene' at all before the last ten minutes, or whether keeping it out until the end makes the ending feel like a trick. And if I do keep it out, what word do I use in the meantime?
