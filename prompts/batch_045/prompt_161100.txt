Our AI safety reading group spent last week on embedded agency, and I came away unsure whether the metaphor we kept leaning on was doing real work or just lending an air of profundity to something an engineer would find boring. The setup: one member of the group argues that because an agent is physically smaller than the environment it sits in, and its model has to be implemented inside that environment, the model can never be complete or perfectly accurate — you cannot fit a map into the territory that contains it, and a map that has to be drawn on the territory itself must include a mark for itself, which must include a mark for that mark, and so on. She draws a fairly strong conclusion from this: no embedded agent can have a self-model reliable enough to reason about its own future self-modification, so no agent can be trusted, from the inside, to preserve its goals across a rewrite of itself, and the only safety lever we have left is to prevent systems from self-modifying at all. Another member, a controls engineer, says this is just the truism that models are approximate, that every feedback controller since the flyball governors has had to model a plant larger than itself, and that nothing specific to agency follows.

What I would like is an audit of the analogy rather than a tour of the literature. Please give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the territory, the map, the cartographer, the scale or resolution, the user who reads the map, and the map's being drawn on the very ground it depicts — and then separate the disanalogies that bear on her conclusion from the ones that are noise. Some candidates I want you to rule on rather than merely ratify: maps are causally inert, while an agent's model is causally coupled to what it models, so a prediction can be self-defeating or self-fulfilling in a way no cartographic prediction is; maps are drawn by someone and read by someone else, whereas an embedded model must be legible to the very system that runs on it, with no outside standpoint from which to check it; a map's virtue is fidelity at a resolution set by scale, while an agent's model is an abstraction selected for what the agent needs to act on, so the size argument may bound detail without bounding usefulness; and the regress of the mark that marks itself arguably only bites if accuracy requires representing yourself representing yourself, since a self-model might instead be a fixed point that is true partly because the agent acts on it. Also flag what the analogy quietly imports: that the world is given in advance rather than partly shaped by the agent's acting on its model, that the model's job is to mirror rather than to steer, that the map is smaller because it is coarser rather than because it selects.

Then the verdict I actually care about: how much of her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are in, and how much of the engineer's deflation is compatible with the parts of the analogy that do hold. Two replacements I have been playing with are a flight simulator running on the aircraft it simulates, and a chess engine that must model the machine and the clock it runs on; tell me which, if either, carries more of what she wants and what each one loses. Finally, please note how confident you are in the main claims of your audit and single out the single step you are least sure of — I would much rather know where the audit itself is on thin ice than receive a uniformly confident-sounding answer.
