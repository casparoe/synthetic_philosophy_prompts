Someone in my decision-theory reading group has been pushing this argument at me for a week and I can't find the broken step, but the conclusion has to be wrong.

Background, so we agree on the target: Good's theorem, the value-of-information result, says roughly that if you're an expected-utility maximizer and you're handed a signal for free, your expected utility measured *before* you see it can't go down. The proof is just: whatever act was optimal before is still on the menu after you see the signal, so you can always do that, and the signal can only let you do better.

(1) Fine. But note the fine print: 'free' has to mean the signal changes nothing except what you know. No fee, no delay, no new acts, none taken away, no change in your tastes, no change in what your acts *do*.

(2) Counterexample anyway. You trade a stock that's at 10 today and will be 20 or 8 tomorrow, 50/50. With no tip you buy: expected profit .5(10) + .5(-2) = 4, versus 0 for sitting out. A friend offers to tell you tomorrow's price, truthfully, for nothing. If you take the message you get wall-crossed and compliance bars you from the trade altogether. So accepting it moves you from 4 to 0. (If the compliance rule didn't exist, the very same tip would be worth +1: you'd buy only in the good state, for expected profit 5 instead of 4.) So here's a perfectly informative, costless message that strictly lowers your expected utility, and you'd pay a dollar not to be told.

(3) The obvious reply is: that message wasn't free, it deleted the act 'buy'. He says this reply eats the theorem.

(4) What is it for an act to be 'deleted'? After you learn something, there are always acts you wouldn't do anymore — that's just what learning is. If hearing 'it'll be 8' counts as deleting 'buy', then *every* informative signal changes your menu, clause (1) is never satisfied, and the theorem has no instances at all.

(5) And if 'deleted' means genuinely, legally infeasible — compliance really does make buying impossible — then (2) is a straight counterexample and the theorem is false, since (1) was satisfied in every other respect.

(6) It gets worse, because the same thing happens with nothing illegal in sight. Suppose that once you know the price your face gives it away and the dealer on the other side adjusts. If you know it's going to 20 your eyes light up and he won't sell at 10; if you don't know, your face is blank and he does. Nothing became infeasible, your tastes didn't change, the message cost nothing, and you're still worse off for knowing.

(7) And here's the bit that actually keeps me up. Look at the proof again. It compares 'see the signal and act on it' with 'see the signal and do the old optimal act anyway'. Both of those are worlds in which the message arrives. It never compares a world where the message arrives with a world where it doesn't. So even in the clean case the theorem doesn't say you should accept free information; it says that if the message is reaching you anyway, you may as well use it.

(8) So: either the theorem is vacuous, or it's false, or it doesn't say what everyone cites it for.

I want to keep the theorem. I think it's true and I think it has content, and I'd rather give up the trader than give up the theorem — but every time I try, 'free' quietly turns into 'free in whatever sense the theorem needs', which feels like cheating.

So: which step do I reject, and why did it look fine? Is (6) really a different case from (2), or the same case in different clothes? And if I give up the trader, what am I committed to about, say, people who turn down a scan for something untreatable — are they being incoherent, or is that a separate thing entirely?
