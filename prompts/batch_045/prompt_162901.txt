Im putting together a prospectus for a 20-page MA thesis chapter and I want to try out an angle before I commit to it. The topic is Berkeleys so-called master argument, the bit in the Principles where he challenges you to conceive of something existing unconceived and says the attempt defeats itself.

My half-formed idea: the argument is an instance of a general template. For any property F, trying to conceive of a thing that lacks F seems to confer F on the object of the attempt: try to think of an unthought truth and you have thought it, try to represent something unrepresented and you have represented it. So either the template is valid and wildly overgenerates, giving you no unthought truths and no unknown objects, or it has scope conditions and Berkeleys use of it has to meet them. My suspicion is that the conditions can only be met if conceiving and perceiving have the same objects, i.e. if the ideational theory is already in place, which would make the argument dialectically useless as an independent route to idealism. But Im not sure the standard critique, that it slides between conceiving-of and conceiving-that, is really the same point as mine or a different one.

Turn this into a proposal: a precise statement of the question, why the existing readings leave it open (the self-refutation reading, the conceiving-of/conceiving-that reply, the flat verdict that it begs the question), what I would actually do section by section including how I would build the taxonomy of template instances and what would count as a counterexample to my own account, what the possible conclusions are and what would decide between them, and what would count as the project failing, for instance if it turns out to be a fancy restatement of the standard objection. Say honestly if it is too big for one chapter. No bibliography, I will add citations myself. And please mark clearly which claims you take to be settled among people who actually work on Berkeley and which are genuinely contested readings, I dont want a prospectus that presents someones controversial interpretation as the consensus.
