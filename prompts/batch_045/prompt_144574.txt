I've somehow ended up as the person who maintains the internal support bot at work. It began as a search box over our documentation, then someone bolted on a ticketing API so it can open and close tickets, and now everyone on the team calls it grounded: it's grounded in our docs, every answer is grounded in retrieved passages, that kind of thing. Meanwhile the same people call its mistakes hallucinations, and we're about to fine-tune it on a big batch of retrieved-and-answered tickets and then drop the retrieval step at inference time, which nobody seems bothered by. I've been reading a bit about the symbol grounding problem in the philosophical sense (Harnad's 1990 paper, the Chinese-room setup, that image of a symbol-to-symbol merry-go-round where every definition just points at another definition) and I'm fairly sure that the thing my team means and the thing the philosophers worry about are not the same thing, but I can't put my finger on where they come apart.

So: what does the practice of calling retrieval- and tool-augmented language models grounded actually presuppose about meaning or content? I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed rather than a reading list, since I have no library access and can't chase references. Does the practice really presuppose that meaning is some sort of causal or informational link to the things words are about, or would it sit just as well with a use-based or inferentialist picture where symbols are fixed by their relations to other symbols and their role in a practice, or even with something slacker like "grounded" just meaning "answer traces back to a source a human vouches for"? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it -- my hunch is that several quite different theories of content would all happily license the word, and if so I want to know where the practice is underdetermined and what it stays silent about.

Three things I'm stuck on and would like you to take a position on. First, I keep wanting to cut the problem in two: one question about how symbols get content at all, and a totally different question about whether the content they got is the correct content, i.e. whether the answers are true. It looks to me like retrieval only touches the second one, because a page about our refund policy is still just more symbols and grounding answers in database rows is grounding them in more symbols again; my colleague's reply is that at some point the regress has to stop and our database is where the buck stops, which sounds like a view of meaning I can't name. Is there a version of the grounding worry that survives that reply, and is there a version of it that just dissolves? Second, I want a real criterion for when it's the world, and not more text about the world, that can correct a system -- something like the world having to be able to force a revision the model's training didn't already dictate, or the system being answerable to something it can't predict from its own input stream -- because I suspect that's where the actual line is, and I also suspect a spreadsheet hooked to a warehouse temperature sensor either clears that bar trivially or fails it for exactly the reason the chatbot does, and I can't tell which. Third, there's the hallucination talk: we describe errors as the model making stuff up about our refund policy, which seems to presume its outputs have truth conditions and are about something, while the rest of the time we describe it as predicting tokens; are two halves of the same practice quietly assuming different things? Then the practical part, which is what I actually need: if we took the demanding view seriously, whatever it turns out to be, what would have to change in how we build and evaluate these things? I'm guessing text-only benchmarks and "does the answer match the doc" grading are precisely the wrong test, but I can't say what the right one is, and I'd rather have the design consequences of each view than a verdict on which view wins. And if grounding is a matter of degree rather than yes-or-no, tell me what it's a degree of, because I hear "more grounded" constantly and nobody ever says what the scale is.
