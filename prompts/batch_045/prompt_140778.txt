I'm the non-clinician member of the ethics commitee at a fertility clinic, which apparently means I get asked to write the memos. We're properly stuck on two cases and I need something I can circulate before next month's meeting.

Case one. We offer preimplantation testing for late-onset, untreatable conditions — the sort where an embryo carries a variant that means the resulting person will almost certainly develop a degenerative illness in their forties and die of it. Normal procedure: biopsy the embryos, rank them, transfer an unaffected one. A mother of a four-year-old born this way has complained to the board. Her argument, as I understand it: we told her we were sparing her child a terrible disease, but if we'd transferred a different embryo she wouldn't have this child at all, she'd have a different one. So nobody was spared anything. The child who exists isn't better off than she'd otherwise have been, because there is no "otherwise" for her — there's just no her. And our consent paperwork, she says, is built on a comparison that can't actually be made. Half the commitee thinks this is clever wordplay with no practical upshot. I'm not so sure, but I also can't see what it's supposed to change about what we do on Monday.

Case two, which is what makes it hard. A deaf couple, both deaf from the same genetic variant, have asked us to select an embryo carrying that variant — they have several, some would be hearing, some deaf. Several members say obviously not, that's deliberately creating a disabled child. But if the complainant in case one is right that embryo choice doesn't harm the child who results, the symmetry is uncomfortable: we're perfectly willing to select away from a trait and totally unwilling to select toward the very same trait, so harm to the child can't be what's doing the work in our policy. Something else is, and we should probably have to say what.

Three things we've floated and can't settle: a "counterfactual embryo note" in the medical record — which embryo we would have transferred instead, and why — disclosed to the resulting person at eighteen on request; a "substitution test," where we only permit selection when we can actually name the embryo we'd otherwise have used, so we can't slide into treating embryo choice as general quality control; and a ban in counseling on comparative phrasing like "spare your child from," replaced with something like "you are choosing which child to bring into existence."

What I want: a decision memo, 700–900 words, addressed to the committee, recommending one concrete course — keep offering this testing, stop offering it for late-onset conditions, or keep it under specified conditions — with a realistic timeline, actual consent-form language we could lift, and a straight yes or no on the deaf couple's request. Please don't spend the memo explaining that the first complaint is a famous argument with a name; I've read enough to know it has one. Tell us what, if anything, follows from it for policy, and give the strongest version of the losing side — the view that none of this should change our practice — rather than a paragraph of strawman.
