I cover science and ideas for a newspaper, and I am working up a piece on Hempel's raven paradox. Before I write it, I need the paradox in the vocabulary I actually think in.

That vocabulary is roughly this: when an observation reaches me — a tip, a document, a dataset — I ask how much it should move my confidence in a claim, what my prior was, what the base rate of the thing is, how the observation was selected (did I look where the disconfirming cases would be, or did a source hand it to me?), and whether what I have is absence of evidence or evidence of absence. I can read a likelihood ratio and a sampling frame. I have no formal philosophy.

Please translate, as faithfully as you can: the claim that all ravens are black; the intuition that a black raven supports it while a red apple does not; the equivalence condition, on which evidence for a claim is evidence for anything that says the same thing, so that the reformulation everything non-black is non-raven must be supported by the very same observations; and the Bayesian reply that the apple does raise the probability, only by far less than the raven does.

Then give me the residue report — what crosses cleanly, what crosses only with distortion, what cannot be expressed in my terms at all — and, for each item, your view on whether the gap is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the paradox turns on something less clear than it looks. Two gaps I suspect matter: my trade treats how an observation reached me as part of the evidence, while the paradox seems to treat that as irrelevant; and my trade has no single word for evidence for — I have raises the probability, which is a matter of degree, and bears on, which is a matter of relevance. If some variant of the setup travels into my vocabulary better than the original does — an apple that arrives unbidden rather than one I went out and sampled, say — tell me what it is and what it changes.
