I spent my working life in physiological ecology — most of it measuring how organisms perform along continuous gradients, and arguing with colleagues about where, if anywhere, a threshold can honestly be placed. Since retiring I have begun reading philosophy, which is the only reason I am writing to you rather than to a former colleague who would simply tell me what our institute has always done.

I sit on the small committee that administers the health scheme for a research institute, covering staff and graduate students alike. The scheme is to pay for whatever restores a person to the level of functioning that was normal for them before illness or injury, and to decline whatever improves a person beyond what is normal for them. The wording comes from a period when our business was mainly fractures and infections.

We now have four applications concerning one and the same intervention. It is a treatment that raises the proportion of newly learned material a person still retains a week later. Let me stipulate away what I take to be the distracting questions: the effect is real and measurable; it is, as nearly as we can tell, the same in absolute magnitude wherever a person starts; it has no detectable side effects; it does not make anyone remarkable; and there is money enough to fund all four applicants. The difficulty is not safety, efficacy, or cost.

The applicants are these. First, a graduate student who sat near the top of the distribution before a viral illness and now sits near the middle of it, and who wants her former level back. Second, a laboratory technician who has always sat near the middle and would like to sit above it. Third, a researcher in his fifties who performs at the level typical of men his age but well below the level he himself reached at thirty, with no illness to point to. Fourth, a technician whose retention is unimpaired on ordinary days but collapses under the rotating night shifts his post now requires.

As a matter of measurement, the distance between the first applicant's target and the second's is small compared with the spread of the whole population, and the first is asking to end up well above the second. Yet the rule as written appears to require us to fund her and refuse him, and it yields no clear reading for the third or the fourth. I would add that we already pay for this same intervention, under a different clause, for staff who must retain safety procedures.

What I want from you is not a decision but a clearer description of what we are doing. Is there a principled line here at all, and does it fall where our rule's wording puts it? If there is a principled line, what work is it doing — what exactly would we lose by ceasing to draw it? If there is not, what ought a committee in our position to be asking instead? I have no access to a philosophical library worth speaking of, so I should be grateful for an answer that proceeds by argument rather than by appeal to authorities, and that does not assume I have read anything in particular.
