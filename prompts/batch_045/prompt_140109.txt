I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, and in three weeks I have to run a single eighty-minute seminar on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem for a mixed group of about fifteen PPE master's students. Roughly a third of them have taken a decision-theory module and have met von Neumann–Morgenstern utility; the rest know utilitarianism only as "add up the happiness", and arrive assuming the theorem is simply a proof that they should. Two or three have also encountered the later impartial-observer presentation, in which the social ranking is the expected utility of someone who has an equal chance of turning out to be anybody, and currently think of that as a separate and friendlier argument. I need a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself: the order in which the components have to be introduced and why, given what depends on what. My own grasp is shaky in two places. First, I cannot tell which premise is carrying the weight — the requirement that social preferences over lotteries satisfy the expected utility axioms, or the Pareto indifference condition — and last time I taught something adjacent, students came away convinced the unanimity condition was doing all the work and that the rest was decoration. Second, I have never found a clean way to answer "why aren't the weights all equal?", because everything I say seems to slide into the point that individual utility functions are only fixed up to positive affine transformation, so a weight and a choice of units trade off against each other.

There are three further things I want handled rather than dodged. A couple of the stronger students will arrive having heard a version of a departmental quip: since each person's utility function is determinate only up to positive affine transformation, the weights in the sum are artefacts of a convention and the theorem has no content beyond linearity — I want to know where in the sequence that move gets defused, and whether defusing it is even right. One student will ask whether a concave transformation of the sum is thereby shown to be irrational, and I do not want the session to end with everyone believing Harsanyi refuted prioritarianism, which is roughly what happened the last time I tried. And I have been playing with a variant I invented: keep the impartial-observer framing, but let the chances be loaded rather than equal, so that an observer's probability of ending up as a given person is proportional to that person's share of total life-years in the population. I cannot decide whether this variant illuminates the role that the equal-chance assumption is playing or merely confuses it, so tell me whether to use it, and if so exactly where it belongs and what to say about it. My candidate carrying example is a choice between a sure arrangement in which one person gets forty and another gets sixty, and a fair coin flip that yields either ninety-ten or ten-ninety depending on how it lands; assess whether that case should carry the session, what it must contain and what it must not contain (does it need a difference in the total? does it need the uncertainty to be about who ends up where, or can it be about anything?), and whether it quietly begs the question in favour of ranking prospects ex ante rather than ex post.

Please write in British English, and use these sections in this order:

- "Session arc" (about 450 words): the ordered steps, with an explicit note after each on what it makes available to what comes later.
- "The case" (about 200 words): the example to build on, why it works, what it must have and lack, and your verdict on my coin-flip suggestion and on the loaded-dice variant.
- "Misconception map" (about 350 words): each misconception the group will bring or form, together with the point in the arc at which to head it off — including the weights-as-convention worry, the concave-transformation worry, and the "Pareto does everything" reading.
- "The check" (about 150 words): the one question I should put to them at the end whose answer would show the idea had actually landed, plus what a wrong-but-confident answer would look like.
- "What I'll leave out" (about 120 words): what to refuse to go into at this level, said plainly, and how to name it as being left out.
- "Settled vs contested" (about 200 words): separate what everyone who knows the material grants about the theorem's assumptions and content from what is genuinely disputed among specialists — for instance whether expected utility axioms are appropriate for social prospects, whether the theorem supports anything recognizable as utilitarianism, and what it settles about prioritarianism — and flag any pedagogical recommendation of yours that is itself a contested reading rather than the standard one.
