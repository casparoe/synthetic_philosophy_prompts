I'm in the last year of a PhD on the collapse of the palace economies in the Aegean around 1200 BC — Linear B tablets, destruction horizons, radiocarbon wobbles, the usual mess — and there's one move that keeps showing up in the literature and in my supervisor's comments and I want to take it apart properly. Whenever someone presses on how we can possibly reconstruct causes from material this scrappy, we get the detective analogy: the historian is like a detective who turns up after the fact, reads the traces that are left behind (a footprint, a forced lock, a bloodstain), and reasons backwards to the best explanation of what happened. In our methods reading group it's the Ginzburg-style lineage, with the physician reading symptoms and the connoisseur spotting a forgery sometimes bolted on. And it's doing real argumentative work for my supervisor: he uses it to argue that working backwards from a known outcome is simply what retrospective inquiry is, that the outcome is just one more piece of evidence to be weighed, and so all this anxiety about hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy is a psychological curiosity about how untutored people think — a disciplined, trace-driven method washes it out. Conclusion: stop worrying that my chapter is a just-so story dressed up as inference to the best explanation, and get on with it.

What I'd like is an audit rather than a verdict. Can you lay out the mapping explicitly — a table of what plays the role of the crime, the culprit, the clue, the motive, the confession, the verdict, on each side — and then go through where it breaks and, importantly, which breaks actually matter for the use he's making of it and which are just noise? I have my own list I'm suspicious of, but I can't tell which of them are load-bearing. One: a detective is handed a death that is already classified as a murder by a rule that exists independently of the investigation, whereas the thing I'm explaining is not given to me at all — a burnt palace only becomes the beginning of the collapse, or a last tablet only becomes the last one written before the archive stopped, in light of what came later. I think there's an old point in analytic philosophy of history (Danto, I think) about descriptions of events that essentially refer to later events; if that's right then the outcome in history is not a neutral datum that triggers inquiry, it partly constitutes the object of inquiry, and that seems like the one place the analogy smuggles in exactly what's at issue. Two: my best evidence is produced by the very event I'm explaining — the fire that gives me a destruction layer also destroys or scatters most of the archive, so the trace record has been filtered by the outcome in a way a detective's crime scene mostly hasn't. Three: detective work has a feedback loop that punishes confabulation — confessions, DNA, convictions — and historical explanation has nothing equivalent, so a tidy narrative just sits there looking tidy. Four: hindsight bias in my material isn't mainly about causal guesses, it's about grading agents — the administrator who kept shipping grain to a region that was about to stop existing obviously should have seen it coming — and a whodunit structure seems to import a culprit, a motive, and a blame-shaped story into a process that may have had no agent at the right scale at all.

So: where does the mapping break in ways that bear on his conclusion, and where does it break harmlessly? Does the conclusion — that retrospective, outcome-driven reconstruction is epistemically fine as long as it's disciplined and evidence-led, and that hindsight distortion is a foible rather than a structural feature — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the detective picture carrying it the whole time? And if there's a better analogy, I'd like it: my housemate is a fire investigator and keeps repeating two rules from her training — you don't call it arson until you've excluded accidental ignition, and you treat operator error as a thing to be explained rather than an explanation — and I've been wondering whether her field, or outbreak epidemiology with its distinction between a case definition and a causal hypothesis and its obsession with how cases get ascertained, is the model I should be using instead, since those are fields that knowingly work backwards from a known outcome and have built explicit norms for stopping the outcome from doing the causal work. Which of the two handles more of what actually goes wrong in my kind of source base, and what does each of them quietly assume that history doesn't have?
