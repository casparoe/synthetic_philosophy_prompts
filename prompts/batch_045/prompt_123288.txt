A guy in my local rationalist group chat keeps leaning on the line that HCH is just a firm — bounded parts, none of which has to grasp the whole, all the capability living in the decomposition and the routing — and he's using it to say that HCH isn't an idealization of anyone's deliberation at all, it's an institution, so there's no more a fact about what HCH "believes" than there is about what the market believes, and the honest version of the amplification story is institutional design, not distilling what I'd think if I thought for a million years.

Can you audit that. I want the correspondence table first: what plays the owner, the employees, the hierarchy, the make-or-buy decision, the profit-and-loss check, the price system? Then the disanalogies, and which ones actually matter for the work he's making it do: every node is a copy of the same human running the same decomposition policy, so there's no diversity of local knowledge to aggregate and no incentive conflict to price in; nothing outside the tree selects on its outputs; the root node can in principle be made to reconstruct the whole chain of reasoning, which no CEO can do for a firm; and the tree is the same thing all the way down instead of bottoms out in genuinely separate agents. Does the no-unified-agent conclusion survive those, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If the firm is the wrong picture, what's the better one — a debate, an appeals court, one person with a notebook and no working memory — and what would that one license instead?
