Running a single 80-minute session next month for a mixed undergrad group — about 22 people, mostly non-majors, zero game theory — on first contact and the Fermi stuff. They'll have read the bit in the second book of Liu Cixin's trilogy where the two axioms get handed over and the "chain of suspicion" gets named, but I don't want the session to be me explaining it. I want them to arrive at the fear themselves.

So: design me a runnable game. Each participant or small team is a civilization somewhere on a coordinate sheet, and on a turn you can stay quiet, broadcast a location, or fire at a location. No real money — points, maybe one small prize at the end. I'd like the scoring to be survival-weighted rather than raw points, so that keeping your head down is genuinely tempting and not obviously a sucker move.

The thing I actually want them to feel, and the bit I think is easy to miss: that the dark-forest conclusion isn't driven by malice or by anyone being especially aggressive. It falls out of a few structural ratios. Two I care most about — the gap between "I can see you" and "I can say something to you" versus the time before a strike lands, and the chance that a neighbour jumps a tech tier while you're still making up your mind. I've been toying with giving each pair a distance value and a hidden per-round "explosion" chance, so that nearby civilizations end up policing each other while distant ones just shoot first. Steal that, fix it, or throw it out — your call — but the mechanism has to live in the rules, not in my debrief.

Also: they will absolutely try to negotiate. Let them. I want a cheap-talk option that's free to use and that fails, and I want to be able to explain why it fails.

Give me rules and materials, the payoff table, a minute-by-minute timeline, what to change for round two (one parameter flip so they can see what breaks the result), and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the axioms, the chain of suspicion, and the tech-explosion asymmetry. Add a note on where the setup drifts from what the hypothesis actually claims, and on which part of it the game can't reproduce at all and I'll just have to state out loud. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.

Two honesty requests. Flag anywhere your design leans on a contested reading of the dark forest — if you think its usually framed as something other than a game-theoretic result, say that. And if the premise is off — if these ratios aren't what's doing the work, or if no payoff scheme I could hand 22 undergrads will actually generate the outcome — say so plainly instead of politely building me a broken game.
