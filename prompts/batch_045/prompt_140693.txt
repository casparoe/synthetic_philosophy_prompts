'There are really two different connectives hiding behind the one word "if". The indicative conditional is just the material conditional: "if A then C" means not-A-or-C, so it comes out true whenever A is false. That's why "if Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" is perfectly fine as an indicative — we know he did, so it's trivially true — but terrible as a counterfactual. The counterfactual is a different animal: you take the possible worlds closest to ours in which A is true and check whether C holds there, and in most of the nearest Oswald-free worlds nobody else shoots Kennedy either. The same point shows up in the probability work: Adams showed that how confident you should be in a conditional just is the conditional probability of C given A, full stop, and that means conditionals don't have truth conditions at all, only probabilities. So the only real puzzle left is why the two behave differently in these cases, and that's basically pragmatic — a counterfactual signals that the speaker takes the antecedent to be false, and an indicative doesn't.'
