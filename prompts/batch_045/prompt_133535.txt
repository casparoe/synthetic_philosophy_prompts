Hey — I'm putting together a handout for a second-year philosophy of science tutorial, and for the session on emergence I want the centerpiece to be a decision tree instead of yet another reading-response essay. Something a student can walk from a starting commitment down to a named position by answering questions about cases, so the tree sorts them by what they're willing to say about a concrete system rather than by which label they think sounds cool.

Here's roughly what I have in mind, but please improve on the structure where it's clumsy:

Build it from the logical space first. Give me 6-8 branch questions, tell me how many answer-combinations the set of them allows (even a rough count is fine), and then locate the actual positions inside that space. Leaves: 8-12, each with a name or "unnamed (as far as I can tell nobody holds this)" plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. For the unoccupied combinations I want a one-line diagnosis each: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive for a specific reason, or because it's just unexplored? And where two leaves really can't be seperated by your questions, say so and tell me what further case would be needed — don't invent a distinction to paper over it.

Positions that must show up as leaves, described substantively (feel free to give them their usual names too):
- macro properties bring genuinely new fundamental laws with them
- macro properties are real and do causal work, but nothing violates the causal closure of the micro-physical
- macro properties are real but causally inert, because the micro-level cause already does all the causing
- nothing metaphysically new; the macro state just isn't reachable without actually running/observing the system
- macro vocabulary is compression or notation and doesn't really refer — keep it as convenience or drop it
- reduction still goes through even when the macro property is realized in wildly different physical stuff, via the role it plays
- the reduction succeeds completely and yet macro-level explanations remain indispensable

Plus at least two unnamed leaves.

Cases I want used as the branch nodes (reword them however you like, and add your own if the tree needs them):
1. A supercomputer simulation of an ecosystem. A recurring large-scale pattern shows up in every run and matches field data perfectly, but nobody has any idea how to get it out of the update rules; the same pattern also shows up in a second simulation built on completely different rules.
2. A property that appears only when the number of parts grows without bound — no finite version of the system has it, even approximately.
3. Two systems verified to be identical in their micro-physical state at a time, which then diverge in their large-scale behaviour.
4. Someone hands you a complete derivation of the macrol law from the micro theory plus bridge principles, on a hard drive, and it checks out.
5. A traffic jam (or a cell's commitment to self-destruct — your pick) that seems to push around the individual cars/molecules, while the micro-level story is complete and law-governed.

Two constraints: no question may just ask whether something is "really emergent" or use "supervenience" or "reduction" as a magic word — it should be answerable cold by someone who's never read the literature. And the audience is students with one term of philosophy of science and no math past "as the number of parts grows" — keep the cases qualitative.

At the end, add one paragraph on which of my five cases turned out not to be diagnostic after all and why. If one of them just collapses into another node, I'd rather find out now.
