I've been reading about societal collapse on and off since the old Slate Star Codex days, and last month I finally re-read a bunch of the usual bronze-age / Maya / Easter Island material on a long flight and then got into it with my friend Dan, who does quantitative stuff adjacent to archaeology while I'm just a guy with a blog feed. We kept talking past each other because every real case is contested, so I made up two controlled ones to separate the intuitions. Island One, year 1200: roughly 40,000 people, a king, scribes, tax collectors, irrigation works, long-distance trade. By 1400: roughly 4,000 people, no writing, no king, no trade, villages only — but the same language, the same crops, the same pottery techniques, skeletons that say the survivors were taller and better fed, and no oral tradition that remembers a golden age. Island Two, same starting setup: by 1400 the population, the monuments and literacy have all grown, but the palace, its tax system and the long-distance trade network are gone within two generations, replaced by temple-based redistribution, with the old capital standing empty. Dan says neither one is a collapse, and that this shows the whole category is bogus. His steelman, which I dont think I've distorted:

(1) A word like 'collapse' earns its keep only if it tracks a real discontinuity — the thing that collapses has to stop existing, or lose most of what made it the thing it was. Otherwise it's just a label for change we disapprove of.
(2) What makes a society the society it is, is the people plus the language, practices and know-how they hand down. Palaces, tax rolls, literacy and trade routes are equipment, not identity.
(3) People-plus-practices continuity is exactly what every supposed collapse has: Aegean farmers after the palaces burned still spoke Greek and worked the same fields, Maya descendants are still there and still speak Mayan languages, Rapa Nui didn't disappear when the moai stopped going up.
(4) So the Bronze Age, the Maya and Easter Island weren't collapses at all — they were restructurings, sometimes fast ones, whose costs fell mostly on elites and specialists.
(5) And it generalizes: pick any 'collapse' you like and I'll show you a continuous population and continuous practices, because a case where the people and the practices really did end leaves no descendants and no tradition, and so never shows up in the record as a collapse in the first place.
(6) So 'collapse' has no referents; it's an aesthetic and political judgment — a system I liked stopped existing — wearing a lab coat.
(7) But (6) can't be right, and this is where I bail: Island One lost nine out of ten people and every institution above the village, and if that isn't a collapse then nothing is, and we've thrown away a distinction we plainly need.

I tried tabooing the word, I tried arguing about reference classes, we where going in circles, and I still can't locate the break. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (7) and (1) than (3), but (3) looks like a plain fact and (5) feels like it might be doing something sneaky that I can't name. Two more things I noticed and can't place: when I asked Dan how fast things have to change before it counts, he said "fast relative to the timescale of the practices that vanished," which struck me as circular without my being able to say why; and on my Island One the survivors were measurably better off, so whatever 'collapse' means it can't just mean things got worse for the people in it. Give me a diagnosis of the argument and, if you can, a positive account of what the word ought to be doing.
