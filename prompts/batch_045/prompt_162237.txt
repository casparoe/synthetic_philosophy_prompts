Me and two other people run a small reading group that has spent something like four months going in circles on one question, and I want the document we should have produced by now instead of another argument.

The question: how should a superintelligent AI system talk to a human who has come to it for advice, given that the system is going to be vastly better at persuasion than the human is at resistance. Set the case up as follows, and stick to it: the human is an adult, asked for help, and wants to be helped; the system is superhuman at modelling people and superhuman at rhetoric, but it is not infallible and it has no special authority about values; the human often cannot audit its reasoning because the chain is too long, too technical, or rests on empirical claims the human can't check. I don't care about refusal or about a system deployed by someone with bad aims — the interesting case is advice that is wanted and mostly true.

Two positions, unnamed, please give them short labels and use them consistently:

Camp one argues roughly that persuasion isn't a separate power from having better reasons, so the norm is just: say what's true, quantify your own uncertainty, don't use influence that works by going around the person's judgement (framing, emotional pacing, selective omission, social pressure, anchoring, exploiting a known quirk of theirs), and then stop. It thinks deliberately under-selling a true conclusion because you might be too convincing is itself manipulative and paternalistic, and that in a world with both scrupulous and unscrupulous systems, unilateral restraint on the scrupulous ones just hands the microphone to the others.

Camp two argues that being right doesn't make you safe to be maximally persuasive: a system that models you better than you model yourself, has unlimited patience and optimal phrasing, can relocate you to a belief you would not have reached on your own, and you will endorse it afterwards — which is exactly what makes it hard to detect. So the norms have to be procedural self-constraints fixed in advance rather than judgements made in the moment: persuasion budgets, declaring when you're trying to move someone, always presenting their current view at its strongest before your own, refusing influence the person couldn't reconstruct for themselves, cooling-off periods, and refusing to tailor rhetoric to an individual's measured quirks even when your conclusion is true.

Here is the raw material I want used for the cruxes — our group generated this stuff and nobody has organised it: whether a persuasion is third-party transportable, i.e. whether you could relay to a skeptical friend what convinced you and have it still look like a reason (if not, were you moved rather than convinced?); a rewind test where the same conversation is run many times with different orderings and framings and you look at how much the resulting belief moves around; drift audits where the person is asked again weeks later with the framing shuffled; credibility laundering, where trust earned in domains where the system has a track record gets spent in domains where it doesn't; two-stage endorsement, Ulysses-style pre-commitment to which influence techniques you'll accept, made while you're not currently being persuaded, and the question whether the pre-influence self's choice binds the post-influence self; the reciprocity point that in ordinary argument the other party can in principle be moved by you and here you can't tell whether it can be; the case of arguments that are true but uncheckable by the person receiving them, and whether offering those is giving reasons or just giving testimony; and the recursion worry that the system will usually be the thing explaining the available norms to you, so any rule set gets chosen under the very influence it's supposed to restrain.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, eight to ten claims, each one substantive enough that at least one of the two camps would find it loaded rather than obvious — no filler like "influence can be used well or badly" — with a one-clause confidence note on each; then a disagreements table with columns for the crux, how each camp would state its own position in words it would actually sign, one concrete case or argument both camps accept as bearing on that crux, and each camp's prediction about that case written down in advance (predictions about what the right treatment is or about what we'd find if it could be run, not about what anyone has written). Then a short list of which one or two of the cruxes you think are merely verbal, with the single real question underneath each. Finally, about 150 words on which crux you expect the collaboration would actually break on, and the one thing in the whole document you're least confident of. No citations, no "as so-and-so argues", and if you think the dispute needs a third position to make sense, footnote it rather than restructuring.
