Write a philosophical essay that diagnoses, rather than adjudicates, a dispute I have been unable to make progress on: whether the all-things-considered relation 'better than' is transitive.

The dialectic as I encounter it is this. On one side, spectrum arguments of the familiar population kind proceed by laying out a long chain of outcomes, each adjacent pair differing only slightly — a small loss along one dimension bought with a large gain along another, or a large gain bought with a small loss — and then pointing out that the endpoints differ enormously. If every adjacent judgment is sound and betterness is transitive, the endpoint conclusion follows; if the endpoint conclusion is unacceptable, something has to give. The intransitivist lets betterness be the thing that gives: the relation is irreducibly comparative, respects are irreducibly plural, and no transitive ordering can honor all the local comparisons we are willing to make, so 'better than' may cycle. The defender of transitivity has three replies I find individually strong: reject at least one adjacent step rather than the transitivity of the relation; treat the chain as a sorites in which 'better than' silently shifts its comparison class or standard from link to link, so that transitivity holds within a fixed context and the appearance of a valid chain is an equivocation; or insist that betterness is constrained by the role it plays in rational choice, and that a relation which cannot be maximized, represented, or used without exposure to a money pump is not a betterness relation at all. Every one of these moves has been answered, and the answers have been answered, and nobody I read has moved in years.

Here is the case I want a diagnosis to earn its keep on, since it is the version of the puzzle I find hardest to dismiss. Take three outcomes scored on three respects — call them achievement, pleasure, and understanding — where within each respect there is a meaningful unit scale, but no respect is assumed to be reducible to any other, and comparisons across respects can only be made by the rough, respect-by-respect judgments we actually make. Let the outcomes be X = (2, 2, 2), Y = (0, 3, 3), and Z = (1, 1, 4). Now adopt only this principle: if one outcome beats another by two units in some respect while trailing by no more than one unit in each other respect, it is better. X then beats Y by two in achievement while trailing by one in each of the others; Y beats Z by two in pleasure while trailing by one in each of the others; Z beats X by two in understanding while trailing by one in each of the others. So X is better than Y, Y better than Z, and Z better than X.

I want four things from the essay.

First, a diagnosis. Is this a substantive disagreement about a single relation, a verbal disagreement in which two sides use 'better than' for different relations — a local, respects-sensitive comparison versus a global ordering — or two questions sharing a name: a formal question about what structure a relation must have to play the betterness role in deliberation, and a substantive question about what we owe to the content of our particular comparative judgments? My own hypothesis is the last, but I want it tested against the obvious intransitivist reply: she will say that it is precisely the all-things-considered relation that cycles, and that the demand for a representable ordering simply changes the subject. Say whether the equivocation diagnosis survives that reply, or has to be recast as a disagreement about the job description of betterness.

Second, whether the formal and pragmatic constraints really bind. Does the money pump argument go through against a cyclical agent who can form plans, commit in advance, or refuse to trade, or does it establish at most that choice, not betterness, must be disciplined? And does the availability of weaker representational structures — threshold representations, semiorders, interval orders — show that transitivity was never the formal constraint it was made out to be?

Third, a feature of the construction that I cannot tell whether to take seriously. Write each pairwise comparison as a vector of gains and losses; the three difference vectors must sum to zero around the cycle, so in each respect the single gain is exactly offset by the two losses. Hence a cycle of this shape exists only if the required gain is no more than twice the tolerated loss; tighten the gain threshold beyond that and the cycle becomes impossible. The construction is therefore knife-edge: the 'big' gain is at most twice the 'small' loss, which is not very big. Is that a genuine liability — do cycles require a kind of numerical fine-tuning that sits badly with the claim that betterness is just our local judgments — or is it an artifact of a symmetric, three-respect, uniform-threshold toy, which dissolves as soon as respects have their own thresholds or the cycle involves more options?

Fourth, and most important, what progress would even look like. Would it be a distinction between betterness and choice-worthiness; a reframing of the question into which formal structure best models the role betterness plays, and is that reframing progress or a change of subject; agreement about which pairwise judgments count as data; or nothing at all? Give reasons for your answer, and give an honest verdict on whether this stalemate is a sign that the question is ill-posed or merely that it is hard. I do not want a survey of positions, and I do not want a verdict on transitivity itself; I want the structure of the disagreement, argued for.
