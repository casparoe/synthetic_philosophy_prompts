I'm working through a chapter on confirmation in a popular philosophy-of-science book, and it presents the raven paradox roughly as follows:

"All ravens are black" is really a material conditional: it says nothing more than that nothing is both a raven and not black, and it is therefore true in exactly the same circumstances as "all non-black things are non-ravens." Now Nicod's criterion says that a thing confirms a hypothesis when it has the properties the hypothesis mentions, and a white shoe is a non-black non-raven; so the shoe confirms the second sentence. Since logically equivalent sentences say the same thing, any adequate theory of confirmation must say that the shoe confirms the first as well: you can learn about ravens without looking at a single raven. Hempel's own conclusion was that this is a reductio of the qualitative programme — confirmation theory cannot respect both logic and intuition unless it becomes probabilistic. And the paradox is now generally regarded as settled. On the Bayesian account, the shoe really does raise the probability that all ravens are black, but by a fantastically small amount, because non-black objects vastly outnumber ravens; so our sense that the shoe tells us nothing about ravens is vindicated once we stop confusing "raises the probability" with "raises it appreciably." The episode is best read as a historical curiosity — an artefact of trying to do confirmation theory before Bayesianism.

Something here strikes me as off, especially the material-conditional framing, but I can't locate the mistake.
