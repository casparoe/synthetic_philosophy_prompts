I'm on a review panel for an interdisciplinary funding programme that backs "mechanistic explanation" projects — mostly cognitive science and systems biology — and my colleague and I have the same argument every cycle. A proposal comes in that explains some higher-level capacity in lower-level terms: a spatial-navigation capacity in terms of the circuitry, an ecosystem's recovery after a perturbation in terms of population dynamics, a memory consolidation story in terms of synaptic stuff. One of us says "that's a proper crane, it does real explanatory work," the other says "that's greedy reductionism, they've explained the capacity away," and neither of us can say what would settle it. So I want actual criteria to argue from.

What I want from you: the list of things a proposed lower-level explanation of a higher-level pattern has to deliver to count as a crane rather than a skyhook, and as a reduction rather than a greedy one. Assume the reader is a reviewer who isn't a philosopher, so for each item give the case or consideration behind it plus roughly the question you'd actually put to the proposal — "does the story say what the phenomenon is indifferent to?," "does it say where the pattern breaks down?," that kind of thing. Not "it must be consistent with fundamental physics," which is toothless, and not one big blob item called "empirically testable."

Here's the part I'm genuinely unsure about and want you to sort out. I've started separating two ways these stories fail: the story can be *too cheap* (it keeps the vocabulary but leaves it doing no work, so the phenomenon gets dissolved rather than explained), or *too expensive* (it saves the phenomenon by wheeling in machinery whose own operation is never accounted for). I like the contrast but I can't tell weather it's really two failure modes or one failure mode seen from two sides — and I especially can't tell whether my "too cheap" test just collapses into "the higher-level claim has to survive the reduction unchanged."

Also: mark which items are non-negotiable and which are a preference of one camp. I think one of us wants reductions to be genuine derivations and the other is happy with a mechanism sketch, and I suspect that's a taste difference rather than a requirement, but I'd like that argued. Flag any pair we can't reasonably demand at once. And tell me which single requirement does the most work at knocking proposals out — and if everything we fund fails it, which one I should relax first. Don't run any of my examples through the list, I'll do that myself, and no citations anywhere.
