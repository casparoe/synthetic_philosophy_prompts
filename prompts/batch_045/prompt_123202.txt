I have agreed to lead a seventy-five-minute session on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the impossibility of moral responsibility, for a continuing-education reading group: about a dozen adults with no philosophy training, most of whom have absorbed a fair amount of popular writing on free will. A few will arrive assuming the topic is brain scans and determinism; one or two will arrive assuming it has something to do with the other Strawson's account of our reactive attitudes and will be baffled when it turns out to be an argument that moral responsibility is impossible. I need a plan for the session, not an explanation of the argument — I have the argument.

Some context that may matter. I spent most of last night arguing about this with a friend, and I could not shift her. Her position, which I have not seen in any presentation of the argument I know, was that Strawson treats moral responsibility as something that must be *originated* by the agent, when it is really something that can be *transmitted and diluted* along a causal chain, the way an obligation can be passed on. On her view an agent is responsible for an action to the degree that the action flows from a character she has come, over time, to endorse under a description she would still endorse; the regress shows only that there is no unmoved mover, and she says we never needed one. I replied that endorsement is itself an exercise of the character one already has, and she said that this is exactly the move that begs the question. I would like the session planned so that her objection gets a genuine hearing at the right point, rather than being either quietly skipped or allowed to eat the hour.

Concretely, then:

1. The order in which the components have to arrive, and what depends on what. In particular: what has to be in place before the three steps can even be stated — does the target notion, the strong desert-involving kind of responsibility the argument is about, have to be pinned down first, or can it be allowed to fall out of the argument? Does the point that the argument is indifferent between determinism and indeterminism have to come before the argument or only after it, and what goes wrong in each order? Do they need to be shown an infinite regress that is benign before they can see why this one is claimed to be vicious, or does that comparison obscure more than it reveals?

2. The single case that has to carry the whole argument. I have been using a mid-level manager who quietly arranges for a subordinate's project to fail, but I suspect it is the wrong case. Tell me what the case must have and what it must lack. My own guesses: it must not be a case of compulsion, coercion, addiction or mental illness, because those invite the reply that of course she is not responsible, which is not the claim; and it must be a case in which the agent wholeheartedly identifies with her own motives, so that the hierarchical-identification escape route is closed. I also suspect the case must not involve an identifiable external agent who shaped her character, since that converts the discussion into an argument about upbringing and luck rather than about the regress. But I am not confident about any of that, and I do not know what the case must positively contain.

3. The misconceptions, and the point in the sequence where each should be met. The ones I expect: that this is the old argument from determinism in a new vocabulary; that it is covertly an empirical claim about heritability or childhood; that its conclusion is that we should stop blaming people, or that nothing anyone does is praiseworthy; the move where a participant grants that the agent cannot be responsible for her character but insists that she can perfectly well be responsible for what she does *given* the character she has — is that a rebuttal, or is it the very step the argument denies, and at what point in the sequence does that become visible to them; the suggestion that genuine self-creation might happen gradually across a life rather than in a single act; the thought that an uncaused chooser, or a soul, would close the gap; and the despairing version, that if the argument works then there is no point in trying to become better. Which of these am I underestimating, which will arrive in an order I am not expecting, and which one or two should I deliberately decline to answer?

4. Where my friend's transmission-and-dilution objection belongs in the sequence, and how much of it I should let the group build for themselves before I name it.

5. One question to end on that would show me whether they have understood the argument rather than merely been persuaded by it — ideally something they can get wrong in a way that tells me specifically where they went wrong.

6. What I should openly refuse to go into at this level, and say out loud that I am refusing.

Please do not give me a reading list or point me towards the secondary literature; I have the material. And please do not restate the argument back to me — the difficulty is entirely in the sequencing, the choice of case, and the map of what this particular audience will get wrong.
