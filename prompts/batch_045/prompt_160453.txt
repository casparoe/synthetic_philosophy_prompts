I spent rather more than forty years in experimental physics, much of it concerned with measurement and with the awkward gap between what an instrument records and what we are entitled to say happened. In retirement I have begun reading in the philosophy of mind, and a small puzzle there has lodged itself in my attention.

The case is the one philosophers call colour phi. A red disc is flashed briefly at position A; after a gap, a green disc is flashed at position B, a few degrees away. Observers report a single disc travelling from A to B whose colour changes abruptly about midway along the path, and they locate that change at a moment that falls before the second disc has so much as been presented. The explanations on offer are generally sorted into two families, which I take to be roughly these. On an *Orwellian* account the observer first undergoes one experience and then, in the light of what comes later, the record of it is quietly rewritten: the earlier state was there, and was subsequently expunged. On a *Stalinesque* account there is no earlier state to expunge: the discrepancy is settled before anything reaches consciousness, and the finished result is presented as having been there all along, rather as a verdict precedes the performance of the trial that appears to deliver it.

The received view, as I understand it, is that nothing could in principle distinguish these two stories, and that the question they pose is therefore empty. I find this too quick, and it is here that my old habits rebel. In physics we are quite accustomed to descriptions that no measurement can tell apart and that we nevertheless decline to call merely verbal: the choice of gauge in electrodynamics, the choice between the Schrödinger and the Heisenberg pictures. We do not conclude that there is no fact of the matter; we say instead that the difference is real but not empirically manifest, or that it is a difference in representation and that the representation is ours to choose for reasons of convenience. I want to know whether the two stories above are like that, or unlike it, and precisely where the analogy breaks if it breaks.

To make the question awkward, let me stipulate a protocol I have sketched. I offer it as a thought experiment rather than as anything I believe to have been done. Run the display as described, but with the gap stretched to the far end of the range over which the phenomenon still obtains — suppose, for the sake of the argument, four hundred milliseconds, so that there is room to intervene. On half the trials, chosen at random, a tone sounds sixty milliseconds after the first disc goes off, instructing the observer to press one of two keys as fast as possible, reporting whether the disc is red or green at that very instant; latencies are recorded. Suppose that on a substantial proportion of trials the press lands before the second disc is presented, and that those presses are predominantly green, as though the colour had already changed while the second disc was still in the future. Suppose further that on a quarter of the probed trials the second disc is silently omitted, so that the sequence ends with the first disc and the probe; and that on those trials the early presses are also predominantly green, even though nothing green was ever shown, and the observers, questioned afterwards, deny that anything moved or changed colour at all and describe a stationary red disc at A. The same early response thus counts, in the observers' own subsequent account, either as a veridical report of a colour change in progress or as a report of nothing of the kind, depending on whether an event occurring after the response ever occurs.

I also suspect that the usual dichotomy is not exhaustive. It seems to me to run together two questions: first, when the relevant content is fixed, that is, when the visual system first possesses a discrimination of a green disc between A and B that can guide a report; and second, when the experience bearing that content is dated in the stream of experience. If those can come apart, then more than two positions are available, and the familiar dispute may be a degenerate case of a larger one.

What I would like from you is a dialogue between two philosophers of mind who have both fully understood the standard argument that the contrast is empty, and who nevertheless disagree: one holding that it marks no real distinction, the other holding that it marks a real distinction which our ordinary ways of talking obscure rather than eliminate. Please observe the following.

Give both of them equal competence. Each must state the other's position in a form the other would endorse before criticising it. Both must engage the protocol: each should say what they would infer from the stipulated results, and the one who denies the distinction must be pressed on whether the dependence of the early response upon a later event is a datum requiring explanation or merely an artefact of putting the question badly, while the one who defends the distinction must be pressed on whether any criterion they propose for dating the experience could return a different verdict for the two stories. Both must confront my analogy with gauge freedom and with the alternative pictures of quantum mechanics, one arguing that it transfers and the other explaining exactly which disanalogy defeats it. Both must also say whether my fourfold way of decomposing the question dissolves the dispute or merely relocates it, and where. If either speaker objects that the results I have stipulated are impossible, the other should press them on whether the impossibility is empirical or conceptual. Let each concede what ought to be conceded, and end with each speaker naming, in a single sentence, the finding or consideration that would most move them towards the other. I would rather they argued from the considerations than from authority, so let them appeal to names, titles and dates as little as possible.

A dialogue of roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words, in a formal register, with each speaker's lines clearly attributed, would suit me very well.
