Quick bit of background before the actual question: I'm retired now — forty-odd years in infectious disease dynamics, transmission rates, dose-response curves, that kind of thing — and in the last year or so I've drifted into philosophy as a hobby. Its become a bit of a hobbyhorse of mine that Tolstoy's late-1890s book on art is basically a transmission model: art is whatever infects its audience with a feeling the artist first felt himself, and the good stuff is the subset that draws people towards the consciousness of sonship to God and the brotherhood of men, while counterfeits give themselves away by borrowing, imitation, effectfulness and diversion.

Fine as a picture, but in my old line of work a transmission model isn't worth anything until you operationalise it, so I've sketched eight candidate tests for whether something is genuinely infecting people in the way Tolstoy cares about. Please sort them into tiers — my four, roughly: "I'd actually use this", "usable with caveats", "only any good as a filter for what isn't art", "actively misleading" — and give each a mark out of 10 with a sentence on why it lands where it does.

1. Plain infection: does it induce the feeling in the receiver at all?
2. Sincerity: was the feeling really the artist's own, or manufactured to order?
3. Transmission fidelity: how close is what the receiver ends up feeling to what the artist reports having felt — think of it as strain-typing the emotion rather than just counting cases.
4. Channel robustness: does it still infect through a degraded channel — a clumsy translation, a plot summary, a piano reduction of an orchestral piece?
5. Repeat-exposure survival: does it still work on the tenth hearing, or was it mostly novelty and startle?
6. Downstream carriage: does anything measurable change afterwards — behaviour, later judgement of other things — or is it an infection that leaves no trace?
7. Naive receptors: does it work on people with no training in the tradition, on children, on people from very different cultures?
8. Brotherhood filter: don't count it at all unless the feeling transmitted pulls people towards love of God and the brotherhood of man.

Two extras. First, where would a sieve built on Tolstoy's own four signs of counterfeit art sit relative to these eight — better than most of them, worse, somewhere odd in the middle — and why? Second, if you could keep only two of the eight, which two, and which one would Tolstoy himself have thought was a daft idea?
