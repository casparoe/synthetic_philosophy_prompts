I'm in the final revision of a novel — fourth draft, manuscript due to my editor in August — and the scene I keep taking apart and reassembling is the one where my narrator, a retired schoolmaster named Halvard who has spent thirty years teaching an evening class on Kant, tells an armed man at his door a flat falsehood about where the man's intended victim is hiding. I need the lie to be morally available to him: not a lapse of nerve but something a scrupulous person could choose and then have to live with, because the whole architecture of the book rests on that. There is a retired philosopher in my writing group who has read the scene twice and has now backed me into a corner I cannot argue my way out of. This is how she set it up.

(1) The universal-law formula tests the maxim the agent actually wills, described at the level of her real end, not some maxim a hostile critic would prefer to substitute for it. (2) A maxim fails the test only if willing its universalization involves a contradiction — in conception or in will. (3) Kant's best-known application of the formula, the one about obtaining a loan by promising to repay when one has no intention of repaying, works, if it works at all, by self-defeat: if everyone made false promises whenever it suited, the practice of promising would lose the credibility it runs on, and the act could no longer achieve its own end. So self-defeat of an act's own means is a genuine contradiction in conception. (4) Halvard's maxim is something like: when someone asks me for information he intends to use to commit a murder, mislead him in order to prevent the murder. (5) Universalizing (4) is self-defeating in just that way. In a world where everyone misled would-be murderers, would-be murderers would stop taking answers at face value, or would invert them, so no one could mislead them and the maxim would no longer achieve its end. Deception, no less than promising, feeds on a background expectation of truthfulness. (6) So by the very mechanism that makes (3) work, Halvard's lie fails the test: he must either answer truthfully or say nothing. (7) But I cannot accept (6), and not only because it ruins my scene. The same mechanism sweeps in actions that plainly are not wrong — driving to the hospital by the route that is fastest only because most people are not taking it, selling one's shares before a run on the bank begins, applying for the post that a hundred others are also applying for. In each case universalizing the maxim defeats the agent's own end, and in each case the verdict is absurd. (8) And if I reject (3) instead, and say that self-defeat is never a contradiction in conception, then Kant's central example loses the only argument that ever made it look convincing, and I cannot see what would be left of the formula.

I know Halvard might simply refuse to answer; I have already used silence in an earlier scene and the climax requires an outright falsehood, so that exit is closed to me. I would rather hold on to (1) and (2) than accept the sweep of (7), and I would rather not go hunting for some third kind of contradiction. Which step do you reject, why did it look sound when it did, and what does rejecting it cost me? Please do not simply report Kant's own verdict on the case — I have read the essay and it has not helped me with the structure of the argument — and please do not hand me a reading list or point me at commentators; I am working from a single room with no library and need to be able to reason it through as it stands. There is one further thing my friend said, about the class of people picked out in (4) not surviving universalization, which I did not follow at all; if that is the real hinge, explain it in terms I could put into a character's mouth.
