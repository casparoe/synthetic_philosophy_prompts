I'm a professor of philosophy with a joint appointment in the public policy school, and this term I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the ethics and epistemology of catastrophic risk. Last month my department co-hosted a workshop with political science on deterrence after the Cold War, and a security-studies colleague — a formal modeler with generally hawkish policy instincts — cut off a question of mine (whether the so-called tradition of non-use does any causal work of its own, or whether leaders have simply been calculating consequences all along) with the line that "nuclear weapons are unusable." He then put that one sentence to three quite different jobs in the space of ten minutes: that the current life-extension and modernization program is pure waste, since the weapons will never be fired; that extended deterrence guarantees to allies are bluffs, and that both sides know they are bluffs; and that further proliferation would not add much danger, because new arsenals would be exactly as unusable as old ones. Earlier in the same panel he had described nuclear deterrence as about as well-confirmed an empirical regularity as international relations has to offer, which sounded to me like it might be in tension with at least some of the things "unusable" could mean. I would rather be fair to him at the next session than merely irritating, so what I want to hand him is a disambiguation rather than a refutation.

The distinctions I have been toying with, which you should feel free to reorganize, discard, or replace with better ones: unusability might be predicated of the weapon (no employment of it could achieve any end worth the name, which is close to the early strategists' thought that the military's job had become averting wars rather than winning them), of the agent (no leader could bring herself to order it, or no order could actually be executed), of the relationship (unusable against a nuclear peer, not against a non-nuclear state or a non-state actor), of the process (any first use escapes control and escalates past anyone's intentions), of the normative environment (no lawful or legitimate use remains), or of "use" itself — a demonstration burst over open water, a single low-yield strike meant as a shot across the bow, a counterforce strike on silos, a high-altitude burst intended mainly to wreck electronics, all of which seem to differ both in whether they count as use and in how thinkable they are. There is also, it seems to me, a gap between what a leader would deliberately do and what she might be willing to risk: a threat can be made credible by raising the probability of an outcome one has no intention of choosing — dispersing forces, pre-delegating launch authority, lowering the threshold at which subordinates may act — and that complicates any flat claim that no one could ever use them.

Could you give me, as a numbered menu suitable for a one-page handout, the precise theses that "nuclear weapons are unusable" might be expressing, ordered from least to most committal (or along whatever axis you find more illuminating)? For each reading I want: the claim stated sharply enough to have a truth value; a verdict on whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical or normative claim, or probably false; and a note on what kind of evidence would count against it. Then tell me which readings, if any, would actually support each of his three uses, whether any single reading is consistent with also holding that deterrence is a robust empirical regularity, and which reading he is most likely to have meant given that he is a formal modeler with hawkish sympathies. If one of the stronger readings turns out to be self-undermining — making the slogan hard to combine with the deterrence story he also wants to tell — say so explicitly and show exactly where the tension bites.
