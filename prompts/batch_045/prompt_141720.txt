hi — I want you to try to break a position I've ended up holding about Dutch book arguments. This is for my own understanding, not for a paper, so please don't be polite about it.

The position, stated too strongly: in the classic betting set-up — the one where your betting quotients for p and for not-p had better sum to one, and likewise for the other axioms — the bookie, the stakes and the guaranteed loss are doing no justificatory work whatsoever. They're a *diagnostic*. The real norm is representational: incoherent credences misrepresent the world, or get accuracy-dominated by some coherent rival, and they'd be defective in exactly the same way in a world with no bookies, no money and no one around to make offers. Strip every mention of betting out and you lose colour and nothing else. Call this the depragmatised reading. I also hold the stronger corollary on the diachronic side: the case for updating by conditionalisation is really an accuracy argument, and the story about a bookie who comes back later and exploits the updating rule you planned to follow is again only an illustration.

I know the accuracy turn here is hardly novel. What I want is a map of what would count against it, ranked fatal → serious → merely awkward, and split by whether a defender of my view ought to concede the point or is entitled to dismiss it. Some things I keep circling:

1. The proxy-randomiser (my own construction, so feel free to dismantle it). An agent whose credences are incoherent — she'd take 0.7 on rain and 0.7 on no-rain — but who never evaluates a bet on its own. Whenever a package of bets is offered, she samples one coherent probability function at random from the coherent functions loosely nearest her own, then evaluates the whole package by that. No coherent proxy accepts both legs of the classic book, so no bookie who can't see the draw can guarantee her a loss, and even one who knows her sampling rule can't if the draw is independent of what's on offer. She looks unexploitable and yet, presumably, accuracy-dominated. Does that vindicate me or sink me? I worry it sinks me: if she's genuinely fine, then my "representational defect" has no teeth and I've slid into incoherence-isn't-criticisable-after-all, which I'd count as fatal rather than awkward.

2. Small incoherence plus real markets. Minimum stake, a spread on every trade, finite wealth. An agent whose quotients sum to 1.02 is not profitably exploitable by anybody. I say: fine, doesn't touch me, I never said the norm was about profit. Is that a legitimate sidestep or have I just relocated the norm somewhere untestable?

3. Self-locating uncertainty and memory loss. This is where I feel most exposed. Once the agent can lose track of which time or which centred possibility she's in, the accuracy story needs extra machinery — centred worlds, and a choice about how to aggregate accuracy across them — and it's not obvious it delivers the same updating rule the diachronic bookie story does, or any determinate one. If the betting story is what pins down those extra choices, my corollary is in trouble: the bookie turns out to be load-bearing after all. Say honestly how bad this is.

4. Strategic unpredictability. In a world full of adversaries, being coherent and therefore predictable is a liability; there look to be interactive situations where a knowingly incoherent but unpredictable agent does strictly better than any coherent one. If that's right, is it fatal to a representational norm, or does it just show that coherence is a norm of one kind (of representation) and not of another (of success)?

Also please do the meta-job: "the betting story is merely a diagnostic for a representational defect" is doing an awful lot of work, and if no possible gap between vulnerability and incoherence could ever count against me, I've got an unfalsifiable view wearing a falsifiable view's clothes. Tell me if that's happened, and if it has, whether it's actually a problem for a claim of this kind — I can imagine an argument that a normative claim about representation is *supposed* to be insulated from practical consequences, and I want to know whether that's principled or just convenient.

Two preferences: cases move me much more than methodology, so if you can produce one where the accuracy story and the betting story disagree and the intuition clearly sides with the betting story, lead with it.
