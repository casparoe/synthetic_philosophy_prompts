I write a column on science and ideas for a magazine, and my editor has asked me for a piece on why careful, well-sourced journalism still turns out to be wrong — the corrections page as a philosophical object, so to speak. I want to use that practical hook to get at something I find genuinely confusing, and I've drafted the essay below as a first pass. Before I develop it into a full column, I'd be grateful for your comments. Housekeeping first: please don't give me citations, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading — I have a library card and a patient research assistant, and that part of the work is covered. What neither of them can give me is a straight assessment of whether the argument actually holds together, and whether it says anything a philosopher wouldn't roll their eyes at.

***

**The Corrections Page**

A reasonably large newspaper checks something like twelve hundred separate factual claims in an average week: dates, numbers, job titles, the spelling of a village in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Suppose each check is good but not perfect — any given claim, once cleared, has about a 99.5 percent chance of being right. Then the expected number of false claims in the week's output is around six, and the chance that the week is entirely clean is roughly a quarter of one percent. The desk knows, in the ordinary flat-footed sense of "knows", that the paper it is about to publish contains errors. It just cannot tell you which ones. And it stands by every individual claim it cleared.

This is the preface paradox in newsprint. An author has good evidence for each of the hundreds of assertions in her book and believes each of them, while cheerfully writing in the preface that the book no doubt contains mistakes. The lottery paradox is its mirror image: before the draw, I am happy to say of each ticket that it will lose, while also knowing that some ticket will win. Both seem to show that perfectly respectable item-by-item confidence can add up to something you are not entitled to.

The usual way out is to raise the bar. Belief, or assertion, requires credence above some threshold; set the threshold high enough and a 99.5 percent claim never qualifies, the air clears, and the paradoxes dissolve. My objection is that this makes the bar move with the size of the batch you happen to be considering rather than with what is at stake. On a threshold view, how confident I must be before I may believe that my train leaves at 9:04 depends on whether I am, at that moment, also entertaining eleven hundred and ninety-nine other propositions. That seems wrong. Worse, a batch is not a natural kind: claims about trains, claims about trains and the weather, claims about trains and the weather and what I had for breakfast — I can gerrymander the collection so as to make the required confidence almost anything I like.

Here is the alternative I want to try out. What a careful institution regulates is not the credibility of each item but the expected number of errors across a practice: an error budget, in the engineer's sense, fixed by the cost of a false claim and the value of publishing at all. A newsroom that tolerates six expected errors a week is not being sloppy; it has decided that driving expected errors toward zero — publishing a fortnight late, or publishing a third as much — costs more than the errors do. The corrections page is not a confession of irrationality. It is the settlement mechanism for the budget.

The general moral I want to draw about the two paradoxes is that both trade on treating the batch as a free logical by-product of the items. Aggregation, I want to say, is itself a move with its own price. Asserting that the whole issue is clean is a *new* assertion, and its expected error count — about six — blows any sane budget for a claim carrying those stakes. Similarly in the lottery: believing of each ticket that it will lose is cheap, since being wrong about any one ticket costs you nothing. What has no budget is the further step of conjoining all those beliefs into the claim that no ticket will win. Closure under conjunction, on this view, is not a law of logic but a licence that has to be paid for out of the same budget.

Where I think this is vulnerable: if budgets are set by stakes, what stops me from budgeting my way into believing p, believing q, and believing not-both? And does the gerrymandering problem simply reappear one level up, at the point where I decide which practice a budget attaches to and how generous it is?

***

Concretely, I'd like your thoughts on five things. First, have I characterised the two paradoxes and the threshold response fairly, and is my "batches aren't a natural kind" objection to threshold views a real objection with a known reply I ought to acknowledge rather than pretend to have discovered? Second, is the error-budget proposal genuinely a distinct position, or does it quietly collapse into one of the things it was meant to replace — is it, for instance, just the claim that belief isn't closed under conjunction dressed up in engineering language, or a variable-threshold view in disguise, given that a budget over n items can always be converted into a per-item probability requirement? Third, is pairing the lottery and the preface at all legitimate? In the preface case the author has positive, if imperfect, evidence for each individual claim; in the lottery my evidence about each ticket is symmetric and thin, and I know the disjunction on independent grounds. Does that asymmetry mean a unified treatment is simply a mistake? Fourth, how much trouble am I in over the gerrymandering worry at the budget-setting level, and is there a principled way to say which practices get budgets? Fifth, my statistician friend insists that what I'm describing is just the familiar decision to control a false discovery rate rather than a per-comparison error rate; I've half absorbed this, and I'd like to know whether the analogy illuminates the philosophical point or merely borrows its prestige.

If any part of the essay simply begs the question or smuggles in what it needs to prove, please say so plainly, and if you can see a better version of the closing section, sketch it. I'd also welcome a verdict on whether the idea is interesting enough to be worth a column at all: I would rather kill it now than publish something a specialist would read as naive. Around eight hundred words of comments would be ideal.
