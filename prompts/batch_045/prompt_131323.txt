I write on science and ideas for a broadsheet, and over the past two years I have found myself defending — in print, and more often at dinner — a fairly strong claim: that collective decisions about policy should be settled by conditional prediction markets rather than by voting on policies directly. The version I defend is not quite the canonical one. With an economist friend I have been working up a variant in which the legislature pre-registers both a welfare index and a fixed horizon before any market opens; traders stake on the value of that index conditional on a given policy being adopted; and settlement is handled not by the realised figure alone but by what we have started calling a 'counterfactual audit' — five years on, a randomly selected citizens' assembly estimates what the index would have been under the runner-up policy, and payouts are computed on the realised-versus-counterfactual gap. The attraction, as I argued in a column last spring, is that it removes the need for the counterfactual branch that a conditional market in its purest form seems to demand, while leaving an assembly with no forecasting burden free to speak to the one thing it can speak to. My friend thinks this is precisely where it breaks: once payouts turn on a panel's retrospective judgement rather than on a published number, traders are no longer betting on the world but on the panel, the market becomes a second-order guessing game about the referees, and anyone able to influence that panel's composition, briefing or mood acquires a cheaper lever than anyone able to influence the world.

What I want is the case against the whole family of views, written as if by someone who understands why they are attractive. Please rank what would count against them, from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about the difference: which developments, observations or arguments should genuinely move a committed advocate, and which are merely embarrassing? I have already had the obvious ones from correspondents — thin markets, participants who hold a position in the underlying outcome, the difficulty of specifying a welfare index that nobody can game, the disappearance of liquidity when the question is genuinely important — so I would rather you set those aside, or say briefly why they are less central than they look, and spend your attention on what remains. In particular: which defeaters would a defender of futarchy concede as counting against it, as opposed to dismissing as resting on a misdescription of the proposal? Are there commitments that the counterfactual-audit variant takes on that the original does not, or vice versa? And is there any case the view simply cannot describe — a class of decision for which the machinery cannot even be stated, rather than one where it merely performs poorly?

I also want an honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into something unfalsifiable. In conversation its defenders retreat very quickly to a narrower claim — that this is only for decisions with a measurable outcome, a pre-committed metric and a body willing to be bound by the result — and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a principled scope restriction or a way of ensuring the view is never tested. If it is insulated, is that a problem for a claim of this kind, or is it what you would expect from a proposal about institutional design? As a reporter I would personally be most moved by a clear account of what a diagnostic failure would look like: the pattern of results from an actual pilot inside a real institution — a city, a regulator, a company with a genuine decision to make — that would tell you the mechanism itself was at fault, rather than merely that the policy selected was unlucky. I am filing this on a train with no connectivity, so please do not point me at papers, links or further reading: I want the arguments themselves, and I want them in British English.
