I sit on a small internal advisory group that is drafting guidance on which digital systems should be counted in a welfare assessment, and I have been asked to write down my own position rather than the group's, so that the others can argue with it. Here it is.

(1) Organizational sufficiency with substrate independence. Whether a system is conscious, and whether it is a moral patient, is settled by the causal organization it realizes. The chemistry is irrelevant: a sufficiently faithful realization of my neural organization in some quite different hardware would be a subject of experience and would matter morally in the way I do.

(2) Implementation is not free. I reject the arguments that conclude that any wall, rock, or bucket of molecules implements every computation there is, and therefore that the notion of implementation is empty. My preferred constraint: a system implements an organization only if the state-to-state transitions are robust — the organization is what explains the transitions, and the system would track the same counterfactual pattern across a suitable range of perturbations, rather than the pattern being an accident of one actual run.

(3) Temporal scale is irrelevant. A realization of the same organization run very much more slowly, or run in fits, is conscious and is a patient to no lesser degree. I have in mind something like this: a realization whose successive steps are triggered by the decay of a long-lived isotope, so that they are roughly a century apart, with the carrier sitting inert in a vault in between — call it the centennial mind. I want to say that it is a patient throughout, and not only during the instants of transition. I also want to say that the corresponding archive — the same sequence of states engraved on plates in a vault, with no causal commerce among them at all — is not a patient, and not a subject of anything.

(4) Spatial scatter is irrelevant, but unity is not. A single integrated organization distributed across a fleet of small devices, none carrying more than a fraction of the total state and none in contact with the others, is one subject and one patient. The mereological sum of a cathedral and my left ear is not a subject, and nothing is attributable to it. I take these two verdicts to be consistent, though a colleague says (4) is ad hoc and another says (5) is where I will get into trouble.

(5) Behavioural equivalence is not enough. A system that matches every input-output pair that will in fact arise, by a different internal route — a trained mimic whose intermediate states have nothing to do with the original's — is not thereby a patient. What matters is the intermediate organization, not the function it happens to subserve.

(6) There are no borderline cases. For any system at a time, it is determinately conscious or determinately not, and there is a determinate number of subjects present; the vagueness lies in our attributions and not in the world.

What I would like is an audit rather than a survey. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are, ranked by severity rather than listed evenly. For each real tension, say precisely what I would have to give up to restore consistency and what that surrender would cost me elsewhere in the set — in particular whether it costs me a verdict about a case I have already made, such as the centennial mind, the archive, the fleet, or the mimic. Say, too, whether any of the apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why, and whether the set has a weak point I have not named. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and if you think the package is defensible as it stands, tell me what it commits me to that I have not noticed. I would rather be argued with about the cases than told which isms my views approximate.
