I'm a journalist putting together a feature on dream science — the sleep-lab business of waking people up and asking what was just happening in their heads, plus the newer attempts to read dream content out of brain recordings — and I want to run a little interactive next to it: a flow chart that sorts readers into a theory of what dreaming actually is. The crux I keep circling is the old fight over whether dreams are experiences at all: the Malcolm-style line that it makes no sense to say someone is having experiences while flat out asleep, so a dream report isn't a report of anything, against Dennett-style replies that rescue dreams as real experiences while dropping the idea of an ordered movie running in your head in anything like real time. I want that whole territory covered, not just the two famous poles.

Build the chart from the logical space first and the names second. Constraints:

- Six branch questions max, seven to nine leaves. Start from something every reader grants: people do sincerely report dreams, and something goes on in the sleeping brain.
- Plain language throughout, no jargon, and no philosopher's name in any question. Each question should be a concrete case or a plain claim someone with no philosophy background can answer without knowing where it leads. At least two branches must be built on concrete cases: one on a lucid dreamer who signals to the lab with deliberate eye movements during REM, one on someone woken after a couple of minutes who insists they just lived through something that felt like an hour.
- Branch on the distinctions I care about: whether there is a fact of the matter about experiencing during sleep when nothing could in principle settle it; whether, granting that, there is a real ordered sequence during sleep or the order only gets assembled at waking; whether dream-time has to track sleep-time; whether what happens during sleep has content even if nobody experiences it; whether the fragments that later get assembled were themselves experienced; and whether there is one continuing subject across the night or only disconnected moments.
- Required leaves: both famous poles, plus a plain full-strength realism (dreams really are perception-like streams, just detached from the senses), plus three views I'm making up and want placed properly in the space: the rehearsal view (dreaming is real, contentful cognitive activity with no phenomenology at all, and the report is a later reconstruction), the time-warp view (real ordered experience, but dream duration is a feature of the experience and needn't match the chunk of sleep it occupies), and the checkpoint view (there are real experiential moments during sleep but nothing binds them into the experience of one continuing subject, so the I who does the reporting is a retrospective artifact). Each leaf gets one sentence on what it says and one on its most awkward commitment.
- Then the combinations the questions allow but nobody seems to occupy: mark them unnamed or empty and give one line on whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Flag any pair of leaves your chart cannot really separate, and flag any question a general reader will simply refuse to answer.
- Don't cite papers, don't hand me a reading list, and don't hang anything on a contested empirical finding. I've got the sources; I definately don't want anything in the piece I can't check myself.

Give it back in these sections, in this order, roughly: The setup (~80 words), The branch questions (~400 words), The leaves (~250 words), Empty cells (~150 words), Where the chart breaks (~120 words).
