i teach a third-year seminar on ancient ethical and political thought and we are doing Aristotle on exchange this week, so of course the one thing i actually have to sort out is the bit i have never been happy with. we run a joint reading group with the economic historians and one of them -- works on medieval coinage, very good, also very annoying -- kept pushing the line that for Aristotle money just is a yardstick. it exists by convention rather than by nature, which is supposedly why its name echoes the word for law; it steps in as a stand-in for need, which is what really holds a community together; it makes a house and a pair of shoes commensurable so the builder and the shoemaker can hit the right proportion and the association can keep going. fine so far. but he then used it as a weapon against the passage in book one of the Politics where Aristotle says money is barren and that breeding money from money is the most contrary-to-nature way of getting wealth -- leaning, as usual, on the fact that the word for interest is also the word for offspring. his argument: if money is a measure, then lending it at a fee is like renting someone a measuring rod. nobody thinks there is anything wrong with renting out a ruler. so the horror of interest is not coming out of Aristotle's own account of what money is; he is quietly adding a moral premise about what money is *for*, and once you notice the addition the argument collapses into a prejudice against traders. the analogy was doing the work, not the text.

i pushed back and did it badly, and i have a lecture on thursday. so please audit the analogy for me.

give me the correspondence explicitly, table is fine and actually helpful since i want to put it on a slide: the ruler is money, but what are the lengths, what are the inches, who is the surveyor, what in the target plays the role of the thing being measured, what plays the role of the table the ruler lies on. then the disanalogies, and -- this is the part i care about -- separate the ones that matter for the use he is making of it from the ones that are just noise. my own suspicion is that the load-bearing problem is that the fee on a loan is paid in the same stuff that was lent: you rent a ruler and get paid back in goods, you lend money and get paid back in money plus money, and a measure that measures more of itself is not obviously nonsense but is not what rulers do either. am i over egging that, or is it where the argument actually turns?

two stress tests i made up and cannot answer. first: imagine a polis that owns the official stone weights and lets merchants use them for a small fee. if that is unproblematic, why is not interest unproblematic; if it is problematic, what is the difference, given that a weight is even more obviously a pure convention than a coin is. second: imagine a currency that is a pure counter with no metal in it at all, so nothing about it is wanted for its own sake and its value is entirely by agreement. does Aristotle's story about how money arises even get off the ground there, and does that make the ruler better or worse as a picture.

i also want to know what the ruler quietly imports that money lacks: that the quantity measured is there before the measuring, that a good measure does not stretch, that the material of the measure does not matter, and above all that nobody wants the ruler. that last one worries me, because Aristotle's whole diagnosis of wealth-getting is that people come to want the measure itself as an end -- if money were a ruler that pathology would be unintelligible, so the analogy makes his central complaint look like a conceptual blunder rather than an objection. if so, the conclusion it was used to carry is partly borrowed.

so: does the conclusion survive? is it that Aristotle needs a premise about proper use that his own measure story will not supply, or is it that the story can be read so that it does supply one, or is it that his conclusion is defensible but for a reason the ruler picture cannot express? and if there is a better analogy available -- a cloakroom ticket, a warehouse receipt, a set of standard weights, a language, scoring counters in a game -- tell me which one and say what it gets right that the ruler does not, and what it costs. also flag anywhere i would be teaching something the scholarship is genuinely split on, since i would rather say so out loud in the room than get caught. please skip the reading suggestions, i have plenty.
