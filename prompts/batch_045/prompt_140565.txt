I'm in a tiny reading group on animal minds — half philosophy grad students, half vet students — and we keep running into the same thing, and I don't have a word for it. Here's the shape of it. We read a paper where crabs with a damaged claw kept grooming the injured area for ages, gave up a shelter they normally liked in order to avoid a place where they'd been shocked, and stopped acting like that when they were given a local anaesthetic. The authors concluded that this is consistent with nociception and doesn't show that the animal is in pain. But in the same week someone brought in a case study of a dog doing a quarter of that and nobody hedged at all — the dog was described as being in pain, full stop. Then with fish it happened again: a fish given something noxious stops doing its usual things, rubs the spot, changes what it'll swim through, and the changes go away if you give it the same drugs we'd call painkillers; and the response from the skeptic in our group was that it's all reflexes and that fish don't have the brain structures that consciousness requires — except the structures he named were mammalian ones, which seems like it settles the question by definition rather than by evidence. What I keep noticing isn't that people are sceptical. It's that identical behavioural evidence gets treated as strong in one animal and as almost nothing in another, and the difference never seems to come from the evidence.

And when you push on it, the person never says what would be enough. I've started asking directly — what would you accept as evidence that a shrimp, or a bee, or a fish, is conscious? — and I get one of two answers: either a shrug and "it's a hard problem, we don't have a test yet", said by someone who keeps arguing as if the answer were no, or a demand for something that couldn't possibly be evidence for anything, like a report from the animal itself, or a neural signature that has already been matched against a human one. And that second one is the bit that interests me most, because if the extra ingredient you're asking about makes no difference to what the animal does or how its behaviour is organised, then no amount of behaviour was ever going to count, and the demand isn't really a demand for evidence. One of the vet students says I'm overthinking it and this is just Bayesian: if you start with a strong prior that mammals feel pain and insects don't, the same evidence rationally moves you different amounts and there's nothing wrong with that. I half believe her, but it doesn't feel like the whole story, because nobody in these arguments owns the prior — they present the asymmetrical treatment as a judgment about how good the evidence is. I've also been told it's just speciesism, but that's a moral accusation and I'm asking about what's going on in the reasoning. And I half remember a word beginning with anthropo- for refusing to see mental states in animals (anthropodenial? I might have that wrong) but that seems like a knee-jerk attitude, whereas what I'm describing is careful, argued, and sometimes right. The thing that makes me think it's a real pattern and not just me being annoyed is the parallel I read about, unless I'm misremembering it, where patients diagnosed as unresponsive were later found, using brain scanners, to be able to follow instructions — same structure, same refusal, and there it turned out to be an actual mistake with stakes.

So: does this have a name, or a literature, or is it just three or four different things I'm lumping together? I don't want a bare label — I want the carving. Roughly: what exactly is the phenomenon (or phenomena), what is it not, and where are its edges? If there's an established concept that nearly fits, tell me how what I described differs from it and whether the fit is good enough anyway; if the nearest concepts are several, say which fits which of my cases. If there's genuinely nothing, coin something for me and define it tightly enough that I could tell whether a given case is an instance, including the awkward cases — like the AI version, where the same move happens in the other direction and every bit of behaviour gets explained away as pattern-matching, or the case where the sceptic turns out to have been right. And tell me whether it's a mistake at all, or just what good reasoning looks like when the prior is doing the work. Anway, one more thing: please flag which parts of your answer are uncontroversial among people who actually work on this, which are one side of a live dispute (the fish-cortex thing, the insect thing, the whole methodology of inference here), and which are just your own take — I don't want to walk into the group on Tuesday repeating a contested position as though it were the consensus, so say how confident you are in each bit.
