My reading group did Nāgārjuna last week and Ive been chewing on it since. One member — a logic guy who'd never read any Indian philosophy before — stayed after and wrote an argument on the whiteboard that he says shows the Madhyamaka tetralemma is either vacuous or self-refuting, and I think it's the sharpest version of that complaint I've heard, but it also seems to prove way too much, and I can't find where it breaks. Here it is, cleaned up a bit (numbering is mine):

(1) The catuskoti says that for a question about how things really are, there are four possible positions — P, not-P, both P and not-P, and neither P nor not-P — and Nāgārjuna'sArguments run through all four and reject each one. The stock examples are questions like whether a liberated being exists after death.

(2) Those four are jointly exhaustive and mutually exclusive: they're just the two independent yes/no questions "does P hold?" and "does not-P hold?" crossed with each other, 2×2, so there is no fifth status a proposition could have.

(3) So "none of the four" isn't an answer to the question at all. Either it's silence, which can't be what a philosophical argument is doing, or it's itself a fifth claim — "no corner applies" — and that claim has to be true, false, both, or neither, i.e. it lands back inside one of the four corners and refutes itself. Either way the tetralemma can't do the dialectical work it's supposed to do.

(4) The obvious reply is that Nāgārjuna isn't asserting a thesis. Emptiness is itself empty; his arguments are reductios that borrow the opponent's commitments and knock them down without putting a counter-position in their place. So there's nothing for (3) to grab onto.

(5) But that reply, he says, detonates the whole position: if nothing whatever has intrinsic nature (svabhāva), then the two truths — conventional and ultimate — have no intrinsic nature either, and the distinction between them is as empty as everything else. Collapse it one way and everything is merely conventional, so "all things are empty" is just one more conventional claim with no ultimate backing and the argument establishes nothing. Collapse it the other way and only the ultimate is real, while every sentence in the text, every inference, every distinction the argument relies on is conventional and so unreal — in which case the argument can't even be stated.

(6) And notice the argument generalizes: whatever licenses rejecting all four corners for the question about the liberated being licenses rejecting all four for any question whatsoever — including whether Madhyamaka itself is true. So the view proves that no argument can establish anything, itself included, which nobody can believe.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd really like to keep (4) — the no-thesis bit — because without it I don't see how Madhyamaka is different from plain skepticism, or from just flatly asserting that nothing exists. And I want to keep (5)'s starting premise, that emptiness applies to absolutely everything without exception, since that seems like the whole point. So if something has to go I'd rather it be (2) or (3), but (2) looks like mere combinatorics and (3) looks like it follows straight from (2). Is there something wrong with (1) instead — some way the "both" and "neither" corners aren't built out of the same negation as the first two, so that the 2×2 never gets off the ground? And if the problem is really in (5), tell me exactly what I'm paying for keeping it, because its not obvious to me that "empty" and "nonexistent" come apart in the way I'd need them to.
