**The question (45-minute take-home, 25 marks).** Throughout, Ω is a finite set of states; agent i has a partition Pᵢ of Ω (her information), so that at state ω she can only rule out states outside her cell Pᵢ(ω). A *belief map* for i is a function bᵢ: Ω → Δ(Ω) that is constant on each cell of Pᵢ and satisfies bᵢ(ω)(Pᵢ(ω)) = 1 for every ω. A *common prior* for a pair of belief maps (b₁, b₂) is a single μ ∈ Δ(Ω) with μ(ω) > 0 for every ω such that, for each i and each ω, bᵢ(ω) = μ(· | Pᵢ(ω)).

*Scenario 1.* Ω = {ω₁, …, ω₅}, μ uniform (1/5 on each state). The event of interest is E = {ω₂, ω₄}. Ann's partition is A₁ = {ω₁, ω₂}, A₂ = {ω₃, ω₄, ω₅}; Bob's is B₁ = {ω₁, ω₂, ω₃}, B₂ = {ω₄, ω₅}. Both belief maps are obtained from μ by conditioning.

(a) [3 marks] Give Ann's and Bob's posterior probability for E in each of their cells, and list the states at which their posteriors initially differ.

(b) [4 marks] They now announce their posteriors for E publicly and simultaneously, and repeat: in each round both hear the other's number without noise, update by conditioning on everything they have heard, and announce again. Run the process at each of the five states until the announced numbers stop changing. State how many rounds are needed and verify that when it stops the two announced numbers are equal at every state.

(c) [3 marks] Show that before any announcement the only event that is common knowledge at any state is Ω itself. Conclude that Aumann's agreement theorem does not rule out the initial disagreement you found in (a), and explain in one sentence why the disagreement has gone by the time the process in (b) stops.

*Scenario 2.* Ω = {ω₁, …, ω₄} = {(h₁,k₁), (h₁,k₂), (h₂,k₁), (h₂,k₂)}, where the first coordinate is the reading of the sensor Ann sees and the second the reading of the sensor Bob sees: Ann's partition is A₁ = {ω₁, ω₂} ("h₁"), A₂ = {ω₃, ω₄} ("h₂"); Bob's is B₁ = {ω₁, ω₃} ("k₁"), B₂ = {ω₂, ω₄} ("k₂"). This time the belief maps are stipulated directly, not derived from any prior:
— Ann, after h₁: (3/4, 1/4, 0, 0); after h₂: (0, 0, 3/4, 1/4).
— Bob, after k₁: (1/2, 0, 1/2, 0); after k₂: (0, 1/4, 0, 3/4).

(d) [9 marks] Does a common prior exist? Either exhibit one and verify the conditioning conditions, or prove that none exists. Two routes are available for the negative verdict: (i) show that the consistency equations have no solution with all entries positive; (ii) exhibit an *acceptable bet*, i.e. transfers f_A, f_B: Ω → ℝ with f_A(ω) + f_B(ω) ≤ 0 for every ω, such that Ann strictly wants to accept f_A given either of her cells (E[f_A | A₁] > 0 and E[f_A | A₂] > 0, computed with her stipulated belief map) and likewise for Bob. State also whether your verdict changes if the requirement μ(ω) > 0 is dropped and zero-probability states are allowed.

(e) [6 marks] Two short questions.
(e1) [3 marks] Distinguish the common prior assumption from the claim that the two agents' posterior beliefs coincide, and explain why the so-called no-trade theorem is not a corollary of the common prior assumption alone: name the extra premises it needs and state its conclusion precisely.
(e2) [3 marks] Return to Scenario 1, but suppose each announcement travels over a channel that drops every message independently with probability ε ∈ (0,1), and that a dropped message leaves the hearer's beliefs unchanged (both agents know ε and the structure of the channel; model this by enlarging the state space to include the transmission history). Is it ever common knowledge, at any state of the enlarged space, what the two agents' posteriors are? What follows for Aumann's theorem?

**The student's answer, reproduced as submitted.**

*(a)* Ann: in A₁, P(E|A₁) = (1/5)/(2/5) = 1/2; in A₂, P(E|A₂) = (1/5)/(3/5) = 1/3. Bob: in B₁, P(E|B₁) = (1/5)/(3/5) = 1/3; in B₂, P(E|B₂) = (1/5)/(2/5) = 1/2. So Ann and Bob disagree at ω₁ and ω₂ (1/2 against 1/3) and at ω₄ and ω₅ (1/3 against 1/2). At ω₃ both have 1/3, so there is no disagreement there.

*(b)* One exchange suffices. At ω₄ Ann announces 1/3 and Bob announces 1/2; hearing 1/2, Ann infers that Bob's cell is B₂, hence that the state lies in A₂ ∩ B₂ = {ω₄, ω₅}, and her new posterior is P(E|{ω₄,ω₅}) = 1/2; symmetrically, hearing 1/3 Bob infers A₂ and also gets 1/2. A further round changes nothing, because each already knows the state is in {ω₄, ω₅}, so 1/2 is announced again. The same happens at ω₅. At ω₁ and ω₂ both end at 1/2: Ann hearing 1/3 infers B₁, hence {ω₁, ω₂}, and Bob hearing 1/2 infers A₁, hence {ω₁, ω₂}. At ω₃ both announce 1/3 at the first round but then each infers the other's cell and narrows to {ω₃}, so both announce 0 from the second round on. So by the end of one round-trip the announced numbers are equal everywhere.

*(c)* Starting from ω₁, Ann's cell is {ω₁, ω₂}; the states sharing a Bob-cell with ω₁ or ω₂ are {ω₁, ω₂, ω₃}; Ann's cell at ω₃ is {ω₃, ω₄, ω₅}; Bob's cells at ω₄ and ω₅ add nothing new. So the common-knowledge component of ω₁ is all of Ω, and the same holds for every other state. The finest common-knowledge partition is therefore trivial, and the only common-knowledge event is Ω. Aumann's theorem says that a common prior together with common knowledge of the two posteriors forces the posteriors to be equal; since nothing beyond Ω is common knowledge at the outset, the theorem has no purchase on the initial disagreement of part (a), and there is no contradiction with the agents sharing the uniform prior. The disagreement has gone by the end of (b) because the public announcements refine both partitions, so that by then the event "both posteriors are 1/2" (or, at ω₃, "both are 0") contains the agents' common-knowledge component — the posteriors have become common knowledge, which is precisely the situation the theorem says cannot arise with unequal posteriors.

*(d)* Write xᵢ = μ(ωᵢ). Consistency with Ann's map requires x₁/(x₁+x₂) = 3/4 and x₃/(x₃+x₄) = 3/4; consistency with Bob's requires x₁/(x₁+x₃) = 1/2 and x₂/(x₂+x₄) = 1/4. These give x₂ = x₁/3, x₄ = x₃/3, x₃ = x₁ and x₄ = 3x₂. Substituting, x₄ = x₁/3 but also x₄ = 3(x₁/3) = x₁, whence x₁ = x₁/3 and so x₁ = 0, which is impossible for a full-support prior. So there is no common prior with full support.

This is, however, an artefact of the positivity requirement rather than a real violation of the assumption. If μ is allowed to put zero probability on some states the system becomes solvable: the obstruction is the state ω₄, which both belief maps make least likely, and once μ(ω₄) = 0 the equations x₄ = x₃/3 and x₄ = 3x₂ no longer have to hold simultaneously, so we can take μ proportional to (3, 1, 1, 0) and recover both stipulated belief maps by conditioning. So a common prior does exist once positivity is dropped, and the correct verdict is that Scenario 2 does not really violate the common prior assumption — only its full-support version.

*(e1)* The common prior assumption is not the assumption that the two agents currently hold the same beliefs; it is the assumption that their current beliefs can both be obtained from one and the same ex ante distribution by conditioning on their respective private information. Disagreement at the interim stage is entirely compatible with a common prior — what a common prior excludes is disagreement that is common knowledge. As for no-trade, that is only the common prior assumption plus the observation that trade is voluntary: if the two agents share a prior, then any contract one of them expects to gain from the other must expect to lose from, so no mutually acceptable trade is possible, which is why the common prior assumption is often described as a "no-trade" condition.

*(e2)* No: in the enlarged space no non-trivial event is ever common knowledge. From any state, each agent must count as possible the history in which the messages addressed to her were dropped, and also the history in which her own messages were dropped; chaining these possibilities together takes you from any state to a state in which nothing at all was heard, and from there — moving through the sensor readings, as in part (c) — to a state with any other value of the underlying parameter. So the common-knowledge component of every state is the whole enlarged space, Aumann's theorem never gets a grip, and the two agents can disagree indefinitely while still sharing a prior. This is the same moral as in part (c): it is not disagreement as such that a common prior forbids, but disagreement that is common knowledge.
