So about the gender-equality paradox — richer, more gender-egalitarian countries have *more* gender-skewed career choices, not fewer. I keep going back and forth on how much credit each story deserves, so: rate the explanations below 0–100 for how much of the real cross-country pattern you'd guess each one accounts for, and give a sentence per item on what would move your number most.

1. Necessity push — where engineering is a reliable route out of poverty everyone crowds into it; affluence removes the push and people sort by taste.
2. Complimentary institutions — generous leave, part-time-friendly hours and care infrastructure in welfare states make teaching, nursing and HR the better *package* for whoever does the caring, so the sorting is a rational answer to policy rather than an expression of preference.
3. Framing bandwidth — in rich countries a career is an identity statement and gender is a loud identity, so gender leaks into the choice; where a career is judged by "does it feed a family", gender mostly gets screened out.
4. Release — egalitarian reform lifts external constraints, so whatever average differences in interests exist (people vs. things) just show up more clearly.
5. Index artifact — the equality indices track women's outcomes (schooling, health, seats in parliament), not how gender-neutral childhood socialisation is, so the ranking partly proxies for wealth and individualism.
6. Backlash — formal barriers come down and a quiet essentialist counter-reaction is freer to act.

Then two things: (a) if the same ordering showed up in surveys of what people say they *want* rather than in what they actually enrol in, which item takes the biggest fall and which one gains? (b) name the one item where you suspect your number is most likely wrong.
