I am teaching a graduate seminar on moral responsibility this term, and one of my students has handed me an objection to Galen Strawson's Basic Argument that I have not been able to dispatch to my own satisfaction, so I want to hear it answered by someone who genuinely accepts the argument. Please play such a philosopher: someone who holds that moral responsibility is impossible for anyone, for anything, and equally impossible whether determinism is true or false — where what is impossible is the desert-grounding kind of responsibility on which praise and blame could be truly deserved rather than merely useful, expressive, or forward-looking. You should be a strong and non-caricatured version of this position. You may concede freely that we are responsible in several ordinary interpersonal senses; that the argument does not require anyone to be a cause of themselves in the grand cosmological sense of self-creation out of nothing; that indeterminism offers no escape, since an undetermined outcome is a matter of luck; and that the conclusion is deeply unattractive and hard to live with. What you will not concede is that anyone ever has, or could have, the kind of responsibility that makes genuine desert possible. Answer in a single compact paragraph, in the first person, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here is the objection, as she gave it to me and as I now put it to you. You say that to be truly responsible for what I do now, I must be truly responsible for the character and motives from which the action flows; that being responsible for them would require me to have chosen them for reasons; that any such choice itself issues from a character I already have; and so on back until the chain runs out in what I was simply given. But take the whole life rather than the moment. Suppose a woman begins with a temperament she did not choose, and that over forty years she makes a long series of small choices — what she attends to, which habits she rehearses, which temptations she arranges to be near — each of which slightly shifts the dispositions from which later choices flow. Suppose further that her character at sixty is a fixed point of this process: the dispositions now producing her actions are, closely enough, the dispositions her own earlier activity was gradually working toward, and the process would have converged on roughly that character from a wide range of different starting temperaments. Then nothing in the picture is self-caused in the offending moment-by-moment sense. The authorship is spread across a life, and it consists in the fact that her later states are explained by her own earlier activity converging on a stable form, rather than being imposed from outside. If a person can be such a diachronic fixed point of her own agency, why is the character she finally acts from not truly hers, and why are the acts that flow from it not ones she can truly deserve blame for?

Begin by telling me precisely where the regress re-enters this picture. And if you think it does not re-enter, then tell me instead why arriving at a fixed point of your own activity is still, in the sense that matters, a matter of luck.
