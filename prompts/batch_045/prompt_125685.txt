My little brother started at a big state university this year and joined ROTC, and we got into an argument at Thanksgiving that I can't get straight. His campus sends out emails every semester reminding everyone that hazing is a crime and that a club can lose its charter over it — and meanwhile his unit has something like a "warrior weekend" where the first-years get maybe four hours of sleep across three days, have to memorize a pile of completely arbitrary trivia and recite it on demand, and get screamed at when they screw up. The sports teams do preseason doubles in August heat. Nobody counts any of that as hazing. But if a fraternity made its pledges memorize trivia and go without sleep, it would be a scandal and probably a felony. I get that there are surface differences the university would point to — consent, who designs it, whether there's a real skill at the end, whether the institution can be held accountable for it — but I'm not convinced those explain the *strength* of the reaction, and I also notice that when you crack down on one ritual the group seems to invent a new one (matching tattoos, secret handshakes, a pointless fourteen-hour road trip to somewhere boring) that has none of the skill content at all.

What I'd like is for you to reconstruct what that whole pattern of institutional practice presupposes about what ordeals *do*. I've read a bit about costly signaling and Zahavi's handicap principle — peacock tails, the idea that a signal is only honest if its cost is differentially lower for the individuals who actually have the quality being advertised, so the waste is the point rather than a defect — and that framing makes the puzzle sharper for me rather than dissolving it. If the bonding or commitment-revealing work of an ordeal comes precisely from its being *useless*, then the sanctioned versions that claim to build skill are, on that story, either secretly relying on the waste or not doing the work everyone credits them with. But there are rival stories I can half-see and can't evaluate: maybe shared suffering doesn't signal anything to anyone and instead *manufactures* the commitment in the person who endured it, in which case whether it's informative to observers is beside the point; maybe the ritual is a way of screening for a trait nobody would name out loud; maybe the crackdown is better explained by liability and PR than by any view about ordeals at all. So: what does the practice strictly require you to believe, what does it merely sit comfortably with, and where is it genuinely underdetermined so that several quite different theories would license the same rules? And be concrete about what would have to change — if a university really accepted the honest-signal story, would it want to design safe, bounded, openly pointless rituals, or would it try to break the signaling equilibrium some other way? I'd also like you to say what the failure mode looks like where an ordeal's cost isn't differential in the right way, so that the people most willing to pay it are the ones with the least attachment to the group and the ritual ends up selecting for the wrong people. Please don't bother with citations or a reading list — I just want the argument.
