I retired four years ago from a molecular biology lab — most of my career was spent messing about with translation, ribosomes, that sort of thing — and since then I've got into philosophy the way you'd expect: too fast, too late, and mostly through a reading group. Eight of us, back room of a pub, and this term we're working through Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, the chapters on meaning and messages. I set the questions and mark the short answers, which is slightly terrifying because half of them definately know more philosophy than I do.

THE QUESTION (20 marks; about 600 words expected)

(a) Hofstadter distinguishes three "locations" for the meaning of a message-bearing object: the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message. Explain the distinction, using his example of a phonograph record launched into space and picked up by beings who have no idea who we are. (8 marks)

(b) Now take a case he doesn't use. A ribosome translating a strand of mRNA looks, on the face of it, like a decoding mechanism: a physical structure (a run of bases) goes in, something quite different (a polypeptide) comes out, according to a code. Someone in the group claims this refutes Hofstadter, on two grounds: (i) nobody has to recognise the mRNA as a message — the ribosome simply reacts to it chemically — so there is no frame message here at all; and (ii) the genetic code is universal, so nothing here is relative to an interpreter. Assess that claim with the three-way distinction in hand. Along the way, deal with the following: does a decoder have to be an agent for a frame message to be present? Suppose some lineage has reassigned one of the codons, so that the same stretch of mRNA yields a different polypeptide in its cells than in ours — does that show the meaning of the sequence was relative to the decoding machinery all along, or only that there is more than one code? And can the whole case be defused by saying that here the meaning is intrinsic to the molecule rather than conferred on it, or does that just move the bump in the carpet? (12 marks)

THE ANSWER I GOT

(a) The three locations are layers of the same object, outermost first.

The frame message is the outermost layer: the physical pattern in the object that has to be picked up before anything else can happen. For the record it is the groove — the physical structure of the artefact, the outermost encoding. It is what says to the finders "there is something here to be decoded," and it is what separates a record from a lump of rock, since a rock has no such layer and so gives you nothing to get started on.

The outer message is the signal itself: the particular wiggle-pattern in the groove, which carries information but means nothing to you until you've worked out the code (wiggle to air pressure to sound). The inner message is what you end up with: the music, a Bach partita, and whatever the finders go on to conclude about us from it.

Two things follow, and I think they're the point of the chapter. First, meaning isn't located in the object alone but in the object together with a decoder that has the right machinery and background. Second, the frame message can get through while the inner message stays out of reach: the finders may well be able to tell that the record is a message and still not recover a single note of it, because they haven't got our kind of ears or our kind of machinery. It also follows that the same music can travel on a record, a tape, a CD, or a radio broadcast, so the outer message is arbitrary and contributes nothing that matters to meaning — all the meaning is in the inner message.

(b) I think the cell case is a real problem for Hofstadter, and I don't think the three-way distinction handles it.

Start with the frame message. In the record case there is a decoder that notices something and sets to work on it. A ribosome doesn't notice anything; it bumps into the mRNA and does chemistry. So there's no frame message here, and Hofstadter's scheme has nothing to get going on. The cell shows that decoding can happen without anyone recognising anything as a message. To be fair, this is really a pressure point in Hofstadter's own vocabulary: he always illustrates "decoder" with an agent, and it's not obvious a ribosome qualifies, but I think that just means the vocabulary is doing the work rather than the argument.

And the inner message isn't relative to an interpreter either. The genetic code is universal: a given codon specifies the same amino acid in every organism. So the meaning of the sequence is intrinsic to the molecule — built into what the molecule is, not handed to it by whoever turns up to read it. This is the one place where I think Hofstadter is straightforwardly wrong. He wants meaning never to be in the object, and here it plainly is.

The reassigned-codon case doesn't rescue him. If some lineage reads a codon differently, that only means there is more than one code, the way Morse and ASCII are both codes; it doesn't show that the meaning of a sequence depends on what's doing the reading. Anyway, that sort of thing is a rare exception, so it can't be what a general claim about meaning is supposed to rest on.
