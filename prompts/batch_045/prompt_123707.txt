I am a professor of philosophy — my own research is in decision theory, so the formal material is not new to me, but I have never before had to make any of it usable by anyone. For the past two years I have sat as one of five voting members of the Resilience and Capital Prioritisation Panel of a regional drainage and flood authority. We meet six times a year for ninety minutes, we rank between twelve and twenty capital proposals per cycle, our rankings go to the authority's board and then into a statutory funding bid that is in principle open to judicial review, and we have two days of one analyst's time per cycle. Two of the five members are elected councillors who will not read an equation; one is the finance officer, who currently ranks everything by benefit–cost ratio at a 3.5% discount rate with an optimism-bias uplift; one is the hydrologist, whose team will only give us probabilities as ranges (the central estimate is 3%, and their tenth-to-ninetieth percentile interval is roughly half to double it), and who has told me privately that his largest damage figures are conditional means over a skewed severity distribution, with perhaps half a per cent of the mass sitting above £500m. I was appointed for ethical input, which in practice means I am the one the chair asks to write down what we are doing, and I have now been asked formally to produce a written procedure that this panel can run in the room and that a future panel could run without me. So: no citations, no literature survey, and nothing that depends on looking something up — I have no library access from the committee room, I need something I can read aloud to a councillor, and every step needs a written artefact because the board's audit committee must be able to reconstruct a decision afterwards.

The problem I want the procedure to solve is that our current ranking minimises expected total social cost, and it keeps producing orders the panel will not vote for: schemes whose entire value is the removal of a small probability of a very large loss lose to schemes that generate a near-certain small saving, and the only lever the finance officer has is to steepen the discount rate or the utility on money, which the hydrologist quite rightly points out also penalises cheap schemes that reliably protect the same households. I want to try the opposite separation. Hold the outcome scale fixed and measurable — linear in millions of pounds of total social cost over a fifty-year horizon (the finance officer has objected that a pound matters more to a flooded household than to our balance sheet; we have noted the objection and parked it for a separate distributional stage) — and put the whole of our caution about tails into a single increasing map r from [0,1] onto [0,1], with r(0)=0 and r(1)=1, applied not to individual probabilities but to the cumulative distribution: rank a proposal's outcomes from least cost to greatest cost, assign the i-th outcome the weight r(P(cost at least c_i)) minus r(P(cost strictly greater than c_i)), and score the proposal as the sum of cost times that weight, lower being better; plain expected value is then the special case r(p)=p. Three things the translation has to be honest about. First, no member of this panel will specify a function; what they can answer is forced-choice questions, and what I think I can get away with is fitting a one-parameter family r(p) = p^k, where k<1 means we weight the bad tail more heavily than its probability warrants and k>1 less. Second, I have noticed that the score depends on how finely the outcome space is resolved: a single flood event carrying its mean damage is not the same number as that event resolved into a severity distribution, and I want a rule about how far down the hydrologists must resolve before we may compute at all, plus a statement of what happens to a ranking as they resolve further. Third, I want r fitted before the cycle's numbers are opened, because I can already see a member reverse-engineering a k that justifies the scheme in her own ward.

Three further constraints, then the cases. (a) Because the weighting applies to the distribution of whatever we finally rank and not to each proposal separately, I assume I cannot score proposals one at a time and add them up; tell me what the procedure does instead, given two days of analyst time — a heuristic ordering, an explicit assumption under which one-at-a-time scoring is safe, or a rule that says we must rank baskets, with a cheap approximation. (b) The probabilities are intervals, and I do not want the panel to come away thinking that re-weighting a midpoint probability is the same thing as being cautious about not knowing the probability; give me a propagation rule and a dominance test for when the intervals overlap. (c) Some questions the procedure must refuse to settle and must refer back to us as value questions, and I want them marked as such in the flow rather than buried: our statutory duty to have regard to the most deprived households (an ex ante weighting is indifferent to who bears the loss), irreversibility and option value, and intergenerational weighting. For the worked applications, assume the elicitation comes out at k = 0.5 and that the panel has signed off on the linear cost scale; the four proposals below, with costs in £m of present-value social cost over fifty years (capital cost included, so the cheapest branch of each scheme is roughly its capital cost plus routine damage), are the whole of the current cycle.

Baseline (statutory maintenance only): 8 with probability 0.55; 30 with 0.32; 110 with 0.10; 300 with 0.03.
C — surface-water separation and urban drainage in three market towns, £11m capital: 19 with 0.80; 26 with 0.12; 112 with 0.05; 305 with 0.03.
D — tidal barrier refurbishment and an offline storage reservoir, £26m capital: 34 with 0.70; 44 with 0.20; 105 with 0.08; 150 with 0.02.
E — managed realignment at Saltmarsh Point, £7m capital: 9 with 0.66; 40 with 0.24; 120 with 0.08; 260 with 0.02. (E is cheap and will score well, and it is the one I expect to cause trouble: it protects about nine hundred homes but takes defences away from a residential care home and an authorised traveller site in the two most deprived wards in the region.)

I also have two standing objections to answer, briefly and in the same format: an economist on the audit committee says the functional form is just rank-dependent or anticipated utility from his own field — something by Quiggin in the early 1980s — and asks whether we may therefore simply use his discipline's calibration results; and my own doctoral student says we should rank by conditional expected loss above the ninety-fifth percentile and be done with it.
