Two weeks from now I am presenting, in person, to the clinical ethics consultation committee at the hospital where I expect eventually to be treated. I am 41 and was diagnosed six months ago with a hereditary mutation that will produce a slowly progressive dementing illness; on the estimate I have been given, I have something like six to eight years of the kind of cognition I would want to keep. I have written an advance directive that refuses antibiotic treatment for intercurrent infections and declines assisted oral feeding once I have reached the stage I currently describe as no longer recognizing my family, and I am asking the committee to record it as binding rather than as a statement of wishes to be weighed later, at the time, alongside the wishes of the person I will then be.

The committee is five people: a geriatric psychiatrist, a palliative-care physician who has sat through several hundred family meetings, a lawyer from risk management, a nurse who has watched families fight one another over feeding tubes, and a bioethicist who holds that precedent autonomy is a legal convenience rather than a moral power anyone actually has.

The argument I intend to give them, compressed: the directive's authority does not derive from its being my considered judgment in the way my present judgments are. Much of its language came from a template, and its stated rationale was never, at any moment, a reason that anyone occurrently had. Its authority derives instead from an asymmetry. The transformation ahead of me is one I can now anticipate and will not then be able to recognize as a transformation; a self located inside it will predictably report contentment and predictably decline to ratify any constraint on itself; and therefore a policy of deferring to whichever self is in the room will resolve a case like mine in one direction only, no matter what anyone writes down in advance.

Prepare me for the hour. Give me the objections in the order they will actually arrive, each phrased the way that particular committee member would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would, with what I should concede up front, what I must hold if the request is to survive, and where each of my two-minute replies is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. My own guess is that it is the request to write down now, exhaustively and in advance, which future states of mind count as no longer recognizing my family, and whether I would sign the same document if I had to; tell me if you think the genuinely dangerous question is a different one.
