so i've been going round on buridan's ass with a friend and i've realised we keep sliding between like four different questions without noticing. before i go read a pile of papers about it, i want the spec: what does a theory of rational choice actually have to deliver about cases where nothing is better than anything else? not "what should the ass do" — i want the requirements list, with the case behind each one.

cases i want it to handle, and distinctions i don't want it to collapse: (a) the classic one-shot — two bales, identical in every respect the ass cares about, it starves. (b) the corridor version — a thousand identical bales, one every mile, the ass only needs one. something changes when the tie is one of a long series rather than a single event, and i can't say what: is rationality supposed to attach to individual choices or to policies, and if both, what's the relation between the two verdicts? (c) the decoy — with {A, B} the ass can't move; add C, which is just A but slightly mouldy, and suddenly A looks pickable. is that a reason appearing out of nowhere, or is the theory just licensing arbitrariness on a technicality? (d) the coin — the ass flips a coin and then refuses to be bound by the result. what does a theory owe us about the step from "the reasons have run out" to "i will now do whatever this device says"? (e) the hard-choice version — two options equally good on every standard the ass antecedently has, but picking one would make it a certain kind of donkey. is that the same shape of case as (a), or a different one? i suspect most theories cheat by treating them as one. bonus: is there room for a fourth relation besides better, worse and equally good — comparable but none of those — and does a theory need it, or is it a prejudice of one camp?

things i'm already suspicious of, please sort them into requirements and non-requirements: whether the theory's output is a set of permissions or a procedure for actually picking one, and whether it's allowed to be the first without the second; whether arbitrariness is a cost or just what rationality looks like once the reasons run out; whether the right answer is allowed to change with the stakes (starvation vs. picking a flavour of icecream) or with how much deliberation costs; whether "the ass can't move" is a normative gap at all or merely a fact about its executive machinery that a theory of rationality can shrug off; and whether a theory of individual choice is even supposed to cover the case where two asses have to end up at different bales.

for each requirement give the motivating case, mark which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste, flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part i most want to see — and say which single requirement rules out the most of the obvious answers (always-left, randomise, permissivism, "you can give yourself a reason"). keep the weight on the criteria; a short scorecard at the end is fine.
