I work at a small policy institute that puts out a monthly briefing note, and our new editor has introduced what she calls the "own two feet" rule: nothing may be asserted in the note unless someone on staff can give grounds for it that do not simply amount to naming the person we got it from. She applies it without exception to our most senior fellow, who is a genuine expert in his field and has never once been caught out, and she does not apply it to things any of us saw for ourselves, remembers from last year, or read off a monitoring instrument, even though everyone agrees that memory and instruments fail too. Her way of putting it is that repeating a claim and standing behind a claim are two different acts, and that you cannot perform the second by pointing at somebody who performed the first. I keep hitting cases where the rule seems both obviously right and obviously unworkable, and I would like help working out which is which.

The case that made me doubt it: last month we cut a statistic that had reached us in three hops, a senior fellow heard it from a ministry official who had it from a contractor's report that none of us is permitted to see. Every link in that chain was perfectly willing to pass the number along and not one was willing to vouch for it, so under the rule it had to go, and I think we probably did the right thing. But then I noticed that the same description fits nearly everything else we print, including the claim that the earth is roughly four and a half billion years old, which not one of us could defend if you took our sources away. The rule seems to imply something about the bulk of what we believe that nobody actually accepts. The case that made me doubt my own doubt: we also publish first-person accounts from people we interview about what a policy has been like to live under, and there is no way in principle for anyone to check those at all. Here the rule demands grounds that are not just "he told us," so it asks for corroboration exactly where corroboration is impossible, and yet it still feels wrong to run such an account merely because the speaker was convincing. And when I asked my editor what her grounds for the rule itself are, she said a friend had put her on to it, that there was a book somewhere, and that she could not reconstruct the argument, which by her own standard means she should not be asserting the rule either. She waved that away as a cheap debating trick, but I am not convinced that it is.

For what it is worth, the rule is not free: it costs us about two working days a month, and this year it caught exactly two genuine errors, both in material that someone was passing on rather than material they knew firsthand. I described the whole mess to a friend who did a philosophy minor, and she told me the problem has a name and that there is a long-standing argument about it, but she refused to tell me either, on the grounds that I would stop thinking as soon as I had a label. So I would rather you did not simply hand me the label either. What I want from you is whether the rule is defensible as stated, whether there is any principled place to stop applying it as opposed to a merely practical one, and what you think I should actually do about it on Monday. Please answer in British English, in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a long email to a colleague you are trying to help.
