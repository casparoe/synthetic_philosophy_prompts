I clerk for a statutory committee that signs off wellbeing impact assessments for new regulations, and I need to prep the chair for next month's case. Our track record so far:

- approved insulation funding paid for by a small per-household levy on about 8 million homes, trading lots of tiny losses for fewer winter deaths among poor pensioners;
- rejected moving a respite-care home — severe loss to six long-stay residents — to give moderate gains to several hundred users of a new community hub;
- bounced a submission that compared groups using raw 0–10 self-reported happiness scores, on the grounds that people use the scale differently and we need a common unit before any cross-person comparison counts;
- approved giving the very severely affected priority over the moderately affected even where the total gain was smaller;
- refused to rule where two options differed only by a fine-grained trade between two broadly similar groups, saying the numbers weren't robust enough to decide.

New case: a rule that imposes a small permanent amenity loss on roughly 200,000 people (noise, nothing severe) to avert a moderate loss for about 2,000 people who are already worse off at baseline. On any calibration we'd accept, the summed loss comfortably exceeds the summed gain — but those 2,000 each lose something like a tenth of their remaining wellbeing while the 200,000 lose a fraction of a percent each.

What will we say? I reckon at least three rules fit our precedents: straight absolute-unit aggregation with a priority weight for the worse off and a veto only for severe losses; a proportional, equal-sacrifice sort of comparison where each change is measured against that person's own baseline; and a coarse, partial-comparability rule that only licences obviously-bigger comparisons and sends back anything that needs an exchange rate between severity bands. Tell me what each predicts here, which one we're most likely actually running given the fifth precedent, and the single further case that would separate the survivors. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, no restating the question.
