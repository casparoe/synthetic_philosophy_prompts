I work on a small analytics team at a state agency that is about to start using a vendor's recidivism-risk instrument, and I've been asked to explain to our review board — mostly lawyers and one retired judge, nobody technical — what the much-discussed "fairness impossibility" results from around 2016 do and don't show. A few months ago I read a magazine feature on the subject and I've been leaning on it heavily, but a colleague who works on causal inference read my paraphrase of it and said parts of it "do violence to the formalism" without telling me which parts. Before I repeat any of it out loud in a public meeting, I'd like an accuracy audit. The piece's rendering of the result, as best I can reconstruct it from memory, went roughly like this: "Fairness, it turns out, is not an engineering problem but a theorem. Around 2016 researchers proved that no risk score — for recidivism, for loan default, for anything — can be fair to two groups at once, and the argument is almost embarrassingly simple. A score is fair, intuitively, in three ways. It should be calibrated: among everyone the model gives a score of, say, 7 out of 10, the same fraction should actually go on to reoffend no matter which group they belong to. It should have equal false positive rates across groups: people who will not reoffend should be flagged at the same rate regardless of race. And it should have equal false negative rates: people who will reoffend should be missed at the same rate. A bit of algebra shows that all three hold together only if the groups reoffend at exactly the same rate, or if the score predicts the future perfectly. Since neither is true in reality, any score you deploy is unfair to somebody. This is also why both sides in the famous fight over the COMPAS instrument were right: it really did flag Black defendants at higher rates, and it really was accurate — a score can be perfectly predictive and racially disparate at the same time, and those facts are not in conflict. The moral is that accuracy and fairness trade off against each other, so the harder you push on accuracy the more unfair you buy, and no amount of cleverness, better data, or human review gets around it, since the arithmetic applies to any decision-maker short of a perfect one."

My own suspicion is that the accuracy-versus-fairness moral is the shakiest part: I've been told elsewhere that the tension is between two rival families of fairness criterion rather than between fairness and predictive performance, and that a genuinely perfect predictor would satisfy everything at once — which makes the article's claim that disparity survives perfect prediction sound backwards to me. Separately, my colleague floated what he called a loophole. Stop reporting a raw risk number, he says; report to each person only their percentile within their own group's risk distribution, and then choose a different cutoff for each group so that the false positive and false negative rates come out equal. Calibration is then automatic by construction, since the same percentile means the same thing within each group, and error-rate parity is guaranteed by the cutoffs — so all three hold at once. I suspect this only relocates the problem (and it would mean using group membership explicitly at scoring time, which I assume matters legally even if it doesn't matter mathematically), but I can't say precisely where it fails, or whether it fails at all. What I'd like from you: go through the passage claim by claim and sort it into what a researcher in this area would accept as stated, what is a defensible simplification for a popular audience, and what is wrong or seriously misleading — including anywhere the piece slides between two different results without saying so, for instance between criteria stated for a graded risk score and criteria stated for a yes/no decision. Then give me the correct statement, with a gut-level intuition for why the incompatibility arises that I could reconstruct with a little algebra and no jargon, since I have to explain it to nontechnical people. And take a view on the percentile proposal: does it satisfy the criteria, does it quietly abandon one family of criteria while satisfying the other, and is there something in it that the formalism can't see but that the ethical and legal debate very much can?

One last thing, because I'll be the one standing in front of the board: be explicit about which of your verdicts are safe — following from definitions and algebra that everyone in the field accepts — and which depend on a contestable reading of "fairness," on how calibration gets defined for a continuous score as opposed to a binary decision, or on assumptions about how far apart the groups' base rates actually are. For each verdict that isn't safe, tell me what would change your mind, and flag where your own reasoning is most likely to break down.
