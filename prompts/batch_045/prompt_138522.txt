hey -- Im on the seven person working group rewriting our university's Algorithmic Accountability Charter before the senate votes on it in three weeks, and we're completely stuck on Principle 1. I need something to circulate before Thursday.

Background, briefly. We're two years into a contract with a vendor (internally we call it Sage) that scores students both for academic-integrity risk and for retention outreach. Half the logging is broken, the vendor leans on a trade-secret exemption constantly, and our own Section 4 obliges them to maintain a public "irretrievable information register" -- facts about past decisions that are known to be lost for good. It currently has ninteen entries.

Principle 1 as drafted says: for every true proposition about how a decision concerning a member of the University was reached, it is possible, in principle, for that proposition to be known. We meant it as an anti-secrecy clause: no permanent, in-principle black boxes, you can't hide behind it's proprietary forever.

Our grad-student rep (Meera) circulated a one page note saying Principle 1 is self-defeating as worded. Her argument as I understood it: take any true proposition p about a decision that in fact nobody knows -- the register is full of them. Then "p and nobody knows p" is also true. Principle 1 says that conjunction is knowable in principle. But nobody can know a conjunction of that form: knowing it requires knowing p, and also knowing that nobody knows p, which your knowing p would falsify. So the conjunction is unknowable, so by Principle 1 it isn't true, so there are no unknown truths about decisions at all -- every one is already known by somebody. Which is absurd, and flatly inconsistent with a register we require to be non-empty. She says it's a known result in epistemic logic, Fitch's paradox I think, and that we should stop trying to say everything is knowable.

Three ways forward are on the table and I genuinely can't tell which is right. (1) Leave the wording and add a footnote saying it's aspirational -- our chair Doug likes this, he thinks the clause is pure rhetoric and the real teeth are all in Section 4. (2) Restrict Principle 1 to first-order facts about the procedure -- features, weights, thresholds, data provenance -- and say nothing about facts concerning anyone's knowledge, including ours. (3) Time-stamp it: for every such truth and every time t, it is possible that at some later time someone comes to know it.

My own guess is that (3) dissolves the argument, since you can perfectly well know in June that nobody knew in March, but (3) also seems to let us off the hook on timing -- a right to eventually find out is worthless before an appeals deadline. And (2) is the one nobody likes: the facts that matter in a grievance are almost always the you knew and didn't tell anyone ones, and I don't want to hand the vendor a charter that is silent about exactly those. Doug also floated naming the knower (knowable by the person affected) and I have no idea whether that helps or makes it worse, but he'll ask.

Can you draft the memo? Two pages max, in a form I can actually send: a clear recomendation between (1), (2), (3) or some combination; concrete replacement wording for Principle 1 that a vendor's lawyer can't drive a truck through and a first-year student can read; and a real paragraph on the you knew and didn't tell anyone objection rather than a footnote. Also tell us honestly whether Principle 1 is doing any work at all or whether the protection should just live in Section 4's duties. Please don't spend the memo explaining the paradox -- we've all read Meera's note.
