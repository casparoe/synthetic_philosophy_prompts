I chair the clinical ethics committee at a large teaching hospital trust. We meet fortnightly, consider three or four referrals a month — mostly from intensive care, paediatrics and oncology — and produce a one-page opinion that goes into the patient's record and is increasingly read by people who were not in the room. Almost every referral reaches us already labelled "an ethical dilemma", and the word has become useless: it covers everything from two clinicians disagreeing about a prognosis to cases where, as far as anyone can tell, someone is wronged whichever option is taken. I want a procedure, not an essay.

What I am asking for is a decision procedure my committee can actually run: the questions, in a fixed order; what should count as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops and returns a verdict of "not a dilemma"; where it escalates because the remaining question is one of values rather than of analysis. It has to be runnable in about an hour by people with no philosophical training, myself included, and written so that the output is defensible in a record.

The distinctions I most need it to draw are these: an epistemic or informational difficulty masquerading as a conflict of values; a hard weighing of considerations that nonetheless has a correct answer; a clash between what someone ought to do and something merely admirable or self-sacrificing; a conflict manufactured by the institution's own rules, rotas or resource limits, which I would rather have routed back to management than resolved as though it were ethical; and, if anything is left over, a genuine dilemma in the strict sense — incompatible requirements, such that moral residue attaches to the agent whatever is done. Please include a step that re-describes the conflict at the level of the reasons rather than of the options, to check whether the opposition survives the re-description, and a step that asks whether an ideally informed and impartial deliberator, re-running the reasoning, would converge on one option. Someone suggested to me that the second of these is doing more philosophical work than it appears to; if so, say so plainly, and say what it presupposes. Mark clearly the points at which the procedure must hand the decision to a particular person — the chair, the treating consultant, the patient or family — because no further analysis settles it.

Then run it on three referrals: one remaining intensive care bed and two patients with comparable claims on it; a sixteen-year-old refusing a blood transfusion on religious grounds with her parents and the clinical team in favour of treating and a live dispute about her capacity; and a family asking the team to withhold a cancer diagnosis from the patient. At least one of these should come out somewhere the committee will dislike — my colleagues treat the first as the paradigm dilemma, and I suspect the procedure will say something less comfortable than that.

Two further constraints. Please ground the steps in what is actually argued about these cases rather than in common sense, but do not give me citations, a bibliography or a reading list: nothing I can follow up, and I would have no way of checking it. And separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — I have the impression that whether "ought" implies "can" settles the matter against genuine dilemmas, and whether moral residue is evidence of real wrongdoing rather than of difficulty, are both live disputes, and I do not want a contested view presented to me as the consensus. Where the procedure is likely to misfire, say where and why. I would be grateful for British English throughout.
