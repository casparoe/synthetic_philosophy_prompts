Writing next week's handout for my third-year rational choice seminar, and I want to open the session on risk-weighted expected utility with a scripted dialogue instead of my usual wall of bullet points. (For anyone reading this cold: the theory I mean is the one where the agent's attitude to risk is carried by a separate risk function r applied to decumulative probabilities, rather than by the curvature of the utility function, so that EU is the special case r(p) = p.) Two characters, both of them good: Rowan, who thinks risk-weighting is a rationally permissible stance that expected utility theory illegitimately rules out, and Iris, who thinks expected utility is in good shape and that everything risk-weighting is advertised as doing is either doable inside EU or is a mistake. They've known each other for years, they've both taught the standard Allais-style cases, and neither of them needs those cases explained. Aim for 1,000–1,300 words, dialogue format, no footnotes, no citations, no name-dropping of papers — I want the argument itself to carry the page.

Here is the specific thing I want them fighting over, and it's a worry I worked out at a whiteboard last week that I genuinely can't tell is devastating or a nothingburger. Set utility equal to dollars, so that all of the risk attitude lives in r. Order a gamble X's outcomes x_1 < ... < x_n with probabilities p_i, write S_i for the probability of getting at least x_i, and define REU(X) = x_1 + the sum from i = 2 to n of r(S_i)(x_i − x_{i-1}), with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. Now mirror the gamble and look at −X. If you grind through the algebra you get REU(X) + REU(−X) = the sum over i of [r(S_i) + r(1 − S_i) − 1](x_i − x_{i-1}); and if r(p) < p for every p strictly between 0 and 1, the risk-averse shape, then every term in that sum is negative, so the total is negative for any gamble whatsoever. Concretely, with r(1/2) = 0.4: an agent who prefers a sure $50 to a 50/50 between $0 and $100 must also prefer a sure loss of $50 to a 50/50 between losing $100 and losing nothing. A single risk function forces risk aversion in the domain of gains and risk aversion in the domain of losses together; you cannot get the reflection effect — risk-averse about gains, risk-seeking about losses — which is the one regularity about risk behaviour I'd have thought any theory of risk ought to be able to swallow. If my algebra is off, have Rowan catch it and show where; better to find out from a handout than in front of thirty undergrads.

My own proposed fix, which I want Rowan to try out and Iris to go after: two risk functions, r-plus for everything above a reference point (take it to be the status quo) and r-minus for everything below, so that the weighting of the upper tail can be tuned independently in the two domains. Then hand them the headache I got stuck on: what happens to a gamble that straddles the reference point? Take a 25% chance of −60, a 25% chance of −10, and a 50% chance of +40, so the increment from −10 up to +40 crosses zero and no outcome sits exactly at zero. Do you weight that whole increment with one of the two curves, or do you split it at zero and weight the lower sliver with r-minus and the upper sliver with r-plus? Under the original single-r theory I convinced myself the two procedures provably agree, so the question never arises; once there are two curves they come apart, and the natural-seeming convention of always splitting at the reference point looks like it delivers something very close to cumulative prospect theory with a linear value function and seperate weighting functions for gains and losses. Which is a gift of a line for Iris: wasn't r supposed to be a normative parameter that rational agents may permissibly differ on, and haven't you now rebuilt a descriptive model of what people actually do, reference point and all? And a decent reply for Rowan: nothing in the permissibility argument ever required a single r; the mirroring result just shows that r's real content is something like concern with the upper tail rather than risk aversion as ordinarily understood; and even in the pure-gains domain r-plus does something no amount of curvature in u can do, since Allais-style preferences violate independence for every utility function there is.

Ground rules for the script: both of them land at least one real hit and both concede at least one real thing, each responds to what the other actually said rather than to a rehearsed version of the opposing position, and neither says anything like "that's just semantics" or accuses the other of being unrigorous. Don't resolve it — I want to end with the class arguing, so leave the last exchange genuinely open, with Iris getting in something I havent thought of yet. Keep the tone like two colleagues at a whiteboard who like each other: a bit dry, occasionally funny, no lecturing, and they should work with the actual numbers rather than gesturing at some gamble or other.
