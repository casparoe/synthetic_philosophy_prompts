Say Machiavelli gets airlifted into 2025, sits through a briefing on nuclear deterrence, and is asked for a memo. The briefing covers: roughly nine nuclear-armed states, no great-power war in eighty years, and a live argument among strategists about whether that's because deterrence is a genuinely stable system or because we've just been lucky — a handful of near misses that only became public decades later, at least one duty officer who decided not to pass a warning up the chain, false alarms from malfunctioning early-warning kit. Then the twist the briefing ends on: the warning chain is being rebuilt around machine-learning classifiers that will compress the decision window at the top from tens of minutes to under three, and some people think that's fine, even good, because a machine doesn't hesitate.

Write the memo he'd actually write, in his voice, around 600 words. I want him working his own gear — fortuna against virtù, occasione, the thing in The Prince about fortune being like a flood you can build dikes and embankments against while the weather is calm but not once the water is up, his taste for the impetuous over the cautious, his suspicion of anything that lets a prince feel secure — rather than generic tough-minded-realism advice. Specifically: how does he classify the long peace, and what does he say to the person who says it hasn't gone off in eighty years so the system must work? Is compressing the decision window an exercise of virtù (no fear, no wavering) or is it handing fortuna the keys? And what would he propose doing with those three minutes, or with the arsenal, instead? Where his framework just doesn't fit this kind of problem, say so instead of papering over it. He's allowed to be rude about us.
