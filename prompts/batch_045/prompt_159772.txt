I sit on my university's academic-integrity committee, and after a messy case last term I started reading about how groups decide things. Our rule is two-part: a finding of misconduct requires both that the student submitted work that was not their own and that no valid excuse applies. We vote by show of hands, and last term we ended up with a majority for each of the two conditions separately but a majority against a finding of misconduct — and then argued for an hour about what we had actually decided. Someone sent me a chapter from a popular book on collective decision-making that seemed to describe exactly our problem, but I don't trust it enough to bring it to the committee. Before I do, I'd like to know which parts of it a specialist in this area would endorse, which are simplifications, and which are quietly wrong.

The chapter's account, as closely as I can reproduce its wording, goes like this: a group can be made up of perfectly reasonable individuals and still be unreasonable as a group. Imagine three judges deciding whether a defendant is liable, where the law makes liability depend on both a duty of care and a breach of that duty. One judge finds a duty and a breach and votes liable; one finds a duty but no breach; one finds no duty but a breach. Two of the three accept that there was a duty and two of the three accept that there was a breach, so if the court votes on the two questions separately it has to find the defendant liable — even though two of the three judges voted not liable. The court's verdict therefore depends on nothing more than which questions get put to the vote, which means it is an artifact of procedure rather than a finding. This is the discursive dilemma. The chapter then says that it is really just Condorcet's paradox of voting applied to beliefs instead of preferences — or, equivalently, Arrow's impossibility theorem restated for courts — and draws the moral that whenever a group has to settle several logically connected questions at once, no voting rule can reliably turn reasonable individuals into a reasonable group. It extends this well beyond law: any committee, board or electorate that votes on a package of related propositions risks endorsing conclusions that nobody in the room accepts, which is why putting complex questions to a popular vote is a dice roll. The only real escapes, it concludes, are to hand the decision to one person, or to break the issue down until every vote is a simple yes-or-no on one thing.

My instinct is that this is unfair in at least one way — it slides from "collective judgments can be inconsistent" to "group decision-making is unreliable generally," and I don't see why a coherence problem about the shape of a group's stated position should tell us anything about whether the group is likely to get the facts right. But I can't tell where the chapter stops being accurate and starts overstating, and I also can't tell whether its practical moral survives correction. So: please audit it claim by claim, saying what someone who works on this literature would accept outright, what they would describe as a common simplification, what they would reject, and what the strongest defender of the view would say the chapter gets wrong about their own result. Then tell me whether the conclusion I actually care about follows from the accurate parts of the account: our committee is considering a "double-majority" fix, where each condition must win a majority on its own and the composite finding must also win a majority on its own, and if the composite fails we record no violation and note only those conditions that did pass. Is that a genuine way out of the problem the chapter describes, is it secretly one of the procedures the literature already discusses under another description, what does it cost us, and does it quietly give up something that the impossibility results are usually taken to require? I'd also like to know whether we would do better simply to vote only on the two conditions and never on the composite finding at all — what we would gain and lose by doing that, in terms the rest of the committee will understand.
