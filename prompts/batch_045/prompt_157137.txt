I've been arguing about blame with friends for years, and there's one move in these arguments that I want a name for. I should be clear up front that I have no academic background — I work in freight scheduling and read this sort of thing on my own — so I'll describe it the way I've actually encountered it, and then say what would count as an answer.

The move goes like this. Someone does something bad, and a story is offered about why they had no real alternative: they were threatened; they were dependent; they were raised so that the other option never presented itself as an option at all. The reply that ends the conversation is: none of that made you do it. You weighed things up and you acted on your own reasons. Whatever would have made you act differently never actually came into play — and if it did come into play, you could have ignored it, since people do. So the "I had no choice" story doesn't do the excusing work it's supposed to do.

What interests me is that this reply seems decisive in some cases, partly right in others, and hollow in still others, and I can't find the variable. Four cases:

**A.** A warehouse supervisor means to steal some stock. Without his knowledge, his employer has set things up so that if he had shown the slightest sign of not going through with it, he would have been made to go through with it. He shows no such sign, and takes the stock entirely for his own reasons. We learn about the arrangement afterwards. My reaction, and everyone else's: it changes nothing about how much he's to blame — even though, given the arrangement, there was no way he was leaving that warehouse with the stock not taken.

**B.** A shopkeeper is told that if she doesn't hand over the week's takings, her family will be harmed. She hands them over. Most people in her position, I think, would refuse and go to the police. Here "you could have resisted — people do" seems like a real point, and it seems to reduce blame, though not to nothing.

**C.** The same shopkeeper, except that she took the loan deliberately, from people she knew the character of, on terms she understood, for a purpose of her own. She is now in a position she chose to be in. Here the reply gets a grip it doesn't have in A — but she still acts on her own reasons at the moment of handing over the money, and arguably still can't do otherwise at that moment.

**D.** A man grows up somewhere a certain cruelty to his younger brother is simply what one does. It never occurs to him at the time that there is another way to behave, and nobody suggests it. Twenty years on he understands that it was wrong. Here "nobody held a gun to you — you could have done otherwise" seems to me to misfire badly, and yet a residue of blame doesn't go away, which puzzles me.

I've tried several candidate variables and none of them sort the cases. Not whether the pressure was actually exercised: that splits A off from B and C but leaves those three tangled. Not whether the person knew about it: in A he didn't and the reply works, in C she did and it works too. My best guess is that it's something about whether the person's own earlier choices are part of the story, but that doesn't seem to touch A, and it doesn't explain the residue in D.

So: does this reply — one move, or several moves wearing the same clothes — have a name? I've tried looking into it a couple of times and kept landing on things that felt adjacent rather than on the move itself: discussions of whether anyone really chooses anything if our brains are doing the causing, and of whether addicts can stop. Both near, neither it.

What I want is not a label. If you reach for some well-known doctrine to the effect that blame doesn't require that a person could have done otherwise, tell me which of A–D it actually settles and which it leaves alone — my sense is that it handles A cleanly and the rest not at all, but I'd rather be corrected than agreed with. Specifically: are A–D one phenomenon or more than one; which, if any, is the one that's actually been worked on and what are the others; what the load-bearing difference is between the cases where the reply lands and where it doesn't; and if there's no established term, a coinage — name, definition, and a case at each edge, one clearly inside and one clearly outside. Please don't send me to books or papers; I can't get at them, and I'm not asking who said what first.

After the discussion, give me a single JSON object summarizing it, valid and nothing outside it, with this schema:

- "has_established_name": "yes" | "partly" | "no"
- "nearest_concepts": array of objects with fields "concept" (the name), "what_it_covers" (one sentence), "cases_covered" (array of "A"/"B"/"C"/"D"), "fit" ("exact" | "partial" | "poor"), "why_not_just_this" (one sentence)
- "case_by_case": one object per case, A through D, with fields "case", "best_label", "move_succeeds" ("yes" | "partly" | "no"), "load_bearing_feature" (the single feature that decides this case), "one_sentence_reason"
- "is_it_one_move": object with fields "answer" and "criterion_that_sorts_the_cases"
- "proposed_term": object with fields "term", "definition", "clear_case", "borderline_case", "excluded_case"

Keep every string value to about thirty words; the point is the sorting, not the prose.
