I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Sequences days, and I help run a small local effective-altruism group; a friend from that group — the one who always wants to shut up and multiply — sent me an argument last month that I have not been able to find an exit from, and it is starting to make me suspect that the cost-effectiveness style of thinking I have sunk years into rests on a mistake. His claim, in brief, is that once you take seriously the possibility that the world is immense — spatially infinite and statistically uniform, or possessed of an endless value-bearing future, or both — then for *any* nonzero credence in that possibility every finite, checkable thing we know about doing good drops out of the calculation, and all that is left is paralysis or a kind of monomaniacal long-termism whose verdicts are completely insensitive to the size of that credence. I cannot accept this, and I also cannot say which line of his argument to cross out. Here it is, numbered so we can be precise.

Call a world immense if, for each act available to me, the sum of the welfare differences that act makes — taken over everyone ever affected — is not a finite real number. His two routes to thinking we might be in one: first, that in an endless future the effects of any act with a lasting residue never stop accumulating, because in a chaotic world differences in the state of the world do not wash out but compound, so acts like funding a charity, having a child, or voting leave footprints whose totals never converge; second, that in an infinite uniform space there are infinitely many subjectively indistinguishable agents in my situation whose choices are correlated with mine. (He anticipates the causal decision theorist: even granting that correlation is not causation, the endless-future route restores the infinite footprint on its own, and imperfect correlation does not help either, since a fixed positive fraction of infinitely many agents is still infinitely many.) Given that stipulation:

(1) I ought to perform whichever available act maximizes expected total welfare difference, aggregating across everyone affected.
(2) The hypothesis that the world is immense has nonzero credence p — it does not matter how big: 0.3, 10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁴⁰ — since nothing below depends on the size.
(3) In an immense world, each available act's total welfare difference is infinite in magnitude, not merely very large.
(4) Expected value is p times the immense-branch difference plus (1−p) times the finite-branch difference; a positive multiple of an infinite magnitude exceeds every finite magnitude, so for any p > 0 the ranking between acts is settled entirely inside the immense branch, and the finite branch — the branch my evidence is almost entirely about — contributes nothing at all.
(5) But inside the immense branch the totals are divergent, so no act's total exceeds another's: comparing two of them is subtracting infinities, which is undefined. So (1) issues no verdict. Call this the paralysis horn.
(6) The obvious repair is to compare rates rather than totals: truncate the world — a ball of radius R around me, or everything up to time T — compute the finite difference between the two acts' effects inside the truncation, and rank A above B when that difference stays positive for all sufficiently large truncations; equivalently, compare limiting densities of value rather than sums.
(7) He says the repair fails twice over. There is no canonical truncation: sum the welfare differences in order of distance from me, or in order of time, or over expanding regions of one shape rather than another, and where the sums are not absolutely convergent you can get different signs for the same pair of acts, so the verdict is an artifact of an arbitrary limiting procedure. And even granted a procedure, the long-run sign is exactly what my evidence is silent about: the asymptotic difference depends on effects centuries or eons downstream that I cannot forecast and that chaos makes fluctuate, so the rate comparison hands me nothing I could act on.
(8) Therefore, for any nonzero credence in an immense world, however tiny, either practical reason is silent, or it is silent in a subtler way (the answer depends on an arbitrary limiting procedure I have no way to choose), or it is monomaniacal: the only thing that matters is the asymptotic profile of the immense branch, and everything checkable — cost per life saved, whether the intervention works at all, whether the organization is competent — is irrelevant, even at p = 10⁻⁴⁰, even when the finite branch favors one option by a factor of a billion. He thinks this shows that either longtermism is the whole of ethics or nothing is.

I want the diagnosis, not a reading list: I have read the usual blog posts and most of them just announce that infinities are counterintuitive, which I already knew. Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs, and explain why the step you reject looked innocent — where exactly does the slide happen, if there is one, between the finite and the infinite branch? If the fault is in the arithmetic of (4), what replaces it, and does the replacement let p matter in the way my evidence should make it matter? If the fault is in (6)–(7), is there a principled truncation or limiting procedure, or a reason the ordering sensitivity and the cluelessness do not actually arise for comparisons like the ones I face? If the fault is in (1), what goes in its place that still underwrites ordinary cost-effectiveness reasoning in a small, finite world? If your answer is eventual dominance, tell me what I am supposed to do when the truncated difference keeps changing sign no matter how large the truncation gets, since that is what I would expect in a chaotic world. For what it is worth, I would rather keep (3) and something like (6) than keep unrestricted expected-value maximization, and what I would most like to keep is the claim that evidence about the finite branch is not worthless — but I would rather know what I am paying than keep any of it for free.
