You are a professional philosopher of mind — someone who has actually taught the debate about the timing of conscious experience — who also writes deadpan satirical nonfiction for a literate general audience. Please think carefully before you draft: start by brainstorming, in a few lines you do not show me, four or five distinct respects in which the task facing my fictional bureau is genuinely impossible rather than merely difficult, then pick the two that will generate the best comedy and build the document around those.

I am a novelist. I need an in-universe document, roughly 800–900 words including its footnotes, that will appear nearly verbatim as an insert in a chapter of my book. I will set it in a world, recognizably like ours and only slightly further along, where an insurance consortium has created a body I am calling the Bureau of Phenomenal Timing. The Bureau adjudicates a single question: under a policy that pays out per occurrence of conscious experience, when did a given experience occur, and did the claimant really have it?

The document should be a ruling — a memorandum, an arbitration award, or a claims determination: choose one form and hold scrupulously to its conventions, including headings, numbered exhibits, defined terms, citation to a procedure manual, and a short footnote apparatus. Its voice should be that of a competent, humane, entirely sincere administrator who does not know she is being funny. Do not wink; do not let anyone in the document notice the absurdity.

Substantive requirements:

1. The exhibit under review is the color phi phenomenon: two small spots flashed in quick succession at separated locations on a screen, the first red and the second green, where subjects report a single spot travelling from the first location to the second and changing colour partway along its path — that is, before the green spot has ever been presented. Describe the case accurately and let the jokes grow out of the phenomenology rather than out of caricature.

2. The ruling must turn on the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction: either the subject genuinely underwent the experience of the moving, colour-changing spot and the internal record was revised afterwards, or the fabrication was inserted wholesale so that the subject only now seems to remember an experience that never occurred. The panel should concede, in properly argued administrative prose, that no experimental test and no first-person testimony can discriminate between these two stories — and should then be legally obliged to issue a determinate finding anyway. That collision is the engine of the piece; the comedy should come from the procedures the Bureau devises to discharge a duty that its own reasoning has shown to be empty.

3. The reductio should land without being stated. A reader who has never opened a philosophy book should finish the page with the distinctly unpleasant sense that she cannot tell, even in principle, whether she had the experience she remembers having, and that some institution could reasonably deny her claim on that basis.

4. Do not name, quote, or impersonate any real philosopher. Target the argumentative situation and the style of discourse, not a person. Invented experts with absurd titles are welcome.

Return the document itself, followed by a brief craft note of no more than a hundred words confirming how you handled requirements 2 and 3.
