I am head of interpretation at a regional museum in the UK, and I personally sign off every piece of public text we produce: exhibition panels (a hard ninety-word limit, no notes or citations, everything must be translatable into Welsh), audio-guide scripts, and school-facing timelines. That is roughly forty panels and six scripts a year, drafted mostly by freelancers with history degrees but no training in historiography, and countersigned by a community liaison where the material concerns a living community.

What I need is a written procedure one of those writers can run on a text in about two hours. The problem is not factual error. It is that our texts present interpretation as plain fact, and my writers cannot be expected to adjudicate between Ranke's ideal of showing the past as it actually was, Carr's argument that facts reach us already selected and ordered by present concerns, and White's claim that the narrative form itself — emplotment into romance, tragedy, comedy or satire — imposes meaning that no amount of source-fidelity can underwrite.

Two rough instruments I have been using, which you should keep, revise or discard as the theory requires. First, sentence-level tagging: source-anchored (holds up against the sources independently of what we make of them), frame-dependent (holds only given a framing we could have chosen otherwise), narrative-collateral (true, but drawing its force from a story we would not stand behind). Second, what I call the particle audit: words such as still, already, finally, only, began to, which give a sentence the shape of a story while asserting nothing checkable. Add whatever else the procedure needs — at minimum a reversibility test (could an equally source-compatible sentence of the opposite valence be written?), a rule for where the procedure stops with a no, and a rule for where it hands the decision to the liaison because the question is one of values rather than evidence. Say plainly which of my distinctions must survive the simplification, which you have dropped, and where your procedure simply assumes that Carr or White is wrong.

Then run it on three texts: a donor-funded panel on a Victorian manufacturer whose model village is the reason we have the collection; an audio-guide script containing the sentence the pit finally reopened after the strike; and a school timeline headed The Age of Improvement, 1780–1840. I expect the first to be the painful one, but do not shape the procedure to spare me — at least one case must come back with an answer I will not like. Close with what the procedure cannot do.

Please write in British English.
