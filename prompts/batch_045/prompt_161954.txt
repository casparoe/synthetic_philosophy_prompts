hi — I write novels, and I need a bit of research done for the new one, so bear with me while I explain the shape of the problem.

There's a character in it who is, annoyingly, a philosopher, and I want her to have one genuinely good argument, not a hand-wavy one. The argument I want is the one about epiphenomenalism and judgements about your own experience: if experiences don't do any causal work, then it looks like a subject's judgement about what her experience is like isn't explained by the experience it's about, and then its specific content — and its being *right* — starts to look like luck. Every version of this I've run into has the person making the judgement at the exact moment she's having the experience, which hands the epiphenomenalist an easy out: one neural state causes both the experience and the judgement about it, they're joint effects, nothing needs to run from one to the other. I want a version where that out is closed.

So: build me the case, and build it as a scene. Present tense, third person, 250 words max, something I could drop into chapter three. She's looking at the same physical thing twice and it looks different to her the second time, and the next morning, or a few days later, she judges, of those two past viewings, that the second was more like *this* than like *that*. You pick the object and the trade — a wall she's painting, the light on a harbor, a reduction sauce at two stages — but keep it concrete and physical, and keep it ordinary: no labs, no scanners, no demons, no perfect recall, no sci-fi, and the getting-it-right has to be something a real person could actually pull off.

The specifications the scene has to meet:

- The world is held fixed and the experience varies. It's settled inside the story that the object itself didn't change. That's supposed to close the exit where the epiphenomenalist says the judgement was really about the object all along and only looks like it's about the experience.
- The judgement comes after both experiences are over, with enough of a gap that no single concurrent state can be the common cause of the experience and of the judgement. This is the one that's supposed to do the real work.
- It's fine-grained and comparative, not "I was in pain again," and she has no non-phenomenal information that settles it — no beliefs about the light, about being tired, adapted, or medicated, nothing she could cite. If she can name a worldly difference that pins down the answer, the case fails.
- It's *true*, and the scene shows that without me having to stipulate it. Ideally she does something with it later and it cashes out publicly. If you can't make that work, say so rather than writing "and she was right."

If it fits without blowing the word count, add a control: the same two viewings on another occasion, nothing different that she could cite, where she forms no comparative judgement at all. If it doesn't fit, tell me why not.

Then, outside the scene, the demonstration. I need to see why epiphenomenalism can perfectly well explain the *occurence* of some judgement or other here — why she opens her mouth at all — while the specific *content*, and its accuracy, are what it can't explain; which exact feature of the scene blocks the joint-effect reply; what happens if I take the delay out and let her judge in the moment (does it collapse back into the standard case where the reply works?); what happens if I let the object change; and what the strongest thing an epiphenomenalist says back is. Give me the good version of the objection, not a strawman, because I want this to survive a smart reader. One honest paragraph at the end on which feature is doing the work and which bit of my setup is doing the least.

Two things I don't want: anything like "as so-and-so argues in such-and-such" — no citations, no names, no reading list, my philosophy friend will fill that in — and nothing that turns on facts about the brain I'd have to look up. And if the verdicts only come out the way I want because I built them into the scene rather than because they fall out of the two positions, tell me, because that's exactly the mistake I'm trying not to put in a book.

Answer in American English, please, and feel free to be blunt about where my spec is confused.
