I serve, as one of the two lay members, on the Direct Democracy Commission of my canton — the body that rules, before signatures are even counted, on whether a citizen initiative may go to a binding popular vote at all, and whether it goes to the ballot whole, in severed parts, or not at all. I was appointed last spring, having campaigned locally for wider access to the ballot, and the first opinion I must draft on my own is due in three weeks. I would be grateful for your help with it.

Our precedents, as I have reconstructed them from the archive:

- We cleared for the vote an initiative entrenching a forty-year ban on commercial airport expansion and transferring the freed land to a public land trust.
- We refused to clear an initiative requiring every municipal vehicle to be electric by 2027, with a named charging contractor and a build-out schedule annexed.
- We cleared an initiative whose only content was procedural: any future sale of cantonal water rights must itself be submitted to a separate popular vote.
- We refused to clear an initiative reducing the pay of the members of the executive council by fifteen per cent.
- We cleared an initiative declaring the canton a nuclear-free zone, although it created no enforceable duty and bound no spending.
- We refused to clear an initiative setting the transport authority's fare structure, including a discounted pass for under-25s funded by a parking levy.
- We returned, as impermissibly multi-subject, an initiative that bundled a data-protection rule with a change in the property tax, inviting its authors to resubmit the halves separately.

The proposal now before us is the Congestion Revenue and Open Models Initiative, which has four operative parts. First, a declaration that the net revenue from the canton's planned congestion charge belongs to the residents of the canton. Second, a requirement that ninety per cent of that net revenue be distributed annually in equal per-resident rebates, the amount to be certified by the cantonal audit office. Third, a proviso that the ninety per cent share may be lowered only by a further popular vote. Fourth, an obligation to publish the traffic model underlying the charge together with its source code and a plain-language statement of the assumptions that most affect projected revenue; this part lapses after eight years unless renewed.

My own hypothesis is that we have been running something like a distinction between frame measures — decisions that fix the terms within which later decisions are made — and line measures, which settle one allocative outcome, and that we have never said so in an opinion. A colleague argues that every precedent fits a simpler test: we defer to the people exactly when a decision is costly for a later assembly or electorate to undo, and we retain what is cheaply revisable. As far as I can tell the two tests agree on the new initiative's first and third parts and disagree sharply on the second and fourth. Both of them, moreover, stumble on the nuclear-free declaration, which was trivially reversible and yet cleared; the minutes defend it as constituent rather than policy, which may be a third test in disguise — that the people may decide whatever the canton is entitled to declare about itself, whatever follows from it in practice.

So: assuming we should be consistent with ourselves rather than with what any of us would prefer on the merits, what should we do — clear the initiative whole, clear some parts and sever others, or return it to its authors? Which precedent is the closest analog, and why? (I do not think the multi-subject precedent settles it, since the airport initiative had two operative parts and was not returned.) For each option, what principle would we be adopting, stated plainly enough to bind us next time? And is there a variant of this proposal, or a hypothetical next one, that would expose which test we have really been using?

One further worry I cannot set aside: the more we sever the technical parts, the more we leave voters ratifying declarations whose operative content is settled elsewhere, so that a yes becomes difficult to interpret as a mandate for anything. Several colleagues think any filtering by us is illegitimate in principle. I would like to know whether that worry is a reason to change the practice or simply a cost of having one.
