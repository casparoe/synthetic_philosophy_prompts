I write about science and ideas for a national magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how arguments from the philosophy of religion keep resurfacing in AI policy. The move I keep encountering in interviews is Pascal's wager: almost everyone arguing for heavy regulation or a pause on frontier-model training eventually says some version of "look, it's just Pascal's wager — the probability may be small, but the stakes are astronomical, so we should act as if the risk is real." A policy memo I was shown (from a think tank I can't name) makes the reasoning explicit in something it calls the Precautionary Wager Test: fund any intervention that reduces the probability of an extinction-or-worse outcome by at least one basis point, provided its cost stays below 0.1 percent of annual GDP, and — this is the clause that made me sit up — explicitly waive any requirement that the probability estimate be well grounded, on the grounds that demanding evidence is itself a way of betting against. I'd like to audit that analogy properly before I let the phrase go to print, because I suspect it is doing considerably more work than it can carry.

So: take the wager as it actually appears in Pascal's Pensées — a single interlocutor, told that he must wager whether he likes it or not, offered an infinite gain against a finite stake, and advised that if he cannot bring himself to believe he should go through the motions until belief follows — and set it beside the memo's test. Give me the correspondence explicitly, a table is fine: what plays the role of God's existence, of the infinite payoff, of the "you must wager" step, of the trivial cost, of the remedy of going through the motions? Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and be discriminating about which breaks matter for this use and which don't. I can see that Pascal's bettor wagers his own soul while the memo's bettor spends other people's money, and that Pascal's option set is binary while the policy menu is not; I'm much less sure how much turns on the shift from a literally infinite payoff to a merely astronomical finite one — eight billion lives plus however many generations follow. My sense is that this is the load-bearing switch: with an infinite payoff any nonzero probability dominates and the quality of the estimate drops out entirely, whereas once the payoff is finite the expected value is hostage to the probability, and a waived evidence requirement isn't a bold feature of the argument but the exact place it comes apart. Is that right? And what should I make of the menu of competing wagers? The many-gods objection seems to reappear here as a many-risks problem — alignment, engineered pandemics, nuclear war, asteroids — each of which can be run through the same test, and some of which appear to conflict with each other, as when one reading of the test licenses a training pause and another licenses racing to build the safer system first.

Two further things I'd want before writing. First, name the claims the analogy is quietly importing that aren't in Pascal at all: I have candidates — that the option set is binary, that the costs of wagering are negligible, that the decision-maker is the party who bears them, and that the situation is genuinely one of unmeasurable uncertainty rather than measurable risk — but I'd like you to check my list and add what I've missed, and to say which of them are merely rhetorical and which are doing real argumentative work. Second, if Pascal's wager is the wrong analogy, give me a better one. One philosopher I interviewed said this is closer to what gets called "Pascal's Mugging" than to Pascal's wager — that it is a puzzle about the credibility of enormous, uncheckable claims rather than a dominance argument — and another said the honest framing is value of information: we are not forced to bet blind, we can pay to measure the probability, and what we should be buying is measurement rather than maximal precaution. I'd like your verdict on both, and a clear statement of whether any defensible version of the memo's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Please answer in American English.
