I record a long-form interview series for the ideas desk at a national newspaper — fifty minutes of audio, later cut down to a shorter print version — and next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent most of her career defending Molinism. Her new book's real ambition is not the familiar use of middle knowledge to reconcile divine foreknowledge with libertarian freedom; it is an argument that Molinism uniquely explains what she calls the fine texture of providence, the thought that God can secure quite specific, individually meaningful outcomes without ever overriding anyone's will. The chapter I most want to press on is the last one, where she extends the account to cases in which an agent's deliberation includes objectively chancy events — a genuinely indeterministic neural process, or a coin flip the agent then responds to — and claims that Molinism handles that kind of luck better than either simple-foreknowledge views or open theism. She is also a very practiced interviewee, and her reliable move is to absorb an objection by saying, in effect, that this is only the mystery of creaturely freedom reappearing, not a cost peculiar to Molinism.

What I need is eight to ten questions, each with the answer she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer, none of which lets her simply restate the threefold distinction among natural, middle, and free knowledge: I have read enough to follow the dialectic, and I will take care of that in my introduction. I want at least one question built around the grounding worry — what makes it true that in circumstances C this agent would freely do A, given that it is not God's decree, not anything the agent actually does, and not fixed by the laws together with the prior state of the world — and I want the follow-up to wedge open the space between her two most likely escapes, treating those truths as brute or grounding them in the agent's character, since the first seems to leave God's plan hostage to a vast set of facts He neither chose nor can explain, while the second seems to reintroduce the very sort of determination her incompatibilism exists to deny. I want at least one question on the chancy-deliberation chapter: if middle knowledge must contain a definite truth about what an agent would do when the process feeding the decision is objectively indeterministic, in what sense is the outcome chancy, and if it need not, where exactly does the resulting gap in providence show up? I also want a question about the sovereignty side of the view — the possibility that every world God could actualize containing some particular person is one in which that person does something wrong, and what she takes that to say about divine power — and a question about whether her version of theodicy, which makes the amount of evil in the world depend on what creatures would have done, really requires there to be a best feasible world for God to have created.

Finally, I want a short sequence of questions that makes her this-is-only-the-mystery-of-freedom move progressively harder to reach for, by isolating some commitment Molinism carries that an incompatibilist with mere simple foreknowledge does not, and one closing question about what could count as evidence against the view, phrased so that it still means something to a secular listener who cares about counterfactuals about people and about prediction rather than about theology. Please keep each question short enough to say aloud, put them in the order you would ask them in if the first two have to work without any jargon at all, and add a line or two after each one telling me what you expect her to say, so that I know where to push.
