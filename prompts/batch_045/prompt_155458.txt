I sit on the advisory board of a small philanthropic fund that has roughly six million dollars to spend over three years on the welfare of farmed aquatic animals — finfish such as salmon, tilapia, carp and pangasius, and crustaceans such as shrimp, prawns and crayfish. We are not funding advocacy or litigation; we are commissioning research, so what we are really buying is answers to questions, and there is room for about eight to twelve projects. What I need from you is a defended agenda, not a survey of the field.

Let me tell you where our discussion has got to, so you can push on it. We keep dividing the territory into acute-event welfare — slaughter and pre-slaughter handling, transport, live chilling in ice slurry, electrical or percussive stunning, crowding and pumping at harvest — and chronic-state welfare: the months of life spent under some given stocking density, water chemistry, lighting regime, feeding schedule and social structure. One of us argues that chronic states must dominate, since an animal lives through months of the one and minutes of the other, and that the money should follow the welfare-days rather than the drama of the killing method. Another argues the reverse: that chronic states are measured badly, that the welfare-days framing quietly assumes an additive model which may not hold when the intensities differ by an order of magnitude, and that acute interventions are the ones where a funder can actually verify compliance on the ground. A third has raised what we have started to call the headcount-offset worry: if welfare reforms lower costs or raise yields, or merely make farmed seafood more acceptable to consumers, the resulting expansion in the number of animals farmed could cancel out the per-animal gain, so a project could succeed on its own metrics and be neutral or worse in aggregate. A fourth raises the mirror image — that funding welfare research confers legitimacy on the industry and slows the shift to alternative proteins, and that the same dollars would do more elsewhere. Underneath all of it sits a problem we have not resolved: the deep uncertainty about whether decapods, and to a lesser extent finfish, have valenced states at all, and how to compare welfare across animals whose bodies and lives differ so much that we have no obvious common currency.

So here is what I want. Rank the open questions you think it is most worth spending this money on answering, and defend the ranking: state the criteria you are using — scale, neglectedness, tractability at this budget and within three years, how many other questions depend on the answer, how much the answer would actually change what a funder or an advocacy group then does — and show how you are trading those criteria off against one another. I especially want your view on the disputes above: whether the acute/chronic split is even the right axis to prioritise along; whether some version of additive welfare-days accounting should be a funder's working tool given that it may be wrong; whether taxonomic sentience uncertainty is best handled as a probability weight in an expected-value calculation, as a reason to prefer interventions that are robust across the possibilities, or in some other way; and how much weight the headcount-offset and legitimation arguments deserve given how speculative they are. For each of your top items, say what kind of study or analysis would genuinely settle it at this budget, and what result would move it up or down your list.

Finally, on register: be explicit about your own uncertainty. For each item tell me whether you are fairly confident, genuinely unsure, or extrapolating from thin evidence, and flag anywhere the question as I have posed it is ill-posed or a better question is sitting next to it. If you think commissioning answers is the wrong use of this money altogether, or that some part of my framing rests on a confusion, say that plainly rather than answering politely on the terms I have set.
