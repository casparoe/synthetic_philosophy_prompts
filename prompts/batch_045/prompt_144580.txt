my animal ethics reading group got completely hijacked last week by one guy who does Wittgenstein. We were talking about whether insecticide use could be a really big deal morally — like, if pest control on a farm kills enormous numbers of insects and even a decent fraction of them are in pain, does that swamp the crop benefits — and he said the whole discussion rests on a confusion that the beetle in the box already dissolves.

His version, as near as I can reconstruct it: everyone walks around with a box containing something, nobody can look into anyone else's box, we all say we know what a beetle is only from our own case, and Wittgenstein's point is that whatever is in there drops out of consideration as irrelevant. Insects, he says, are boxes we can never open, so there is nothing for the word pain to latch onto in their case, and the question of whether they suffer isn't a question at all. Which gets him to: the insect suffering stuff is noise, and spraying the aphids is not the moral emergency it's been made out to be.

I don't buy it but I can't put my finger on where it goes wrong, partly because I'm not even sure what the beetle thing is meant to establish in its home context. So can you audit the analogy as he's using it? Concretely:

- What maps onto what once you move it from the passage in the Investigations to insects. Who are the box-holders, what's in the box, what word is doing the work, what plays the role of nobody being able to look in. A table would be easiest for me to read out.
- Where it breaks. In the original the language game is already up and running and the private object drops out for everyone symmetrically; with insects we arguably don't yet have a practice of ascribing pain to them at all, and we share no form of life with them. Do those differences matter for his conclusion, or are they irrelevant in the same way the contents of the box are?
- Whether the picture is even neutral here. It feels like it's built to deflate one claim (that a sensation-word names some private item) and he's using it to deflate a different one (that there's a fact of the matter about insect pain, maybe a discoverable one). Are those the same claim? My gut says no but I can't say which one the beetle actually kills.
- One of the others proposed a candidate public criterion: the creature gives up something it would normally want — food, mating, a safe hiding spot — in order to guard or groom an injured body part. Someone thought they'd seen work like that on bees and on crabs, I have no idea if that's real. If criteria of that kind were available, does the beetle picture have any bite left at all, or does it just evaporate?
- Does anything genuinely Wittgensteinian survive that supports him — about concepts being tied to a form of life, about pain being a word we learn alongside human pain-behaviour, about there being no extension of a concept beyond its use? And separately: is the moral conclusion even meant to follow from a claim about meaning? Even if pain were just indeterminate as applied to insects, I don't see what that tells me to do about the aphids.
- If the beetle is the wrong picture, is there a better one? Someone suggested it's more like ascribing intelligence to a chess program: a concept getting stretched into a new region on the basis of partial criteria, where the verdict is part decision and part discovery. Is that closer?

I have to give a response next week so I want something I can say out loud, not a tour of the secondary literature.
