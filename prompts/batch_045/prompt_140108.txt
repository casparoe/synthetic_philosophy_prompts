**Question 3 (25 points).** In the section of the Pensées on justice and force, Pascal argues, roughly, that justice without force is impotent and force without justice is tyrannical; that because disputes about justice are endless while everyone agrees about who has force, we were unable to make the just strong, and so we made the strong just. For this question use the three labels from the handout: the legibility thesis, the substitution move, and the concealment condition.
(a) Explain how the three fit together into a single line of argument. Be explicit about which of them is supposed to explain what: make clear whether the legibility thesis is itself the engine of the substitution move, or whether it does some other job. (15 points)
(b) Suppose the argument works. Does it follow that a subject has a moral reason to obey a law she correctly believes to be unjust? Answer yes or no, and defend your answer. Keep apart the epistemic and the constitutive readings of the substitution move. (10 points)

**Student answer (about 650 words).** Pascal's target is the comfortable thought that law governs us because it is just. His first move is the legibility thesis: ask people what justice requires and you get endless dispute; ask who has force and you get immediate agreement. From this it follows that the strong come to be called just — people can see who wins and cannot see what is just, so the word "just" ends up attached to whichever side has force behind it. That is the substitution move: we could not make the just strong, so we made the strong just. Custom then does the rest, since a rule that has been in place long enough stops looking like something anyone decided and starts looking like the nature of things. This is why the origin has to be kept out of view: if you trace a law back to its beginning you find conquest or usurpation, and once people see that, the law's authority dissolves. So the concealment condition is not an add-on; without it the substitution would be visible, and a visible substitution wouldn't work. A further consequence is worth noticing: the account is self-limiting in a way Pascal seems to enjoy. If it is true, it cannot be announced from the pulpit or the law courts without undoing the thing it describes, since telling people that their laws rest on usurpation is precisely what dissolves their allegiance. For (b): yes, she has a moral reason to obey. On the epistemic reading she cannot know that the law is unjust, because Pascal's point is that disputes about justice are interminable and that human reason, after the fall, is unreliable here; so her belief that this law is unjust is exactly the sort of claim she has been given no way to settle. But there is also a distinctively Pascalian argument available from the part of the book that deals with the wager: obeying costs her relatively little, while resistance risks the collapse of order and civil war, which is the worst outcome, so — as with the bet on God — prudence points decisively one way. And Pascal is a Christian; for a Christian prudence is not mere self-interest but a genuine reason, so this is a moral reason and not simply a threat. Notice, too, that the two readings we were told to separate actually converge here: on the constitutive reading the established rule is what fixes what justice requires, and since the epistemic reading says we can never get behind the established rule anyway, there is no practical difference between them. Two last things. If law is really force plus concealment, the practical advice to a ruler is clear: invent a founding story and make sure everyone is taught it, which is presumably why Pascal says we must not look behind the curtain. And the upshot is that there is nothing more to a law's being just than its being established and able to enforce itself — whatever justice is in itself, it does no work in explaining why anyone obeys anything.
