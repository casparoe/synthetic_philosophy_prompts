I work in applied forecasting and sit in on an informal machine-learning theory reading group, and I keep meeting the same sentence doing three apparently different jobs. The sentence is Occam's razor in the form everyone repeats: *prefer the simplest hypothesis that fits the data*. In the reading group one member used it as a dismissal — our model with two extra lag terms is worse, because it is more complex, end of discussion. Another used it as a boast, more or less as follows: this isn't a hunch at all, it's a theorem, because the universal prior charges each hypothesis a probability proportional to two raised to minus its description length, so simplicity is just probability theory with the right prior, and the resulting predictor is known to converge to any computable data-generating process with its total expected error bounded by something proportional to how complex that process is. And last week a friend used the same sentence on a completely different subject, to argue that a conspiracy theory is unlikely because it posits far more agents and steps than the mundane story. I don't think those three uses are the same claim, and I suspect at least one of them is buying something the slogan doesn't sell.

Here is roughly where my own confusion sits. First, which simplicity? The number of free parameters in a regression, the number of kinds of thing a theory says exist, the degree of a polynomial, the length of the shortest program that emits the data on some universal machine — these order hypotheses differently, and as I understand it what Solomonoff's prior actually trades in is prefix-free program length, which isn't any of the ordinary scientific notions of simplicity. It also looks language-relative: couldn't I make almost any hypothesis come out simple by picking, or simulating, a machine on which it has a very short name? Second, how much does simplicity buy? If the razor only breaks ties between hypotheses that fit the data exactly equally well, it is idle in every real case, since exact ties don't occur; the readings that do any work must specify a rate of exchange between fit and complexity, and here I notice that AIC charges a flat penalty per parameter while BIC's grows with the logarithm of the sample size, which is a different rate again from one bit of prior per bit of program — and I can't tell which of these is the razor, or whether one of them falls out of the description-length view once you also charge for the precision you'd need to specify a parameter at all. Third, what is the razor delivering: better predictions, or truth about unobservable structure? Those come apart — a penalised model can forecast better while being false about the mechanism, and a baroque theory can be true. Fourth, is the razor's justification independent of its conclusion? If the convergence result for the universal prior is stated relative to a weighting over possible worlds that itself discounts complex worlds at the rate of their complexity, then the success of simplicity seems to be assumed in the very prior that was supposed to establish it — though I may be misunderstanding the status of that result. I also don't know what to make of the fact that the constant in the bound can be astronomically large and the prior is not computable, so the theorem concerns an ideal agent rather than my model choice on Monday morning.

What I'd like, then, is a disambiguation rather than an explanation. Take the slogan in the form I quoted and lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical: the methodological or economic versions (simpler models are cheaper to fit, easier to search, easier to communicate), the predictive versions (complexity penalties as insurance against overfitting), the probabilistic versions about priors and description length, the versions that make it a claim about what probably exists, and anything strong enough to be what people call *Bayesian Occam's razor*. For each reading, say in a sentence or two exactly what it asserts, whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or merely unsupported, and — the part I actually need — whether it would support the three uses I described: dropping my lag terms, calling the razor a theorem, and ruling out the conspiracy theory. Where a reading is only defensible given some empirical or methodological assumption to the effect that the world is simple, say so, say what kind of assumption it is and how someone might argue for it, and flag the readings that are circular. Please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list or tell me who proved what; I have plenty of that and it isn't what I'm missing. And if one of my own framings above is confused, I'd rather be corrected than humoured.
