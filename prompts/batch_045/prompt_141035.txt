I was appointed to my city's utilities and infrastructure oversight commission three months ago, after a long neighborhood fight over water shutoffs two streets from my own, and I have been handed the file on a procurement that has turned out to be harder to classify than I expected. The commission's standing rule is that a procurement counts as a *political* decision when the artifact itself carries political weight — in that case it must come to us for a recorded vote and a stated rationale — and as a technical decision, delegable to staff, when it does not. I have been reading a little of the literature on whether artifacts have politics, which distinguishes designs that merely happen to serve someone's ends from designs whose very operation requires or rules out a particular distribution of power, and I think that distinction is doing work in our own track record, though not consistently.

Our precedents, as I read them from the minutes:

1. Mid-century parkway underpasses built with clearances just under nine feet, too low for buses; the beaches they served were reached mainly by transit riders who were largely Black, brown, and without cars. Engineering files cite fill and sight lines; a historian later argued the exclusion was designed in. Ruled political, 6–1; the majority memo said the exclusion was known when the clearance was set and has proved impossible to undo.
2. Replacement of transit-mall benches with divided seats having center armrests, justified by staff as seating that cannot be slept on. Ruled political, 4–3; the dissent called it a furniture choice.
3. Conversion to single-stream recycling with an automated sorting facility, eliminating roughly forty unionized sorting jobs. Ruled not political: the job losses were said to be a matter for the labor agreement and the budget, not a property of the machinery.
4. Replacement of manual thermostats in city buildings with a networked building-management system, moving discretion over temperature to facilities staff. No one alleged intent. Ruled political, 5–2, because the system concentrates control over workplace conditions and makes handing it back expensive.
5. An eleven-foot lane-width standard in the resurfacing manual, which cyclists said precludes protected bike lanes. Ruled not political: a design standard, revisable on ordinary engineering cycles; bring it back as a bike-lane proposal.

The new file: replacing roughly 9,200 residential water meters with networked meters that support remote flow restriction. The stated rationale is leak detection, conservation, and billing accuracy, and the restriction feature is standard on the vendor's meter rather than something we asked for. About 3.4% of accounts are in arrears; roughly 40% of the affected households rent, and the account is held by the landlord, so restriction at the meter lands on tenants. The vendor's software can set restriction to a minimum trickle rather than full closure, and whatever we choose about notice periods and minimum flow stays ours to set later. In the next budget cycle, staff propose eliminating the four field crews and two trucks that currently perform disconnections, saving about $1.1M a year; the meters are on a fifteen-year replacement cycle. No one has alleged exclusionary intent.

I would like your recommendation among three options: require a recorded vote on the procurement itself; delegate the procurement but reserve the arrears and restriction policy for a recorded vote; or delegate both. Please set out the two or three rules that fit our five precedents about equally well, say what each predicts here, identify which precedent is the closest analog and which pulls the other way, and give me your best guess with the reason for preferring it. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, tell me plainly what principle we would be adopting with each choice — including whether a decision here would commit us to treating items 3 and 5 differently in future. Finally, name the one further case you would want in front of us to separate the rules you have identified. I am less interested in the correct answer than in the shape of the rule we would be committing to.
