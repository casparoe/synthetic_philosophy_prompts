I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the screening that now surrounds the beginning of a life — the NIPT panels routinely offered in early pregnancy, the embryo grading and monogenic-disease testing that fertility clinics run as a matter of course — and about what the legal architecture around all of it seems to assume about disability. What I can't get straight is the shape of the package. In jurisdiction after jurisdiction, termination is permitted on the ground that the fetus has a diagnosed severe anomaly, and in several the general gestational cut-off is relaxed or removed for exactly that case; the same legal order then singles out termination sought because of the fetus's sex for separate prohibition. Meanwhile the disability-rights framework in force in those same countries insists that a life with a disability is not a lesser life, and the clinical picture adds its own twist: clinics will screen an embryo for a serious inherited disease, decline to screen for height or for sex absent a medical reason, and in doing so are arguably choosing among candidate children rather than benefiting any child, since the alternative to this child was never a healthy version of this child.

A disability-rights lawyer I interviewed proposed what she called a linkage rule: a health service should be permitted to offer screening for a condition only if it also guarantees, at comparable generosity, the lifetime support a child born with that condition would need — the thought being that the objection to screening isn't the technology but the message sent by a state that will pay to prevent a kind of life while underfunding the people already living it. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of the status quo and then test it against that proposal. Does the disability ground really presuppose a comparative claim about disabled lives being worse, or can the whole package be reconstructed without it — as parental autonomy plus resource allocation plus something about the severity of the burdens a family may reasonably decline? Is there a principled line between the "medical" traits we may select against and the social ones we may not, and what happens to that line if the social model of disability is right that much of the disadvantage of impairment is produced by social arrangement rather than by the body — does disability selection then become structurally like sex selection, which the same legal system forbids? Is the expressivist objection really about what the state says, so that a linkage rule would dissolve it, or does it survive in some other form — for instance in the worry that once screening is offered, declining it starts to look like an irresponsible choice?

Finally, I want to know how much of this the practice strictly commits us to and how much is merely compatible with it. In embryo selection especially, if the justification can't be that the child is benefited, then what is actually doing the work — and does that undercut the procreative beneficence framing, or just relocate it to the parents, the siblings, or the health system's budget? Could several quite different theories license the same package, so that the practice is philosophically underdetermined rather than committed? And if you think the pieces genuinely conflict, tell me which piece should give, and describe concretely what screening policy would look like if it gave.
