I am preparing an application for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship and need the research proposal itself: two pages, roughly a thousand words. The area is the controversy over expected-value fanaticism — the family of arguments which hold that, provided the payoff is large enough, one ought to favour an arbitrarily small chance of it over the certain delivery of something modest — together with the main ways of resisting that conclusion (bounded value functions, risk-avoidance, discounting astronomical payoffs) and the recent defences of fanaticism proper. Assume throughout that the payoffs in question are finite but arbitrarily large, and set aside both infinite ethics and uncertainty about the probability model itself.

My half-formed idea, which I want you to sharpen rather than restate, is this. The dispute has been conducted almost entirely as a disagreement about the shape of the value function, or about whether expected value is the right rule at all, and it has been conducted over single decisions. But the arguments that make fanaticism vivid work by aggregation — a mugger's offer of a tiny chance at an astronomical payoff, a long run of such offers, an indefinitely extended future in which they recur. I suspect that once one asks how a decision is to be individuated across time, the familiar positions shift in ways that have not been mapped. Call this the individuation question: is the proper object of normative assessment a single act, a package of independent acts, or a policy, i.e. a function from one's evidence to acts?

The conjecture to be tested is as follows. A view can avoid fanaticism in the single case only by becoming package-sensitive: by letting the verdict on each of a long run of independent, tiny-probability bets depend on how many such bets will be available. And package-sensitivity is, I think, either a holism that is diachronically unstable — the agent's verdict at a time depends on facts about her entire future in a way she cannot use when deciding — or else it is best reconstrued as a claim about policies, in which case fanaticism reappears one level up, as a feature of the choice among policies rather than among acts. If that is right, the payoff of the project is not a verdict on whether fanaticism is true but the discovery that fanaticism is transmitted across levels of description, so that the real question is which level, if any, is normatively basic.

What I want the proposal to contain:

a precise statement of the question the project would answer, sharpened so that it admits of a determinate answer and is not merely the question whether fanaticism is true;

an account of why the standard positions leave that question open, and in particular why none of them has had to take a stand on individuation;

the method, set out month by month across the twelve months, including the minimal formal apparatus I would need — I have in mind a toy model of a finite run of independent offers and a small class of decision procedures — and a defence of that apparatus against the obvious alternatives, which I take to be further decision-theoretic axiomatics, an empirical study of how people respond to tiny probabilities, and a straight survey of the literature;

a description of what a result would look like: enumerate the possible conclusions (that the problem dissolves, that it relocates to the level of policies, that it is amplified there, or something I have not anticipated) and say which considerations would decide among them;

and finally an honest failure condition, together with a note on the most likely way the project becomes unmanageable within a year.

Do not cite anything and do not supply a reading list; I shall add the references myself. Please write in British English, keep the whole thing near a thousand words, adopt my conjecture without pretending to have proved it, and close with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing, in no more than ten words, the assumption on which the project stands or falls.
