Writing a short panel thing for a writers festival in a few weeks — me and three other novelists on "AI and the future of the sentence", or whatever the programme people end up calling it — and I realised I dont actually know whether my own position hangs together. I'd rather find that out now than into a microphone. And I dont care about the research or citations or what any study found; I want to know if the set of things I believe is consistent. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Drafting is how I find out what I think. I dont start with an idea and then translate it into words; the words are where the idea happens. So the sentences have to be mine in a fairly strong sense.

2. None of that is the reader's business. A novel should be judged as the object it is. What I meant, or what I had to do to get there, isn't part of what makes it good — I've never had any patience for authorial intention as a criterion for anything.

3. The models don't understand anything. There's no one home. Fluent, useful, occasionally startling, but no grip on any subject matter.

4. Even so, leaning on them erodes something in me. Not because I'm importing their confusion — see (3) — but because the skill atrophies, and the skill is the thing that does the finding out in (1).

5. Actual cases, which is where I trust myself most. I use a thesaurus, a rhyming dictionary for a verse section, spellcheck, and Google finishing my search query, and none of that feels like cheating. "Continue this paragraph" does, instantly. My friend dictates whole novels by voice and I dont think she's outsourced a thing. I think paying a retired editor to restructure your third act is completely legitimate and costs you nothing. I did try the thing once, on a chapter I was stuck on, and the prose was fine — better than fine. That's what frightened me.

6. Reading is real understanding. A careful reader who has never written a novel understands Middlemarch as well as I understand my own books, probably better. I hold no maker's-knowledge line about the reader's side of the transaction.

7. I cant navigate anywhere without my phone and I cant remember a phone number, and I have genuinely lost nothing that mattered. So I'm suspicious of myself here — maybe this is just the complaint every generation makes about the new tool.

8. What I'm actually afraid of isn't bad prose. It's what I've started calling thin style: every sentence locally competent, none of them paid for, so the book ends up with no accent and nobody could tell who wrote it. And I want to say that's a property of the text, not a fact about my biography.

So: is that a package? Which of these actually fight, which only look like they do, and which single commitment is cheapest to give up? Rank the tensions by how severe they are rather than listing them as equal, and for each one say what would have to move. If you pushed me right now I'd guess (3) and (4) are the shaky pair, but I cant say why.

Two things about how to answer: tell me which commitment goes, with reasons — dont hand me a menu of options and leave me to choose, I can get that from the festival programme. And mark the places where you're reading my psychology or my motives rather than the logic, because I'd like to know which parts of your answer I should discount.
