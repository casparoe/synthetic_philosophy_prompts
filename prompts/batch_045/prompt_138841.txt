I run a "science on screen" evening each term for the first-year course I teach on scientific method — mostly engineers and life-science majors, bright but with no philosophy of science — and next month we are screening Nolan's The Prestige (2006) at the end of the fortnight we spend on Lakatos. I want the film to do real work as a case rather than serve as decoration, and I am not yet convinced the reading I have in mind survives contact with the film. Here is the reconstruction I have been playing with, which may well be wrong: treat Borden and Angier as two rival research programmes sharing a problem domain (the Transported Man) and a shared, never-surrendered hard core — that a man can appear to be in two places at once, and that the audience must never be told how. On my reading the two programmes differ entirely in their protective belts and heuristics: Borden's belt consists of ordinary, craft-transmissible means pushed to the limit of a whole shared life (the double life, the sacrificed fingers, the cost paid by everyone around him), governed by a negative heuristic that refuses any appeal to mechanism; Angier's belt moves from the hired double to Tesla's machine, governed by a demand that the effect be genuinely produced and reproducible by anyone. The anomalies that drive each programme are, I think, fairly easy to point at: Angier's failure to reproduce Borden's version of the trick, the rehearsal in which Julia drowns and whose cause is genuinely ambiguous, the night the Real Transported Man appears and Borden is left asking how it was done. I would like you to take this reconstruction, correct it where it is wrong, spell out in plain language what the hard core, the protective belt, the positive and negative heuristics, and the progressive-versus-degenerating distinction are actually doing in each career, and name the scenes you would stop the film on as the anomalies and the would-be crucial experiments.

Two wrinkles are what I really want help with, and neither is in the secondary literature I have read. First, the Tesla machine appears to do something genuinely new: real duplication, not illusion. By Lakatos's own criterion of novel facts, that would make Angier's programme progressive and Borden's a degenerating patchwork of ordinary means — which is exactly backwards from the film's moral verdict. Worse, the machine's novel fact is one that must be destroyed every night for the trick to survive, so the programme produces novelty and consumes its own evidence in the same act; I have been calling this self-erasing novelty, and I want to know whether Lakatos's vocabulary has any honest place for it, whether the notion is even coherent, and what verdict a Lakatosian should return: progressive, because novel facts, or degenerating, because nothing accumulates. Second, the film's nested narratives — one magician's journal read inside another's — are an obvious analogue of Lakatos's rational reconstruction of a programme's history, and in the film that reconstruction turns out to be a plant, a deliberate deception. Read that against Lakatos's distinction between internal and external history and his legitimization of rational reconstruction: is the film quietly arguing that the internal history of a programme is itself a prestige, and can a Lakatosian answer that charge, or only a Feyerabendian? Relatedly, Cutter's closing claim that the audience wants to be fooled looks like a sociological point of the sort Lakatos tried to keep outside internal history; I would like a response to it that is more than anything goes with better vocabulary.

Please also tell me plainly where the reading fails, because I would rather cut the exercise than mislead a room of eighteen-year-olds. The obvious worry is that Lakatos's machinery was built for public programmes with shared standards of evidence, whereas magic succeeds by concealment, so that novel fact and corroboration misfire from the start; if that is fatal, say so, and say what the film would still buy me. If it is not fatal, give me three or four scenes to screen with a one-sentence note on each, and a short framing paragraph I can put on a handout that explains the Lakatosian vocabulary in terms a first-year engineer can follow. Assume I cannot check quotations and that you cannot look anything up: describe scenes rather than quoting dialogue, and do not cite page numbers, papers, or edition details. Please write in American English, in continuous prose rather than bullet points, at roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words.
