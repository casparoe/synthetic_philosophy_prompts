I am writing an essay on the nature and value of privacy and want to open it with a sustained reading of Hitchcock's *Rear Window* (1954). I need a reading that takes the film and the philosophical debate equally seriously, and I would be grateful for your comments on the reading sketched below: where it holds, where it cheats, and where the film resists it.

Two things to set aside first. The well-worn feminist reading of the film in terms of the male gaze and voyeuristic pleasure is not what I am after — I know that one, though I would like to know if my reading quietly depends on it. And I do not care about Hitchcock's intentions. The debate I want to bring to the film is the one about what privacy consists in and why it matters: the reductionist claim that there is no distinctive wrong in a loss of privacy, only the further wrongs (injury, trespass, breach of confidence, distress) that violations of privacy typically bring with them, so that an act of watching with no such downstream effects would be innocent; the control claim, that privacy is a person's determining who has access to her and on what terms; and the context claim, that privacy is the appropriateness of an information flow relative to a practice or setting, so that the very same item of information can pass innocently in one context and wrongly in another.

My proposal is that the film's central drama is not about information at all, and that this puts pressure on all three accounts. What is wrong with Jeff's watching, on my reading, is that it converts co-presence into one-way access. Neighbours see one another and can be seen seeing; they can wave, or be asked what they want, or be shamed. That mutual answerability is what makes casual observation across a courtyard benign, and it is destroyed by surveillance — not the confidentiality, not the information, and not the control, since nobody in the courtyard has much control over what a neighbour can see through a window anyway. Call this the answerability account. What makes me think it is more than a rewording of the control account is the prediction it generates: the return of the gaze should be experienced as a restoration, even when it is terrifying, because the watched person's looking back changes the relation at once while changing nothing about what is known. That is what I take the last act to stage: Thorwald crosses the courtyard and asks Jeff what he wants, and Jeff, immobilised in his chair and then blinded by his own flashbulbs, is suddenly the one who cannot see. Mutuality is restored and it is intolerable — which, if the reading is right, shows that privacy's value lies not in being hidden but in being able to set the terms on which one is available to the attention of others, and that unmediated mutual exposure is not the good of which privacy is the privation.

Questions, roughly in order of how much I need them answered:

1. Is the answerability account genuinely a fourth option, or does it collapse into the control account on one side (answerability as one more thing one controls) or the context account on the other? If it collapses, say where, and say what the film would then be evidence for.

2. Where does the film resist the reading? I can see two places, and I want to know whether they are fatal. First, the plot's vindication: the surveillance catches a murderer, and on any broadly consequentialist view of privacy's value the wrong seems to wash out. Second, our own position: the film requires us to do exactly what Jeff does and rewards us for it, which makes it an odd vehicle for a critique of watching — unless the point is precisely that the wrong of watching is not the sort of thing consent or good consequences remove.

3. A sharper version of the same worry. Jeff also watches Miss Lonelyhearts and Miss Torso, and those neighbours never know. The film invites us to enjoy his interest in them and treats it very differently from his interest in Thorwald. But on the answerability account the structure of attention is the same in both cases. Is that a counterexample to my reading, or is the film making a point I have not articulated — that answerability matters only among people who might otherwise be answerable to one another, that the wrong of surveillance requires something like a standing relationship that is being betrayed?

4. Finally: which scenes would you build the argument around, and what, if anything, does the film add to the philosophy that the arguments alone do not? I am equally interested in the reverse question: what in the concept of privacy can a film like this not stage, and should I say so plainly or leave it implicit?
