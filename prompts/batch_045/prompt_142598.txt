I work on methodology at a forecasting platform, and I need a document I can actually circulate, so let me give you the situation in some detail. Several hundred forecasters file probability vectors weekly on about forty resolved-twice-a-year questions, each question a partition of two, three, or sometimes five outcomes; they file numbers to two decimal places, and they are scored on a leaderboard by Brier score and paid in tournament standing redeemable for modest cash prizes. My colleague's team has built an automated auditor that reads each filing and, when the filed vector is incoherent, constructs a portfolio of bets at the forecaster's own stated rates that guarantees them a loss; we then flag the filing, auto-rescale it to the nearest coherent vector, or dock points from repeat offenders. I have to decide within about six weeks whether to keep, rewrite, or scrap the audit, and I have to defend the decision to a vocal group of forecasters who say that being arbitraged by a bot is a gamified stunt with no bearing on whether their probabilities were any good — a couple of them have started filing deliberately incoherent vectors as a protest, and one has taken to reporting 0.5 on every cell of every partition, which is coherent, uninformative, and useless to us.

Two of our advisors have been arguing about the justification for roughly a year, and I want their argument converted into the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. As I would characterize them, and I would like you to redraw each in the words its own side would accept rather than in mine: the coherence camp holds that the sure-loss argument is the whole story — incoherent credences are defective because an agent who has them is exploitable, this is a defect of the attitudes themselves and not merely of their consequences, and the same style of argument run diachronically is what grounds updating by conditionalization. The accuracy camp holds that sure-loss avoidance is at best a proxy for what really matters, accuracy under a strictly proper scoring rule, and that in our setting the proxy breaks down: the auditor's stakes are bounded and its guaranteed take per flagged filing is small; forecasters are paid in tournament standing, so their utility is not linear in the currency the auditor trades in; the auditor sees only the numbers typed into a form and never anyone's actual betting dispositions; about a third of filings come from two-person teams who file jointly and who sometimes do not agree with each other at the moment of filing; and we permit interval reports and no-opinion on some questions, about which the auditor currently says nothing. Please produce, in this order: (1) a numbered list of eight to twelve claims both camps could sign, including at least three about what the audit does and does not establish given reports-versus-attitudes, bounded stakes, and points-rather-than-money; (2) a table of remaining disagreements, with one column for the proposition each camp endorses in its own preferred wording and a column saying whether you judge the disagreement genuine or merely verbal, and, where verbal, which definition or fact dissolves it; (3) a table of cruxes, each with the concrete test both camps would accept as bearing on it — preferably an actual computation over our archived filings and outcomes, or a small hypothetical filing worked arithmetically at stakes of one unit on a three-cell partition — the prediction each camp should record in advance, and what we should do to the audit if the crux resolves one way rather than the other. Two cruxes I especially want treated: the accuracy camp's claim that any incoherent vector is accuracy-dominated by some coherent one (check whether that holds for the Brier score specifically, and whether it survives two-decimal rounding and mixed binary/three-way questions, since our rescale policy hangs on it), and the gap between expected accuracy at the time of filing and realized score on the leaderboard, which is what makes forecasters angry when we overwrite numbers that would in fact have scored well. Please also give the camps' own terms for three distinctions I have had to improvise: a sure-loss book (guaranteed loss, scalable without limit), a stake-bounded book (guaranteed loss whose size is capped), and a one-sided book (no possible gain, some possible loss) — and say whether vulnerability to each is a defect, and of the same kind.

Please be committed rather than balanced: where you think one camp has to give ground, say which and why; where you think a crux is verbal, say so flatly instead of hedging; and where a crux is genuine, say which way you expect it to resolve and what would move you. End with your own one-sentence recommendation on whether we keep the audit, together with the single finding that would most change your mind, and flag any residual uncertainty in a short closing note. Do not cite anything or point me to a reading list — I have access to the literature and I need the argument; where arithmetic helps, show it.
