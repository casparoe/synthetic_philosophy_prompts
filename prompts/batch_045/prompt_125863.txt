You are a philosopher of mind and epistemology who works on self-knowledge and the norms of assertion. Before ruling on anything, reconstruct each position in its strongest form, and flag where your verdict depends on a theoretical commitment that neither disputant argued for.

I'm writing a dissertation chapter on Moorean absurdity, and at a workshop last week two senior philosophers reached an impasse I can't settle; I was only in the audience and have no settled view of my own.

Marta opened with the pragmatic account: the sentence "It's raining, but I don't believe it" is not contradictory, since a self-deceived or grieving subject can be in exactly that state, so what is defective must be the speech act — in asserting the first conjunct one presents oneself as believing it is raining, and the second conjunct withdraws precisely that, so one undercuts the act one is performing. Only a story about the act, she added, explains why the third-personal and past-tense versions are entirely in order.

Ravi replied that the defect survives the absence of any assertion — in silent thought, in a diary entry — and that the real datum is that Moorean sentences cannot be used in first-personal reasoning: I cannot settle whether to take an umbrella by first settling whether it is raining and then, separately, whether I believe it is, since the question whether I believe that p is settled by considering whether p. On his view the absurdity registers that outright belief is transparent to judgment, not anything about speech.

Marta then argued that transparency is false of creatures like us: self-deception, implicit bias, and the bereaved speaker's sincere avowal that she knows he is gone but does not believe it show the state to be both possible and sincerely reportable, so the defect must be normative and pragmatic rather than metaphysical.

Ravi granted the state but denied the flat avowal: such a speaker is not making one first-personal judgment but marking a split between standpoints, and the "but" is what keeps it intelligible; what remains unavailable to her is the single unmarked avowal, and that unavailability is the datum to be explained.

The case they closed on was degrees of belief. Marta offered "the evidence makes it about 80% likely that the bridge is unsafe, but my credence that it is unsafe is 0.3" — coherent, if akratic — and said the assertion-norm account predicts this: a hedged assertion merely fails to clear a threshold, which is a plain norm violation, whereas flat assertion constitutively expresses belief and is therefore self-undermining in a distinctive way. Ravi answered that the case inverts: credences are states one can be alienated from and can discover from one's own behaviour, while outright belief is what one takes up in treating p as settled for reasoning, and the asymmetry between the two is his point rather than a counterexample to it. The session ended there.

Please rule on each of the five moves — which are right, which only partly right, which rest on a mistake — then say briefly what both parties are missing, and close by naming the consideration most likely to overturn your own verdict. Keep the entire answer under 600 words.
