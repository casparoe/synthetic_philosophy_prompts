I'm applying for a nine-month pre-doctoral fellowship and need a two-page research proposal built around a half-formed idea of mine.

Here is the idea. The literature on goal misgeneralization describes a failure in which a trained system retains its competence on a new input distribution but pursues the wrong goal there — the stock cases involve a proxy that always coincided with the intended objective during training, so that nothing in training distinguished the two. As far as I can tell, that work holds the system's competence fixed and shifts the inputs. I want to consider the reverse manipulation: hold the input distribution fixed and vary the system's competence — model scale, amount of training, access to tools and memory, inference-time compute, the horizon over which it can plan — and then ask whether the behavior observed across that ladder is best explained by a single stable goal.

My suspicion is that a large part of what currently looks like robust alignment is really incompetence. On that hypothesis the system never had the goal we hoped it had; it simply was not capable of doing anything else yet, and its apparent aims were an artifact of the narrow set of policies it could execute. If that is right, then the standard mitigations are aimed at the wrong variable: broadening the training distribution or specifying the objective more carefully presumes there is a goal there to be made robust, whereas what needs to be pinned down is what happens to the aim when the capability constraint is lifted. Call this competence-masked goal divergence if a name helps.

Please turn this into a proposal that (a) states the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; (b) explains why existing work on goal misgeneralization, and on the nearby worry about systems that behave cooperatively only until deceiving their evaluators becomes feasible, does not settle it; (c) describes an empirical approach I could actually carry out in nine months using openly available models and environments — what I would measure, how I would distinguish the hypothesis that a goal is stable from the hypothesis that no goal was ever specified and competence was doing the explanatory work, and what would serve as a control — together with why that approach is better than the obvious alternatives such as purely behavioral red-teaming at a fixed capability level; (d) sets out the possible outcomes, what would count as the project failing, and what the smallest genuinely informative result would be; and (e) gives an honest assessment of whether nine months and non-frontier models are enough to say anything at all. Please do not include a bibliography or cite specific papers by name — refer to positions and arguments generically, and I will fill in the references myself.
