I chair the Collections and Repatriation Committee of the board of trustees of the Alden Museum of World Art, a mid-sized independent art museum in a mid-sized American city, supported by endowment income, admissions, and a small municipal grant. At the full board's meeting in three weeks we vote on what to do with our Benin holdings, and I am expected to circulate a recommendation memo one week ahead of that vote. I would like you to draft it.

The holdings, and the facts as we currently have them:

Nine objects — commemorative cast-brass heads and palace plaques — are documented as having been removed by British forces from Benin City during the punitive expedition of the 1890s and bought by our founder at a London auction in 1902. Six further objects were purchased in 1911 from the estate of a colonial administrator; we know nothing about how he obtained them, and it is an open question whether they are part of the same removal or were acquired by some less violent route. Four objects come, on our own curators' reading of the iconography, from a shrine in a town some hundred and fifty miles from Benin City that Benin forces sacked in the earlier nineteenth century, the regalia being carried back to the capital; a descendant association of that town has written to us to claim them, on the ground that the Kingdom of Benin was itself an appropriator and cannot be the beneficiary of a restitution claim.

Four parties are before us. The court of the Oba, the hereditary ruler, asks for return to the palace and asserts that the commemorative heads are not property of a transferable kind at all but ritual apparatus that cannot be alienated, so that even a lawful purchase would be void and their present display as sculpture is not the residue of a past wrong but a continuing one. The elected state government has passed an edict vesting antiquities in the state and proposes a new museum in Benin City that does not yet exist as a building. The federal antiquities authority asserts title on behalf of the nation and has floated a compromise: title stays with us and the objects alternate every three years between our gallery and theirs. Finally, an association of families of Edo descent in our own city has asked us not to return anything, arguing that the objects now belong to the history of the city the diaspora actually lives in, and that removal to West Africa would sever them from the descendants of the people whose displacement produced the diaspora.

Two constraints. The 1902 purchases were funded under the founding bequest, whose terms require objects acquired with that money to remain on display in this city in perpetuity and forbid deaccessioning; permanent return would require the charity regulator to approve a departure from the trust, and we have no assurance it would. The 1911 purchases were made with unrestricted funds. Separately, the Benin gallery is our single largest draw, and the founder's family has told us in writing that a pledged eight-figure bequest will be withdrawn if any object leaves permanently.

What I want from the memo:

A bottom line the board can actually vote on, given our legal powers — a disposition for each of the three groups separately, with a timetable, and an explicit statement of what we should do if the regulator refuses to permit transfer of the bequest-funded objects (petition, seek a modified trust, or return only what is ours to return).

A statement of the principle you are relying on, and an argument for it rather than for its rivals: whether the claim here is best understood as repair of a historical taking, as present-tense recognition of a political relationship, as a consequence of the inalienability of certain kinds of object, or as a question about who is entitled to speak for a community — and why the alternatives fail, in particular why the inalienability framing does or does not survive the case of the shrine objects taken by Benin forces before the British arrived.

A position on the rotating-loan offer. Our curators call it a fig leaf that treats inalienable objects as alienable while adding conservation risk and recurring freight and insurance cost; several trustees think it is the only honest compromise available. Say which is right and why.

Then the hardest thing, and the part I expect the board to press me on: give the strongest version of the case against your own recommendation, and treat it seriously rather than gesturing at it. If you recommend return, that means engaging the worry that we would be handing objects to an unelected court or to a state authority that is not accountable to the people on whose behalf the claim is made, that the federal authority's storage has had a documented failure, and that we would be resolving a contested question of representation in favour of whichever claimant wrote to us most. If you recommend retention or delay, it means engaging the worry that "we need more time and better provenance research" is what a self-interested institution would say, and saying what would have to be true for delay to be more than that.

Say plainly how much weight the attendance figures and the pledged bequest should carry, and how a memo can register them without looking like a document written to protect the museum's balance sheet.

Format it as a real memo, up to about 1,200 words: recommendation first, then findings, then reasons, then the strongest objection, then the concrete steps with dates. No citations or bibliography — our counsel will add whatever legal footnotes are needed.
