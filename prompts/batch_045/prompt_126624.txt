I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy with a maths background (measure theory, a bit of operator algebras, no real physics past undergrad QM) and I've got roughly two years before I have to commit to a dissertation topic. I want to spend them on the Born rule problem in the Everettian picture, but I can't work out which thread to pull, and I'd rather sort that out now than two years in.

The way I've been carving it up: (1) the "what even is probability here" worry — every outcome happens somewhere, so what's left for probability to be about; (2) the quantitative worry — grant that there's something for it to be about, and why amplitude squared, rather than amplitude to the fourth, or branch count, or anything else; (3) the branch or set-selection problem — probabilities have to attach to branches, and the formalism doesn't hand you a unique way to carve the universal state into them, so which carving and why; (4) the technical bit — most derivations I've seen are run in finite dimensions on tidy equal-amplitude cases and then waved onward to continuum spectra and unbounded state spaces, and I want to know whether that generalisation step is load bearing or just bookkeeping. There's also a fifth thing people keep telling me matters, which is that branching isn't global once you take relativity seriously, so there's no fact of the matter about when you branch and self-locating uncertainty has nowhere to locate you — I suspect that one's a rabbit hole, but tell me if I'm wrong.

Then there's something a friend said in a reading group that I keep turning over and can't decide is deep or confused. Her distinction: call a derivation credence-first if it starts from constraints on what a rational agent who knows she's in a branching universe should believe, and recovers the squared amplitude as the betting odds she's rationally required to use. Call it mass-first if it starts by arguing that branches literally carry some physical quantity — thickness, weight, measure, whatever you want to call it — and then argues credences should track it, the way credences about a coin track its bias. Her claim is that basically every extant derivation is credence-first in disguise, that credence-first derivations need a uniformity or indifference assumption about how to spread credence across branches at exactly the point where the answer is supposed to come out, and so the real gap is one step upstream of where everyone's been digging: not "why squared" but "what is the stuff that gets distributed." If she's right then (2) is downstream of (3) and of some theory of branch mass, and I should be working there. If she's wrong I want to know where exactly the circularity charge fails.

So: given about two years, and given what I bring to it, how would you order these — tell me if I've carved them wrong — and what are you ordering them by? Which are upstream of which, which are tractable for someone who isn't going to derive anything from QFT, which should I drop because the answer wouldn't change much downstream anyway, and what — a formal result, a distinction, an experiment looking for higher-order interference, whatever — would move something up or down the list in the next two years. Don't give me a reading list and don't bother with citations, I've got plenty of both; what I want is the argument about what's worth the years.
