I'm finishing a feature — working title, "Why simple?" — on the instinct scientists have for the simplest curve through the data, and on whether that instinct has ever been more than a taste. The same sentence keeps coming back at me from the machine-learning researchers I've been interviewing, usually in the last five minutes of a call when they get ambitious: that Solomonoff induction is just Occam's razor with the hand-waving removed, so that when we prefer the simpler hypothesis we aren't expressing an aesthetic preference or a pragmatic rule of thumb but doing something as objective as updating a prior. The move is load-bearing in my reporting. It is how one researcher gets from "our system compresses this data better than the alternative" to "our system has latched onto the structure that actually generates the data, so the usual worry about over-parameterized models memorizing noise has been answered in advance." I need to know how much of that bridge the comparison can carry.

So audit it for me. Set the correspondence out explicitly — a table is fine — saying what plays the role of the hypothesis, the data, the razor, the prior, and the verdict, and then where the mapping fails. Three places I suspect it fails, where I'd like you to be specific rather than diplomatic. First: the razor scientists actually wield prefers theories with fewer free parameters, or more symmetry, or that unify two phenomena that looked separate, and I can't see why any of those should be shorter to describe on a universal Turing machine; elegance and brevity in code strike me as different properties, and a theory can be clumsy to state and still be the one with three parameters. Second, a philosopher I spoke to pressed the machine-dependence point hard: by the invariance theorem the choice of reference machine shifts complexities only by a constant, but that constant is unbounded, so for any finite body of evidence and any two hypotheses you like there is some machine that reverses their order — and the standard reply that this washes out asymptotically seems to lean on a limit nobody ever reaches. She also pointed out that you can always shorten a theory by gerrymandering the formalism: bake the data into the notation, pick units and coordinates so the constants come out round, choose a language in which your pet hypothesis is one word. My interviewee's answer was that the invariance theorem bounds the gerrymander too. Is that fair, and if it is, what survives of the claim that simplicity, here, is the objective language-independent quantity being sold to me? Third: almost all short programs look like noise, and the convergence result I was handed is about total expected error across an unending run of predictions, not about ever being in a position to say which hypothesis is true.

Two more things would make this usable for the piece. Some of my interviewees want the comparison to license a further step: that a model which compresses well has thereby done what a good scientist does, that prediction and understanding are one achievement. Say plainly whether anything in the Solomonoff setup supports that, because I think that is where the article either holds together or doesn't. And if the comparison can't bear the weight they're putting on it, give me one that can — I'd rather end on something honest than on a demolition. Please don't just announce that the disanalogies matter for the intended use; tell me which ones actually sink the particular conclusion I've described and which are merely things a philosopher would wince at. My editor won't run citations or a further-reading box, so I need the argument itself rather than pointers to it, and my readers have never heard of Kolmogorov complexity, so draw every distinction in plain words before you reach for notation.
