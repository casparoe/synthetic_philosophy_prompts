I'm writing my term paper for a philosophy of science course on Popper's evolutionary epistemology, and I want to lean on the Darwinian analogy that runs through it: tentative theories play the part of variations, error elimination plays the part of natural selection, and theories themselves are the organisms. My supervisor's objection is that the analogy is carrying the whole argument, and I need to find out how much it can actually bear.

I am using it for two claims. (i) The growth of knowledge is a selection process rather than a directed one, so inquiry cannot be steered from above — the analogue of not being able to choose which mutations arise. (ii) Because the selecting is done by something independent of what gets selected, survival confers on a theory something more than mere survival — the analogue of fitness tracking a genuine fit to the environment. Please audit the analogy: set out the correspondence explicitly (a table is fine) — organism, variation, inheritance, fitness, environment, generation, and whatever plays the gene — then the disanalogies, sorted into those that bite on my two claims and those that are merely cosmetic. The cases I cannot settle myself:

- Inheritance. Inheritance under the modern synthesis is Weismannian: what an organism acquires is not passed on. But what a theory acquires in the course of testing — its reformulations, the auxiliary hypotheses bolted on to save it — is precisely what gets passed on. Is that a different setting of a parameter, or does it put the analogy on the far side of Lamarck?
- Opting out of selection. An organism cannot make itself un-selectable; a theory can be made effectively immune to refutation, and lowering testability seems to raise survival. If fitness can be improved by hiding from the environment, what is left of (ii)?
- Blindness. Popper holds that conjectures are not random in the way mutations are, only uninferred, whereas the Campbell-style version of the scheme speaks of blind variation. Does the variation term have to be blind for the analogy to deliver (i), and if so am I reading Campbell into Popper?
- Niche construction and theory-ladenness. Biologists now hold that organisms partly build the selection pressures acting on them, and philosophers of science hold that observation is shaped by the theories we hold. Does this improve the analogy, or does it destroy the one feature I need from it — a selector independent of what is selected?

One last thing: can the analogy transfer scientific status in the direction I want, given that Popper's own best-known remark about Darwinism is that its central principle is closer to a metaphysical research programme than to a testable theory, a judgement he later softened? And is there a better comparison available, or does every candidate inherit the same defects?
