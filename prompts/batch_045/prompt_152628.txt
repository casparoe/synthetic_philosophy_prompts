I have been following the debate about panpsychism and the combination problem for a while now, and it has the texture of a loop. The critic says: experiences belonging to a vast number of micro-subjects cannot compose into the single unified experience of a human subject; any phenomenal bonding relation posited to do the work is either brute, in which case panpsychism has reintroduced the very emergence of consciousness from something non-conscious that was supposed to be its advantage over physicalism, or else unexplained, in which case the hard problem has simply reappeared one level down. The panpsychist replies, characteristically, with three moves: first, that this is the same structural mismatch any reductive account of consciousness faces, so it is a shared burden rather than a refutation; second, that material composition looks equally unobvious and we accept it anyway, so mystery is not disproof; third, that the versions of the view which make the macro-subject merely grounded in rather than composed of the micro-subjects, or which start from a cosmic subject and face a decombination problem instead, have relocated rather than inherited the difficulty. The critic answers that relocation is not dissolution, and the exchange restarts.

Reading it, I have started separating out what seem to me to be four distinct questions that the argument slides between without marking the transitions. One: why is there any unified macro-subject here at all, rather than just the micro-subjects? Two, which I have not seen isolated under its own label: given that a brain at a time contains an enormous number of overlapping collections of micro-subjects, why is there exactly one subject for this system rather than a bloated multiplicity of coincident subjects of different sizes and shapes, or none for some subsystems that look equally eligible? Three: why does whichever subject exists have the qualitative character and phenomenal structure it has, given the character of the micro-experiences? Four, my own addition: whatever relation fuses micro-subjects synchronically should in principle fuse the successive temporal stages of a single subject as well, and if panpsychists block that consequence by requiring a specific synchronic unifying mechanism, they have made a metaphysical composition relation hostage to an empirical condition, which seems worth noticing.

My hypothesis is that the impasse is partly a difference about what an explanation of unity would have to do — the critic wanting a metaphysical grounding story, the panpsychist offering something closer to a lawful correlation of the kind we accept elsewhere in science — and partly an unmarked disagreement about which of these sub-problems is load-bearing, with the second one doing all the work while the third is really a separate problem that physicalism inherits too.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey of the positions. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what composition or subject amounts to, or two questions being pursued under one name? Is the analogy with material composition clarifying or corrupting? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: an axiomatised phenomenal mereology with models, an explosion result showing that no non-trivial composition principle avoids the multiplicity worry, empirical constraints on unity from split-brain or dissociative cases, a change of question, or nothing at all? Please mark how confident you are at each step, distinguish what follows from the structure of the dispute from what is only your sense of the dialectic, and say so plainly if my four-way taxonomy is doing work it cannot bear — if the sub-problems collapse into one another, or if the selection problem is just the old subject-summing problem renamed, I would rather be told that than have it used politely.
