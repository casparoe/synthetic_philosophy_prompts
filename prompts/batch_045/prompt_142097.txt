I'm a retired field biologist — 35 years of it, mostly plant ecology — and I've lately got into philosophy of science. I run a small discussion group at the local library: 15-18 people, mostly other retirees, 90 minutes, one whiteboard, and nobody there has done any philosophy. Next month's session is on whether discoveries are inevitable, the Newton/Leibniz and Darwin/Wallace kind of situation, and I really don't want to just tell the stories and tabulate pros and cons on the board.

Can you design an activity where they run into the problem before it's named? Two things I want them to feel rather than hear. First, the pull of the time was ripe line: given the same shared pile of evidence, people working independently really do converge on the same idea, and that's a bit unnerving about credit. Second, something I've been chewing on and don't have a name for: even when the finding itself is overdetermined, the packaging isn't. Fluxions vs. differentials, or the shape natural selection would have taken if Wallace's version had set the agenda — whoever is first doesn't just get the credit, they decide which formulation the next fifty years inherits. So the distinction I care about is replaceable finder, irreplaceable formulation, and I'd like the exercise to stage that somehow rather than have me assert it.

Ideally credit should actually get handed out in the room — a vote or some small token — so that whether it matters who was first isn't an abstract question. No real money and nothing that needs preparation beyond what I can carry in a bag. Give me rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the two points above. The famous historical cases shouldn't come up until afterwards. And be honest about what will go wrong the first time I run it, plus which part of the idea the game just can't produce and I'll have to say out loud myself.
