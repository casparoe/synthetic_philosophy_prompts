Two friends of mine got into it at dinner, I promised to stay out of it, and now they've both asked me to relay the verdict, so here I am. Nadia's been into the modern Stoicism thing for a couple of years — podcasts, the control-what-you-can-control mugs, the works — and Theo read some of the ancient material in college and thinks the modern version is a corruption of it.

(1) Nadia: the dichotomy of control is just cognitive hygiene. Sort stuff into what responds to your effort and what doesn't, put your energy in the first pile, stop burning yourself up over the second. Its basically what a CBT worksheet tells you to do, and it works.
(2) Theo: that's a different claim wearing the same name. For Epictetus the point wasn't energy management, it was that the only thing that can be genuinely good or bad is your own judgment and character, and that everything else — health, money, reputation — belongs to the class the Stoics called indifferents. The calm is downstream of that claim about value. Modern Stoicism kept the calm and deleted the premise that was generating it.
(3) Nadia: the premise is the part that's obviously false. A sick kid is bad, full stop, and it isn't up to anybody, so dropping it is an improvement, not a corruption. Also, "up to me" is doing two different jobs, and the strict version picks the wrong one: a panic attack or a short temper isn't something I can switch off by deciding, so causally it isn't up to me — but everybody, ancient Stoics included, treats working on it as precisely my job. So what we really mean is closer to "this is mine to answer for," which is a responsibility notion, not a control notion.
(4) Theo: then the technique has no engine left. "It's not up to me" only settles you down if you also accept that what isn't up to you can't be bad in the way that matters; otherwise the bad thing is still bad, you just didn't cause it. And your responsibility reading makes it worse, since responsibility doesn't stop at the effort boundary — I'm answerable for outcomes I couldve foreseen. As for athletes repeating "control the controllables," that helps because worry tanks performance, which is a contingent fact about attention, not a philosophical one.
