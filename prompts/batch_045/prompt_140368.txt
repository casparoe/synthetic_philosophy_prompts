I host a very small philosophy podcast — me, a friend, and about three hundred patient listeners — and next week's guest is a philosopher who's just published a book arguing that the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism has been badly overrated. Her version, as I understand it: the objection only really bites against ex post tradeoffs, the move where you tell the loser that their loss is fine because the total went up; it dosn't touch ex ante aggregation, where you choose institutions by maximising each person's expected well-being from behind a veil of ignorance and everyone gets equal and separate weight; and its real content is a complaint about imposing unconsented risk on identifiable people, not about trading well-being as such. She also has a tu quoque she clearly enjoys: maximin and the difference principle aggregate too, so the Rawlsian can't lean on the objection either.

The reason I don't want to wing this one is personal. I joined my hospital's clinical ethics committee about four months ago as the lay member, and we keep running straight into this. Last month we spent an hour on whether to move a chunk of nursing time off the palliative ward and onto the stroke pathway — the numbers clearly favour the switch, but the paitents who'd lose out are named people on the ward right now, while the people who'd gain are statistical. I found myself wanting to say something Rawlsian and being completely unable to say what. I'm definately out of my depth on the theory.

So: draft me eight to ten interview questions, and for each one a note on the answer she's most likely to give plus a follow-up that presses on exactly that answer. I want at least one question on each of these:

- whether the ex ante move really answers the complaint or just relocates it — the person still ends up worse off, so what does it do for them that the choice was made earlier and under uncertainty;
- whether the single impartial chooser behind the veil is itself the conflation of many people into one that the objection was about, or whether it's only a calculating device that smuggles nothing in;
- the analogue inside a single life: prudence lets me trade my own good now against my own good later, and presumably lets me be compensated within a life in a way I can't be compensated across lives. Ask her what's doing that work — psychological continuity, ownership of a future, something else — and what happens to it if you go reductionist about personal identity so that the unity of a life is itself a matter of degree;
- her maximin tu quoque, and whether the Rawlsian has a real reply in the thought that comparing the positions of representative people isn't the same thing as summing their gains and losses;
- whether her view is secretly just prioritarianism, or just contractualism, in a costume — and if it's a hybrid, which half is doing the work in the hard cases;
- the numbers: if what's wrong is unconsented risk imposition rather than aggregation per se, does her view still get to save the many rather than the one, and does it give the answer most people actually have when it's one identifiable victim versus a large number of statistical lives;
- my committee case, or a cleaned-up version of it, where I want her to commit to something concrete rather than restate the framework.

End with one question about what would count against the view — what finding or argument would make her give it up — and give me the follow-up for when she replies with something like 'that's just a fact about how people feel, not an argument.'

Constraints: no 'what got you into this' filler, and no question that just lets her restate her own position for five minutes. Phrase the questions so a listener who's never read any of this can follow them; I'm happy to say technical terms out loud but I can't spend two minutes defining each one.
