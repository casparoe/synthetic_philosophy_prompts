You are a professional philosopher who works on decision theory and formal epistemology. Please think carefully and set out your reasoning: before you rule on anything, state in your own words what this result actually says and which of its assumptions are load-bearing, since the whole dispute turns on where those assumptions fail.

I have no academic training; I've been reading about this on my own. Two members of a reading group I sit in on got into an argument I couldn't settle, about something I've seen called Good's theorem: the claim that a fully Bayesian agent can never expect to be made worse off by information that costs nothing, because she always retains the option of acting exactly as she would have acted had she not received it.

Jonah's opening position: it's a theorem, so it cannot be false given its premises; the only real question is where the premises break. Most alleged counterexamples, he said, are cases where the information wasn't really free, or where receiving it changed which options were available.

Miriam conceded that much but said the load-bearing premise is the one about being able to ignore what you learn, and that this is precisely what usually fails. Her example was a judge or jury exposed to evidence that is then struck from the record, with an instruction to disregard it: the legal system's own practice assumes that un-knowing is not really available.

Jonah replied that this misdiagnoses the case. If the law obliges you to disclose what you know, or if a decision-maker cannot help being influenced, then the model was too sparse: the knowledge, together with its psychological and legal downstream effects, belongs in the description of the consequence. Put it in the consequence space and the result holds again.

Miriam said that maneuver makes the claim unfalsifiable — every awkward case gets absorbed by redescribing the outcome — so it does less work than he thinks. And she offered a case she said survives it: the information is genuinely free, she can act on it, she responds to it perfectly rationally, and she is still worse off, because what harms her is the record of her having gone looking. The damage comes from being seen to inquire, not from what she learns.

Jonah said that then the observation simply wasn't free — the trace it leaves is part of what you acquire — though he granted that these traces are where the interesting action is.

Miriam then made what she called her strongest move: ignorance can protect you from your own future irrationality. If she learns the name and face of the single person her policy choice will harm, she will then make the choice she herself would call worse, and she knows this in advance; she would prefer to choose from behind a veil. Here the information costs nothing, she is free to act on it or not, and she is still worse off for having it.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: rate each move as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and flag anywhere that one of them is helping themselves to something they haven't argued for. Then say who is more right overall and what both of them are missing — in particular whether anything here threatens the result itself as opposed to its application, and whether the distinction between expected value beforehand and how things turn out afterwards settles any part of the disagreement.
