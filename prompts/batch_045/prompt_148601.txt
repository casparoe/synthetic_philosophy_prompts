You are a philosopher of property who is also fluent in museum and archival practice — someone who knows what a registrar actually does all day and who writes for readers who think in accession numbers. Work carefully and show your preparation: before you draft the answer, spend a short scratch section listing the mappings you considered for each theory and crossing out the ones that only look like translations but quietly import something my vocabulary does not contain.

I have been registrar at a small regional museum for sixteen years, and the vocabulary I genuinely work in is the vocabulary of provenance: chain of title and the gaps in it, accession and deaccession, custodianship as something different from ownership, original versus reproduction, orphan works, inalienable holdings, and claims for return. Last spring that vocabulary ran out on me.

The case is a bequest of eleven boxes from a textile workshop that operated here from roughly 1948 to 1963 — call it the Halvorsen material. It contains working drawings and pattern sheets in at least three identifiable hands, with overlapping marginal annotations, plus a family of motifs that local weavers say descends from designs older than the workshop by a century or more. A commercially published pattern book from 1961 reproduces around forty of the sheets, rescaled and recoloured, with no attribution to anyone. There is a living granddaughter of the workshop's founder who says the museum must not publish the scans. There is a weavers' association whose claim is not about money and, as far as I can tell, not about copies either. And there is a digitisation grant that requires us to release the scans under an open licence within eighteen months.

What I need is a translation. Take four accounts of intellectual property that I keep meeting in the reading I have been doing since this started — the labour-desert account in the Lockean tradition, including its proviso that others be left enough and as good; the personhood account on which a work is an extension of its author and some rights in it are therefore not alienable; the instrumentalist account on which protection is a temporary statutory bargain struck to elicit future production rather than a property right at all; and the sceptical account on which ideas and patterns are not the sort of thing that can be owned because one person's use does not diminish another's. Restate each of them, as faithfully as you can, in my vocabulary — provenance, chain of title, custodianship, original and reproduction, inalienability, return. Where authorship is concerned, use the attribution vocabulary I actually reach for: autograph, workshop of, after, circle of, follower of — a graded vocabulary, unlike the binary legal one.

For each of the four, tell me what it says about four questions: whether anyone owns the sheets; whether anyone can own the pattern as opposed to the paper it is drawn on; what the 1961 book did; and what I should do in the eighteen months I have.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. First, what in these four accounts has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, and is each such gap a defect in how registrars talk or a sign that the philosopher's claim was less clear than it looked? I suspect at least three: the backward-looking orientation of provenance against the forward-looking character of the incentive bargain, the abundance claim that makes non-rivalry central, and the proviso's question about what is left for others. Second, and more interesting to me: what does my vocabulary contain that none of the four accounts can say — inalienability, custodianship without ownership, a gap in provenance treated as a stable status rather than a failure, a claim for return that is neither a claim for money nor a claim to control copies? Say whether that residue is noise or whether it points at something the philosophical accounts are missing about ownership itself.

Be committed rather than even-handed. Rank the four accounts by how well they handle the Halvorsen material, tell me which one you would actually rely on and why, tell me what you would publish and what you would withhold, and say plainly where two of them are not really disagreeing because the dispute is about words. Attach a confidence level to each ranking and to your recommendation, and mark the specific places where your answer depends on a detail of law or of workshop history that you are not certain about, so I know what to check with counsel. Do not hedge everything: I would rather have a definite recommendation with its weak joints exposed than a survey of the options.
