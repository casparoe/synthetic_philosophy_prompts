Hey — I run this very low-stakes lunch-and-learn thing at my company, once a month, 45 minutes, usually 8–10 backend and infra people in a room with a whiteboard and no projector. Everyone has the kind of quantitative intuition you'd expect from engineers but bascially nobody has taken a philosophy class, and last week in Slack someone dropped Pascal's mugging into an argument about whether we should buy insurance on a weird tail risk in our deployment pipeline, and it deraled the whole thread. I volunteered to do it as next month's topic. Please don't explain the problem to me — I've read enough to know the rough shape of it — what I want is a plan for how to *teach* it to this specific room.

The thing I keep tripping over is ordering. My instinct is to open with the hoodie guy who offers to spare some astronomically large number of simulated people in exchange for five dollars, but I think that's a bad opener for this crowd: the sci-fi framing makes them treat it as a joke, and "magic powers from outside the simulation" basically invites the escape hatch of setting the probability at zero. So: what has to be in place *before* the mugger shows up? My guess is that something like "expected value is a tool you should actually follow when the stakes get large" has to be agreed to first, otherwise nobody feels the pull at all and the session is forty minutes of smugness. And then what is the one case that should carry the whole thing, and what does it have to have and lack? I've been playing with a version where the mugger first does something small and verifiable — reads off a long random string you generated on your laptop two minutes earlier, no network involved — and only then makes the astronomical offer, and then a second mugger shows up offering more than the first, and a third. I like it because it seems to shut the two escapes my coworkers will definitely reach for (the probability is effectively zero; and fine, I'd pay the first mugger, so what?), but I'm worried the ladder does something illicit, like letting me scale the payoff for free and smuggle in the conclusion. Tell me honestly if that's cheating and what the non-cheating version looks like.

Then the misconception map, with a position in the sequence for each, since I suspect the order matters more than the answers. The ones I'm expecting: (a) "this is just Pascal's Wager with extra steps," which could turn into a twenty-minute derailment about religion if I don't place it right; (b) "expected value literally says pay, so refusing is irrational" — which I think is the thing I want them to *feel* rather than something to correct, and I'm not sure how to make it land as a problem and not a gotcha; (c) "it's solved, just cap your utility function / refuse to let utility grow without bound as the number of people grows," which is the reply I most want them to generate themselves instead of me handing it to them, and I have no idea how to set the room up for that; (d) someone will say "so expected value is just the wrong tool" and I don't know whether that's the same reply as (c) or a different and worse one. Also tell me what I should flat-out refuse to explain in 45 minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing (I'm guessing: anything with infinities, the Saint Petersburg stuff, formal results about "fanaticism", whatever decision theories have names in capital letters). And give me one question to end on that would show me they'd understood the actual problem rather than just memorized a rebuttal — the win condition is that they're still arguing about it in the Slack channel on Monday and at least a couple of them are a bit unsettled about their own expected-value habits.
