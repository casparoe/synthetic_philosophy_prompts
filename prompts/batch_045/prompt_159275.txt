My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Dennett's ladder of creature-kinds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian — after a paper we read for our reading group on animal cognition, and we got to the point of restating our positions at each other rather than advancing them, so I'd like a careful explanation of what is actually at issue rather than a summary of the taxonomy (which I can get from the book) or a verdict on who won.

The crux was the Popperian tier. As I understand it, what is supposed to distinguish a Popperian creature from a merely Skinnerian one is that it can eliminate candidate actions in an inner environment before acting, so that the bad options are discarded without the creature's having to try them in the world and bear the cost — the slogan usually attached to this tier is that a hypothesis dies in the creature's stead. My friend's objection, which I could not answer, is that this cannot be the mark of the transition, because plenty of creatures we would happily call Skinnerian avoid the fatal trial without anything that looks like foresight: a rat that samples a novel food cautiously and stops after one bad experience, an animal with strong innate neophobia, a youngster that freezes rather than investigates a predator-shaped silhouette. In each case the organism declines to run the lethal experiment, and it does so by means of a cheap, general-purpose disposition installed in advance rather than by simulating this particular situation. When I replied that the difference is that the Popperian creature represents the outcome of an action it does not perform, she accused me of helping myself to exactly the notion — inner representation — that the tier was supposed to explain, and I think she is half right. So: what, if anything, separates eliminating an action because one has modelled its outcome from eliminating an action because one has a standing or learnt disposition against that whole class of actions? Can the distinction be drawn non-circularly — in terms of what the creature is sensitive to (features of this situation versus features of a class), in terms of the informational economy of the system, in terms of what the creature can subsequently do with the rejected option — or does it really bottom out in a claim about representational content? And a related worry: is Popperian-ness one competence or two, the off-line generation of candidate actions and the off-line evaluation of them, and could a creature plausibly have one without the other?

The second disagreement, which I suspect bears on the first, is whether the ladder is a ladder. The Gregorian creature, on my reading, is a Popperian creature whose hypothesis space has been reorganised by inherited thinking tools — words, numerals, maps, diagrams — which raises the question of whether the higher tiers modify the lower ones rather than sitting neatly on top of them. My friend wants a fifth kind, which she calls a Baldwinian creature: one whose acquired practices feed back into the selection pressures on its own innate architecture, so that what began as a culturally transmitted trick becomes, over generations, part of the creature's Darwinian equipment — the Baldwin effect applied to the animal that does the learning rather than to the learning itself. She claims this makes the scheme circular rather than cumulative, because the top tier reaches back down and rewires the bottom one, and that the four kinds are better read as four sources of design than as four grades of mind. I half agree: I think the circularity is real, but I do not think it touches the Popperian question, since even a thoroughly retooled brain still has to solve the problem of not dying whilst it learns. Please explain what would settle this: whether the Baldwinian kind, as she defines it, is a genuine fifth tier, a degenerate case of the Gregorian tier, or something belonging outside the taxonomy altogether; whether accepting it changes what the Popperian tier is for; and where, if anywhere, my friend and I are each getting something right.
