I sit on the renewal panel for a mid-sized national research council. We fund whole groups, not single projects — five-year programmatic grants, renewable once, and the money is big enough that turning off the tap basically ends the group. We get an afternoon per renewal. And we've recently managed to get it wrong in both directions: we renewed a group that is now four years and zero real results deep, and we axed one that most people now think was about eighteen months from landing something. So I want a procedure, not vibes.

The thing I half-remember from a reading group years ago is that Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes is meant to be exactly the tool for this kind of call — hard core, protective belt, progressive versus degenerating problem shifts, novel facts, the idea that a programme is allowed to be wrong for a good while as long as it keeps turning up something new it didn't set out to explain. Please turn that into a checklist I could hand to a working scientist who has never opened a philosophy book: questions in order, what counts as a yes at each step, where the procedure says stop-and-terminate, where it says stop-and-renew, and — this bit matters as much as the rest — where it has to stop and hand the call to us because the question is one of values and not of fact. Plain language on the front page, plus a short mapping back to the Lakatosian terms at the end so I can defend it to the two philosophers on the panel.

Four things it has to handle, because these are the actual arguments we have in the room:

1. It must not reduce to "count the novel predictions." The case that keeps wrecking us is a programme that looks empirically flat but is flat because the instrument it needs hasn't been built yet, versus a programme that is flat because its belt has quietly become a machine for absorbing whatever happens. Those should come apart, and I want to know which question does the separating — and what to do when the honest answer to that question is "we can't tell yet."

2. There's a pattern I have no name for and that I want built in. The group's central commitment gets restated in a milder form at every review, so nothing could have contradicted it in the first place, and the restatement is always presented as deepening understanding — "we now see the mechanism as more general than we originally thought," that kind of thing. I've started calling it core-softening. As far as I can tell Lakatos's own machinery files any change outside the core as belt work, so it's not obvious this even registers as a distinct move, but it's the single best tell we have and our reviewers keep rewarding it. Give me a question that separates a core-softening move from a legitimate belt adjustment, and say what it does to the verdict when you catch one.

3. Related but different: some belt additions generate a genuine, confirmed, unexpected prediction, and yet the added hypothesis can only ever be checked through the programme itself — there's no independent way to get at it. I have a vague memory that Lakatos has a graded taxonomy of ad hoc-ness with three levels, where this kind is the least bad rather than the worst, but don't go hunting for references: no bibliography, no citations, just use the idea if it's right and say plainly if I've got it garbled. Either way the procedure needs a question that catches it, because this is the thing that lets a programme look healthy for six years and then turn out to have been talking to itself.

4. We only ever have the group's own write-up plus one outside referee. Nobody on the panel can read the field or rerun anything. So every question needs a stated default for "we don't know" that isn't just "then renew."

Then run it on three files, and keep each worked example tight — half a page max:

A. A group whose programme holds that a specific immune mechanism drives a family of chronic diseases. Five years, forty-odd papers, and technically every one reports a confirmed novel prediction — but each new prediction has been made in a different tissue than the one they said they were going to explain, and the flagship clinical prediction has slid to the right at every single renewal.

B. A group whose whole programme predicts a signal nobody can currently detect, because the detector is under construction and is about eighteen months from first data. Four years, no confirmed novel facts at all. They have, however, written down in advance exactly what count would vindicate them and what pattern would make them abandon the core.

C. A group whose central claim started as "mechanism M causes outcome O in all mammals," and has since become "M causes O in most mammals under metabolic stress," and is now "M is best understood as a regulatory principle that may be realized differently in different lineages." Same group, same funding line, presented each time as progress.

Tell me which of the three comes out with an answer I'm not going to like, and why.

Constraints on the deliverable: fits on two pages; usable by someone with a PhD in a relevant science and no philosophy, in half a day; no step that secretly amounts to "has it been falsified" — if a step does amount to that, flag it and say why Lakatos wouldn't want to stop there, because we already tried running the panel on naive falsificationism and it just made everyone timid. Finally, be straight with me about which parts of the procedure are judgment calls dressed up as steps, and about what the procedure systematically cannot see — I'm thinking of things like a programme that is clearly degenerating but is the only framework anyone in the field is trained in, so that killing it kills the field's capacity to ask the question at all.
