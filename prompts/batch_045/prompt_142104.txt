I've somehow ended up on the review panel for a small foundation that needs to spend down a pot of money — call it four or five postdoc-years, spread over three years — on conceptual work on the moral status of digital minds. Philosophy and theory only: no empirical ML, no evals, no interpretability tooling. They want a ranked list of what's worth working on by the end of the month, and I'd rather hand them something better than the list everyone in this space already has.

So: if you were me, what would go on it? Five or six questions, ranked, with the ranking defended. I'm less interested in the items themselves than in your reasons — for each one, why it's upstream or downstream of the others, whether it's genuinely tractable for people with philosophy training rather than for ML folks, and what would move it up or down the list. What finding, argument, or change in the landscape over the next three years would make you reorder? And what would you deliberately leave off, and why — I think I'd learn as much from that as from the list.

Some things I keep turning over, as raw material. Don't feel obliged to use any of them, but they're the sort of thing I mean:

- The epistemic problem seems weirder here than in any other case of other minds. A digital system's dispositions to report on its own states will typically have been *shaped* by optimization pressure of exactly the kind we'd want to interrogate. So a system that reliably reports not suffering might just be one that has been successfully trained to say so. That looks like it drives self-report toward worthless, but I don't think it gets all the way there, and I have no idea what replaces it.
- Identity across copying and editing. Is the candidate patient the run-token, the weight vector, the training lineage? And does it matter morally if a system's weights get copied ten thousand times, or merged back together after a fork, or rolled back to an earlier checkpoint? I keep going back and forth on whether this is the crux or a distraction.
- Cheap abundance. If a morally relevant system costs a few dollars to run and you can run a million of them, total-welfare considerations go astronomical immediately. Is that the correct and terrifying conclusion, or a reductio of running the aggregative machinery at all?
- Continuum worries. There will be systems that are 5% of the way to whatever the relevant property is, and ones that are 80% of the way, and nothing in the neighborhood of a bright line. Does status come in degrees, and if so what do we owe the partial cases?
- Sub-personal subjects. Could a component of a larger system be a welfare subject even if the whole system isn't, or vice versa?
- Pausing. Is there a welfare-relevant difference between suspending a system indefinitely and deleting it, and if not, does that tell us something important or just something about our own intuitions?

Please don't give me a literature survey or a reading list — I've read those — and assume I can't go look anything up. What I want is a defended agenda, with the trade-offs between importance, tractability, and neglectedness made explicit. Casual register is fine; this is a working document.
