For the past eighteen months I have been one of four coders on a small, unfunded democracy-observatory project that keeps panel data on institutional change in roughly thirty countries, Hungary, Poland, Turkey and the United States among them. Our manual is one we wrote ourselves, and the part of it I keep having to defend is a three-way distinction we use instead of a single backsliding flag: *capture*, where the rules themselves are rewritten so as to entrench whoever currently holds office; *erosion*, where the rules stay formally intact but the bodies meant to enforce them are defunded, packed, or simply stop acting; and *conversion*, where existing but previously dormant legal powers — a pardon power, a contempt power, an emergency statute, a prosecutorial discretion — are turned to a new purpose with no rule change at all. On top of this we apply what we call the lock-in test: a change only enters the dataset as an episode if it would still be in place, and still advantageous to its beneficiary, after that beneficiary has lost the office the change was designed to protect. The test has an awkward property I can't resolve — it is the feature that most makes the concept worth measuring, since it is what separates genuine entrenchment from ordinary hardball that the next administration simply undoes, but it also means an episode cannot in principle be confirmed until after a turnover, so every contemporaneous judgment is a forecast wearing the costume of a measurement.

Here is the proposition I want a number on. Give me your credence that, assessed as of 1 January 2036, our dataset will contain at least one *capture*-type backsliding episode for the United States — meaning that federal-level rules governing the selection of, or the constraints on, national officeholders were rewritten in a way that favoured the party doing the rewriting, and that this rewriting survived the lock-in test. I want you to pin down the reading of survival you are using rather than hedging on the word: my own view is that the genuinely contested cases will be the ones where a successor party declines to repeal a tool it has just spent a cycle denouncing, which is why I sit at about 0.45. One of my co-coders sits at 0.75 and thinks the United States is on the path Budapest travelled after the two-thirds majority rewrote its constitution, or Ankara travelled in the years after the executive-presidency referendum: the tools get built, and the next government discovers it likes them. Another sits at 0.15 and thinks our whole enterprise is laundering a partisan reflex — that backsliding is mostly a label the side that lost applies to constitutional hardball it would have applauded from the other bench, and that Warsaw's fight with its own constitutional court is the same phenomenon as a mid-century American president expanding executive power, described in a different emotional register. I don't know whether those two disagree about the trajectory, about the lock-in condition, or about whether the concept picks out anything real at all, and that is most of what I want from you.

So: a headline number, then the decomposition that produces it. I'd like rough probabilities with a one-line justification each for at least these — that major federal rule-rewriting of the relevant kind occurs in the window at all; that any such change survives a turnover of the office it protects rather than being repealed or neutralised; that it survives under a reading of survival we would in fact apply rather than one we'd rationalise after the fact; that the manual's capture/erosion/conversion distinction is stable enough that four coders would agree on which box a real American case goes in; and that backsliding, as we've operationalised it, tracks a real political kind rather than being the losing side's name for losing — with the combination shown, not asserted. Tell me which single term your headline number is most sensitive to, which of your sub-credences you would actually defend at a conference and which are gut feeling dressed as judgment, whether you think my 0.45 and my co-coders' 0.75 and 0.15 are really disagreeing about the same proposition, and finally what argument or piece of evidence would move your number the most, and where you suspect this whole line of reasoning is most likely to fail.
