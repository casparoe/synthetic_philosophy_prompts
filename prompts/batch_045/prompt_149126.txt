I am a retired chemical physicist — forty years, most of them on relaxation phenomena and the statistical mechanics of liquids near critical points — and for the past two years I have been auditing a graduate seminar in philosophy, which is the only reason I have been asked to do what I am about to describe.

Next month I am chairing the opening session of a two-day invitation-only workshop on factored cognition and deliberation. I am not a participant. The organisers wanted someone from outside the field to "frame the question," and the workshop is the first in a series funded by a bequest from an old friend of mine who believed, wrongly but endearingly, that I understood this material. I have eight minutes before the floor is handed over.

The room will be about thirty people, and it will be split. Roughly half are machine-learning researchers who take the HCH construction — a human consulting an idealised, recursively defined version of itself, delegating subquestions to fresh copies of itself at arbitrary depth and breadth — to be a device for generating training signal for a system that will end up better than any of the copies, and who are openly impatient when philosophers start treating it as a normative ideal. The other half are philosophers who take that same construction to be a candidate model of what good deliberation is: something like what a careful reasoner would do if she had unlimited time and as many helpers as she wanted. At the back of the room, because the host institute insists on it, will be four of its trustees and my wife — intelligent people with no technical background at all, who must not be made to feel that they wandered into the wrong lecture.

My role rules certain things out. I may not take a side, and I may not pretend to a settled view I do not have; I may not tell the experts what they already know, or flatter the non-experts; there are to be no jokes about mind uploading, emulations, or copies of persons, since several people present have written about those in earnest and it would read as flippant; and nothing in the talk may imply that a human being is a computation, or that people who disagree are merely nodes in someone else's tree.

What I would like the talk actually to do is put one question in front of both camps, drawn from my old field. When you coarse-grain a system near a critical point, the answer you get depends on what you integrated out, and it stops depending on it only if the transformation has a fixed point to run to. In my reading, the analogous worry for factored deliberation is that the verdict of an amplified deliberator may depend on how the top-level question was decomposed into subquestions — that two equally natural decompositions, each amplified to the same depth, need not agree, and that asking the copies at the next level down which decomposition is better simply relocates the problem rather than dissolving it. I have started calling this the decomposition-dependence problem; I do not know whether it already has a name in the literature, and I do not want the talk to imply that I coined anything. If you are confident it has a standard name, you may use it; otherwise describe it without naming it. A second, related thing I want raised as a question rather than an answer: whether the thing distributed across the nodes can be said to deliberate at all, given that no copy persists, remembers, or can be held to a commitment it made one level up.

I would also like one concrete physical example to carry the talk — the failure of naive coarse-graining near a critical point is the one I keep returning to, but if you know a cleaner one from physics or chemistry, use it instead — and I would like it to be doing real work, not illustrating.

Please write the eight minutes: roughly a thousand words, in a register appropriate to a formal opening but spoken rather than read, with bracketed stage directions where the reaction of the room matters. It should end in such a way that both camps go into the first session wanting to talk to each other rather than past each other, and that the trustees at the back feel the trip was worth making. After the text, add a short note — no more than a paragraph — flagging any place where you took a risk I might want to cut, and why.
