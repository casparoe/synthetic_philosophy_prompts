I teach a postgraduate seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, and one of my doctoral students has begun using the line "a white horse is not a horse" as a conversation-stopper. Her thesis is that the ontology of discrete, countable objects is a projection of count-noun syntax rather than a discovery; the School of Names, she claims, saw this, and the "White Horse" dialogue is the argument for it. Whenever I press her on what, precisely, the sentence is supposed to say, she replies that the dialogue's own reasoning settles it — roughly, that a request for a horse can be satisfied by a brown or a black specimen whereas a request for a white one cannot, and that in a second strand "horse" names a shape while "white" names a colour, so the two cannot be the same thing — and that I only resist because I am covertly reinstating an Indo-European count noun.

I should like to hand her a disambiguation rather than a rebuttal. Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making: at least the claim about extensions, the claim about intensions or modes of presentation, the claim about what satisfies a request, the mereological claim about parts and portions, the claim about sortals and criteria of identity, and any stronger reading you think worth isolating, such as one on which the white horse is not a horse because colour and shape are aspects or stuffs that never compose an object at all. For each, give a verdict — trivial, substantive but defensible, or false — and say what else would have to be true for that reading to be true.

Then the questions I actually care about. Which of those readings, if true, would carry her conclusion? Does the request-and-satisfaction premise discriminate between the readings, or is it neutral among them — that is, could someone who accepts objects and count nouns grant every step of it? Does the shape-and-colour premise do better, and if so, which reading does it favour? And is the move from "the extension of the one term is properly included in the other" to "there are no objects" valid without some further premise about what nouns denote?

To keep this from being an exercise in recall, please test your readings against a case I have constructed for the purpose. Imagine a language — call it Chromese — with no colour-neutral common noun for horses: it has colour-specific count nouns on the model of "white-horse", "bay-horse", "chestnut-horse", a bare mass term meaning horse-stuff, and nothing playing the role our word "horse" plays. For each reading on your menu: is the sentence formulable in Chromese at all, and if it must be rendered differently, does that alter which claim is being made? Would a Chromese speaker's ontology be more or less object-free than mine, and would that tell us anything about whether ontology is read off grammar? Does Chromese strengthen or weaken the dialogue's argument?

Finally, say which reading you think she most likely has in mind, and whether it survives. Please answer in British English, present the menu as a list with a verdict attached to each entry, and keep the whole reply under about 700 words.
