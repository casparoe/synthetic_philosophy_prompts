I spent a little over thirty years as a comparative physiologist, mostly on the energetics of locomotion in small mammals, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of biology reading group at the university. I have been asked to give a short paper laying out where I stand on the adaptationism dispute, and before I write it I would like a consistency check. What follows is the position of a working biologist who has read the critiques and does not feel refuted by them. Several people in the group insist that I am helping myself to things I have not paid for, but they disagree among themselves about where, and I cannot see it.

Here is the set, as I would present it.

(a) Adaptation is a historical notion. To call a trait an adaptation is to say that selection in fact built it, or at any rate maintained it, because of the effect it now has. That a trait is useful now, or even that it currently enhances fitness, is not by itself enough. I take myself to have learned this from the spandrels literature and I think it is right.

(b) Selection is the only process known to me that can produce complex functional fit. Drift, recombination, gene flow and developmental bias can alter form and can fix forms, but they cannot accumulate contrivance of the kind that makes an organism look as though it were engineered. At the same time I say, without embarrassment, that selection is only one cause of evolutionary change among several, and that it is the only cause of adaptation. A philosopher in the group calls the second half of that sentence an equivocation; I call it a distinction.

(c) Almost all molecular variation is neutral or nearly so, drift dominates at the level of nucleotide sequence, and sequence evolution is largely decoupled from the evolution of the phenotypes I spent my career measuring. A colleague says this is in tension with (b). I do not see it, but I am prepared to be told that I am changing the subject.

(d) Methodologically, the engineer's question comes first. One should begin by reverse-engineering the trait: build the optimality model, ask what design would best serve the function given the constraints, assume good design, and surrender that assumption only when the data force it. This is how the programme actually makes progress, and I do not think it is Panglossian to work this way if (b) is true.

(e) Convergent correlation settles the matter. When the same trait turns up in independent lineages facing the same ecological problem, I treat that as establishing adaptation, and I do not think one needs anything further — in particular I do not think we need, or can normally get, direct evidence about the selective history of any particular lineage. A younger member of the group calls the opposing worry constructional mimicry: bias in which variants arise, or in what a developmental system can readily produce, will generate exactly the same pattern of repeated fit that selection would, so the pattern cannot discriminate between the two. I think that is a real possibility in principle and empirically negligible in the cases I care about, and that insisting on it as a standing alternative makes adaptationist explanations unfalsifiable in the opposite direction.

(f) The spandrel charge only bites for functionless traits. Once a by-product has been co-opted for a current use and is maintained because of it, it is an adaptation like any other, and the burden falls on whoever wants to call it a spandrel to show that the trait does nothing now. I take this to follow from (a) together with a little charity.

(g) Gould and Lewontin's 1979 critique landed a genuine hit. A great deal of that literature was untested story-telling, that was a methodological failure rather than a merely rhetorical objection, and the right response to it is to test adaptationist hypotheses rather than to multiply them.

What I want is an audit, not a summary of the debate and not a reading list — I have a library card and do not need references. Please: (i) identify which pairs are in genuine tension and rank them by severity, rather than listing them all as equally bad; (ii) for each, say what exactly would have to give and what giving it up would cost me, both intellectually and in terms of what I could still say from the podium; (iii) say which of the apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why, since I would rather be told that two of my commitments are fine together than have every item flagged; and (iv) finish with the single cheapest revision that would clean up the most, if there is one. If you think two of my items are in tension in a way I have not paired them above, say so.

Finally, and this matters to me as much as the audit: close by telling me what would change your mind about your most severe verdict — which argument or consideration would lead you to reorder the list or drop an item from it altogether — and say where you think your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong. I would rather have a verdict with its weak point marked on it than a confident one.
