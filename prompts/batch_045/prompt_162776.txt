ok so I just had a two hour argument with my friend about eliminative materialism — the view that there aren't really any beliefs or desires, that "she believes it's raining" is on a par with "she's possessed" or "the phlogiston is escaping", i.e. it's vocabulary from a folk theory of behaviour that's a stagnating research program and that a mature neuroscience will just stop using, the way chemistry stopped using caloric. I went in skeptical and came out kind of defending it, which worries me, so: tell me what would count against it.

And I mean the real defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I already know the cheap stuff, so skip the "but I feel like I have beliefs" reply. What I want:

- Does the everyday predictive success of belief/desire reasoning actually count against it, or does the eliminativist have a good answer (the kind of point where a partly false theory can still get a lot right)? Be honest about how much weight this one bears, because it's the thing in the argument that moved me most.
- The self-referential thing. If there are no beliefs, what's going on when someone asserts that there are no beliefs? My friend said they can dodge it by saying they aren't stating a belief, they're just producing a sentence under the influence of neural states. Live escape route or word game?
- Whether the argument overgeneralizes: if it's a good argument against beliefs, doesn't an exactly parallel argument take out a bunch of other non-basic categories in the special sciences that nobody wants to give up? And does the eliminativist just have to bite that bullet?
- What would the eliminativists themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to what critics like to say counts against them. I want the version of the exposure map they'd sign off on.
- Whether the view is actually risky at all, or whether it's been insulated by qualifications — e.g. retreating from "there are no beliefs" to "beliefs are radically unlike what folk psychology says" or "elimination is a bet about the far future of neuroscience, so nothing now can test it." If it has been insulated like that, say so, and say whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just a normal feature of claims about future science.

One more thing from the argument, since its the part I keep chewing on. My friend posed this case: suppose we build an AI, or meet aliens, whose internal physical states we have complete access to — every weight, every neuron, every particle, a genuine God's eye view — and yet the only way anyone can predict or manipulate what they do is by interpreting them as having beliefs and desires, and it stays that way no matter how good our physical theory of them gets. My friend thinks that's decisive against eliminativism. I think the eliminativist can just say "fine, useful instrumental stance, still no beliefs" and nothing has changed. Who's right, and what does that tell us about whether this dispute has any empirical stakes at all?
