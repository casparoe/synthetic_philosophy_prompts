hey — I'm drafting a paper on the special composition question (when do some things compose a further thing?) and I want you to play defense attorney for a view I've been toying with. I'll call it Best-System Composition (BSC):

Some things compose a further thing iff that further thing belongs to the ontology (the way of carving the world up into objects) that figures in the best system for the world — where a system gets scored by that best-system style tradeoff between strength and simplicity in systematizing everything that happens, and where "best" is meant globally, over the world's whole history, and not relative to us and our interests.

So roughly: organisms and tables might get in because biology and the other special sciences earn their keep; the sum of my left shoe and Cleopatra's last breath doesn't, because quantifying over it buys no strength in exchange for the cost in simplicity; plain "always" loses because an ontology containing all the fusions is wildly less simple for no gain; and straight "never" is just the special case where the only system worth having turns out to be fundamental physics.

What I want from you is a straight-up argument for BSC, written as if it were a section of the paper: 1000–1400 words of prose, no bullet points except for the case list at the end. Things I specifically need handled:

- Don't frame it as "here are the four familiar answers and I pick one." Assume a reader who already knows the landscape. Argue for BSC on its own terms, and only bring in "always" / "never" / "brute and unknowable" / "only in the ordinary cases" where the contrast actually does some work.
- Objection 1 (metaphysics vs. bookkeeping): best-system-hood is an epistemic or descriptive virtue; existence isn't the sort of thing that can depend on it. Why isn't BSC just "ontology is whatever our best theory quantifies over" with an epistemic-to-ontic slide smuggled in? I want a reply that doesn't merely assert that projectibility tracks joint-carving — I havent settled on a scoring rule yet and I dont want the view to secretly depend on one.
- Objection 2 (uniqueness): there will be ties, or near-enough ties, between candidate systems. Is the view then committed to indeterminacy in composition? And if so, is that indeterminacy better or worse off than the vagueness that the usual argument from vagueness threatens restricted answers with?
- Objection 3 (coincidence): the statue and the lump both seem to earn their keep in different bits of discourse. Does BSC hand you two coincident objects there? If yes, say why that's a feature rather than the old embarrassment; if no, say which one survives and why the choice isn't arbitrary.
- Objection 4 (holism): because bestness is global and takes in the whole history of the world, whether these atoms here compose a table depends on what happens in a distant galaxy and on how the future goes. Either bite that bullet and tell me why it's ok, or block it, but do one or the other explicitly.

Then a short closing bit, 250 words tops, saying what BSC predicts about these four cases and why: (a) a table whose parts are all replaced, one per day, over a year; (b) a hurricane; (c) a cloud-shaped swarm of water droplets with fuzzy boundaries, i.e. the problem of the many; (d) the fusion of every human being alive right now. For each, flag where your answer comes apart from the "only the ordinary cases" answer and say what turns on the difference.

Please dont cite anybody or hand me a bibliography — I'll fill that in later, and I want a version I can defend from premises alone. Define any technical term you lean on. And dont hedge everything into mush: I'd much rather have a strong version with one conceded cost than an even-handed one that commits to nothing. If you think the view is dead on arrival, fine, say so at the end — but build the strongest version you can first, and only then deliver the verdict.
