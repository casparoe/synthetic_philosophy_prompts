I work as a mediator — mostly family businesses, and a steady stream of restorative-justice referrals — and over about fifteen years of brokering apologies and reconciliations I have noticed a recurring shape in these conversations for which I have never found a word. I am hoping you can tell me whether there is an established concept for it. Whatever the answer, I want the carving rather than the label: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. A bare name would not settle anything for me.

Let me give you the shape by contrast. Start with the case I take to be familiar. A senior colleague once advised a junior manager: "Tell yourself the new system is going to work — you will be far more effective if you believe it will." Everyone in the room saw the point, and also saw what was off about it: the consideration is perfectly real, it may well be effective, and yet it is not a consideration bearing on whether the thing is so; and if you try to bring the state about *for that reason*, what you get is not really that state — at best a settled policy of acting as though. I gather philosophy has things to say about this configuration. It is not quite what I am asking about, except as a baseline.

Here is what I actually want to know about. A brother who had been defrauded by his sister said, in a session: "I have decided to trust you again, because the firm will not survive if I do not, and I would rather have you in it than gone." Two things struck me. First, unlike the belief case, this works. Over some months, partly by acting as if, he arrived at something recognisably like trust toward her; it was not pretense, and it was not merely outward. Second, both of them treated the resulting state as not quite the real thing. He would say, "I don't trust you the way I used to — I have to make myself trust you," and she flinched at it: not at the word, but at the reason. And the state has a peculiar fragility. Whenever the reason was made salient — in a session, in an argument, once in front of a client — it partly dissolved, and he had to rebuild it.

I see the same thing with gratitude and with admiration, and I do not see it with forgiveness. Clients say constantly, "I forgave him because the resentment was making me ill," or "because I wanted to be free of it." Nobody flinches at that; therapists recommend it; it does not seem to leave a defective residue or to dissolve when made explicit. Yet on the face of it the structure is identical: a consideration about the goodness or usefulness of *having* the attitude, offered in place of a consideration about whether the attitude is *called for*.

So my questions. One: is there a name for what I described in the trust case — a consideration that can genuinely bring an attitude about, that is causally efficacious, that is not the right sort of consideration for it, and that leaves behind a state which is recognisably that attitude and yet compromised and unstable under reflection? Two: is this the same phenomenon as the belief case? My instinct is that it is not, or not only: in the belief case the wrong-sorted consideration seems unable to produce the state at all, while here it produces something both real and compromised, and the compromise is the sort of thing a person can be resented for. If it *is* the same phenomenon under a difference of degree, say so and say what the degree consists in. Three: why is forgiveness different? Does it have a partly self-regarding element that trust lacks, or does my sense that the forgiveness case is unproblematic rest on a mistake about what forgiveness is?

If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and give me its boundary cases — including which attitudes it does and does not cover, and why.

Two further requests. Say how confident you are about each of the three verdicts separately, and what would change your mind on each: what case or argument would show that the trust case is just the belief case plus a difference in how suggestible the attitude is; what would show that the talk of a "compromised" state is avoidable in favour of something cleaner; what would show that the forgiveness case is off-key after all. And please mark, as you go, where you are reconstructing a genuine disagreement among people who work on this rather than reporting something settled — in particular where your answer depends on a contested view of what fixes which considerations are the right sort for a given attitude. I would rather have the disagreement laid out than a confident line I cannot check.
