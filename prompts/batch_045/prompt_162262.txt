I am helping a private foundation draft the call for proposals for a single, tightly scoped research program: roughly eight researchers for three years, working mostly in formal epistemology and decision theory, with one practitioner-facing deliverable at the end. The intended users are institutional risk committees — pension-fund trustees, hospital protocol committees, national pandemic-modelling groups: bodies that must commit to a course of action while the uncertainty they reason from is, by everyone's admission, indeterminate, and that must afterwards defend that choice in public, to a regulator or a court. Please answer in American English, and please do not spend your words on a survey of the field or on suggested reading; I want the reasoning, not the map.

Take the background framework to be the familiar one in which uncertainty is represented not by a single probability function but by a convex set of them, or equivalently by lower and upper probabilities for events and lower and upper previsions for gambles, with a coherence condition playing the role that probabilistic coherence plays in the precise case. On that basis, here is my own rough inventory of where the pressure lies; correct it where it is wrong:

(i) the gap between a set of probabilities and a decision, and the rivalry between maximizing the worst case, ruling out only options that are dominated under every distribution in the set, and various compromise rules in between;
(ii) how to revise the set in light of new evidence, including the phenomenon usually called dilation, in which learning the value of an event whose own probability is unknown leaves one strictly less precise about the hypothesis of interest;
(iii) the several inequivalent notions of independence that coincide in the precise case and come apart under imprecision;
(iv) whether the set is best read as a genuine doxastic state of a single agent or as a report of what a divided evidence base, or a divided committee, permits;
(v) how to elicit interval-valued uncertainty from experts, and how to communicate it to people who will eventually ask for a number.

Three further constraints, which I have not seen addressed together and two of which I am stipulating myself. First, I want the program to treat separately indeterminacy that comes from sparse evidence, indeterminacy that comes from disagreement among sources, and indeterminacy that a legal or constitutional precautionary norm imposes regardless of evidence; my suspicion is that a rule apt for the third is being smuggled in as an answer to the first two. Second, call it terminal sharpening: any committee procedure must, by some deadline, output a number, so the program owes an account of when and by what rule a set may legitimately be collapsed. Third, call it the one-page constraint: the procedure must yield a justification a lay auditor can follow in a page, without the committee pretending to a precision it does not have.

What I want from you is a defended agenda of four to six open questions that this program should take up, in order. For each: what makes it worth three years of someone's career; which other questions its answer is upstream of, and which depend on it; whether it is tractable by theorists working with existing tools or is really stalled pending empirical or computational work; and how you would divide the eight slots among them. Then tell me explicitly which items you would leave out — because the answer would not change much downstream, or because the question as I have framed it rests on a confusion — and what result, proof, or piece of evidence appearing in the next two or three years would move an item up or down your ordering.
