I'm a journalist finishing a feature on hinge epistemology, the Wittgensteinian development of the idea in *On Certainty* that some propositions are not known but held fast, and that doubting them is a breakdown rather than a mistake. Across three interviews, one philosopher who works on this has given me a stable track record. Hinges: that she has two hands; that the Earth existed long before she was born, notwithstanding that geology can produce evidence for it; that physical objects persist when unperceived; that other people have inner lives. Not hinges: that water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level; that Napoleon existed; that she was born in Cleveland; that the bird at her feeder is a goldfinch. She also allows that what stands fast can shift across generations and across forms of life. When I raised the Holocaust informally last month, she said only that the question is political and declined to rule.

I have one interview left, and I want to put this case to her: that the Holocaust happened. It is learned from testimony, documented and in principle checkable, denied by an organized minority, and its denial in many countries is treated not merely as an error but as something legally prohibited and morally corrupt.

Predict her verdict, and more usefully, reconstruct the rule she is running. Several fit her precedents equally well and come apart here: an evidence-practice rule, a never-learned rule, a presupposition rule, and a doubt-as-breakdown rule. Say what each predicts about this case, which one you think she is actually on and why, and which of her earlier verdicts you have to explain away. Then name the further case I should put to her that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, and tell me what would change your own prediction: which consideration, if she offered it, would force you to say hinge after all, and where the rule you have reconstructed is most likely to fail.
