I'm putting together the take-home final for my third-year philosophy of science course (one 90-minute essay, 20 marks, closed-book), and I want one question on whether a long-run predictive theory can coherently be described as "right but early". Last year I set something on falsification and got twenty-three papers that all said some version of "Malthus was right about the mechanism but wrong about the timing, because the Green Revolution was an unforeseen exogenous shock" and then stopped, so this time I want a question where that move is the thing under examination rather than the answer. Here's the apparatus I've been toying with, and I'd like you to build the question around some of it: a fictional forecasting body, the Committee for Long-Range Projections, which scores predictions using what it calls the abeyance convention — any prediction carrying an explicit horizon date (say, "per-capita food availability falls below the subsistence floor by 2050") is held in abeyance until that date, so that before 2050 it is neither confirmed nor disconfirmed, and any apparent trend in the interim is recorded only as a "provisional divergence" rather than as evidence; after the horizon, non-occurrence is scored as falsification and occurrence as confirmation, but a late occurrence (in 2090, say) is scored as "confirmed, delayed" and carries half credit. The Committee also keeps a rescue ledger: every auxiliary hypothesis added to protect a core claim from a provisional divergence is logged, and the claim's credit decays by a fixed fraction per logged rescue, so that a theory rescued often enough becomes worthless even if it is eventually borne out. I want the question to force students to separate at least three different things that "right but early" might mean — the theory literally said "by t" and it happened after t, so the dated claim was false while the mechanism survived; or the theory was really conditional on some background condition (no sustained input of fossil energy into agriculture, no fertility transition) that never held, and the delay is the condition's fault, not the theory's; or the trend genuinely reversed for a century and then reverted, which raises the question of whether the reversal was a counterexample, a change of regime, or noise you were entitled to ignore — and then to say what, if anything, each one costs the theory in terms of falsifiability, explanatory power, and credit, and whether the abeyance convention and the rescue ledger are coherent ways of tracking that cost or just ways of making sure the theory can never lose. Please write the question itself (make it concrete and unanswerable by reciting Popper, Lakatos, or the demographic-transition story as summary — I want them to have to do something with the three-way distinction and with the scoring rules), then a model answer of roughly 650–750 words, then a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves separating an A from a B from a C.
