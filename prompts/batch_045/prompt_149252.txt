I spent much of last night arguing with a friend — a vision scientist, not a philosopher — about whether introspection is reliable, and I would like to convert the point we got stuck on into a final-exam question for my upper-level philosophy of mind seminar: twenty students, closed book, ninety minutes, one essay chosen from four, 1,200 to 1,500 words expected, marked out of 20.

Her position, which I half conceded, was that the philosophical debate keeps asking a question it has no way to answer. To call a belief-forming process reliable, she said, you need a benchmark: an independent record of what the target states actually were, against which the process's outputs can be scored as hits or misses. For beliefs, desires and intentions you can at least gesture at behaviour and choice as such a record, however contestable. But for what you are currently conscious of — whether the ache is throbbing or stabbing, whether the two greens look the same, whether you are annoyed or merely tired — the only record we have is what subjects say, and what subjects say is itself the output of the very process being assessed. She gave this a name, the orphaned-standard problem, and pressed it with a hypothetical. Suppose we had a decoder, trained on other people, that predicted from your neural activity alone and with high accuracy which of a fixed set of descriptions you would give of your current experience. When the decoder disagrees with you, has it caught you in introspective error, have you merely changed your mind about how to describe something, or is the apparent disagreement not a disagreement at all — the decoder tracking something you never claimed about? Her claim was that nothing in the setup settles which, and that the standard moves on both sides — introspection is a faculty with a specifiable error profile; first-person reports of experience are authoritative and only weakly corrigible; the confabulation literature shows introspection is hopeless — each quietly assumes an answer to it.

What I want is one essay question that students who have read the standard material and can recite it fluently cannot answer by reciting it. It should force them to separate questions that the literature tends to run together: reliability in the truth-ratio sense from first-person authority and from incorrigibility; misses, in which you are in a state you do not report, from misclassifications, in which you report one state and are in a neighbouring one; introspection whose object is a conscious experience from introspection whose object is an attitude, where the two seem to have complementary problems — one has a third-person check but a contested interpretation, the other has an unusually strong first-person claim but no check at all. And it should make them say what would even count as evidence here, rather than trading intuitions about whether introspection feels trustworthy. The decoder vignette seemed to me the right lever, because it looks like a way of escaping the orphaned-standard problem and may or may not be one.

Please give me three things.

First, the question as I would hand it to students: self-contained, with the vignette written out, phrased so that a student cannot tell which verdict I favour.

Second, a model answer of roughly 850 to 950 words that actually takes a position and argues for it rather than surveying positions, and that distinguishes the case of experience from the case of attitude explicitly.

Third, a marking scheme built around five must-hit moves worth 4 marks each: for each, the specific thing a top answer must do to earn the marks, what separates an A answer from a B answer that has the vocabulary but not the argument, and one line on how candidates typically get it wrong — for instance by sliding from we have no independent benchmark to introspection is unreliable, which is exactly the inference the orphaned-standard problem was supposed to threaten.

Two constraints. The question must not depend on any empirical result a student would have to look up, or on citations: I want it answerable from the armchair, using only what is stipulated in the vignette and the distinctions the seminar has drawn. And the decoder should stay a hypothetical device, not a claim about what current neuroscience can do.
