I am a senior at a large public high school, and I run the school's Philosophy Club: about fifteen members, mostly juniors, none of whom has studied philosophy, meeting for fifty minutes on Thursday afternoons with a whiteboard, no projector, and no reading between meetings. In two weeks I am leading the session on Quine's indeterminacy of translation.

I chose the topic for a reason I should state at the outset. Most of the club has already met a popular version of the argument. A widely shared video essay presents the *gavagai* case — a field linguist who hears a word uttered in the presence of a rabbit and cannot tell whether it applies to rabbits, to undetached rabbit parts, to rabbit stages, or to the property of rabbithood — and concludes from it that meaning is subjective, that no translation is better than any other, and that strictly speaking we never understand one another. I want the club to be able to say why that conclusion does not follow, and to be able to state positively what the claim actually is.

So please do not explain the argument to me; I can follow it. Plan the session instead.

What I need, specifically. First, the order in which the components have to be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit: what must be in place before the *gavagai* case can even look puzzling rather than merely silly. My guess is that this includes some grip on what a translation manual is, on the evidence available to a field linguist being behavioral and dispositional, and on what kind of fact would settle a disagreement between two manuals — but I would like you to correct the guess and to say what breaks if I reorder.

Second, the one example that should carry the fifty minutes, and what it must have and what it must lack. Here I want your judgment on a real choice: whether Quine's rabbit scenario is the right carrier, or whether a case in which the rival manuals differ in something other than a straightforward swap of reference — a language with no count/mass distinction, or two manuals that disagree about whether a given utterance is an observation sentence at all — would carry the point better for this audience, and what each option costs me.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order in which they will form, with the moment in the sequence where each should be headed off. The ones I anticipate are: that this is skepticism about other minds or about whether communication is possible; that it is simply the underdetermination of theory by evidence restated, and therefore nothing new; that the puzzle is a mere shortage of evidence and would dissolve with a larger corpus, a longer field season, or a brain scan; that if no manual is uniquely correct then every manual is equally good; and that the whole issue concerns only exotic languages and leaves our own untouched. One member will also try to convert the session into a general argument about relativism, and I would like one sentence prepared for that.

I also want your view on two structural choices. I am tempted to drop the natives entirely and open with two speakers of the same language disagreeing about what one of their own sentences means, on the grounds that it makes the puzzle ours rather than anthropological. Tell me whether that helps, or whether it makes the case look like an ordinary disagreement that more evidence would settle — and what an opening example would have to contain to avoid that collapse. Separately, tell me whether I should attempt, at this level, to distinguish the inscrutability of reference from the indeterminacy of translation for whole sentences, and if I should not, what I must say instead so that the distinction's absence does not mislead.

I know I cannot fit the apparatus into a high school session: the proxy-function argument, ontological relativity, the underdetermination of analytical hypotheses. I intend to leave it out. Tell me exactly which claims I must therefore avoid making, so that the simplifications I do use are not quietly false, and tell me where I should mark an honest blank rather than paper over a gap.

Finally, I need an ending. Give me two short cases for the last ten minutes that the club can run the distinction on: one where the indeterminacy claim does genuine work, and one where it does none at all because the disagreement is one further evidence would settle. And give me a single closing question whose answers would show me which students have the claim and which have only the slogan.
