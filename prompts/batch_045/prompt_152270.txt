Suppose we finally had a neural read-out: a device that infers, from brain activity alone, whether a person is in pain at a given moment, and that, tested across a large and varied sample, matches those people's own moment-by-moment pain reports about 90% of the time. I take this to settle the old question about the reliability of introspection quite plainly: we would then have an independent, objective check on first-person reports, they would fail that check one time in ten, and the sensible attitude toward introspection would be the one we already take toward eyesight in poor light — useful, but defeasible and in need of corroboration before we trust it on any particular occasion. Why, then, do philosophers continue to treat first-person report as authoritative about conscious experience and to build theories of consciousness around it? If the reply is that the device had to be trained and validated against self-reports in the first place, and so cannot be an independent tribunal, that strikes me as too quick: could we not recalibrate it purely against behavior and bodily circumstance, and then let it judge us? Is there a genuine argument for the authority of introspection here, or is it only an unexamined methodological habit?
