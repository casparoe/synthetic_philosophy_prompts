I'm writing the framing section of a dissertation chapter on scientific progress, and I want the spine of it to be a diagnosis of why the debate about verisimilitude has gone nowhere. I need you to argue for a diagnosis, not summarize a literature. I know the literature and will add references myself, so please don't name-drop, don't reconstruct any history of who said what, and don't state or cite anything you couldn't check offline.

The state of play as I see it. The Popperian core claim is that science aims at truth but mostly hits near-misses, that a false theory can nonetheless be closer to the truth than the theory it replaced, and that this is what makes the history of science a history of progress rather than of mere change. The natural first definition was consequence-based: one theory is closer to the truth than another if it gets more true consequences and fewer false ones. As I understand them, two independent results from the 1970s showed that this notion collapses: comparisons of closeness among false theories come out empty or trivial. The standard repair was to stop scoring consequences and start scoring likeness — put a metric or similarity structure on the space of states or models, and rank theories by how near the actual state lies, or by expected distance from it. That restored comparative verdicts, but at the price of two extra choices: a metric, and a partition (or description language) of the space of possibilities. Most of the argument since has been about those two choices.

Here is the pattern I keep seeing, and it is the thing I want explained. Someone proposes a measure of closeness; someone else produces a toy universe in which the measure returns a preposterous verdict; the proponent replies that the toy universe was described in a gerrymandered framework, or that the partition was chosen adversarially; and the exchange ends there, with neither side having moved, because neither side has a way to say what the right framework is. Take the simplest version: a magnitude has one true value, and the question is which proposition is closer to the truth when the true value is 10 — that the value is 9, that it is 11, or that it is either 9 or 11. Every proposal I know returns a verdict only once a metric and a partition are fixed, and the verdicts flip when the space is re-described. Meanwhile, from outside the definitional argument, there is a standing worry that even a perfectly well-defined notion would be epistemically idle: we have no way of telling, in the cases that matter, which of two rival theories is closer, so the notion cannot do the work the realist wants it to do.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that this is several questions sharing a label, and that the part of it which is genuinely substantive is not the definitional part at all. Specifically: the notion is being asked to do three different jobs — to license retrospective judgments that science has gotten closer to the truth; to rank live rival theories now, for purposes of theory choice; and to be usable as an evidential indication of progress — and the impossibility results bite on the first, the metric approaches answer the second only relative to choices the disputants cannot adjudicate, and the third is not really about definition but about evidence. On this reading, the framework-dependence worry is the live core, and it bottoms out in a metaphysical disagreement about whether the similarity structure of possibility space is discovered or imposed, i.e. about whether there is an objectively natural way to carve it. A related suspicion of mine is that the sides are often scoring different objects: much of what actually improves in science is idealized models that are false by construction, and it is not obvious that the truthlikeness of a theory is the same quantity as the adequacy of a model for a question.

Tell me if that is right, and be direct about where it is wrong.
