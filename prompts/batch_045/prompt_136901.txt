I host a podcast on science and ideas, and in an upcoming episode I have to carry a segment of about eight minutes, audio only, no slides and no notation the listener can see, that gets an audience whose entire formal logic is truth tables to see why a mathematical constructivist can withhold the law of excluded middle without thereby being a sceptic about mathematical truth or a relativist about correctness. Don't write the segment; give me the plan for it.

What has to be in place, and in what order, before the refusal of excluded middle even sounds like a position rather than a blunder? My guess is that I need first some grip on what a proof is supposed to *do* for you, and only then the idea that some questions come with a guaranteed method for settling them and some don't — but I want the dependency structure argued, not confirmed.

The case I want to build the segment around is one I made up rather than took from a textbook: a verification program scans a long finite object, prints a single yes/no flag, and then loses the witness it found, so we know a pattern of a certain kind occurs but no longer know where. My instinct is that this gets across existence-without-a-witness, and the gap between having the negation of a negation and having the thing itself, faster than the usual standbys such as the irrationals raised to an irrational power, or a number defined by the first occurrence of a given digit string in the expansion of pi. Tell me what such an example must have and must lack in order to carry the whole idea, whether mine does, and whether I should add one of the classical cases or hold to a single case throughout.

Then the misconceptions in the order listeners will form them, and the point in the sequence where I should head each off: that constructivism just means we don't know which side is true; that denying excluded middle is a step toward tolerating contradictions or to saying 'neither'; that it is really finitism or distrust of infinity; that it is really a thesis about machines, so that the arrival of proof assistants settles it. Give me one question to pose aloud at the end — I was going to use the claim that there are either finitely or infinitely many twin primes — that would show the idea landed rather than the vocabulary, and say what I should openly refuse to explain at this length and say out loud that I am refusing.
