I am a retired climate physicist. Most of my career was spent building and interrogating coupled ocean–atmosphere models, and the last decade of it working on detection and attribution: given an observed change, how much of it can be assigned to which driver, with what confidence, and relative to which counterfactual world. Since retiring I have been reading environmental history and the philosophy of history, largely on my own, and I have been working through the literature on non-human historical actors: pathogens, weather, volcanic eruptions, soils, droughts. I read it with a mixture of recognition and irritation. The causal structures look like ones I handled professionally for thirty years, but I cannot tell whether the claims being made are the same claims as mine, dressed differently, or genuinely different claims that my vocabulary cannot formulate. I would like a systematic translation, and I would like your judgement about what the translation fails to carry.

There are three things in the neighbourhood I want translated together, because I suspect they are not three independent claims.

First, the strong-sounding thesis that these entities are actors or agents rather than scenery: that a bacillus, a monsoon failure, or a stratospheric aerosol veil does something in history and not merely to history.

Second, the counter-charge: that accounts of this kind slide into environmental determinism, that they explain human outcomes by background conditions in a way that quietly removes human beings and their reasons from the story, and that the slide is not merely an intellectual error but has a political history, since such explanations have been used to make colonial conquest look like the working out of geography.

Third, the conciliatory position I encounter most often in practice: conjunctural causation — nothing acts alone, the outcome is a product of a coupled human and non-human system, and asking which one did it is the wrong question.

The vocabulary I would like them rendered into is my own, and I am fluent in it. Forcings and feedbacks; boundary conditions and initial conditions; externally imposed variability versus internally generated variability; a detection-and-attribution statement as a claim about the difference between an observed world and a defined counterfactual ensemble; attributable fractions and their dependence on how a shared interaction term is decomposed; response functions, sensitivities, and thresholds; timescale separation and the distinction between an event and a background state; structural uncertainty as opposed to parameter uncertainty; the fact that the choice of counterfactual world is not dictated by the physics but is interest-relative; and the general point that a causal claim is only well posed relative to a specified contrast, a specified system boundary, and a specified variable.

Two ideas of my own have grown out of this reading, and I want to know whether they are doing real work or whether they are just my professional reflexes reorganising a debate I have not understood. The first I think of as the coupling criterion: perhaps what historians want when they call something an actor is not causal efficacy at all, but two-way coupling plus internal state — a component whose own dynamics respond to what the rest of the system does, and which therefore cannot be held fixed as an imposed forcing. A pathogen that evolves under the selective pressure of human settlement and sanitation qualifies; a volcanic aerosol veil, imposed from outside and indifferent to what happens beneath it, does not. But if that is the criterion, it seems to make almost every biological entity an actor and to exclude exactly the cases the literature likes most, so I suspect it is the wrong reconstruction. The second I think of as the timescale-dependence of actorhood: the same pathogen is an event, and so looks like an actor, if you write a chronicle of a plague year, and is part of the boundary conditions if you fit a century-long demographic series; and I have started to suspect that a good deal of the argument in this area is an unmarked dispute about timescale and about whether the variable of interest is a level or a rate of change.

What I want back is this. Render each of the three positions into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and do not soften them into versions you find defensible. Then give me the residue, in three parts: what translated cleanly, with no loss I should care about; what translated only with distortion, naming for each item exactly what had to be bent to make it fit and in which direction; and what could not be expressed in my vocabulary at all. For each item in the second and third parts, tell me whether you think the residue indicts my framework or the original claim — whether it marks something the causal-attribution vocabulary genuinely cannot reach, or whether it shows that a claim which looked substantial is, once the metaphor is cashed, thin. I would like you to resist the easy dissolution into the observation that it is causation all the way down; if that really is your conclusion, argue me into it rather than asserting it.

I am particularly interested in whether the political half of the determinism objection has any home in my vocabulary. My own guess is that it partially maps onto something I know well: that the choice of which world counts as the counterfactual baseline, and which factors are treated as signal rather than as background, is a normative choice that presents itself as a neutral one, and that the authority of an attribution statement depends on that choice having been smuggled past the reader. But I cannot tell whether that captures the objection or merely a structural analogue of it, and I suspect there is more to the objection than a complaint about conventions passing themselves off as findings.

Finally, a request about honesty in both directions. I would like you to flag any place where the translation would illegitimately import an assumption of the attribution framework and then present the result as what the historians were saying all along; I would rather be told that a premise is being added than be given a clean-looking equivalence that is not one. And I would like your assessment of my two conjectures — whether either survives contact with the literature, and which of the three positions each is really an attempt to reconstruct. I do not want citations, bibliographies, or reading suggestions; I have no library access worth speaking of. If one of my moves is already a standard one under a name I do not know, give me the name and nothing further.
