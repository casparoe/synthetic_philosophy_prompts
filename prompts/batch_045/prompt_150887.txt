**The question (set last week; marked out of 100)**

*Classical invariantism holds that whether a subject knows a proposition depends only on truth-relevant factors: her evidence, whether the proposition is true, and whether her belief was formed in a reliable or suitably sensitive way. It holds that no merely practical fact — nothing about what is at stake, or about what it would be rational to do — can bear on whether someone knows.*

*(i) State classical invariantism, epistemic contextualism, and pragmatic encroachment (also called subject-sensitive or interest-relative invariantism), and say what each of the latter two takes to vary and what it takes to hold fixed.*

*(ii) The familiar bank case: on Friday afternoon Hannah and her partner need the cheque to clear and much depends on the bank being open on Saturday; on Saturday morning nothing does. Her evidence is the same on both days. Explain how each of the two views handles the case.*

*(iii) Case P. Marta is authorised to consent to a risky procedure on behalf of her unconscious sister, and hospital policy requires her to record the sister's own wishes. She asks: "Does my sister's flatmate know that she wanted this procedure?" The flatmate's evidence is exactly the evidence of an ordinary low-stakes case: she clearly heard the sister say so once, nine months ago, and has no reason to think she changed her mind. Stipulate that nothing whatsoever is at stake for the flatmate, that she is unaware of Marta's decision, and that Marta's reliance on her answer gives the flatmate no new reasons for action or enquiry. Marta, for her part, has everything at stake. Some weeks later, the crisis past, Marta says of that same belief: "She knew all along — I simply could not afford to act on it."*

*Do epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment disagree about Case P, or does the case show their dispute to be merely verbal? Discuss Marta's later remark if it helps you.*

**The paper as written**

[1] Classical invariantism says that whether S knows p depends only on S's epistemic position with respect to p — her evidence, the truth of p, the reliability or sensitivity of her belief — and never on any practical fact about what anyone has at stake. Contextualism and pragmatic encroachment both reject this, and they reject it because of the same datum: hold the evidence fixed, raise the stakes, and our willingness to ascribe knowledge falls away.

[2] Epistemic contextualism makes the variation a semantic matter. The sentence "S knows p" is context-sensitive: the standard it sets for S is fixed by the context in which it is uttered. In a low-stakes context the standard is low and the sentence may be true; in a high-stakes context the very same sentence, about the same subject with the same evidence, is false. Contextualists explain our resistance to this by pointing out that context-sensitivity is frequently invisible to speakers, so that we mistake a shift in the proposition expressed for a change in how well placed S is.

[3] Pragmatic encroachment, or subject-sensitive invariantism, makes the variation a matter of what knowledge itself demands. What matters is the practical situation of the subject: when a great deal rides on being right for S, S needs a stronger epistemic position in order to know. The argument usually offered runs through action — if S knows p, then p is a good enough basis for S to act on; in the Friday bank case it is not rational for Hannah to act as if the bank is open; so she does not know. The view remains a form of invariantism, because the truth-conditions of knowledge sentences do not shift with conversational context; what shifts is the subject's practical position, which is something in the world rather than something in the semantics of "knows."

[4] The two views differ, then, only over where the sensitivity is located. Contextualism locates it in the context of the ascriber; encroachment locates it in the practical situation of the subject. One is a thesis about the semantics of knowledge attributions, the other a first-order thesis about what is required for knowledge. In the ordinary run of cases these move together, because the person with the most at stake is usually the person making or assessing the ascription, so the two views agree on all the familiar examples.

[5] Case P is no exception to that agreement. A great deal is at stake in Marta's situation — a life, a lifetime of guilt, the hospital's rules — and that is the whole point of the case. On contextualism, the standard in Marta's mouth is high, and so the flatmate does not know: full stop. On encroachment the verdict is the same, because encroachment is really contextualism with the context reconceived; instead of a conversational context fixing the standard, a practical one does. Both views are driven by the single thought that knowledge is stakes-sensitive, so both must withhold knowledge here.

[6] The proxy case therefore does not separate them. What remains — whether the mechanism is semantic or practical — is verbal; the theories are notational variants of one claim, that practical factors bear on knowledge. If I had to choose I would choose encroachment, since it at least explains why Marta is irrational to act on the flatmate's belief, whereas the contextualist can only observe that Marta's sentence comes out false, which is a remark about language rather than about knowledge. As for Marta's later remark, it is simply inconsistent with what she said at the time, and no theory should be asked to accommodate it.
