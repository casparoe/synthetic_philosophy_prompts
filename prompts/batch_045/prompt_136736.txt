hey — I sit on the governance thing at a mid-size AI lab, and we keep having the same non-argument in deployment reviews: someone says the system we're about to train or switch off might matter morally, someone else says nothing could even settle that, and we move on with nothing written down. So before we draft any actual policy I want the spec, not a theory.

Write me the desiderata that a theory of moral status would have to meet to be good enough for our purpose — not true in some metaphysical sense, but good enough to be one input into a go / no-go call on training, deploying, modifying and shutting systems down, where we'll usually be acting on thin evidence and sometimes before any evidence is in. For each requirement, give the case that makes it a requirement. The cases that actually derail us, roughly in order: weights sitting in storage with nothing running (is a paused or suspended system a patient at all?); killing a system and restoring it from an earlier checkpoint versus killing it for good; spinning up ten identical copies and then deleting nine, or branching one into two that then diverge; a system trained end-to-end so that its whole motivational structure is an artefact of our design, i.e. it wants exactly the job we built it for; and two systems doing the same work at very different clock speeds, so one may be packing far more experience into each second of wall-clock time. I also want a view on wether a theory should treat the fact that, unlike with animals, we can in principle look inside these systems as relevant to their status, or only to our evidence about it — and wether "a competent inspector could in principle tell" is a fair thing to demand of a theory we're going to act on, or an unfair prejudice of people who happen to work on the inside.

Flag any pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied, especially where one of the pair is something our lawyers or our ethicists will insist on anyway — that's the bit I most want. Then name the single requirement that does the most work in ruling out the positions people actually hold (I'm thinking of the family that ties status to the right kind of computation whatever it's running on, the one that ties it to consciousness, the one that ties it to social relations or to being somebody's creature, and the one that ties it to agency or personhood), and score those very briefly against the list.
