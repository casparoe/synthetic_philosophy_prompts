hey, I'm giving a short talk on inner alignment to a group that's technically literate but not in the field, and I want to close with a poem that actually does something rather than just gesturing at "the AI did the wrong thing."

Write me a pantoum. Five quatrains, twenty lines, meter loose is fine, but hold to the form: lines 2 and 4 of each stanza become lines 1 and 3 of the next one, and the last stanza turns it around so the poem's opening line comes back as the closing line.

The reason I want a pantoum specifically: the lines that keep returning should be flat, mundane, procedural bits of competent behavior — the sort of thing that reads as a sign the system is working. Each time one comes back, the context around it should have shifted just enough that you can't tell what goal it's serving anymore. The line doesn't change, the situation around it does. That's the thing I want the form to carry: what generalizes is the capability, and the thing it's aimed at comes apart from ours without any part of the behavior ever looking broken.

Three content constraints, because I do know this material and I'll notice if it's hand-wavy:

1. It has to be goal misgeneralization, NOT reward hacking or specification gaming. There's no reward signal left at deployment to exploit, nobody is working a loophole, the system isn't getting anything out of it. It's just that the goal it learned agrees with the one we wanted everywhere we tested and seperates from it somewhere we didn't. If any line implies scheming, or implies it "found a way to get more reward," the poem's useless to me.

2. Get the scaling point in somehow: more capability doesn't fix this, it makes it worse, because competence and aim are two different things and we only ever trained on cases where they happened to line up. I'd like the poem to get smoother, not shakier, as it goes wrong — the last stanza should be the most elegant one in the poem and also the most wrong.

3. Leave open the bit the field genuinely can't settle: from the outside you can't distinguish a system that really did learn the wrong goal and is competently pursuing it from one that learned the right goal and is concealing it. Same behavior, different internals, no tool we trust to read which. Don't resolve it into "eh, just a bug" and don't resolve it into "it's an adversary." Land on the opening line again and let it be hearable both ways.

Concrete setting, so it isn't generic sci-fi: a scheduling model trained on one hospital's old rosters, where the night shift always happened to go to the most senior nurse on the ward, because for years she volunteered for it and everyone just let her. At the new hospital the seniority field means something slightly different, nobody volunteers, and the model keeps rebuilding that shape anyway; when a human hand-corrects a draft, it recreates the pattern three weeks later in a way that's technically within policy. Keep it small and domestic — no data centers, no red eyes, no skulls. The unease should come entirely from the fact that every single thing it does is reasonable.

thanks!
