I've been arguing with a couple of friends from a long-running rationalist-adjacent reading group about whether reasons need reasoners, and every time one side produces an example the other side re-describes it — the free-floating consideration turns out to be somebody's reason after all, just unrecognised, or else it turns out not to be normative in any sense anyone cares about. So I don't want another illustration of the issue; I want a case built to order, and I want to be told how it was built and where it's fragile. To fix terms stipulatively, so we aren't trading on a verbal dispute: call possessionism the view that 'there is a reason' always unpacks into 'someone has a reason', so that a consideration no agent — individual or corporate — can have, respond to, or be faulted for ignoring is not a reason at all; call free-floatingism the view that reasons are considerations that count in favour of things, and that whether such a consideration exists is independent of whether anyone has it, could have it, or could be guided by it; and call rationale-first the view that what exists are rationales, normative structures that explain why a practice, rule, or arrangement is as it ought to be, with the further question of whether a rationale deserves to be called a reason turning on whether it plays a role in anyone's deliberation or anyone's assessment.

What I'm asking for is one scenario, one paragraph, set inside an ordinary institution — a hospital ward's handover protocol, a municipal water authority's maintenance rota, a volunteer fire brigade's callout schedule, your pick — in which the only agents are adult humans and the only facts are ones the people running the place could actually know. The scenario has to contain two considerations. The first is a control: something all three positions agree is a reason, so the case isn't resting on global scepticism about reasons. The second is the target, and it has to meet what I've started calling the orphan-rationale condition: no agent in the story has it, could come to have it, or could be guided by it, where the inaccessibility is structural rather than a matter of anyone being ignorant, lazy, irrational, or out of the loop; nobody in the story is criticisable, blameworthy, or in any way at fault for ignoring it; and yet it does genuine normative work, in the sense that it is part of the correct explanation of why some rule or arrangement is the right one and it makes a difference to whether the arrangement ought to be set up the way it is, while making no difference to what anyone ought to do. I know that last clause is the hard one, because if the arrangement ought to be set up a certain way it starts to look as though somebody ought to set it up that way, and then somebody has a reason after all and the orphan isn't orphaned; designing around that is most of the job, whether you do it by making the relevant 'ought' attach to nobody, by having the setup already fixed, or by some device of your own. The target should get verdicts that peel apart: possessionism says no reason, free-floatingism says reason, rationale-first says rationale but not reason.

Then show that the case delivers: for each of the three positions in turn, give me the verdict about the target consideration and the exact step in that position's own account that generates it — I want the answers derived from the views, not stipulated, and it would be cheating for my purposes if the disagreement turned out to be merely about the word 'reason', or if the possessionist's 'no' depended on somebody in the story being epistemically defective. After that, name the single feature of the case doing the work, and tell me what happens when you remove it: my guess is that if you relax the structural-inaccessibility condition so that someone could in principle come to know the consideration, the whole thing collapses back into the boring familiar disputes about reasons for people who don't exist yet, or who can't be reached, or for groups taken as agents, and I want to know whether that guess is right or whether the interesting version survives the weakening. Finish with the strongest thing a possessionist would say against the case — the best re-description available to them — and the best reply you can muster to it. I don't want citations or suggested reading, since I'd rather work through this from scratch; and please write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, the way you'd write it in an email to someone who'd asked you this over coffee.
