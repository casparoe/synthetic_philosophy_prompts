I'm finishing a PhD in philosophy (mind and ethics) and I've been asked to help a small conceptual group set its agenda for the next two years: three people, no empirical ML capacity, and a brief to produce accounts of honesty and deception in AI systems that someone building evaluations could actually apply to transcripts rather than admire. The output has to be operationalizable, and I have to decide what we spend the time on.

The framing I've been using sorts failures into three bins, and I want the agenda organized around whether that's the right cut: (1) propositional falsity — what the system asserts is false; (2) inferential steering — no individual sentence is false, but selection, ordering, and emphasis are arranged so the user predictably lands on a false or unsupported conclusion; (3) self-presentation — the system misrepresents its own states or standing, e.g. performing a deliberative confidence it doesn't have, affectively implying a concern or first-personal history it lacks, or blurring that it's a model at all.

Which of these deserves the two years, and in what order? Defend the ordering rather than surveying the options: which do you take to be upstream of the others, in that a good account of it settles or dissolves the neighbouring cases; which are tractable for philosophers working from published model behaviour rather than from new experiments; and which you'd drop because they collapse into another bin or because nothing downstream turns on the answer. I'd also like a verdict on a fourth candidate I keep circling: whether the taxonomy smuggles in the assumption that there's a fact of the matter about what the system believes, and whether that's worth a chapter or is a rabbit hole.

Two constraints. Say explicitly what would move things around in your ranking — which consideration, argued better, or which kind of result from the ML side, would reorder it — and where your own reasoning is likeliest to fail. And skip the literature: I can find it. I want the defended agenda.
