I'm putting together a long survey section on the surprise exam paradox for a second-year logic course I'm helping to redesign, and I keep getting anoyed that every solution I read seems to be graded on a different standard: one family of answers thinks the job is to show where the backward induction goes wrong, another thinks the job is to work out what the teacher's announcement even means, and a third seems happy just to establish that the announcement can be true. So before I write any of it, I want the specification rather than the solutions: what does an adequate treatment of the surprise exam paradox actually have to deliver? The purpose is fixed — I need to be able to tell students which solution to believe and why the others fail, ideally by the end of the fortnight I've blocked out for this section — so grade the requirements against that, and don't pretend to be neutral where you don't have to be.

Here's the rough list I've got, which I want you to sort, argue with, cut down, and add to. (a) It has to save the announcement: whatever the teacher says has to come out true, informative, and sincerely assertible, and the exam has to arrive as a surprise — not merely as "not deducible by the pupil on the night before". (b) It has to locate the exact step where the backward induction breaks, and I want a diagnosis that either applies uniformly from n=1 up to n=100 or explains why the small and the large case come apart; a solution that only works because the numbers are big strikes me as suspicious. (c) It has to handle what I've been calling the open-ended variant, where there's no fixed final day at all — just "sometime this term" — so the induction has no last case to get purchase on; my hunch is that any solution leaning essentially on there being a terminal day is defective for exactly that reason, but tell me if I'm wrong. (d) It has to keep surprise and unpredictability seperate, and say which one the announcement actually requires, since on one reading the pupil can have a perfectly good argument for Friday and still be genuinely surprised on the Wednesday. (e) It has to be a story about the epistemic structure of the case, not about the psychology of being startled — anything that works only because real pupils are bad at logic hasn't solved anything. (f) It should say whether this is the same paradox as the unexpected hanging, and whether Moore's paradox and the knowability paradox are genuine relatives or just lookalikes; I suspect one of those two is a red herring and ought to be struck off the list entirely. (g) It mustn't cheat: no rendering the announcement meaningless, no self-reference manoeuvre that leaves it without a truth value, and nothing that quietly turns the teacher into someone choosing a day by a rule the pupil can't reason about at all.

What I most want is the part where the list fights itself. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp — the self-reference people, the Moore's-paradox people, the blindspot people — and then find the pair or triple that can't all be had at once. My guess is that "the pupil is entitled to trust the announcement" and "the exam can still be a surprise on the last possible day" are in real tension, and that whatever reconciles them has to give something up. Also say which single requirement does the most work in knocking candidates out. Then, briefly — a few sentences each, I'll fill in the detail myself — how the main families of solution do against the list.
