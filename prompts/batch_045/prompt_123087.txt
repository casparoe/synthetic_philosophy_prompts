Hey — I'm one of the two parent reps on my district's curriculum committee, we vote Monday on whether to pilot a new 7th-grade unit on the Anthropocene, and I recieved the draft too late to think about it properly, so I need something to circulate Sunday night.

The situation: the unit was written by a middle-school science teacher on release time. It's centerpiece is a timeline activity where kids get three cards — 1610, 1950, and "roughly 8,000 years ago" — and have to argue for one as the point where the Anthropocene starts. 1610 is a dip in atmospheric CO2 in Antarctic ice cores that some people read as forests regrowing across the Americas after European contact killed off a huge fraction of the population; 1950 is the Great Acceleration plus the plutonium fallout from bomb testing that shows up in sediments pretty much everywhere; 8,000 years ago is early farming, deforestation and rice paddies, when CO2 and methane stopped doing what they'd done in earlier interglacials. Near as I can tell, none of the three are wrong — they're picking out different things — and that's both what makes the activity good and what makes it impossible to grade.

The objection: Marv, who runs the science department, says every item on the end-of-unit assessment has to have one defensible key, and that we don't let kids vote on when World War II started or on what counts as a planet. He also pointed out — correctly, I checked — that the international stratigraphy body voted the Anthropocene proposal down in 2024, so on the official Geologic Time Scale there is no such epoch, and he'd rather we just teach "human impacts on Earth systems," which is what our standards actually say. The teacher's counter-proposal was to make the test item "which start date does the Anthropocene Working Group favor," and Marv says that just tests memorization of what some committee happens to think. A history teacher on the committee says if it isn't an official epoch we shouldn't use the word at all, and one parent has already emailed the board saying we're teaching kids that there are no facts.

The bit I can't get straight: my gut says the question in the unit isn't the same kind of question as "when did WWII start," and that Marv's fix — drop it, teach the standards language — throws out the interesting part, which is exactly the fight over who gets to decide and what they're deciding about. But he's right that we need something we can put a number on, he's got the committee's trust, and the superintendent has said she'll read whatever we do here as precedent for how we handle other contested topics.

Could you draft the one-page memo I'll send out? Recommendation up front — adopt as drafted, adopt with conditions, or don't adopt — then the reasoning, and I want it to take Marv's objection seriously instead of steamrolling it, including the strongest thing on whichever side loses. If there's a version of this unit where the end-of-unit item has a single defensible key without gutting the activity, say what that item should be. One page, no citations or reading lists — we don't have journal access anyway.

Last line, in exactly this form: \answer{adopt as drafted} or \answer{adopt with conditions} or \answer{do not adopt}.
