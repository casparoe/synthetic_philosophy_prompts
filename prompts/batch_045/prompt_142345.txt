I'm the TA for a graduate seminar on consciousness, and our last session was on higher-order theories — specifically the actualist higher-order thought view associated with Rosenthal, on which a mental state is conscious when it is the object of a suitable higher-order thought, with the further requirement that the thought be non-inferential (finding out about your own state by consciously inferring it is not supposed to make that state conscious). We ran out of time on two things I couldn't get the group to settle, and I'd like to close the term with a poem about them rather than another handout. The first is the targetless higher-order representation problem: a higher-order thought that ascribes a first-order state to you when no such state is present. The second is a reply one of my students proposed on the spot, which I've been calling the merely-intentional reply: a higher-order thought is an intentional state, and intentional states can take objects that don't exist, so a targetless higher-order thought is simply a thought about a merely intentional first-order state — no worse off than thoughts about unicorns, and so no reason to think the theory falls apart. Half the room thought this was obviously a cheat; the other half thought it was obviously fine; nobody could say why.

What I want is a pantoum — five quatrains, lines 2 and 4 of each stanza becoming lines 1 and 3 of the next — in which the form does the theory's work. The returning lines should be a single first-order state, so that each recurrence is the same content showing up again as the content of a higher-order representation, altered by its new surroundings in the way a line of a pantoum is altered by the stanza it lands in. Somewhere in the middle the poem should let the targetless case happen: a recurrence with no first-order state underneath it, and the merely-intentional reply should be available in the lines around it without being announced as a reply. For the closing, use the pantoum's final return of the opening lines to stage the stopping point — either the higher-order thought is itself unconscious and the regress halts, or the state does the work on itself and there was never a second state — and let the last two lines make that choice felt rather than argued. Aim for something that would read as correct to someone who knows the literature and still as a decent poem to someone who doesn't; I'll be handing it out, so no jargon that needs a footnote.

After the poem, add a short note — 150 words or so — on how the repetitions are meant to map onto the theory, where the poem takes liberties with it or leans on a contested reading, how confident you are in the philosophical claims the poem makes, and which single claim you are least confident about.
