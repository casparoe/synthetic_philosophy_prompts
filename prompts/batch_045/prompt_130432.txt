I'm in an upper-level seminar on models and idealization in the social sciences, and last week we went through the Friedman essay on the methodology of positive economics — the one with the expert billiards player who is said to play as if he knew the relevant geometry, and the notorious twist where descriptive falsity in a hypothesis's assumptions gets presented as a mark of importance rather than a cost. Two people in the seminar, Nadia and Teo, stayed behind afterwards and argued about it for about twenty minutes. I couldn't referee, because I think each of them is onto something real and I can't see how to keep both insights at once.

The case they were arguing over is the standard one. Everyone agrees that firms do not literally maximize expected profit; no one in the room was defending the assumption as description. The defence on the table was the selection story: competitive pressure tends to eliminate firms that deviate too far from maximizing behaviour, so the market as a whole behaves as if its firms maximized. Nadia and Teo then went at it as follows.

(1) Nadia: The selection story lets us relocate where the truth of the model sits. The assumption is false of individual firms, but what the model gets right is a claim about a population undergoing selection, and the causal work is done by a real mechanism — differential survival — not by the psychology of managers. She called this level-shifting, and said it is what makes the model more than a black box: it can be explanatory, not merely predictive, even though its central assumption is false.

(2) Teo: Level-shifting is a bait-and-switch. The model itself contains no selection dynamics at all; it simply imposes maximization on every firm as a constraint. So if selection is the explanation, the model does not represent the mechanism, only the outcome the mechanism is alleged to produce. That gives the model the right-answer-for-the-wrong-reasons shape, and its predictive success would confirm the selection story only if no other process could generate the same aggregate pattern — which nobody has checked. He called the two-part move a spurious bridge: the mechanism is used to license the assumption, and then the model's success is used as evidence for the mechanism.

(3) Nadia: That demand cannot be met by any model. Every model omits detail, and requiring it to contain a representation of every process that generates its own assumptions would make modelling impossible. What matters is whether the bridge is independently checkable in principle. Here it is: you can look at exit rates, at whether survival correlates with efficiency or with margins, at what happens when competitive pressure is removed. If the bridge is checkable, the model's success is evidence for it by inference to the best explanation, not theft.

(4) Teo: He granted that omission is unavoidable, but pressed two points. First, an asymmetry: false assumptions that come with a surrogate story like this one get a kind of credibility that equally false convenience assumptions — a linear functional form, a representative agent, costless adjustment — never get, even though the bridge is untested in both cases. Second, the check Nadia points to is weaker than it looks: selection could at most deliver something like surviving firms are not too badly behaved, and the aggregate consequences of that weak claim are nearly indistinguishable from those of exact maximization. So the bridge is underdetermined by precisely the evidence that is available, and calling the model explanatory smuggles in a causal claim the model cannot earn.

(5) Nadia: The near-identity of the implications cuts both ways. If the weak hypothesis and the strong one have the same observable consequences, then nothing is lost by using the strong one: the false premise is a compact stand-in for an indefinite disjunction of true ones — any of many behavioural rules compatible with survival — and the model's results are indifferent to which disjunct actually obtains. She called this a disjunction shorthand, and said this is exactly what descriptive falsity buys you: one false premise doing the work of an open-ended list of true ones.

(6) Teo: But then she cannot also have (1). A disjunction shorthand is a summary of candidate causes, not a citation of the actual one; and because the model is indifferent among the disjuncts, it cannot tell you what would happen under an intervention that changes which behaviours survive — a subsidy, a regulation, anything that alters the selection environment itself. So compactness and explanation come apart, and what happens in practice is that practitioners help themselves to whichever of the two the dialectical situation calls for.

They asked me who had won and I genuinely couldn't say. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is right, partly right, or wrong, and why. Then say who is more right overall. Most importantly, tell me what both of them are missing — I have a suspicion that the whole thing turns on the difference between predicting within a fixed selection regime and making claims about interventions that change the regime, and that both of them keep sliding between those two projects, but I can't tell whether that is the real hinge or a red herring I've invented to avoid answering. I'd also like a principled way of telling a legitimate stand-in assumption from a merely convenient one, if there is one. Please assess the arguments themselves rather than telling me which named positions in the literature they correspond to.
