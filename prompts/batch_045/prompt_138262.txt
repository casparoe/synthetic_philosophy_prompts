I'm in a final-year philosophy seminar on suffering-focused ethics, and I'm drafting a short response paper on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. Our handout doesn't cover the view, so I've been working from an encyclopedia entry that characterises it roughly like this:

"Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism holds that pleasure and pain are commensurable: they can be placed on a single hedonic scale, so that a pleasure of intensity 3 and a pain of intensity 7 are measured in the same units. But pain above a certain threshold is lexically dominant — it matters infinitely more than any amount of pleasure, so no quantity of mild pleasures, however vast, can compensate for a single episode of severe agony. For that reason the view is a form of negative utilitarianism: once the threshold is crossed, pleasure drops out of the accounting entirely and only suffering carries value-relevant weight. The threshold is also what rescues it from the standard objections to negative utilitarianism. Classical negative utilitarianism has to say that an empty world is better than a world full of happiness containing one stubbed toe, whereas the lexical view can say that ordinary, sub-threshold miseries simply do not matter very much. And the empirical literature suggests the threshold is crossed constantly in nature: most wild animals die painfully before adulthood, so wild-animal lives are net negative, which means habitat destruction is on balance a good thing — it prevents net-negative lives from being lived — and conservation is close to a moral disaster."

I have a hunch that two claims here cannot both be quite right — that pleasure and pain sit on a single scale, and that pain above the threshold "matters infinitely more" — and that calling the view "a form of negative utilitarianism" quietly collapses it into the classical position it was meant to avoid. But I can't locate the mistakes precisely enough to say so in a paper, and I don't want to build an argument on a misreading.

Could you audit the passage claim by claim, marking what a defender of the view would accept as stated, what they'd accept only with a caveat, and what they'd reject as a misrepresentation? Four things matter most to me:

1. Whether commensurability and lexical dominance really combine the way the entry describes, or whether the view needs the distinction between being commensurable and being compensable — and what work the "single scale" claim is doing for it, given that lexical priority looks like a refusal to trade rather than a measurement.
2. Where the threshold is supposed to sit: on the intensity or duration of a single episode of suffering, or on the total suffering contained in an outcome? The entry seems to use the first reading for the agony example and the second for the claim about nature, and I suspect the line about one agony outweighing a civilisation's pleasures only survives on one of them.
3. What the view implies when no single episode crosses the threshold but an enormous number of them sit just beneath it — whether sub-threshold suffering can accumulate into something that outweighs above-threshold suffering, or whether the lexical structure makes the view indifferent to any amount of it.
4. A variant my professor floated, on which the threshold is indexed to the subject's own hedonic range, so that what counts as above-threshold agony differs between a human and a mouse. Does that variant help with the worries above, or merely relocate them — and can the view coherently aggregate across subjects whose thresholds differ?

Finish by saying whether the conclusion about habitat destruction and conservation survives once the passage is corrected. Please keep your entire reply under 600 words.
