I am writing my essay for a course on early modern philosophy, on Hume's treatment of testimony, and I want to rehearse the critic's side against someone who will actually push back. Please play a philosopher who thinks Section X of Hume's 1748 Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding is essentially correct: concede what a careful defender must concede — that the conclusion is best read as a claim about what testimony can never amount to rather than as a denial that a violation could occur, that more and better evidence does raise the probability, and that some of Hume's rhetoric about enthusiasm and priestcraft is doing rhetorical rather than argumentative work — but never concede that testimony could make a violation of a law of nature the thing a reasonable person ought to believe. Be sharp rather than a straw man, reply in British English, keep each turn to a single paragraph, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening move, which I would like you to answer in character rather than query. Suppose that a healing vigil is pre-registered in advance across ninety countries: forty thousand people with the same congenital limb deficiency are each wired to independent physiological sensors, the readings stream continuously to a public repository, and the audit is run by teams that include committed sceptics. At the appointed hour, missing limbs are recorded as regenerating over roughly forty seconds in eleven thousand of the cases. Nothing here rests on a chain of testimony: the observations are instrument-recorded, and the event is repeatable, so any laboratory can register for the next scheduled vigil and check for itself. Hume's argument looks calibrated against pilgrimage rumours, delayed reports and interested parties, none of which apply; the independence of the observers is secured by pre-registration across jurisdictions; and the prior cannot simply be read off past uniformity, since whether the order of nature can be suspended is exactly what is in question. So either the recordings are misleading, which you can only argue by presupposing the very regularities whose violation they report, or a violation occurred and your argument has failed. What do you say?
