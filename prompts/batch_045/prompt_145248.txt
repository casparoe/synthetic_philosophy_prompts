I help run a casual philosophy night at the public library — six sessions, mostly retirees plus a handful of undergrads who come for the free coffee — and I'm doing the last one on Descartes and animals. Everyone's skimmed the part where he says beasts are basically machines, and the room is split between people who think he's obviously nuts and people who think he's sneakily hard to kill, and I want to give them something better than "but my dog clearly feels things." It's also weirdly personal right now: I've been volunteering at a wildlife rehab place this year and there's a crow there that does stuff I genuinely can't picture as clockwork, and I keep turning it over in my head.

Here's what I need built. As I remember it, Descartes offers two ways to tell a machine from a real mind, somewhere in the Discourse (part five, I think, where he's talking about animals and the soul): first, a machine could never use words or signs the way we do, arranging them to express thoughts — and he heads off the obvious objection by saying magpies and parrots can make the sounds but can't really say anything to the point; second, even if a machine did some particular job as well as or better than we do, it would infallibly fail at others, which reveals that it works not through knowledge but through the arrangement of its organs — since each organ needs its own special setup for each particular action, while reason is one general-purpose instrument that works in every contingency, it's practically impossible for a machine to have enough different organs to get along in all the situations of life the way reason lets us. (I may be mangling that; correct me if I've got it sideways.) So: design one case, one paragraph, present-day Earth, a real species you can name, no sci-fi and no hypothetical implants or gene editing, in which an animal fails the first test as cleanly as possible while passing the second one as hard as you can honestly push it — flexible, open-ended, general-purpose coping with genuinely novel situations, not a trick it was trained to do. Cheating rules: don't stipulate the verdict by inventing a crow that does your taxes; the behavior should be the sort of thing people who actually work with these animals report, and stay in a range where nobody in my group would roll their eyes.

Then show me the machinery, since I have to be able to defend it out loud: why the second test pushes Descartes toward conceding there's a thinking thing in there; why the first test still lets him refuse; whether the two tests were ever meant to work independently or whether the second one is only a negative test (proving mechanism when it fails, proving nothing when it's passed) — that's my guess and I want you to argue me out of it or into it. Also tell me which single feature of your case is doing the real work and what happens to the case if I take that feature out. And give me the strongest thing an actual Descartes defender would say to make the whole case evaporate — I need to be able to hand that to the half of the room that thinks he's defensible. Finish by being honest: does a case like this actually damage his position, or can he just absorb it and move on? I think he absorbs it; my co-organizer thinks it's fatal, and we've got a standing bet on it.
