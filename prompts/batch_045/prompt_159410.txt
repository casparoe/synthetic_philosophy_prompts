The situation: there's a reading group at my library that's crawling through book 5 of the Nicomachean Ethics, the part where Aristotle tries to explain how a builder and a shoemaker can trade fairly. As I understood it, his picture is roughly this: things can only be exchanged if they're somehow comparable, so people introduced money as a kind of middle term that measures everything, which lets you say how many shoes are worth a house. But then — and this is the bit they fought over — he says the real standard is actually need or demand, since that's what holds a community together, and money merely stands in for it by convention. That's supposedly why the word for money is tied to the word for custom or law: money isn't a thing of nature, it exists by agreement, and it can be changed or made worthless whenever people decide to. (Separately, in the Politics he grumbles that lending at interest is the most unnatural form of wealth-getting, because money was invented for exchange and interest is money born of money — offspring from parent.)

Two members of the group, Hal and Marta, went at it for about forty minutes. Reconstructing it fairly:

(1) Hal said the whole money-as-measure move is empty. Money measures value, he said, but value in exchange just is what things fetch in money — how many shoes for a house. So the measure isn't discovering anything; the ratio the two parties settle on is doing all the work, and money is only a numbering device draped over it.

(2) Marta said that's too fast, because Aristotle anchors the measure in need, which is not conventional: there's a fact about what people actually require, and money is only the conventional token that represents it. So value isn't merely whatever the price says.

(3) Hal came back saying need can't be a quantity. Needs are heterogeneous and there's no way to add your need for a house to my need for shoes, or to say mine is exactly twice yours; there's no unit and no operation for putting two needs together. So "measured by need" is hand-waving, and if need were really doing the work Aristotle would owe us a unit and a procedure, which he doesn't give.

(4) Marta said a measure doesn't have to be additive to be a measure. What the trading pair needs is a ratio — builder to shoemaker as shoes to house — and you can compare needs comparatively without a unit of need, the way an index number tracks a basket of goods without there being any natural unit of "cost of living." So money can be a genuine measure in a thinner, index-like sense.

(5) Hal replied that if ratios are all you need, you don't need money at all: the ratio is just whatever the two of them will agree to, so money drops out again. And worse, he said, a measure whose own size drifts can't measure anything — you can't use a ruler whose length changes — and money's value is exactly the thing that isn't fixed by the very agreement that's supposed to fix it.

(6) Marta said instability isn't fatal: a measure can be an agreed standard that's simply held fixed for the purpose at hand, and convention doing that work is not the same as the measure being arbitrary. Then she turned to me and asked whether that isn't exactly what a standard is — the old metre bar, the international prototype kilogram, both just things we agreed on and then treated as fixed, and now replaced by definitions tied to constants. (7) Hal's last shot: if Marta is right that money is a pure convention, then Aristotle's own line about interest collapses. Marta answered that conventional doesn't mean arbitrary — a thing whose whole point is to be handed over in exchange has no use separate from being handed over, so charging for its use is charging for nothing.
