I'm 68, retired from a career in plant physiology (mostly enzyme kinetics and how organisms cope with fluctuating environments), and since I stopped working I've been going to a philosophy reading group in town — mostly retired people, one of them, Ilse, is a proper Hegel person and won't let anyone get away with anything. Right now we're on Nietzsche, and I've got completely stuck on the business of diet and health, which I expected to be a sideshow and turns out to be everywhere: the complaints about German cooking and beer and heavy stomachs making for heavy minds, the digs at vegetarians, the fuss about tea and chocolate and alcohol and climate and where you eat, the claim that entire philosophical outlooks are downstream of how someone's digestion is doing, and then this other, more interesting idea of a great health — a health that isn't the absence of symptoms but the capacity to be sick, to expose yourself to dangerous ideas and come back from them.

So here's my own attempt, which I inflicted on Ilse and which she called physiologist's revenge: what Nietzsche is groping at, I think, is not optimality but breadth. Call it the narrow-envelope rule. An organism that is very well tuned to a narrow, stable band of conditions — perfectly homeostatic, every variable pinned to its set point — pays for that with a shrinking tolerance envelope: it loses the ability to buffer novelty, and anything unfamiliar (a new food, a new thought, a hard book) reads as damage. Decadence, on my reading, is exactly that shrinkage: not a disease but a loss of range, and the shrinking of the range of perspectives is the same event as the shrinking of the physiological range. I even ran a small experiment on myself and followed something like his own regimen for three weeks — no coffee, one warm meal a day, long walks — and, well, my sleep got better and my temper got worse, which Ilse says proves nothing and which I think proves something.

What I'd like from you: act as a professional philosopher who also knows enough physiology to keep me honest, and think carefully — do some actual brainstorming before you settle on anything. Compare two things, my narrow-envelope reading and his great health, and work out which of them is doing more explanatory work for the specific, scandalous claim that a whole philosophical outlook can be *caused* by what someone eats and how they digest it. Is my version a fair cashing-out of his, or does it quietly domesticate him into a nineteenth-century hygiene tract and throw away the part where he's talking about self-overcoming and risk rather than about robustness? And I want you to commit: tell me which framework you'd defend, why, and where you think I'm just doing physiology with the serial numbers filed off (or, if you think the whole thing is really just metaphor and provocation on his side and I'm over-reading, say that — but defend it, don't sit on the fence). Please don't give me a reading list or papers to chase up; I've had enough of those from Ilse.
