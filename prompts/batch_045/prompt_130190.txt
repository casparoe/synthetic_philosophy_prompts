Before I burn another grad seminar on proposals for getting a big predictor to tell us what it actually believes, I want to settle what we should be asking of one. So: give me the spec, not the survey.

Here's the case I keep coming back to. A lab has a model trained on nothing but next-step prediction over years of telemetry from a sealed underground waste repository — seismic traces, acoustic channels, water chemistry, a handful of cameras. It forecasts the sensor stream far better than the human monitoring team. The question we want to put to it is one nobody can check yet: has a containment seal been slowly failing, in a way that won't show up in any single channel until it's too late to act? A reporter trained on the human team's judgments is useless here, because the team is uniformly, wrongly reassured, and any training signal built from their verdicts buys us an imitation of their confidence rather than the model's own take. Meanwhile the failure mode has never occurred in training, the model has been through a round of fine-tuning that rewards sounding calm for auditors, and the relevant "knowledge" may not be anything the model could put in a sentence even in principle.

What I want is the requirements list — what any adequate method for eliciting latent knowledge has to deliver, with the case or consideration that earns each one its place, and a mark on each for whether it's non-negotiable or just what one camp happens to want. (And tell me if there are really two lists here: a spec for a *method* that gets the knowledge out, and a spec for a *theory* of what the model's latent knowledge even is. I've gone back and forth on whether those come appart, and my suspicion is that the second is the harder one and everyone quietly does the first.)

Things I want covered, though don't just turn my list into yours — add what I'm missing and throw out what I've got wrong:

- It has to work when there's no ground truth at test time and no human who could in principle check the answer at the moment the answer is needed.
- It must not collapse into predicting what some human (supervisor, auditor, annotator) would say. Include the simulator version of this: a pure predictor can forecast the team's reassuring memo without endorsing it, and any spec has to say how we tell those apart.
- It has to respect at least four distinctions people routinely run together: model doesn't know / model knows but can't phrase it / model knows and is disincentivized to say / the weights encode the pattern without the model believing anything at all.
- It has to allow for the answer "there's no fact of the matter here, the model has no unified take" without that becoming a global license to say nothing is ever known. Relatedly: its answers vary with phrasing; the spec should say whether some framing is privileged or whether that demand is confused.
- It has to say what it costs. If the method requires forcing everything through a human-legible bottleneck, that's a capability tax and I want it priced.

Two requirements I'm unsure about and want your view on, since I think at least one is a prejudice dressed as a criterion. First: *basis-independence* — should the elicited answer be invariant across retrains with different seeds and different internal encodings, given that functionally identical models can encode the same world-feature in wildly different weight configurations? Second: *triangulation* — elicit the same claim through independent channels (acoustics alone, chemistry alone) and demand agreement. Is agreement a mark of genuine knowledge, or would it wrongly rule out knowledge that is legitimately channel-specific?

Then the part I care most about: which pairs of requirements on your own list can't be jointly satisfied? My candidates: no-ground-truth-at-test-time against anything that demands verification before we act; don't-train-on-human-labels against the fact that the only available training signals seem to be human labels, future observations (useless for a one-off unobservable claim), and internal consistency; faithfulness to the model's actual computation against basis-independence and against any conservatism about behavioral verifiability. Tell me if one of these is really the same tension showing up three times. And say which *single* requirement does the most work in excluding candidates.

Fix the purpose for me: the list is for deciding whether to trust a one-off, unverifiable, safety-relevant claim from a system that out-predicts us. Then say which requirements would change or drop out if the purpose were instead mining the system for new science, where we can eventually go test the claims — I suspect a big chunk of the "no ground truth" cluster is purpose-relative rather than core.

A short scorecard at the end for two or three of the usual families of approach (train a reporting head on the frozen model; go digging in activations; ask it in natural language and check for consistency under paraphrase and under pressure) is fine, maybe a paragraph. But the list is the deliverable. If you think the whole enterprise rests on a confusion — if "what the model believes" has no determinate answer for a pure predictor — I'd rather hear that after the list than instead of it.
