I help run a small reading group on formal epistemology (mostly graduate students and two people from the decision-science side of econ). Last week a friend from econ walked us through an argument I have not been able to shake, and I would like a professional judgement on it before I take it back to the group. The argument starts from Good's theorem on the value of information — the result that a free observation cannot lower an expected-utility maximizer's ex ante expected utility, because the maximum of an average is never greater than the average of the maxima. My friend writes the proof out as: for a free experiment with realizations s, a fixed act set A, a utility function U(a,w) depending only on act and state, and a posterior P(w|s), we have Σ_s P(s)·max_a Σ_w P(w|s)·U(a,w) ≥ max_a Σ_w P(w)·U(a,w). He then builds the following chain: (1) that inequality is Good's theorem; (2) the derivation uses only five assumptions — (i) the signal is free, (ii) the act set is the same before and after, (iii) utility does not itself depend on whether the signal was received, (iv) posteriors come from conditioning, (v) after seeing s the agent picks an EU-maximizing act; (3) so for an ideal agent there is no such thing as unwelcome information — whenever someone would rather not know, one of (i)–(v) has failed, and the harm is attributable to that failure rather than to the information itself; (4) generalizing from one decision to every decision and from one agent to any agent, for every agent, every decision problem and every free signal, accepting the signal weakly dominates declining it — call this the no-bad-news principle; (5) hence every deliberate refusal or destruction of information is irrational, or rational only because one of (i)–(v) fails; (6) but the familiar practices of destroying or withholding information — blind auditions, sealed bids, blind trusts, anonymous grading, instructing a jury to disregard testimony, the medical right not to know one's own genetic results, refusing to read a spoiler before seeing a film — are not defended on that basis: defenders of orchestra screens say the screens make the outcome better even when the information is free and the judges are competent, and say nothing about compensating for defective reasoning; (7) therefore either (4) is false, or (6) is false, or these practices work only because one of (i)–(v) fails in a way nobody has yet pointed out. My friend takes the third option.

What makes this harder for me is that I cannot localize the damage, and the two places I keep circling may both be wrong. I first thought the trouble was that the theorem guarantees only ex ante value while people rightly care about the value of information once a particular result has come in; then I thought the trouble was the move from a fact about which act is optimal to a prediction about what I will actually do; now I am not confident about either. I have also invented a class of cases that I think is not in the textbook discussion and that I would like adjudicated: I call them mandatory-response regimes — settings in which acquiring a signal legally or institutionally obliges you to respond on someone else's utility function (a clinician who, once she has reasonable cause, must report; a disclosure duty that removes the option of staying silent; rules binding an official to a published forecast). Here learning appears to remove the very act of proceeding as if one did not know, rather than merely to inform it. I would also like a verdict on the mirror-image legal doctrine that deliberately not knowing is no defence and can be treated as knowledge, which seems to make ignorance costly rather than valuable, and so seems to cut against (4) in the opposite direction from the blinding cases. And there is a methodological worry I cannot settle: if every apparent counterexample is reclassified as a failure of one of (i)–(v), is (4) unfalsifiable and therefore empty, or is it just a conditional theorem being quoted outside its antecedent? For what it is worth, of the claims in play I would rather keep (1), (2) and the claim that these institutional practices are sometimes sensible than keep (4); but I want to know exactly which words in (4) do the damage and what else I lose if I give it up.
