I've been reading about panpsychism on and off for a couple of years and I keep running into the same shape of argument, and I don't know what to call it. Is there an established name for it, or is it just two people talking past each other?

Here's the shape. Person A says: the whole selling point is that you never have to get consciousness out of something that isn't consciousness. If the stuff at the bottom already has a bit of experience in it, you're explaining consciousness with consciousness instead of squeezing it out of a stone. Person B says: fine, but now you have to explain how an enormous number of tiny experiences becomes one experience, and that step looks every bit as unexplained as the step you were trying to dodge. Person A: no, my step is strictly *easier*, because both ends are the same kind of thing. Stone-to-feeling is magic; faint-feelings-to-one-rich-feeling is just composition.

What I keep noticing is that A never says how you'd check whether their step really is easier. The measuring stick seems to come from inside their own picture: both relata are experiences, and experience is the sort of thing that plainly does compose — look at a visual field, look at how the smell of the coffee and the sound of the kettle show up together in one moment. But push on those examples and they're all cases where there is already one subject doing the having, while the thing that needs explaining is how there comes to be one subject at all. So the "easiness" seems to be borrowed from a case that is itself on the list of unsolved stuff.

Two more instances, so this isn't just one grumpy exchange I had:

1. A defender says the micro-level feelings can be almost nothing at all — no self, no memory, maybe not even a full-blown "what it's like," just the thinnest possible what-it's-likeness — and that makes the summing step less mysterious because there's less to add up. But then the slogan "consciousness all the way down" is doing its work with a notion of consciousness that's been thinned to the point where it's hard to see why it was supposed to help, and the answer to that is always some version of "it's still experience, just minimal."

2. From the other direction: people who think there's one big subject at the top and everything else is a fragment of it get told they have the same problem running backwards — how does one experience get chopped into many — and they reply that decomposition is fine because it's what the physical world is already doing (split brains, that kind of thing). Again: the difficulty is claimed to be smaller because it now sits next to something we already accept.

So what I want a name for is this: answering "you've only moved the mystery" with "yes, but I've moved it somewhere cheaper," where the exchange rate is set by the theory being defended and where the comparisons used to show the new location is cheap are themselves unexplained instances of the original mystery. I've been calling it difficulty laundering to myself, which almost certainly isn't a real term. It's not quite question-begging — nothing is smuggled in as a premise exactly — and its not quite motte-and-bailey, since the claim isn't quietly weakened and then re-asserted; it's more like a claim about the *size* of an explanatory gap that can't be audited from outside.

Does this pattern have a name of its own? If there's something nearby, I want to know how what I've described differs from it and which one fits better — I'm after the carving, not the label: what exactly this is, what it isn't, where its edges are. In particular: is relocating a mystery onto relata of the same kind ever genuine progress (people do seem to count it as progress in other places, and I can't tell what the difference is), what would have to be true for it to be, and are my two instances really the same phenomenon or two different ones?

One more thing: I'd rather have the honest answer than the confident one, so say what would change your mind — if you think it's X, what would make you say it's actually Y, or that there's no phenomenon here at all, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail.
