hey — I'm in a first-year seminar on early modern skepticism and we just started Montaigne's chapter on cannibals (we're reading the old Cotton translation that's up on wikisource, the one Hazlitt edited, so the spelling is all over the place). We spent like an hour in my study group on just the opening paragraph and got nowhere, and I've read it four times now and it still dosn't settle for me. Here's the bit:

When King Pyrrhus invaded Italy, having viewed and considered the order of the army the Romans sent out to meet him; "I know not," said he, "what kind of barbarians" (for so the Greeks called all other nations) "these may be; but the disposition of this army that I see has nothing of barbarism in it."—[Plutarch, Life of Pyrrhus, c. 8.]—As much said the Greeks of that which Flaminius brought into their country; and Philip, beholding from an eminence the order and distribution of the Roman camp formed in his kingdom by Publius Sulpicius Galba, spake to the same effect. By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report.

What I can't work out is why he opens there. You'd expect an essay about cannibals to start with the cannibals, and instead you get three anecdotes about Greeks and Romans calling each other barbarians, and then that moral about judging "by the eye of reason, and not from common report." My roommate thinks this is the key to the whole chapter: Montaigne isn't saying the Brazilians have their standards and we have ours, he's saying there's one correct standard — reason — and that we Europeans are the ones who flunk it. I read it the other way, as a kind of deflation: "barbarian" basically just means "not us," Pyrrhus's joke is that the word has no content once you detach it from who's speaking, and "the eye of reason" is just about checking for yourself instead of repeating what everybody says. A friend who hasn't read the essay says we're both wrong and Montaigne is being ironic about the whole classical apparatus he's lugging in.

We also invented a test we couldn't agree on how to apply. Call it the Pyrrhus test: when Montaigne flips a judgment about savagery, is he (a) reassigning the predicate — the Europeans are the real barbarians, the Brazilians aren't — or (b) dissolving the predicate — nobody is really a barbarian, the word is only an indexical for "foreign"? Those give very different readings of everything downstream: the prisoners who taunt their captors and keep up their defiance to the end, the three men brought over and shown around France who were reportedly baffled that half-starved people would put up with the rich instead of setting fire to their houses, the jab at the Wars of Religion and people being torn apart by dogs and swine, which he treats as worse than eating the dead.

So: what is the eye of reason actually doing in that paragraph? Does it commit Montaigne to some universal standard, which would make him less of a relativist than everyone says, or is the reason-talk purely about testimony and secondhand report rather than about a cross-cultural moral yardstick? And is the Pyrrhus test as I set it up a real distinction or a fake one — do both halves collapse into the same reading once you look closely? If you think the paragraph is doing something else entirely, say so, but please be concrete about which words in the passage are carrying the weight, because that's the part we kept talking past each other on.
