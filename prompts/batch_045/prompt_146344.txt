I work on preference-based fine-tuning of language models, and I recently read a chapter in a book of popular essays on algorithms and democracy that used Arrow's impossibility theorem to argue that a lot of current work on AI alignment is chasing something provably impossible. I am not in a position to tell which parts of it I should trust — I use phrases like IIA and Pareto as engineering slogans more than I understand them — so I would be grateful for an audit. The passage, from an unnamed essay that I have abridged slightly but otherwise left unchanged, reads:

"Arrow's theorem says that no voting rule can turn the individual preference rankings of a group into a collective ranking while respecting a handful of conditions that each sound too weak to argue with: the rule must not simply hand one voter's ranking to the group (non-dictatorship), it must rank A above B whenever every voter does (Pareto), the group's ranking of A against B must depend only on how the voters rank A against B and not on some third option C (independence of irrelevant alternatives), and it must work for any possible pattern of individual rankings (unrestricted domain). No rule satisfies all four at once. So every actual voting system is broken in some way, and the centuries-long search for a fair one has been proved hopeless. The conditions are individually so weak that the theorem is best read as a fact about information rather than about fairness: the only input allowed is an ordinal ranking, which throws away how strongly each person prefers A to B. Restore that information — let people report intensities of preference, or let a planner weigh one person's gain against another's loss — and the impossibility evaporates. This is why surveys that ask people to rate their own happiness from 0 to 10 and then average the numbers escape Arrow's conclusion entirely. And it is also why the project of aligning a powerful AI with human values is hopeless in a precise mathematical sense: any procedure that combines many people's preferences about an AI's outputs into a single objective is a social welfare function, and Arrow proved that no such function can avoid being arbitrary, so somebody's values must end up imposed by fiat."

My instinct is that the final application is where it goes wrong. In my day job we fit a reward model to a pile of pairwise comparisons using something like a Bradley–Terry model, and that feels much closer to estimating a population parameter from a sample than to conducting an election: the "voters" are drawn from a pool of crowdworkers, and the "alternatives" are model outputs that did not exist when we started collecting data. But the middle part, about intensities and information, sounds right to me, and if it is right then the happiness-averaging claim seems to follow — which makes me think I am missing something about the difference between cardinality and interpersonal comparability. I would also note that I have heard the strategic-voting point attributed to a different result (Gibbard–Satterthwaite?), and this essay never mentions strategic voting at all, which I took as a sign that the author knew what they were doing.

Please give me your assessment as exactly six numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. whether the statement of the theorem and the gloss on its four conditions is fair — in particular, whether describing IIA as too weak to argue with is something a social choice theorist would accept;
2. whether it follows that every actual voting system is "broken" and that the search for a fair one is "proved hopeless";
3. whether the theorem is best read as a result about information, and whether letting people report intensities of preference by itself makes the impossibility evaporate;
4. whether averaging 0-to-10 self-reported happiness scores escapes Arrow's conclusion in the way the author claims;
5. whether the kind of aggregation I described from my own work is the sort of object Arrow's theorem is about at all — and, if one tried to map it onto the theorem, what would correspond to a dictator;
6. which of the essay's three headline conclusions — that no fair voting system is possible, that happiness surveys escape the theorem, and that AI value alignment is hopeless in a precise sense — survive once the misreadings are corrected.

Where the author is wrong, say what a defender of the standard understanding would say instead; and where the author is right but I am inclined to doubt it, tell me that plainly.
