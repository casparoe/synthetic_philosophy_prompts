Last night I argued for most of an evening with an old friend about the problem of dirty hands in politics — the claim that someone who holds office responsibly will sometimes have to do what is morally wrong in order to do the job properly, and that doing so does not by itself show they have governed badly. My friend spent eleven years as counsel to a state ethics commission and now teaches administrative law, and she has unusually settled intuitions about these cases. I am not going to talk her out of any of them, but by the end of the evening I was convinced she was running a rule I could not state, and I would like that rule stated better than I can state it — because there is a real case on my desk and I have to advise on it next week.

Her verdicts, as I noted them down afterwards:

1. A mayor who, on specific and credible intelligence of a bombing plot, authorised surveillance he knew to be unlawful; the plot was averted; once the emergency had passed he disclosed what he had done, pleaded to the statutory offence, paid the fine, and resigned. Her verdict: he had to do it, and he had to pay for it. What he must not do — this was the part she was most insistent about — is go on to claim that it had not really been wrong.

2. A governor who used a lawful power of pardon to release a political ally in exchange for the votes needed to prevent a fiscal collapse. Her verdict: not a case of dirty hands at all, only of corruption. Nothing tragic in it, nothing to admire.

3. A police commissioner who, minutes after a child was abducted, authorised a coercive interrogation the law forbids; the information was obtained and the child was recovered alive. A clean alternative — tracing the call — was available and had a real chance of working, though nobody now knows whether it would have got there in time; he never attempted it. Her verdict: unjustified, and he should have resigned at once. When I asked whether the verdict would change if the clean route would in fact have been too slow, she said no, because the point is that he never tried it.

4. A civil servant who, after internal channels ignored her, leaked classified material showing that her department was running an unlawful surveillance programme; nobody was in imminent danger; the programme was shut down. Her verdict: she did the right thing and she owes nothing further — no apology, no resignation. My friend was explicit that this differs from the mayor's case: there the wrongness was his, here it belongs to the law.

5. A prison director who authorised force beyond what was lawful in order to end a riot in which guards were being held hostage; the force prevented deaths; he never disclosed it, and it surfaced years later in an audit. Her verdict: the act itself was justified, but he should now be prosecuted and barred from office — the concealment, she said, broke something the act itself had not broken, and an official who does this and then hides it is worse than one who does it in the open.

The case on my desk is this. Eighteen months ago, during a shortage, a state immunisation director authorised the use of doses held past their regulatory expiry date, which is unlawful and which the evidence for potency only weakly supported. The doses went to clinics serving a poor, largely unrepresented population. Recipients were told nothing: the consent materials stated the doses were within specification, and he chose not to run the sort of public campaign that would have cut uptake sharply. The projection he was working from was a large outbreak over the following winter, with the best available modelling putting it in the thousands of cases. The outbreak never came — a mild season and a neighbouring state's campaign changed the dynamics — so the doses appear to have done no measurable harm and probably some good, but no measurable good either. He documented his reasoning at the time, disclosed the decision voluntarily to the legislature nine months later, says he would do exactly the same again, and has declined to apologise or to accept any sanction, on the grounds that he did nothing wrong and that a public apology would damage confidence in the programme. The recipients have still not been told. No one consulted them or anyone representing them. And there is one further fact I find hard to place: in an email to a colleague he wrote that he had learned from earlier scandals that the officials who get destroyed are the ones who let themselves feel it, and that this was one reason he preferred this option to the alternatives.

What I want:

(i) State the smallest set of rules that fits all five of her verdicts, precisely enough that a third party could apply them. I assume there is more than one: I could think of two immediately and could not choose between them, and I suspect there are others that also fit — perhaps one on which the verdicts track how things actually turned out rather than the evidence available at the time, and perhaps one on which they track who was made to bear the cost.

(ii) For each rule, what verdict does it give on the immunisation director? Keep two questions separate: whether the act itself was justified, and what should now follow for him.

(iii) Which verdict do you expect her actually to give, and what makes that rule the best fit to the five? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so rather than producing a tidy answer.

(iv) Give me the minimal variant of this case, or the single further stylised case, that would do most to separate the surviving rules — the one whose answer would eliminate at least one of them — together with what each rule predicts about it. I would rather have that test than a confident verdict.

(v) Finally, tell me which single further fact about the real case would most change your answer, so that I know what to go and establish before I raise it with her.
