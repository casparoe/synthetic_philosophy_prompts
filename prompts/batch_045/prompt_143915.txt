I sit with a colleague on a working group that has to classify online communities as epistemically problematic, and she has supplied most of our judgments. Her verdicts so far:

1. A forum whose moderators delete every link to an outgroup outlet, even though members who encounter outgroup arguments elsewhere find them persuasive — a bubble, not an echo chamber; exposure is the remedy.
2. A group that reposts outgroup arguments only in order to mock them and circulates a standing explanation of why every outside institution is captured — echo chamber; exposure would not help.
3. A specialist listserv that ignores all non-specialist sources on questions inside its expertise — neither, and no criticism due.
4. A citizens' group that boycotts academic sources as bought, on a question where specialists overwhelmingly reject its position — echo chamber, and worse than case 2.
5. A debate forum that requires both sides to be represented and where each side can state the other's best argument accurately, but where no one ever changes their mind — not a bubble; something else is going on there.
6. A scattered set of people who each independently reached the same false conclusion from the same one-sided summary, without ever interacting — neither; there is no community to blame.

Next week's case: a 300-member community in which no source is barred, no one is ridiculed, no capture narrative circulates, and each member, asked privately, is genuinely willing to move on a credible source from the other side — but everyone reads a personalized feed that never surfaces one, and each member wrongly believes the others are far more confident than they are, so doubts never get voiced. She has never ruled on a case where the exclusion comes from a recommender system rather than from the community's own practices, or where the obstacle is not exposure but the merely believed cost of speaking.

Predict her verdict and her label. First set out the two or three rules that fit her six earlier verdicts equally well — I have in mind something like exclusion versus active discrediting, counterfactual sensitivity to contrary evidence, and the believed social cost of dissent — and say what each predicts here. Then give your best guess with the reason for preferring it, and name the one further case that would best tell the surviving rules apart. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so and say which principle she would be adopting with each choice.

Lead with your bottom line, verdict plus label, in the first sentence; then the candidate rules, the prediction, and the discriminating case. No preamble, no restatement of my question, and no citations.
