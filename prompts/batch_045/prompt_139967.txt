I'm in the final term of an undergraduate seminar on formal epistemology and machine learning, and I've been given a ten-week summer fellowship attached to it. I'll be working alone with a philosopher as supervisor — she is comfortable with decision theory and computability, less so with contemporary ML — and the deliverable is meant to be one paper of roughly twenty-five pages, not a survey.

The cluster I want to work in is the one around the claim that the universality of the Solomonoff prior is also its vulnerability. As I currently understand it: Solomonoff induction predicts by weighting every computable hypothesis by its simplicity, roughly two to the minus length of the shortest program that generates it, and the resulting distribution dominates any computable competitor up to a multiplicative constant that depends only on your reference machine. The unsettling observation is that among the very short programs are worlds containing consequentialist agents who would gain from steering whoever relies on the prior — agents who can afford to spend complexity on modelling the predictor because they have a lot to gain from its being wrong at particular moments. And because the dominance result buys you only a constant factor, not overwhelming weight, you cannot reply that the manipulators are an exponentially negligible minority of the hypothesis space. So the prior may be shaped partly by the aims of agents with an interest in the predictor's errors rather than by the structure of the environment being predicted.

Beyond that shared starting point, the cluster seems to me to come apart into several questions, which I'll state as best I can:

(a) A reading question: is the threat essentially that the predictor is running inside a simulation built by agents who want to influence it, or is it only a counting worry — that short programs which fit the data well include worlds containing agents who would prefer that this predictor be misled, whether or not anyone is simulating anyone? Do the two readings demand different replies, and does the second one collapse into ordinary underdetermination of theory by data?

(b) A location question: is the malignancy in the prior at all, or in grafting the prior onto expected-utility maximization? Would a predictor that only assigns probabilities and never acts be exposed to any of this, or does the manipulation need the agent's outputs to feed into something that agents have preferences over?

(c) A transfer question: does any of it survive the passage to computable approximations — bounded search, practical compressors, learned priors — or does the bound change things qualitatively rather than just blurring them?

(d) A significance question: if the argument is sound in its own terms, what does it actually tell someone doing ML or epistemology? Is it a reason to change practice, a limitative result about a certain idealization, or a diagnosis that mostly relocates familiar problems such as Goodhart-style gaming and deception?

(e) A repair question: are there principled modifications — restrictions on the hypothesis class, different scoring, forms of ignorance or fragility in the update rule — that block the argument without giving up what made the prior attractive, and what do they cost?

I can find the literature myself, so please don't spend the answer on citations or a reading list. What I want is a defended decision about where my ten weeks should go. Rank the questions — adding or splitting any you think I've got wrong — and defend the ordering on explicit criteria: how much of the rest of the cluster depends on the answer, whether someone with my background could make genuine progress rather than restate the puzzle, whether the question is crowded or neglected, and how much difference a correct answer would make downstream. Where criteria conflict, say so and say which one you're weighting.
