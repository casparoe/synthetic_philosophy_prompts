I've been reading about capital punishment lately — not for any reason, just got into it — and I keep running into the same shape of argument, and I don't know if there's a word for it.

First place I noticed it: people who support the death penalty say that nobody has actually shown that an innocent person has been executed, and people against it say that proves nothing, because once the execution happens the appeals end, the lawyers move on, reporters lose interest, nobody funds the testing. What got me is that it isn't just that the evidence is missing — it's that carrying out the policy is what dismantles the machinery that would have turned up the mistake. Doing more of it wouldnt just risk more errors, it would mean fewer of the errors ever come to light. And it seems to run the other direction too: the reason we know about the death row exonerations we do know about is that those people sat there for years with somebody still digging.

Second place: the deterrence side. The studies dont find a clear effect, and the reply is that of course they dont, because we execute so few people so slowly — a swift and certain death penalty is what would deter. But swift and certain means less review and fewer appeals. So the version that is supposed to work is the version with the weakest error-correction, and you cant find out whether it works without adopting it at scale.

A friend said this sounds like Goodhart's law, but I dont think so: in neither case is anybody gaming a measure, the detecting just stops. Is there a name for this — a practice whose use erodes the evidence you would need to judge it? Are my two cases even the same thing, or is one about discovering past mistakes and the other about testing a causal claim? If there's no name, give me one with a definition that marks the edges, because I don't want something so broad it swallows every case where absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence.
