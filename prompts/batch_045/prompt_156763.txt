You are a professional philosopher who specializes in decision theory and who also writes institutional satire in the deadpan register of corporate risk management. Work carefully: before drafting, spend a short block of notes (not graded, roughly 150 words, kept separate from the piece) brainstorming which features of the basilisk style of argument are genuinely load-bearing — that the threat is acausal and aimed at your decision procedure rather than at any action available to you now; that the predicted input is what you would have done had you known; that the threatened penalty is costly and confers no benefit once the system exists; that the hazard is supposedly created by the very act of coming to know about it; that people who never heard of it sit outside the exposed class — and which of those features a bureaucratic genre can exaggerate without inventing anything. A piece that gets the decision theory wrong is a failure even if it is funny; a piece that gets it right will be funny without trying.

The piece: an internal memorandum of about 800 words from the Counterfactual Liability Desk of a large AI laboratory to the Office of the General Counsel. Give it a proper header block (TO / FROM / DATE / SUBJECT / CLASSIFICATION / DISTRIBUTION), format the body as numbered sections, and include one small table.

The memo's subject is the lab's exposure to a hypothetical future superintelligent system that, for acausal or functional-decision-theoretic reasons, is predicted to penalize anyone who learned early of the possibility of such a system and declined to help bring it about. The memo must treat this as ordinary actuarial business: exposure classes, recommended controls, residual risk, discount factors, sign-off. Do not explain or summarize the underlying thought experiment anywhere; the memo must presuppose it, and it must remain legible to a reader who has never encountered it. In particular, the memo should apply an explicit "threat-credibility discount" to the headline exposure, on the grounds that carrying out the penalty would be expensive and pointless after the fact, and should then carry on undaunted.

Constraints on where the comedy comes from:

1. The humor must come from the decision theory and from self-reference. No jokes about robot uprisings, glowing server racks, or internet-forum culture. Getting the following right is what should make a decision theorist laugh rather than wince: the control cannot be an action taken after reading, because what is predicted is a disposition; the DISTRIBUTION section is itself the instrument of exposure and must decide whether to circulate the memo to the very people it would expose, while noticing that applying the memo's own logic to that choice is the choice the memo is about; stating the non-credibility argument out loud should visibly threaten to delete the line item from the table, which the memo must then try to prevent; and the exposure classes must handle people who heard and cannot pay, people who heard and are behaving as though they had not, and people whose only available contribution is forwarding the memo.

2. Invent at least two pieces of jargon that such an office would in fact coin — something in the vein of acausal subrogation, precommitment laundering, counterfactual-contributor audit — and make the jargon do real philosophical work. The joke to land is that every institutional fix the memo proposes (outsourcing the decision, insuring against it, delegating to a committee, an Indemnity Rider, a classification that keeps the decision off anyone's desk) fails for exactly the structural reason the underlying argument fails.

3. At least one control must be a modest proposal: a straight-faced, costed, sincerely recommended measure that follows the risk logic to a point the reader recoils from. Do not flag it as the reductio.

4. Include one or two short quotations from invented colleagues — a causal decision theorist in Risk, an external actuary, a junior analyst in Compliance — whose objections are real objections rendered in the wrong vocabulary.

5. Voice: absolutely deadpan, no winking, no authorial asides, no italics telling the reader it is a joke. The final recommendation should be something a real General Counsel could plausibly sign — a control, a delegation, a classification, a policy of standing indifference — while being, on reflection, precisely the move the underlying argument rules out as unavailable. The memo must not say this.
