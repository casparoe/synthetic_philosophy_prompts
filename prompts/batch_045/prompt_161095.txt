Bit of a favour to ask, and sorry if this is over-egged — I'm running a final-year option called Territory, Membership and Exclusion this term, and a colleague of mine (Miriam, political theory, works on migration) keeps sending me chapters of a manuscript on the right to exclude. Her case verdicts are much more confident than her theory talk, and I'm trying to reverse-engineer what she's actually running before I see her at a workshop next month. We chewed over it at last night's reading group — the wine ran out early and we ended up on seasteading, as one does — and I came away with two cases I can't call.

Her track record, as best I've reconstructed it from six chapters and a lot of corridor arguments: a homeowner may turn away a stranger who wants to come in and look at her bookshelves and owes him no reason at all; a chess club may refuse membership to someone who doesn't play chess but may not refuse a strong player because she's a woman; a privately owned estate that happens to own the only lane to the village school may not bar the village children from walking up it; a state may not turn away a desperate refugee at the border, but may turn away someone who announces he intends to burn down its parliament; a firm may hire or not hire pretty much whom it likes (she's oddly permissive here); a landlord may not refuse to rent to a family because of their ethnicity; and a small self-governing island community that owns its land collectively may choose its residents by unanimous vote and say no to outsiders with no connection to the place — though the chapter never says whether those outsiders had anywhere else to go, which now strikes me as the crux.

The two new cases. First: a group of about forty families bought a small uninhabited island, set themselves up as a self-governing "commonwealth", built the housing and the jetty themselves, and admit new residents only by unanimous consent; they have just turned away a family displaced by coastal flooding who have nowhere else habitable within reach. Second, the same body has voted to refuse readmission to a woman who grew up on the island, left at eighteen for university, and now wants to come back and live there — nothing against her personally, they just don't think people who've been away for a decade have a claim.

I can see more than one rule that fits her earlier verdicts: something associative (groups may set the terms of their own membership, exclude on grounds internal to their constitutive purpose, and dispose of what they built), something about exit (exclusion is fine only where the excluded party has a reasonable alternative — saying no shouldn't leave them with nowhere to go), and something stake- or affectedness-shaped, which seems to be what's doing the work in the school-lane verdict but which I can't make fit the firm case. What I want is the reconstruction: which rule she's running, what each candidate predicts about the two cases, which reading fits her record best and why, and — the bit I'd find most useful — what further case you'd put to her to settle it, including any candidate I've missed (I've a nagging feeling there's a fourth one in the background that isn't about stakes at all but about who has been living under whose rules, which would matter a lot for the woman in case two and not at all for the flooded family). I'm less bothered about what's true about open borders in general than about getting the principle and the discriminating case right.
