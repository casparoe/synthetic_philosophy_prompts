Write me a short story — 900 to 1200 words — set in a Britain where advance directives have been extended with something called 'weighting clauses'. When you file one you don't only list treatments you refuse; you also assign your future self a say over different regions of your life, a number from 0 to 100 for how much weight your later testimony gets on that topic. You can also opt into a 'symmetry condition': your later self may overturn a clause only if she can first state, to a clinician's satisfaction, the reasoning that led the earlier you to write it.

The character is Nadia, 43, a hospital pharmacist, an atheist, a long-distance runner, someone who thinks serenity is a form of defeat. She files hers after a scare. She gives 90 to reports of pain, 0 to religious conviction, 40 to any claim about being happier now, and she adds a note: if I turn into someone who's content with a small quiet life, that isn't me getting what I wanted, that's me being made smaller.

Cut to her at 74 with early-stage Alzheimer's, who has joined a church, is calm, has a partner called Bill, and says the woman who wrote that document was frightened and didn't know what she was talking about. The clause now in dispute says she isn't to be taken to services; her daughter Ruth has to decide whether to stop Bill taking her on Sundays. Make the last scene turn on the symmetry condition — Nadia recites her earlier self's case, and it's genuinely unclear whether she's reconstructing it or just parroting something she's been told — and end with Ruth's decision made but not endorsed. I'd rather the story earned the dilemma than explained it, so no tidy resolution, no character giving a speech about personal identity, and definately no narrator summing up.
