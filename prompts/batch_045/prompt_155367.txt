I'm giving a talk in a couple weeks at a joint session of a metaphysics reading group and a bioethics / population ethics group, and I'm way less worried about the talk itself than about the Q&A, because the room is a weird mix. Roughly: two Kripkean essentialists (one of them an animalist who thinks I'm essentially a human organism and thinks most puzzles in this area dissolve the second you say that out loud), one loud reductionist of the broadly Parfitian sort who thinks "would it have been me or someone else" is an empty question, two population ethics people whose only real interest is whether any of this rescues or kills person-affecting views in the non-identity problem, one decision theorist who is going to want an actual sample space and will not accept hand-waving about "the same possible person," and one EA-adjacent grad student who works on embryo selection and just wants to know whether picking which of ten embryos to implant can be good *for* the kid who results.

Thesis, short version: I want to push back on the standard origin intuition (that I couldnt have come from a different sperm and egg) without denying that origins matter, by arguing that the cross-world question is less determinate than people assume — and then arguing that the normatively load-bearing notion isn't identity anyway. Call two possible zygotes *c-candidates* (candidate-identical) iff they're alternatives in the same selection class: roughly, they're the things among which a single chancy process or a single choice at a single decision node could have picked. Three moves: (1) the "different sperm" intuition feels strong because in the ordinary case there's no salient selection node — nobody is choosing among sperm, so there's nothing to make the alternatives vivid; (2) the timing argument: if my parents had conceived a day later it would have been a numerically different sperm and quite possibly a different egg, and nobody thinks a day's delay yields a different child, so gamete identity can't be what's doing the work — it's the causal-historical slot; (3) embryo selection: if the ten embryos are c-candidates, then on a person-affecting view choosing among them has a reading on which it's better for the child who results, without pretending that child would have existed otherwise. I also lean on monozygotic twinning as the case where this beats identity talk: before the split there's one organism, after it there are two, and I want to say the pre-split thing stands in this relation to both.

What I want from you is my prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll *actually* get from that specific room, not the ones I'd get from a general metaphysics audience, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it (I want the animalist to sound like an animalist and the decision theorist to sound like a decision theorist), each with a roughly two-minute answer and an honest note on where the answer is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes rather than get forced into later, and which single reply has to land for the whole thing to survive. And include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks — I think it's something about c-candidacy not being transitive and so not an equivalence relation and so not remotely a candidate for being identity, but give me the strongest version of it and tell me whether I can bite that bullet without sliding all the way into the Parfitian's position, which is definately not where I want to end up.
