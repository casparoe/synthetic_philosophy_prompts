Two programmes within the biotechnological project of abolishing suffering — the project that takes the hedonistic imperative as its charter — have spent a decade arguing past one another. Take the two labels below as stipulated, whatever either camp is called elsewhere.

The conservationists hold that the machinery of aversion is functional and worth keeping: pain, fear, grief, nausea and frustration carry information, motivate avoidance and repair, and give structure to achievement and attachment. Their target is the dynamic range, not the mechanism — cut off the upper tail of suffering, compress what remains, and raise the floor of default hedonic tone, while leaving the signals themselves intact and in working order.

The abolitionists hold that the machinery is the defect. Any system that must make an organism's experience bad for it in order to get the organism to act is coercive engineering, and a mature biotechnology should replace it outright: non-aversive states carrying the same information and motivating the same behaviour, plus a ceiling on attainable well-being far above any present human baseline.

Both camps already accept, and the document may take as given, the following: that the hedonic set-point and its range are heritable traits and therefore engineering parameters; that the familiar objections — the experience-machine worry, the claim that depth and achievement require struggle, the charge that a life without suffering would be shallow or boring — are serious and must be answered rather than waved away; that germline interventions raise a distinctive problem because the people who live with them cannot consent to them; that any intervention should be evaluated by its effects on the whole affective economy, not only on the channel it targets; and that wild and domesticated nonhuman animals are inside the scope of the project.

A foundation has funded a two-year adversarial collaboration between them, and the deliverable is due now. Three concrete proposals forced the collaboration, and the document must address each:

The Ceiling Protocol — a heritable intervention that leaves the motivational and informational role of aversion intact but installs a hard upper bound on how bad any episode can feel, roughly at the intensity of a bad toothache, together with a raised floor of baseline mood. The open questions include whether the ceiling should be uniform or developmentally indexed, whether it should be fixed or titratable by a reversible pharmacological or genetic key held by the person themselves, and whether distress about the ceiling's own existence must be capped recursively as well.

The spillover problem — the empirical hypothesis that compressing one aversive channel displaces distress into others (anxiety, itch, nausea, restless dissatisfaction), so that total aversion is conserved rather than reduced. Both camps have staked reputations on opposite predictions here.

The Reference Cohort — the proposal to maintain, by voluntary and rotating consent, a small population with unmodified affective architecture and an archive of first-person reports, so that later generations can compare what was lost or gained. One camp is tempted to call this a moral atrocity maintained for epistemic reasons; the other to call it the only way the project could ever know what it had done.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. Format it as follows.

Section A: a numbered list of eight to twelve claims both sides accept. They must be non-vacuous — nothing either side would dismiss as a truism or as its own position restated in weaker language — and where a claim is accepted for different reasons, say so in a clause.

Section B: a table of the residual disagreements, one per row, with columns for the issue, the conservationist formulation, the abolitionist formulation, and a verdict. Each formulation must be one its own side would actually put its name to, not a version chosen to look weak. The verdict cell should say whether the disagreement is substantive, verbal, or partly each, with a one-line justification.

Section C: the cruxes — the three to five sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side's prescription about the Ceiling Protocol, the Reference Cohort, or both. For each, give the sub-question itself; the study, case, or form of argument both sides agree in advance would bear on it; the conservationist's prediction; the abolitionist's prediction; and, separately, what each side would count as a result that should move it. At least two cruxes should have predictions recorded in a form that could later be scored, and at least one should be a crux on which both sides name in advance the finding after which they would change their own funding recommendation rather than the other side's mind.

Section D: a short closing note stating which of the disputes turned out to be about words, and giving the re-description that dissolved it, or explaining why none did.

Two constraints. First, argue at the level of functional architecture: the document should not rest on contested neurobiology, and if any receptor, circuit, gene or technique is named, it must be flagged as an illustrative placeholder. Second, the statement should be self-contained and cite nothing — no bibliography, no named studies, no appeal to what some author has argued; the evidence each crux appeals to should be described in enough detail that a reader could run or reconstruct it without looking anything up. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words.
