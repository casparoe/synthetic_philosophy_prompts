So I'm giving a talk next Wednesday in the development economics seminar here, and it's the chapter of my thesis I'm least sure about, and I've realised I have no idea what the Q&A is going to look like, because the room is basically not made of philosophers. It's about fifteen applied micro people — mostly experimentalists, a couple of political-economy/historical types who work on colonialism and state formation — plus three or four folks who come down from the aid agency and the finance ministry, and one political philosopher who's a cosmopolitan consequentialist and is on my side in spirit but thinks my whole framing is a detour.

The thesis I'm defending: what rich countries owe poor ones is a liability, not a need. More specifically, I argue that the size of the obligation tracks what I call the co-imposition ledger — the density and severity of the institutional arrangements the two countries are jointly locked into and that harm the poorer one: trade and investment rules, tax treaties and the transfer-pricing architecture, the debt restructuring regime, arms transfers, carbon emissions. Not how poor the recipient is, and not how cost-effective a marginal dollar would be. The sketch of the argument is (i) the standard interactionist/relational accounts are right that mere causal interaction is too thin to generate duties but wrong to stop at that, because what matters is coercion-backed institutional exposure rather than trade volume; (ii) so the right scoreboard for whether a donor is meeting its obligations is not net ODA as a share of national income — which is a nonsense statistic anyway, counting in-donor costs and tied procurement — but an estimate of harms traceable to imposed institutions; (iii) and because the duty is owed to the people who were harmed rather than to their governments, the transfers that discharge it should look like compensation rather than projects: unconditional, routed to households rather than through donor procurement, framed in legal rather than charitable terms. Expected side effect, which I half embrace: on this metric a lot of middle-income countries rank above the least-developed ones, because that's where the joint institutional exposure is.
