I've been reading rationalist blogs since the old LessWrong era, and right now I'm building a small benchmark of Bongard-style problems: six figures on the left all have some property, six on the right don't, and the solver (LLM or human) has to state the rule. My two coauthors and I are grading the rule descriptions ourselves and we keep loosing the same argument, so I want to hand it to undergrad RAs.

The arguments are always one of three: (a) the rule fits all twelve boxes but obviously isn't the one I had in mind when I drew them (I intended something like "the outline is closed"; the submission says "an even number of separate marks"); (b) the rule fits only as a disjunction of facts about individual boxes; (c) the rule is arguably my intended rule stated differently, or is simpler than mine and agrees with mine on every figure my generator could ever emit, so I can't tell whether to mark it right without making my gold labels circular.

Write me a grading procedure an RA with no philosophy and no ML background can run in about five minutes a problem, with me not in the room. I need: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point where the procedure just stops with "not solved", and the point where it has to escalate to me because what's left is a question about what the benchmark is even measuring rather than about the submission. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat — anything involving simplicity especially, since I suspect most of our disputes are really about whose priors get to decide what the "natural" rule is. Decisions have to be reproducible across RAs and loggable in one line, and I'm the only one who reads escalations, so cap how many a batch can generate.

Then run it on three submissions: (1) the even-number-of-marks rule above, against my intended closure rule; (2) a five-way disjunction that fits the twelve exactly and is shorter to type than my rule; (3) a rule that isn't mine, is simpler, and agrees with mine on every figure the generator can produce — where I suspect the honest outputs are "the model solved it" and "my problem is broken and should be dropped." Finally, say what the procedure can't do at all.
