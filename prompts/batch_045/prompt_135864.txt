ok so context, because I think it matters: I work part-time as a study coordinator on a study that enrolls older adults with mild cognitive impairment, and separately I help run the consent desk at a local rope jam a couple of times a month. Both of those keep throwing the same problem at me from opposite directions, so I finally sat down and wrote out what I actually believe, and I want to know whether the package hangs together. Here goes, roughly in the order I wrote it down.

(1) On deception: I think a lie invalidates consent only if the truth was a dealbreaker for that particular person. If they'd have gone ahead anyway knowing the thing they were lied to about, the consent stands, and the lying was wrong for some other reason — not because it broke the consent. That's why I think the guy at the last jam who badly overstated how much experience he had did something shitty, but I don't think his partner's yes was void: she told us afterwards she'd have tied with him anyway, she just wanted to have been asked honestly. (2) Threats invalidate consent and offers don't, and I want to keep that line. (3) But I also want to say that a yes to a merely okay offer can fail to count, or at least count for less, when the menu the person is choosing from was shaped by injustice — no threat, no lie, fully competent, they'd give the same answer tomorrow, and I still think something has gone wrong with the consent. I've started calling these 'menu complaints' as opposed to 'hand complaints' about how the choice itself was made, and I've noticed this is the only item on my list that isn't about what's going on inside the person's head at all. (4) Competence is decision-relative: the threshold slides with what's at stake and how complicated the decision is, so someone can be competent to say yes to the low-stakes thing and not the high-stakes one, and being a bit drunk slides you down the scale without putting you at zero. (5) The point of all these conditions is self-governance, not preference-matching: consent isn't just a signal of what you want, it's you taking the reins. Relatedly, I have no grip on 'human dignity' talk and I'd rather not have to start leaning on it. (6) You don't need to have actually understood the information, you need to have had a genuine shot at it — if the form was in front of you in your own language and you didn't read it, that's on you and the consent is fine. (7) And here's the case I can't get straight. Last month one of our newer research assistants walked a participant through a consent form and said something like 'honestly it's all standard, you don't need to read the whole thing.' The risks were in the form, she signed, and when we caught it and called her she said she wasn't bothered and would have signed anyway. My gut is very firmly that this was NOT valid consent and I've been pushing to re-consent her — but on my own list, (6) says she had the shot and didn't take it and (1) says she'd have signed anyway, so I seem to owe the opposite verdict. (8) At the jam our house rule is that we never act on a yes from someone who's clearly intoxicated, even though by (4) I think some of those yeses are morally valid. I've been treating that as a practical rule rather than a moral one and I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or a dodge.

So: audit this for me. Which pairs are genuinely pulling against each other and which only look like they are — I'd bet at least one of the things I'm losing sleep over is me conflating two different questions. Rank what you find by severity instead of listing eight equal-sized problems, and for each one say what specifically would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere on the list. I'd also like to know whether the whole thing can be repaired by dropping exactly one commitment or whether the foundations need rebuilding, and please tell me how confident you are about that and where you're going out on a limb — hedged and honest beats smooth and certain. And push back plainly if any of this is confused: if my reaction in (7) is just me being annoyed at the research assistant rather than a verdict about consent, if (4) and (5) actually do conflict, if 'menu complaints' is a distinction without a difference, or if the (1) call about the guy at the jam is me being squeamish. I'd much rather be told one of these is a mess than have the whole set politely reconciled.
