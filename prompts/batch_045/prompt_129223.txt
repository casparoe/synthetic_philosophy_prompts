I run a small reading group — six of us, most with some graduate philosophy but no formal theology, plus one machine-learning engineer — and we have spent the last two sessions on divine foreknowledge and human freedom, specifically on the cluster of views around middle knowledge. We make a handout each session, and the flowchart we attempted last time failed in an instructive way: every branch was phrased in terms of labels ("do you accept middle knowledge?", "are you a compatibilist?"), so it just sorted people by which name they liked. I want to do it properly this time. Please build me a decision tree that takes a participant from a plainly stated starting commitment to a named position, where every branch node is a single question about a concrete case or claim, phrased so that someone who has never heard the word "Molinism" can answer it and only learns the name of where they landed at the leaf. The first two or three nodes should not mention God at all — start from something like whether there is a truth about what a particular person would freely have done in a circumstance that never arises (a colleague who is never offered the bribe; a voter who never sees that ballot; a tie-breaking choice in which her settled character favors neither option) — and bring the theological commitments in only once the counterfactual question has been settled.

Three structural requirements, if you can manage them. First, build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: pick four or five of your diagnostic questions as the load-bearing ones, lay out the grid of answer combinations they allow (16 to 36 cells, whatever the shape actually is), and then locate the real positions in that grid, marking which cells are occupied, which are empty, and why each empty cell is empty — incoherent, merely unattractive, collapsing into a neighbor under pressure, or just unexplored. Second, the leaves must include: the Jesuit middle-knowledge view; its congruist variant; the Dominican/Thomist position with physical premotion or premotion-style divine concurrence; Reformed or broadly Calvinist compatibilism; simple foreknowledge without middle knowledge; the view that trades on a hard-fact/soft-fact distinction about what God believed in the past; the timelessness/eternalist response; open theism; the position that such counterfactuals are true but brute or ungrounded; and the position that they are true in virtue of the agent's own individual nature and hence knowable by someone who grasped that nature exhaustively (I have seen this given a name, but the label matters less than the cell). Include, too, any cell you think is genuinely unoccupied but worth naming as a hole, such as the combination that accepts the counterfactuals and accepts exhaustive divine knowledge but denies that they are knowable in principle. Each leaf should get one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where two leaves cannot be separated by your questions — say because they agree on every case you can pose and differ only in metaphysical gloss or in what they say about grace — flag the pair explicitly rather than inventing a question to pry them apart.

Finally, my engineer wants a second pass, and this is the part I care most about: run a translation test on the finished tree. Replace God with an engineer who has a perfect simulator of a system's training run but no power to alter it, and replace the agent with a system that is asked what it would have output on an input it never in fact received. Which nodes survive the translation unchanged, which become unaskable or change their meaning, which leaves collapse into one another or become attractive for the first time, and does anything in the secular version correspond to the disagreement about whether the counterfactuals are grounded? Keep the whole thing under about nine questions and fourteen leaves, write for a reader who is smart but not a specialist, and define each label in one line at the leaf instead of assuming it. One thing I would rather you not do: no passages quoted from primary sources and no suggestions of what we should read next — our group is reading from memory and argument only this term, and inventing a quotation or a bibliography would be worse than useless to us.
