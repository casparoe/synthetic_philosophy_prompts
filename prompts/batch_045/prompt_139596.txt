I work as a research nurse and I coordinate the consent conversations for a clinical research unit at a teaching hospital. I have been asked to run a ninety-minute training session for the unit's staff: sixteen people, mostly study coordinators, plus two junior physicians and a data manager, in a window nobody thinks is long enough, in a windowless room with one long table, a whiteboard and a stack of index cards. Six weeks ago I was on the other side of that conversation. My mother, who has early-stage vascular dementia, was enrolled in a drug study, and I was the person initialling the boxes and saying yes on her behalf. Almost nothing about that conversation was wrong in the way the textbook says consent can go wrong, and I have been unable to stop thinking about it since.

So I do not want the session to be a refresher on information, voluntariness and competence as three separate boxes to tick. Everyone in that room can already pass that quiz, and the conversations that actually go badly in our unit are not the ones where somebody lied, or issued a threat, or enrolled a patient who was plainly not capable. I want them to run into the situations where all three conditions appear satisfied and something is still off.

Three such situations matter most to me, and I would like them built into the activity rather than named by me.

The first is what I have started calling, privately, the authored option set. Nobody lies and nobody threatens; the patient is competent; and yet the person asking has decided which options exist at all — what is on the menu, what is not, what the default is, what has been described in detail and what has been summarized in half a sentence. The influence sits entirely in the construction of the choice, and it is invisible if you inspect only the moment of saying yes.

The second is sequencing: a chain of small agreements, each of which is trivially fine on its own, arranged so that the later ones are hard to refuse because of the earlier ones. Individually voluntary, jointly engineered. I have watched our own staff do this without any intention to do it.

The third is temporal: capacity that fluctuates, consent given clearly at one point in time, and dissent — sometimes articulate, sometimes not — at a later one. My mother agreed to be in that study in a lucid week and objected in a bad one. I want them to feel that the validity of a consent is not settled by the moment it was signed.

What I need from you is the activity itself. I would like the sixteen people to be doing something — roles, a staged consent conversation, a sequence of real choices under real but small pressure — arranged so that they generate the problem themselves before anyone names it. Ideally people rotate, so that everyone spends time both as the one asking and as the one being asked. I would like each participant to produce something short and concrete during the exercise that can be read out afterwards, so that the debrief is driven by what happened in the room rather than by me asserting a view from the front. Please give me the setup, the roles, the rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief: which moment in the exercise maps onto which of the three problems, what I should say when people resist the mapping, and how I close.

Three constraints, and one worry. The constraint is that some of the people in the room are sceptical that there is a problem here at all: their view, stated in advance, is that an informed, competent and uncoerced yes is consent and that is the end of it. I want them to run into the residue rather than being told it exists, and I do not want the labels up front. No one should be asked to disclose anything about their own medical or family history, because several of them have been patients here. And the exercise has to work with paper, the furniture and the whiteboard — no pre-reading, no video, no preparation on their part.

The worry is this: an exercise about steering people's choices is also a training manual for steering people's choices. I want them to leave less able to extract a signature, not more able to. I would like you to design against that explicitly, and to tell me where you think the risk is unavoidable.

Finally, please be honest about what the exercise cannot do. It cannot reproduce illness, dependency on the people in the room, fear, the asymmetry of knowing that these are the people who will be caring for you next month, or what it is actually like to lose and regain the thread of a conversation. Some of that I will have to supply from my own experience, and I would rather know in advance which parts are mine to carry. Tell me also what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the derailments, the gamers, the people who will overplay the patient, the parts that will overrun — and what I should do about each.
