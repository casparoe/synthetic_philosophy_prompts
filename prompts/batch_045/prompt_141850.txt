You are a professional philosopher of art who has taught aesthetics many times and is skilled at building classroom instruments. Take your time and think carefully: before you write anything, map out the logical space for yourself — which combinations of answers the cases below actually permit — and only then attach labels to the combinations. Do not start from a memorized list of theories and work backwards to questions; build the questions first and let the positions fall where they do.

I am writing a one-page diagnostic handout for a first-year aesthetics course. I want a case-based quiz that sorts students onto a position about the definition of art — eight questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto: aesthetic or functionalist essentialism, the institutional view, the intentional-historical view, cluster or weighted family-resemblance accounts, and neo-Wittgensteinian anti-essentialism (the view that there is nothing to define). No question may ask what art is, or ask students which theory they accept; they must be sorted by what they say about cases. Every question should be answerable by someone who has never heard the labels.

The cases must include (you may add up to three of your own): a physically perfect forgery of an acknowledged masterpiece that everyone still believes is genuine; a ritual object made in a culture with no category of fine art, later installed in a museum, then repatriated and put back into ritual use — can a thing stop being art and start again; an object physically identical to a famous painting but produced by a community that has no art practice at all; a work never shown or described to anyone and destroyed on its maker's death; a mathematical proof and a composed chess problem, both widely admired for their elegance; and an unaltered natural object placed in a gallery and simply declared a work.

Build the discriminating questions around these axes: whether art-status can supervene on perceptible features alone or must be relational; whether the relational property required is present institutional uptake or a historical-intentional relation to earlier works; whether status can be acquired or lost over time; whether the concept applies to objects made before any art practice existed; and whether an evaluative or functional component is built into the definition or whether there can be wholly unqualified bad art.

Finally, flag two things: any combination of answers your cases permit that no named position occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of why it is empty (incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored); and any pair of leaves your eight questions cannot separate, naming the additional case that would pull them apart. Note which single question does most of the classificatory work. Keep the whole thing compact enough to photocopy.
