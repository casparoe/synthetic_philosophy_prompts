I edit a fortnightly in-house journal for risk and compliance staff at a mid-sized bank, and for the next issue I would like to run a piece of satire: a leaked set of internal documents from a fictional institution — say, the Office of Epistemic Clearance at Meridian Trust — that has adopted a position in the debate about pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism and attempted to implement it as policy. I encountered this material years ago in a philosophy seminar and came away both convinced by it and unable to take it entirely seriously; I would like the piece to work for a reader who knows the literature well while remaining legible to a compliance officer who has never met the phrase pragmatic encroachment. My target is not a person but a position — the family of views on which whether someone counts as knowing depends partly on what is at stake for someone — together with the style of institutional discourse that would try to administer it.

The documents I have in mind are: (1) a compliance circular, with numbered clauses and a schedule of stakes-to-evidence exchange rates, announcing that attributions of knowledge are now indexed to the practical environment of the decision in which they are made; (2) anFAQ for branch staff, covering what may be said to a customer under the new attestation ladder, why the phrase I know has been withdrawn from the approved script, and how to handle a customer whose evidence has not changed but whose epistemic standing has; and (3) a short disciplinary case file in which an employee is cited for failure to know on a Friday, with the file conceding that her evidence base was identical to Monday's. Two wrinkles I would particularly like worked in, because they seem to me to follow from the view rather than from anything the bank invented: the workaround in which one large high-stakes decision is decomposed into a chain of many small low-stakes ones so that the relevant knowledge claim stays true at every step — marketed to clients as a fee-generating service called epistemic structuring — and a classified-stakes provision under which the considerations that lower an applicant's standing are themselves non-disclosable, so that no applicant can ever determine whether they know anything. A friend who works in model-risk validation also pointed out that the Office's own rulings set the stakes, and that the stakes then settle what the Office itself can be said to know; if there is a clean way to let the circular tie itself in that knot, please use it. Keep the whole thing deadpan — no winking, no asides to the reader, no disclaimer that this is satire — and let the humor come from stretching what these views genuinely say rather than from attributing to them something they do not; a specialist should finish the piece feeling that the target was understood and, grudgingly, that the shot was fair. Around 800 to 1,000 words total across the three documents.

Two things after the piece. First, a brief apparatus note of no more than 250 words, since some of our readers will want to know what is real here: please separate what is relatively settled among people who work on this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not let the documents present a contested reading as the consensus. My own understanding, which you should correct if it is wrong, is that the claim that ordinary knowledge attributions shift with stakes in the familiar bank-style cases is comparatively well supported, while whether that shift is a matter of semantics, of conversational pragmatics, or of the property of knowledge itself is contested, as is whether knowledge is the norm of action and assertion, and whether any of these views can handle stakes that the subject has no way of access. Second, a table with the following columns: Section (which of the three documents, and which clause or exchange within it); Doctrine_sent_up (the specific position, argument, or move being parodied, named plainly); Real_feature_exaggerated (what that position actually says that the passage stretches, in one sentence); Liberties_taken (where the passage rests on a contested, partisan, or simplified reading, or where the joke requires something the position does not commit to — say none where there are none); and Status (settled or contested, plus one clause identifying the live dispute). One row per joke-bearing passage, roughly six to ten rows.
