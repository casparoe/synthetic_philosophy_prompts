You are a professional philosopher who has taught the socialist calculation debate many times — Mises's argument that an economy without market prices for factors of production cannot perform economic calculation, Lange's market-socialist reply in which a planning board sets accounting prices and adjusts them by trial and error, and Hayek's move from the calculation problem to the problem of dispersed, local and partly inarticulable knowledge — and who has taught it specifically to audiences of engineers and computer scientists.

I run a fortnightly reading-and-argument evening at work for about a dozen people on our machine-learning platform team: strong engineers, effectively no formal economics, and unanimous in advance on one point — that Mises and Hayek were complaining about the computing power and data collection available in the 1920s and 1930s, and that a planner with telemetry everywhere and a large enough model makes the whole debate a historical curiosity. I have fifty minutes, a whiteboard, and no slides. Nobody will have read anything beforehand, and I want no reading list: everything has to come out of me in the room.

So plan the session for me; do not give me the explanation itself. Think the structure through carefully before you write.

- The order the components must go in, and what depends on what. In particular: tell me where in the sequence to introduce what I'll call the PriceBot stipulation — grant them, for the sake of argument, a planner with costless, perfect, real-time information about every input, technique and preference in the economy, and unbounded computation. My worry is that introducing it early buys their attention but makes Mises look trivially answered, while introducing it late makes it feel like a rhetorical trick. Tell me where you would put it, and exactly what has to be in place beforehand for it to do the job I want, which is to separate the three things my audience will otherwise run together: a complaint about calculation, a complaint about knowledge that exists only in forms no one can transmit, and a complaint about incentives and discovery.
- One concrete case to carry the whole fifty minutes, with the properties it must have and the properties that would make it useless. Constraints: no factories standing in for capitalism, no pencils, no framing in terms of who owns the means of production. It has to force a decision about intermediate goods whose worth is not readable off the physical inputs, and it has to be a case where "just simulate it" does not obviously settle the matter.
- The misconceptions this audience will bring or form, and the precise point in the sequence at which I should head off each. The ones I anticipate: the complaint about computing power above; "Lange answered it, so that was the end of the objection"; "the conclusion must be that planning is inefficient, full stop"; and an unnoticed slide from the calculation question into questions about incentives and innovation.
- One question I can put to them in the last five minutes that would show me whether they have grasped the shape of the disagreement rather than merely re-stating the verdict they arrived with.
- What I should openly decline to go into at this level, and say that I am declining it.

Lead with a single sentence — the one thing you want them still to believe in six months' time — and keep the whole answer under 700 words.
