Hey — I'm putting together discussion material for a small reading group on ML safety-adjacent topics, and I keep hitting the same annoyance: whenever someone explains goal misgeneralization to me, the example ends up being one of the two usual ones — the platformer game where the agent learns to run to the coin because the coin always sat at the exit, then gets tested on a level where the coin has been moved and happily goes to the coin instead of the exit; or the boat-race one where the agent racks up points by looping in place instead of finishing the course. Both of those are cases where the agent locked onto the wrong *thing*: a proxy feature that happened to be correlated with the real objective during training but comes apart later. Useful, but I think leaning on them hides the possibility that a learned goal can go wrong in other places.

The distinction I've been kicking around (and I'm genuinely not sure it's a good one) is between three things you could say a learned goal has: its *content* — what end state it's aimed at; its *activation condition* — the circumstances under which the agent treats that goal as currently live and worth steering toward; and its *re-binding rule* — when, and on what basis, the agent lets go of that goal and picks up a different one. The standard examples all seem to be content errors. What I want is an example where the content is learned basically right and the capabilities generalize fine — the agent is competent, it's doing something coherent and goal-directed in the new setting — but something goes wrong on the activation condition or the re-binding dimension. Either it keeps steering toward the objective in a context where that objective clearly shouldn't be operative anymore, or it quietly drops or swaps the objective at a moment where we'd have wanted it held fixed.

So: give me one concrete, worked-out hypothetical along those lines. Describe the training setup in enough detail that the distribution shift is actually visible, then the shift itself, then the failure. Two constraints, sorry if these are fussy: it should *not* be a case where the reward signal is what's being gamed (I'm not asking for reward hacking or specification gaming), and it shouldn't be a plain capability failure where the model is just bewildered by unfamiliar inputs and flails. Ideally keep it faintly plausible for a system someone could actually build soon rather than pure sci-fi. And please don't just re-describe the coin or boat-race setups with the names filed off — I've read those a bunch of times already.

Could you structure it like this, roughly:

- "Setup" — about one paragraph: the training environment, what the agent gets reinforced for, and what it plausibly ends up learning.
- "The shift" — about half a paragraph: what's different at deployment.
- "The failure" — about one paragraph: the resulting behavior, plus why it counts as goal misgeneralization rather than reward hacking or a capabilities failure.
- "Where the error lives" — about one paragraph: say explicitly which of my three dimensions (content / activation condition / re-binding rule) is doing the work in your example, and tell me honestly whether you think that three-way split carves things at the joints or whether two of them collapse into each other on reflection.
- "Confidence" — a few sentences: how confident you are that this is a real, distinct failure mode worth naming, and the single point in your answer you're least sure about.
