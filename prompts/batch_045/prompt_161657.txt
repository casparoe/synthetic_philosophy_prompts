I have helped run a persistent multiplayer sandbox world for about eleven years: a game with player-built structures, a player-run market, and characters that some people have played continuously since the server opened. I keep meeting a pattern in how our players judge the things in that world, and I want to know whether it has a name in philosophy and, if not, what I should call it.

The pattern in one sentence: our players will say, when asked, that these things are not real objects at all, and yet they treat differences in how one of them came into existence as mattering enormously, including in cases where the two candidates do not differ in any respect anyone can observe.

Three cases. First: an item obtainable only during a one-month event years ago. An operator can create a new one with a single command; the client cannot tell the two apart, nothing about the second one is different on screen, and both behave identically in every way. Players will not trade the old one for the new one, they will expel anyone who makes new ones, and they describe the new ones as not really being that item. The same players will also tell you that nothing real is lost when the server is finally shut down.

Second: a cathedral built on the server over two years by some forty people; someone demolishes it. Our players are certain a bad thing happened, and they are equally certain that what happened was bad for the builders rather than for the cathedral. But when an operator restores the structure by pasting in an exact copy of it as it stood, built by no one, they say the cathedral was not brought back. They prefer a rollback of the whole world to before the demolition, even though the rollback also destroys two hours of unrelated work that they then have to argue about.

Third, a case that makes me doubt my own description: some items in the world carry scripted descriptions attributing to them a history that the developers invented and that everyone playing knows to be invented. Players pay more for these and treat them as having a history. So whatever is going on is not simply sensitivity to actual causal origin.

Two contrasts I have noticed. Nobody cares which individual coins they are paid in, only how many, so the pattern seems to attach to things treated as individuals rather than as quantities. And I have wondered whether this is only the familiar business about first editions and perfect forgeries, with nothing virtual about it at all — except that a first edition differs physically from a reprint, and my two swords do not differ in any respect whatsoever.

I do not want a label on its own. I want to know what the phenomenon really is: whether it is a claim about identity, or about value, or about the norms of a practice, or about what reasons people take themselves to have; whether the players' own insistence that the objects are unreal is essential to it or incidental; how it sits with the nearby things I have half-heard of — the worry about perfect copies of artworks, the puzzle of why we respond emotionally to characters we know do not exist, the view that money and property are real only given a shared practice — and which of those it is not; where its edges lie, given the fungibility contrast; and, if nothing established fits, a defined term together with the cases it covers and the cases it should be kept apart from. References are not needed.
