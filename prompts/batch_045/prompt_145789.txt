I'm writing the framing chapter of a dissertation on the unity of consciousness, and before I measure the existing theories against anything I want the specification itself. Write the desiderata for an account of what makes a set of experiences belong to a single conscious subject. Fix the purpose in advance: the criterion has to be good enough to guide clinical and legal ascription of agency and responsibility, and to support claims about what it is like for the patient.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Draw at minimum on: the standard post-callosotomy picture, where the verbally reporting left hemisphere appears to lack access to whatever drives the left hand and confabulates a rationale for it; people born without a corpus callosum, and children who have had a hemisphere removed, who look unified in ordinary life; and the phenomena within a single hemisphere where experience and report come apart. Then two cases I stipulate rather than cite: first, a callosotomy patient asked the same question through headphones in both ears at once writes two incompatible answers, one with each hand, each answer disclaiming knowledge of the other, and yet when asked which of the two answers is hers both hands indicate the same one; second, an implanted cortical link of adjustable bandwidth bridging the two hemispheres, or two people's cortices, so that integration can be dialed continuously up and down.

Include the distinctions the criterion must not collapse: unity at a time against unity over time; phenomenal co-consciousness against availability for report, reasoning, and action; how many subjects there are against how many persons or agents. Say whether the theory owes a determinate count in every case or may return a matter of degree, and say what would make the split-brain evidence bear on experience at all rather than only on report pathways. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I expect trouble between refusing to reduce phenomenal unity to functional access and demanding a standard third parties could in principle apply to a subject who cannot speak, and between treating unity as graded and needing a verdict in the legal case. Keep the weight on the criteria. Close with two or three lines each on the main families of view — access or workspace accounts, phenomenal or co-consciousness accounts, integration accounts, higher-order accounts, and the view that there is no determinate answer to how many subjects there are — and say which single desideratum does the most to narrow the field.
