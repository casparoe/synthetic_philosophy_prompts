I teach a 90-minute seminar for about nineteen second-year undergraduates on media, opinion, and democracy. They have read nothing by Kierkegaard so far; next week they read a short essay of his from the 1840s on the press and the present age, and I don't want to spoil it, so no lecture up front and no mention of him, the essay, or the technical sense of "the public" until the debrief. What I need is a runnable activity that makes them feel, before it is named, the difference between a disagreement among people who are actually present in a room and an aggregate of anonymous opinions — the thing where nobody is answerable, nobody can be replied to, and the resulting view is one that each of the individuals who composed it would quietly disown. Here is how I understand the claim I'm trying to stage, and I'd like you to correct me first if any of it is off before you design around it: the public is not a people, a generation, a community, an assembly, or even a crowd but an abstraction brought into existence by the press; it is never present anywhere; nobody is a member of it, not even the person who writes for it; it can be addressed but cannot answer or act; it levels qualitative distinctions into a kind of mathematical equality of opinion ("about so and so many uniformly make one individual," as he puts it somewhere in that essay); and it lets people hold opinions about everything while escaping the actuality of deciding and being answerable for a decision, which is why the counter-ideal is the single individual who has taken an ethical stance prior to any uniting, rather than a better-managed press.

Build it out of the room: index cards, the board, sticky dots, movable chairs. I'd rather not depend on phones or wifi, though a variant that runs through an anonymous shared document would be a useful backup. There should be real but small stakes: the seminar's remaining €40 of course budget really does go to whichever of two local mutual-aid funds the group settles on, and a colleague has agreed to come in for the last ten minutes to receive whatever the group decided, in whatever form the group actually produced it. My instinct — improve on it as you see fit — is to run the same deliberation twice under different media conditions: once face to face, where the person who proposes a course of action is the person who has to deliver it and sign it, and once through an anonymous board where statements must be posted in fewer than fifteen words and without any first-person pronoun, are sorted by endorsement counts, and where endorsements are frozen at the moment they are given and cannot later be withdrawn, so that by the end the winning position is a snapshot that no one in the room still holds. I also want the leveling to show up as a loss of qualitative distinction rather than mere moderation: ideally the formulation that wins is one that people who genuinely disagree can all accept, for incompatible reasons. Give me the rules, the roles (at least one participant should be the person affected by the decision and one should be whoever compiles and reads out the board), a minute-by-minute timeline, the aggregation or scoring rule you settle on, and a materials list.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in what will actually have happened onto specific parts of the claim, including the moment when someone tries to object to or argue with the aggregate and discovers there is nobody there to receive it. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect at least the quiet-student problem, someone who treats the endorsement counts as a game to be gamed, and the risk that the whole thing lands as "anonymous posting is bad" or "have the courage of your convictions," neither of which is the point (and note that Kierkegaard was himself a prolific and pseudonymous user of the press, and was publicly chewed up by a satirical paper, so the moralistic reading is not available). Be frank about which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce and which part I will simply have to say: my suspicion is that it cannot reproduce the claim that the public has no actuality at all, since even an anonymous classroom still has a facilitator, a shared room, and a grade at the end. Finally, tell me where your own design is most likely to fail to produce the intended experience, which of your choices depend on a contested reading of Kierkegaard, and what observable thing in the room should make me abandon the design mid-session and fall back on something else.
