Ok so me and my friend had a really long argument last night about Confederate monuments, and it collapsed into a fight about one sentence. The actual disagreement was about why the "Lost Cause" version of the Civil War is the one that ended up in schoolbooks and on courthouse lawns for generations afterwards, given that the Confederacy lost about as decisively as it is possible to lose. He kept saying "well, history is written by the victors," as if that settled it. When I pointed out that the Confederates lost, he said I was being too literal: the victors in the relevant sense were the reunified white establishment afterward, who needed a story where both sides were brave and nobody was really to blame, so they absorbed the Southern version because it was convenient. I said that just redefines "victors" as "whoever's story is the one still standing," which makes the line impossible to ever be wrong about. Two hours, nowhere.

There's a bit in there I'd like you to keep. It seems to me losers sometimes have a structural *advantage* at myth-making: a defeat is a humiliating thing that demands an explanation, and it keeps demanding one for generations, while winners can just say "we won" and stop there. Grievance is a renewable fuel in a way that triumph isn't. Call it the grievance asymmetry. He liked that and then turned it on me: who has a grievance worth nursing is itself downstream of who had standing to lose, so it's the victors' story again in the end. I genuinely can't tell if that's a point or wordplay. And he also dragged in Dunkirk — Britain lost the battle and still got to write the story, which he thinks is the same shape; I think that's a case of the losers writing it, and he says that's naive.

So what I want is that one-liner taken apart. What are the distinct precise claims "history is written by the victors" could be making? I have a few axes but definately not all of them: who controls the records, the schools, the monuments in the first place; which accounts get suppressed or simply fail to find a publisher; whether the surviving narrative flatters whoever holds power; whether audiences are disposed to trust the winning side.

Please lay them out as a menu, roughly from the near-trivial ("the side that wins runs the schools afterwards" — sure, often true, so what) to the strong or skeptical ones, and for each one say: whether it's actually plausible; whether the Confederate case is a counterexample to it, a confirming instance, or neither; and whether it's testable at all, meaning what kind of evidence would count against it and which versions are armored so that nothing could. Run each reading past the Confederate case and past at least one of the others — Masada, Kosovo, Dunkirk, whichever makes the distinctions sharpest. What I care about most is which reading he was actually invoking when he used it to end the argument, and whether that reading does the work he needed it to do or whether it's the unfalsifiable one. Please write in American English. And don't tell me who first said the line or what book to read — I don't care, and I can't look anything up from where I am right now.
