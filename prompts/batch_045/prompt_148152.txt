I'm presenting to the science advisory board of a land trust next month and need to be ready for the question period. The board is five conservation biologists (two wetland ecologists, one avian demographer, one restoration ecologist, one invertebrate specialist), one environmental philosopher of a broadly Leopoldian stripe, one programme officer from an animal-welfare foundation, and the trust's director of development.

My thesis is that some of the trust's candidate acquisitions are net-negative on welfare grounds, and that its ranking metric should change accordingly. Compressed, the argument is: (i) the welfare-relevant quantity for a hectare is not the headcount of animals on it at a moment but the flow of sentience-years per hectare-year, disaggregated by life-history stage; (ii) for high-productivity, r-strategist-dominated habitats — say a seasonally flooded grassland supporting enormous numbers of short-lived invertebrates plus the broods of small birds — the great bulk of those sentience-years belong to juveniles who die within days of hatching, and on any reasonable assumption about the welfare of such a life the flow is net negative; (iii) so preserving such a hectare plausibly adds suffering rather than preventing it, and the trust should rank candidate acquisitions by expected welfare flow, favouring low-productivity, K-dominated habitats, and in some cases should simply not oppose low-intensity conversion of high-productivity sites; (iv) two caveats I build in myself — "compensation rebound," since density-dependent response recaptures part of the averted births over time, making my estimate an upper bound on the gain, and "welfare leakage," since if the population is limited by a bottleneck elsewhere the deaths may be displaced rather than averted.

Prepare my prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get — from this audience in particular, in their own vocabulary, not the ones I would get from an effective-altruism forum — ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, identify the single reply that has to land for the ranking proposal to survive, and flag the question I should most hope nobody asks.
