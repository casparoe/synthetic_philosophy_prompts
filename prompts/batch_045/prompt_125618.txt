I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about everyday conventions — who stands where, whether you leave money on the table, when you step off the pavement — and about the odd disproportion in them: that failing at one can leave you feeling, and being made to feel, guilty in a way that seems to have nothing to do with anybody having been harmed. I have recently managed to get inside this question rather than merely report on it, and I'd like your help with the particular case before I file.

I'm five weeks into a reporting trip in a Nordic city where, as I understand the local arrangement, service is included in the price and hospitality wages are set by collective agreement rather than supplemented by gratuities. Three small things have happened.

First: at a neighbourhood restaurant I added roughly fifteen percent when I paid. The server followed me onto the street to return it, visibly embarrassed, and a local friend later explained why. In her account the gesture is not simply unnecessary; it imports a labour model the country has deliberately rejected, it implies that the person standing in front of you is not adequately paid, and it is the sort of thing that, repeated often enough by visitors, begins to loosen the arrangement locals spent decades building. I experienced this as a small act of generosity and am now being told it was something closer to a small act of vandalism.

Second: at a tram stop in the same city there is, to my eye, no queue — people gather in a loose cluster and board. I joined the cluster and boarded with everyone else. A colleague subsequently told me that there is in fact a strict order there, legible only to people who know where to stand on the platform and whose eye to catch, and that I had effectively pushed in ahead of two or three people. I could not have discovered this by looking. I still feel I did something wrong.

Third: at an empty intersection after midnight I waited for the signal. A passer-by laughed, and another local later observed that pedestrians here cross whenever it is clear, that waiting marks you as a visitor, and — this is the part I keep turning over — that a child who watches a row of adults wait at an empty crossing learns something false about when a signal can be trusted.

In each case I did what would be unremarkable, or right, at home. In each case I got it wrong here, and not in a single direction: I over-performed in one and three, and under-performed in two.

Here is the crude principle I've been using to sort this out, which I haven't seen stated anywhere and which I suspect is wrong. Call it the reliance-and-visibility test: a convention generates a genuine obligation for a visitor only if it is load-bearing, in the sense that people have taken jobs, set prices, or planned journeys that presuppose it will hold, and only if it is legible, in the sense that an attentive outsider could have worked out that it existed without being told. The plain queue passes both; the invisible queue at the tram stop fails the second condition, though it arguably passes the first; the local no-tipping convention, on my friend's account, passes both, which would mean I owed it to her not to tip. But my test seems to make obligation hostage to whatever political project a local majority happens to be running — if a city had a convention of not acknowledging service workers at all, my test would apparently tell me to adopt it — and it says nothing about why I felt guilty at the empty crossing, where nobody relied on me and nobody was harmed.

So, concretely: what should I actually have done in each of the three cases, and why? Is there a principled asymmetry between over-conforming and under-conforming, given that skipping the invisible queue seems to take something from an identifiable person while over-tipping seems only to give, and yet both drew the same resentment — and is that resentment moral, or is it political or aesthetic wearing moral dress? Does my status as someone who will leave in a fortnight change anything, especially given that locals know I will leave and may be extending me the latitude routinely extended to guests: do I owe it to them to learn a convention I cannot see, and is accepting their latitude itself a kind of condescension? And where exactly does the harm my friend described come in — does my single tip bear any real relation to the erosion of a wage system, or is that a way of moralizing an objection that is really about who gets to set the terms of life here? If you think my framing of all three as one phenomenon is the mistake, tell me where the disanalogy lies.
