For context, I should say who is asking: I spent a working life as a quantitative geneticist in animal breeding — my days were spent estimating variance components from pedigree data — and since retiring I have been auditing a philosophy of science seminar and attending a small reading group on genetics and human differences. The same argument comes up every month and never moves. On one side are people who treat heritability coefficients for cognitive and educational outcomes as among the better-replicated quantities in differential psychology: substantial, stable across designs, and genuinely constraining, in the sense that any purely environmental account has to explain why they come out where they do. On the other side are people who insist that the coefficient reports only a partition of variance in one population exposed to one range of environments, that it therefore says nothing about what an intervention would do, nothing about differences between groups, and nothing about the causes of an individual's trait, and so carries no policy weight at all. Both sides are entirely familiar with the other's points — the critics can recite the equal-environments assumption and the points about gene-environment correlation and interaction as fluently as anyone, and the defenders know that heritability is population-relative and says nothing about malleability — and yet after a year of lunches nobody has shifted by a millimetre, and the discussion has started to feel like a ritual rather than an inquiry.

I have a candidate diagnosis and I want it tested rather than flattered. Working from my own field, I have been toying with a quantity I call levelled heritability: not the ratio of genetic to phenotypic variance in the population as it is, but the value that ratio would take in the same population if exposures were equalised to the best currently feasible standard — a counterfactual parameter, defined relative to an intervention, rather than a description of the actual spread. I like it because of a feature I keep meeting in practice: improve and homogenise the environment and the heritability estimate often goes up, so that a high coefficient can be a marker of successful equalisation rather than of fixed fate. My hypothesis is that the two camps in my reading group are implicitly reaching for different quantities — one for the ordinary variance ratio in the population as found, the other for something closer to my levelled or counterfactual parameter — and that the second is not identifiable from pedigree, twin or molecular data of the kinds we actually have, so that a hidden question about identifiability is doing most of the work. But I can't tell whether this carves the dispute at a joint or merely relabels it.

So please take the role of a philosopher of science who is genuinely literate in quantitative genetics, and think this through carefully before writing. Begin by brainstorming at least three candidate diagnoses and only then commit to one, saying why you rejected the others. Is the impasse verbal, a clash of standards of evidence, a disagreement about which populations or designs count as data, two questions sharing a name, bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, an ill-posed question, or simply a hard one? Is my levelled-heritability distinction useful, and does it collapse into something the field already has under another name? Does either side actually need the counterfactual parameter, or is a defender entitled to say that only the actual-population ratio was ever at issue? Above all, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a formal identifiability result, a within-family or sibling-based design of the kind I gather now returns smaller estimates than population-based ones, a sharpened distinction, a change of question — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and why. Argue the case on its merits; I do not want a survey of positions, a balance-sheet, or a reading list, just a diagnosis with reasons and a verdict on what would move it.
