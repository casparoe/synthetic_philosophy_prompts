You are a philosopher of probability who has spent years working alongside statisticians and machine-learning researchers, and I would like you to pressure-test a line of argument before it goes into print. Please work in stages: first, brainstorm and lay out every substantive assumption that my team's procedure relies on, flagging any that seem to assume what needs to be proved; then tell me which of those assumptions a reasonably informed philosopher of probability would reject and why; and only afterward give your overall verdict, stated as the strongest version of the objection rather than as a diplomatic summary of both sides.

Some context. I work on a clinical prediction-modeling team at a large hospital, and we recently built a model that estimates a particular patient's five-year recurrence risk after tumor resection. The case that started the argument is a fifty-eight-year-old man with an unusual combination of features: a stage II tumor of a comparatively rare histological subtype, a specific biomarker profile, type 2 diabetes, and a surgery performed by one particular surgeon at our center. Depending on which reference class you place him in — all stage II patients, all patients with his biomarker profile, all patients operated on by that surgeon, all diabetic patients with this histology, and so on — the observed frequency of recurrence ranges from roughly eleven percent to roughly forty percent, and the intermediate classes give intermediate values that are not all mutually consistent with one another. My colleagues and I have converged on what we call the anchored-class rule, and we think it settles the reference class problem for practical purposes. The rule is: define every reference class that can be expressed using the covariates in the data set, fit a separate calibrated model on each, discard the classes whose frequency estimates are not statistically reliable at whatever confidence threshold is fixed in advance, and then choose among the surviving classes by cross-validated predictive performance on held-out data from our own hospital. If two or more classes remain tied, we break the tie in favor of the class that would have been specified at the time of the patient's diagnosis, before anyone could have known his outcome, on the grounds that this removes any suspicion of having gerrymandered the class to fit the result.

My supervisor, who is a philosopher, says that none of this touches the real problem, and — this is the part I cannot follow — that adding more data, which we are about to do by linking three more hospitals, will make things worse rather than better. Her view seems to me backwards: the more data we have, the finer the narrowest class with reliable statistics becomes, so the spread of admissible probabilities should tighten, and in the limit the problem should simply evaporate. She also maintains that reporting a set of probabilities, or an interval, rather than a single number would not help either, which I find even more puzzling, since an interval looks strictly more honest and ought to shrink as the data accumulate. A referee on an earlier draft added that the reference class problem is basically the base-rate fallacy wearing philosophical clothing, and I am unsure whether to read that as a helpful deflation or as a misunderstanding.

So here is what I would like you to assess, in the terms of the philosophical debate rather than of statistical practice. First, does selecting among reference classes by predictive accuracy on a population actually answer the question of what probability to attach to this one patient, or does it answer a nearby question while quietly changing the subject? Second, is my supervisor right that more data can aggravate rather than dissolve the problem, and if so, what exactly is the mechanism? Third, does my formulation in terms of the narrowest class with reliable statistics smuggle in something false — for instance, the idea that candidate reference classes can be ranked by narrowness the way numbers are ranked by magnitude, so that there is always a determinate winner? Fourth, is the referee right that this is all just the base-rate fallacy? And fifth, would moving to interval-valued or set-valued probabilities, or to a fully Bayesian hierarchical model in which the class structure is itself estimated from the data, make the problem disappear, or merely relocate it? Where you think we go wrong, please say precisely what the mistake consists in and what the underlying disagreement is really about.
