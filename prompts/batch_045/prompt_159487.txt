I'm taking a seminar on collective decision-making, and our final session is on judgment aggregation — the discursive dilemma in particular. Each of us is supposed to bring something short to read aloud at the end, and I would rather bring a poem than a restatement of the argument. Could you write me a sestina?

Here is the version of the case I want it built on. A court of six has to settle three connected questions: whether the defendant owed the plaintiff a duty of care, whether breach of that duty caused the harm, and whether the defendant is therefore liable. Every juror accepts the connection — liability holds if and only if duty and causation both hold — and every juror answers consistently. The votes fall into three blocs of two: two jurors accept duty and causation and therefore liability; two accept duty but deny causation and therefore deny liability; two deny duty but accept causation and therefore deny liability. The consequence is the one I want the poem to carry exactly: each question is settled by the same four-to-two margin — the first two affirmatively, liability negatively — no individual juror is guilty of any inconsistency, and yet the set the court endorses as a whole cannot be true. Someone in the room should be able to reconstruct that vote table from the poem alone.

What I would like the form to do is work rather than decorate. The rotation of the end-words in a sestina seems to me to be the right mechanism for two things, and I will leave it to you how they combine. First, under sequential majority voting, the order in which the court takes up the propositions changes the verdict, so the same six jurors and the same opinions yield different outcomes depending on the agenda. Second, the two procedures we discussed are each plainly majoritarian and they disagree: deciding issue by issue on the premises and then drawing the conclusion convicts, while voting on the conclusion directly acquits. Neither is a cheat, and there is no rule that is anonymous and decides each proposition on its own that escapes this. That last point matters to me — I do not want a poem that suggests some cleverer voting rule would have saved the court.

The voice I had in mind is the clerk who has to draft the opinion the next morning and cannot write down both the chain of reasoning only two of the six endorsed and the verdict four of them endorsed. Please let her stay with that difficulty rather than resolve it.

For the mechanics: six unrhymed stanzas of six lines with the standard sestina permutation plus a three-line tornada, around ten syllables to a line. I would like the six end-words to be duty, cause, liable, four, reasons, and opinion — each of them has a second sense the poem could lean on — though if you have a stronger set, use it and tell me in one sentence why. What I do not want is a sneer at democracy, blame laid on any juror, or a tornada that smooths the contradiction into paradox-flavored ambiguity; the closing lines should hold the premise-based verdict and the conclusion-based verdict together in one breath so that a reader who knows the problem recognizes a flat inconsistency. Please write in American English, and keep it to something readable aloud in about two minutes. After the poem, add three or four lines noting which stanzas carry the premise-based and conclusion-based routes and where the agenda-order point enters.
