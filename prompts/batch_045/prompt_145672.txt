I'm a high school senior, and for an independent project in my school's psychology club I ran a small version of a well-known experiment in philosophy and psychology — the one sometimes called the side-effect effect. In the original study, people read about a company chairman who is told by a vice president that a new program will raise profits but will also harm the environment. The chairman says he does not care about the environment at all and only wants profit. The program runs, profits rise, and the environment is harmed. When asked whether the chairman harmed the environment intentionally, roughly four out of five people said yes. In a second version, identical except that the program will help the environment and the chairman says he does not care about helping it, fewer than one in four said he helped it intentionally. The usual reading is that people's moral reaction to the outcome leaks into what is supposed to be a non-moral judgment about the agent's aims.

I tried to push on that reading. My club ran three versions with about forty students, using a story about a student council president approving a fundraising plan. In the harm version the side effect was damage to the courtyard garden; in the help version it was repair to the courtyard garden; in the third version the side effect was that the plan would make the school skip a district paperwork rule — a rule everyone at our school thinks is pointless. The president said in every version that she did not care about the side effect at all and only wanted the money. We got roughly the usual gap between harm and help, but the paperwork version landed in the middle: noticeably more yes answers than the help version, noticeably fewer than the harm version — even though, when we asked afterward, almost everyone said skipping that rule was not morally wrong. (I know the sample was tiny and not random. Please don't spend your answer on that; the question below is meant to be answerable even if you grant me the data.)

Here is the argument I built out of it, which I now think proves far more than I believe.

(1) A person's answer to a made-up case is evidence about a concept only if that answer isn't being moved by factors that aren't part of what the concept is about.

(2) What "intentionally" is about is the agent's aims and foresight: whether the outcome was her goal, or something she foresaw but did not aim at. Whether the outcome is bad, good, or against the rules is a separate question.

(3) The original result shows the answers splitting with the valence of the side effect while the agent's aims and foresight were held fixed. So something besides aims and foresight is moving them.

(4) My third condition suggests the mover isn't specifically *moral* badness: my classmates said the rule-breaking wasn't morally wrong, and the yes-rate still rose above the help condition. So the mover is something broader — roughly, whether the side effect is something that shouldn't happen.

(5) So answers about intentionality aren't evidence about intentional action. And (4) makes it worse than the original study suggested: "shouldn't happen" is present in almost every case anyone bothers to build, since cases get interesting when something goes wrong.

(6) The problem isn't confined to "intentionally." I've been told that people's judgments about what *caused* an outcome shift with whether someone violated a norm, and I've seen it claimed that "knows" behaves similarly.

(7) Causation, knowledge, and intentional action are the load-bearing concepts in the method of cases: intended versus merely foreseen, cause versus mere background condition, knowledge versus lucky true belief. If verdicts involving these are contaminated, every distinction drawn by appeal to cases is unsupported.

(8) Nor can we retreat to a clean subset of cases, because of (5): the contaminating factor appears wherever anything goes wrong, which is where all the interesting cases live.

(9) So the method of cases has to be abandoned everywhere, not just for "intentionally."

(10) But the evidence for (3), (4), and (6) is itself nothing but people's answers to made-up cases. If (9) is right, that evidence is worthless too — so the argument saws off the branch it is sitting on, and it discredits the very researchers whose work inspired it, since they use the same method.

I can't accept (9), and (10) convinces me that something went wrong earlier rather than that I should swallow the conclusion. The claims I'd be unhappiest to give up are (3) and (4), because one of them is my own data, and (1), which just sounds like common sense about evidence. If something has to go, I'd prefer it to be one of the steps that generalizes, (6) or (7).

Please tell me which step you'd reject, why that step looked solid enough to get into the argument, and what each of the other options would cost me if I rejected it instead — including what happens to the intended/foreseen distinction and to the way the law uses words like "intentionally" if I give up (2). I don't need a reading list or any citations; I'd just like the reasoning straightened out. Please answer in American English.
