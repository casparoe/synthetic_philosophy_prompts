So I've been going in circles on endurantism vs perdurantism and I realized I don't actually know what the standard medical practice around advance directives and Ulysses contracts commits you to, metaphysically.

The practice, as I understand it: you fill out a living will, and the hospital asks about it on intake. While you have capacity, your current say is absolute — you can withdraw consent five minutes before surgery, for any reason or none, even if refusing kills you, and even if you signed a form saying the opposite an hour ago. But once you lose capacity, the thing flips: the document written by the earlier you starts speaking for you, and the demented you's actual present protests (I don't want the feeding tube, let me go home) get gently overruled by reference to what you "really" wanted. Meanwhile, the stickier version of this — psychiatric advance directives, where you precommit to being forcibly medicated during a future episode you know you'll refuse treatment in — gets honored way less reliably, and in a lot of places is treated as close to unenforceable. And then on the ward itself nobody can keep the story straight: when the resident with advanced dementia seems content, staff will say "she's not suffering, she seems happy" and treat that as a real reason to keep going, while giving her current *stated wishes* basically zero weight. So the present time-slice counts as a welfare subject but not as an agent, apparently.

I want to know what theory of persistence, or of diachronic identity, this package presupposes — if any. Reconstruct the implicit view: does it only make sense if the earlier self and the later self are literally numerically the same substance wholly present at both times, so that the earlier one's will is just authoritative over its own life full stop? Or does perdurantism license all of it too, or better? I keep tripping over the fact that perdurantism isn't one package: on the worm version the earlier stage is literally a part of the same person, which looks like the strongest possible support for precedent autonomy, and on the stage version the later stage is only the same person in some derivative, comes-in-degrees sense, which gives you a natural reason for the earlier self's authority to decay as psychological connectedness decays — but then the all-or-nothing capacity threshold looks like an artifact rather than something the metaphysics wants.

And please separate out what the practice strictly *presupposes* from what it merely fits comfortably with, and where it's underdetermined and several theories would license the identical behavior. My suspicion is that the refusal/request asymmetry in particular is doing no identity work at all and is really just an asymmetry about vetoes vs. compulsion wearing metaphysical clothing — but I'd like that argued.

Last bit, the thing I actually can't find discussed anywhere: imagine you junked the threshold and made it graded. The directive's weight is a decreasing function of some operationalized continuity measure (memory overlap, value stability, whether the current person still endorses the old projects — pick whatever you can defend), no bright line, and the decision goes to whoever currently has the strongest continuity claim. What would that practice presuppose? Would a perdurantist have to be a revisionist about the current one? And is the honest answer that the existing binary is metaphysically underdetermined and we only have it because the law needs bright lines — or does it actually fall out of one view and not the other?
