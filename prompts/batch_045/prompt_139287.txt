I'm writing a seminar paper and would like a close philosophical reading of Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through the lens of relational conceptions of personhood in the Ubuntu tradition. Please answer in American English. Assume I know the novel and the standard positions: don't summarize the plot, don't survey the literature, and don't cite anything. What I want is an argument that moves between specific scenes and a set of distinctions I'll now set out, and that is honest about where the reading stops working.

The relational claim comes in at least three strengths, and I want them kept apart. Call a view causal if relations are how persons are formed, nourished, and enabled to flourish, while the status of being a person is something the individual already has; call it conferral-based if personhood is a status a community bestows and can therefore withhold, withdraw, or grant only partially; call it constitutive if the relations are metaphysically prior, so that an individual standing outside all of them is not a person at all. A further, separate thesis cuts across these: that personhood is an achievement extended over a life rather than a birthright, and so something one can fail at or forfeit.

Apply these to the creature. First, the interval at the start: Victor abandons him immediately, and for a stretch the creature has no relations with anyone. What does the constitutive view say about that stretch, and can it date the moment at which he becomes a person, or does it have to treat the transition as vague in a way that looks like a defect? Second, the De Lacey cottage: the creature acquires language and a moral vocabulary by observing a family that never addresses him, and the one attempt at direct contact ends in Felix's violence. Is one-sided, unreciprocated participation in a community enough to constitute or confer personhood, or does the tradition require mutuality? Does it matter that the recognition he gets is only negative? Third, the counterfactual: had Victor completed the second creature and the pair withdrawn to live apart from all human beings, we would have a two-member community. Say what the minimum size of a we is on each of the three views, whether a dyad whose only shared relation is mutual dependence on, and shared grievance against, an absent creator would count, and whether relationality has to be non-instrumental to do its work. Fourth, Victor himself: he is thickly embedded in family, university, and friendship, and yet he unravels. Is he best read as a counterexample, a socially situated individual whose refusal to acknowledge what he has made is itself a failure of personhood? Does the novel go further and suggest that withholding recognition degrades the withholder?

I also want the resistance to the reading stated plainly. The novel's form works against it: the creature is given an eloquent first-person narration, he addresses Walton and, through Walton, the reader, and the reader is moved to sympathy by interiority alone. Is that an individualist criterion smuggled in, and does the creature's namelessness, along with Victor's refusal to name him, cut the other way, as a dramatized refusal of the recognition a name would confer? Consider too whether the creature's murders are meant to be read as forfeiture, whether his solitude at the end returns him to the pre-personal state, and whether his disappearance is the novel conceding that personhood cannot survive the loss of its relations.

Ground each claim in a scene you can name, and flag the one scene a hostile critic would use against you. Close by saying what, if anything, the novel shows about relational personhood that the arguments don't: something about the asymmetry of recognition, the temporality of acquiring and losing it, or the dependence of the self on being addressed by someone who can refuse.
