hey — I'm in a decision theory seminar this term (phil dept, cross-listed with econ, mostly grad students in the room which is honestly intimidating) and we spent this week on money pump arguments and whether diachronic rationality really requires your preferences to be transitive. I get the basic move: if you strictly prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, someone can sell you A for B, B for C, and C for A, take a cent each time, and you end up holding what you started with and slightly poorer, and the argument is supposed to be that a preference ordering which does that to you is somehow incoherent. What I cant get straight — and my study group fought about it for like an hour on Tuesday — is whether the pump is a criticism of the preferences or just of being the sort of person who can't say no to a trade. Mark (econ major, insufferable) kept insisting that if you choose "sophisticatedly" — plan the whole sequence backward from the last node and refuse the trades you'd regret — the pump never gets going, so cyclic preferences are basically costless, and I said that's just relabeling the problem, since now you're paying in self-control instead of cents. Priya said the resolute chooser beats both of us: she just decides in advance to stop somewhere and does it, ends up with whichever option she picked, and pays the fee once.

Then Mark came up with a variant he calls the carousel and I can't stop thinking about it. Not three options but forty, arranged in a ring, where you strictly prefer each one to its clockwise neighbour — so there's no best option anywhere on the ring — and the exploiter charges a flat entry fee to get on plus a cent per move, and there is no last round: no terminal node, no point where the ride just ends, you can keep moving as long as you like. I think this breaks Mark's answer, because backward induction needs somewhere to start and this thing has no end, so "choose sophisticatedly" doesn't actually hand you a plan — but Im not confident enough to say that out loud in front of the grad students, and I dont know whether the resolute chooser is even allowed to pick an arbitrary stopping point when every move on offer is, locally, an improvement.
