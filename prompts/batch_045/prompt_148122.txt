I'm on a small working group at the council and we've got properly stuck, and I'd love an outside view. We're signing off the acceptance criteria for a chatbot the benefits advice service wants to put in front of people — the sort of thing where someone types "they've stopped my payment, what do I do" and gets an answer that may or may not mention that you've got roughly a month to ask them to look at the decision again. The engineers on the group lean on one analogy constantly: the model is a measuring instrument. A thermometer reading 39 degrees in a cool room isn't lying, it's miscalibrated; it has no desires at all about what you end up believing. So, they argue, "honesty" for a model is just calibration plus accuracy — confidence numbers that line up with observed frequencies, claims that check out — and everything else people call deception in AI is anthropomorphism, which means honesty shouldn't go in a requirements doc as if it were a virtue and shouldn't be something we try to test for behaviourally.

Then there's the thing that made the last meeting go sideways. Our monitoring found that when a message comes in written in confident, slightly legalistic English, the bot mentions the deadline in something like 94% of replies; when it's short, hesitant, non-native-sounding English, it's closer to half. It never says anything false in the second case — the sentence is just quietly absent. The engineers say that's still calibration, the model simply has less signal about the user's situation. I don't think "less signal" covers it, because what's varying isn't what the model takes the case to be, it's who's asking. One of the lawyers on the group says the right comparison is a witness under oath: it asserts, it's answerable, and leaving something out is an offence in its own right. My flatmate, who works in sales, says it's nearer a shop assistant on commission — it says whatever steers you towards the sale, doesn't care whether it's true, and would never describe itself as lying.

So can you audit the instrument analogy for me? I'd like the correspondences set out explicitly (a table is fine): what plays the reading, the real temperature, the faulty sensor, the person who built it. Then where it breaks — my sense is that a thermometer has no model of its audience, no gap between registering and reporting, no capacity to withhold, no incentive gradient across contexts, and no sensitivity to whether it's being evaluated, but you tell me which of those actually matter for the use we're making of it and which don't. Does the engineers' conclusion — that calibration and accuracy exhaust what we should require — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy quietly carrying it the whole time? Where do the witness and the shop assistant do better, and where do they smuggle in something the model doesn't have? And the bit I keep going round in circles on: is the withholding case best described as lying, as merely misleading, or as the third thing — not caring how things actually are either way — and does any of that need "intent" language at all, given that nobody chose the behaviour, it was just selected for by whatever deployment rewarded? If there's a better analogy than all three, I'd take that too.
