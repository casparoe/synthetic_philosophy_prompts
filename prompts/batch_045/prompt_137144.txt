hey — I'm writing a final essay for a 3rd year paper on consequentialism and its critics, and I want to make sure I'm not building the whole thing on a dodgy reading of Williams. Our TA put together a study guide and the section on the integrity objection reads like this:

"Williams's integrity objection is basically a demandingness objection. Consequentialism tells you to give up your own projects whenever that maximises the good, and since that could be almost all the time, it asks more than any human being can actually give, so it can't be an acceptable moral theory. The mechanism is the doctrine of negative responsibility: consequentialism says I'm responsible for what I fail to prevent just as much as for what I do, so there's no such thing as keeping your own hands clean — that's the point of the George case, the unemployed chemist who's offered a job developing chemical weapons and knows that if he turns it down someone less scrupulous will take it. What gets destroyed by all this is integrity. By integrity Williams means something like being true to your own values and acting consistently with what you say you believe: an agent who has to weigh his deepest commitments — the so-called ground projects — from an impersonal point of view gets alienated from them, treating what is most his own as just one project among many. The one thought too many case makes the same point in miniature: a man who can rescue either his wife or a stranger shouldn't have to think to himself that she's his wife and that rescuing your wife is the sort of thing that's permitted; if that thought even occurs to him he's having one thought too many, which shows he doesn't really love her. And that's the knockout: any theory that lets the agent reason in that partial way has stopped being consequentialist, so consequentialism is refuted — it can't allow agent-relative reasons at all and has to demand that we deliberate from a purely impersonal standpoint about literally everything."

My worry is that some of this is subtley off, but I don't trust myself to say where. The "asks too much" framing especially bugs me, because we did a seperate reading on the demandingness objection (Scheffler, the limits of benevolence, that sort of thing) and Williams seems to me to be doing something different — the complaint feels like it should still work even if the sacrifice demanded is tiny, which would make it not a demandingness point at all. And the bit where integrity gets defined as consistency with your own values sounds more like everyday integrity than the technical thing, since I thought his ground projects don't have to be moral commitments at all. But maybe I'm the one who's confused.

So: could you go through it roughly sentence by sentence and tell me what someone sympathetic to Williams would let stand, what they'd quietly rewrite, and what they'd reject — and then whether the handout's conclusion (consequentialism refuted, no agent-relative reasons allowed) actually follows from whatever survives?
