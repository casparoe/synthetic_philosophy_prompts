I sit on the Acquisitions and Access Committee of the Marshfield and District Record Office, one of two members appointed by the council; the rest of the table is three professional archivists, a historian from the university, and a representative of the district tenants' federation. We have just accessionned two deposits in the same week. The first is the administrative records of the former Marshfield Borough Housing Department, 1948–1996: some 340 boxes of rent ledgers, repairs requisitions, allocation files, committee minutes and correspondence. The second is what is left of the Ravensmoor Tenants' Association: a shoebox of newsletters, a petition, some photographs, and a cassette of one meeting. Ravensmoor was cleared and rebuilt between 1974 and 1981, precisely the years for which the housing department's rent and allocation series have a gap, while the equivalent series for the Aldergate estate run unbroken. A retired housing officer has also offered us an oral history interview, but only on condition that his name is withheld from the catalogue and that he may veto passages before release. Our cataloguing backlog is roughly four years, so realistically we can describe one of these two deposits properly in this round, and the committee has to decide which, and how.

The proposal that has split us comes from our historian member, who wants us to adopt what she calls 'apophatic description': inserting lacuna records — 'silence notes' — into the catalogue wherever a series that ought to exist does not, and, at collection level, a statement about whose voices the deposit does not contain. In draft form her note for this deposit would read something like 'no rent or allocation records survive for Ravensmoor between 1974 and 1981; the series resumes in April 1982', alongside a collection-level paragraph naming tenants, and especially tenants' wives, as the absent parties. She has two arguments I take seriously: that a catalogue which records only what is present makes the department's version of events look complete, and that a researcher who cannot see the shape of the gap cannot even ask why it is there (she calls this 'the second silence' — not the missing records but the missing indication that records are missing). Against this, our senior archivist argues that a silence note is an assertion the record office cannot verify, that it hardens into falsehood the moment a stray box turns up in 2031 and nobody revises it, that it quietly spends our scarce cataloguing budget on describing things we do not hold, and that it is itself an exercise of the archivist's power to say what ought to exist — and, in the tenants' federation representative's version, that a tidy official note about absence lets the council's successor authority look documented rather than silent. There is also a fourth, quieter worry which is my own: that these notes will accrete, will be read in fifty years as evidence of what we in 2026 believed, and will be very hard for anyone to contest once they are in the finding aid.

You are a senior records professional who also works on the philosophy and political theory of the archive, and I would like you to think this through carefully — sketch the options you can see, including ones none of us has put on the table, and be explicit about which of them you are rejecting and why — and then draft the decision memo I can circulate to the committee before our March meeting. It should run to no more than about 1,000 words, be addressed to the committee, and open with a single clear recommendation with a timeline and an indication of who does what within our existing cataloguing round: adopt apophatic description as standing policy; pilot it on the Ravensmoor and housing department deposits under conditions you specify; restrict it to procedural statements about incomplete series and refuse any editorial claim about missing voices; or something better. Whatever you recommend, the memo must give the strongest honest version of the option you are rejecting — I would like the historian and the senior archivist each to recognise their own case in it — and it should say plainly what the record office should decline to assert, and what we should tell the retired housing officer about his conditions. It should also say how, if at all, a silence note gets revised or withdrawn when new material arrives. Do not cite standards, guidance or literature; reason from the case.
