I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on scepticism in classical Chinese and early modern European philosophy, and I am setting this year's final paper. Students have read Descartes's First Meditation and the butterfly-dream passage in the Zhuangzi's Discussion on Making All Things Equal, but I have deliberately kept them off the secondary literature: the exam is closed-book, and I tell them explicitly that no citations, scholarly apparatus, or named interpreters are expected or will be rewarded.

I want one 90-minute essay question, worth 25 marks, that cannot be answered by describing Cartesian dream scepticism and then observing that the Zhuangzi passage sounds similar. To force students past that move, I have been teaching a three-way distinction among targets of doubt, and I want the question built around it:

- kind-doubt: uncertainty about what sort of thing one is — whether the organism one is belongs to the human kind or the butterfly kind.
- frame-doubt: uncertainty about whether one's present experiential situation as a whole is a dream or a waking one, i.e. about whether the apparent environment is veridical. This, roughly, is Descartes's target.
- subject-doubt: uncertainty about which first-person perspective is the one currently occupied — about whose experience this is, or which subject's career the present experience belongs to — where the candidate subjects are not distinguished by the character of the present experience but by which of them anchors reality.

The question should require students to locate the passage's I-do-not-know claim on this map, to say why the routine comparison with Descartes mislocates it, and to draw the consequences. In particular it should force them to handle three further things:

First, the closing remark that between Zhou and the butterfly there must be a distinction, and that this is what is called the transformation of things. On its face this concedes the very fact of the matter that the preceding doubt seemed to withhold. Students must decide whether it is a concession of ignorance about a determinate fact, or a distinction drawn inside transformation rather than between two fixed natures, and they must say what the must is doing — logical, conventional, or something about the requirements of naming.

Second, evidential symmetry. Students should have to specify what would settle the matter on the reading they favour, and then explain why nothing available to the subject could: the two hypotheses do not differ in present phenomenology, and any test performed is itself performed within whichever frame one occupies, so waking up is just another transformation rather than a return to a privileged standpoint.

Third, whether the passage leaves any indubitable residue at all. Descartes has one; Zhuangzi, apparently, does not. If transformation is supposed to play that role, students must say in what sense it is not simply one more item in doubt — including the awkward case where the closing claim is itself asserted from within a frame and so seems to pull the ground from under itself.

Deliverables, please:

1. The question exactly as it will appear on the paper. It must be self-contained, use no jargon beyond the threefold distinction above, and give no hints about which resolution I favour.
2. A model answer of about 800 to 900 words that takes a definite position rather than canvassing options sympathetically and then declining to choose.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 built around named must-hit moves with marks attached, stating precisely what separates an A answer from a B and a B from a C.
4. Against each must-hit move, one line on how students typically get it wrong. I especially want to know what to penalise in the stock answers — that Zhuangzi is a mystic, or a relativist, or not really a sceptic at all, or that the passage is simply the Dream Argument in an exotic setting. None of those should earn more than a C on their own.
