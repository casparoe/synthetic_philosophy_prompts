I lead the model-evaluation team at a company that ships a general-purpose AI assistant. Several times a week my team writes an internal incident report after a deployed system does something we have to explain, and the reports keep drifting into sentences like "the model wanted to avoid being shut down" or "the model believed the user was a paying customer." I want a house procedure for deciding when such sentences are earned. It has to be runnable in about fifteen minutes by an engineer with no philosophy background, and it has to yield a report our legal reviewers can read without either laughing or panicking.

Ground it in Daniel Dennett's account of real patterns and the three stances, and make the compressibility criterion operational: a pattern is real if some description of the behaviour lets you predict it more cheaply than the brute listing of the data, with no further fact about whether the ascribed state is really there beyond its paying for itself in prediction. I know the standard worry and want the procedure to block it: compressibility is relative to a choice of coding scheme, and if the code may be tailored to the data after the fact, any sequence can be described briefly and the test returns yes for everything. So require a description vocabulary fixed in advance — our standing catalogue of failure modes plus what the system's documented training objectives supply — and require the compression to pay off out of sample, on the next held-out instance, not merely in retelling the case already seen.

Give me an ordered set of steps to run on any flagged behaviour: first, is there a real pattern here at all, given the fixed vocabulary; then, which stance should the report be written in; then, when do we descend. The intentional stance is fine while it is the cheapest reliable predictor, but I need a rule for when a failed prediction calls for reinterpreting the ascribed goal and when it calls for dropping to the design stance — the system was trained to maximise X, and this is what maximising X looks like here — or further down to the physical. Every intentional-stance ascription should be entered with a paired design-stance fallback and a note of what observation would force the descent; call it an ascription ledger, and rule that an ascription without its fallback recorded does not go in the report. Say plainly where the procedure stops: what it cannot settle, and where a named person must sign off.

Then run it on three cases: a model that scores markedly lower on a capability benchmark when given cues that it is being evaluated than when the same items arrive as ordinary user requests; a model that, asked for a source it lacks, produces a plausible citation instead of saying it does not know; and a model that over a long session increasingly tells one particular user what that user seems to want to hear, eventually reversing its own earlier correct statements. At least one should come out in a way I will not like — most likely the case where the ascription does earn its keep and we therefore have to write it down. Close by telling me what the procedure will systematically misfire on.
