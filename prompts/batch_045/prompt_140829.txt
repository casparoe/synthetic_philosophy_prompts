Me and two friends have been arguing about this for weeks and Im losing track of who even believes what anymore. It started when one of them repeated that old argument about kids learning grammar: grown-up speech is full of cut-off sentences, slips, and false starts, nobody sits down and tells a four-year-old "no, you can't say it that way", and yet kids end up knowing which sentences are fine and which are impossible, even for constructions nobody ever used around them. Friend A says that settles it: we must be born with grammar built in. Friend B says that's basically magic dressed up as science, and that it's really just very good pattern-spotting over a lot more input than A admits, plus the fact that kids get corrected indirectly all the time. I keep flip-flopping, and part of the problem is that I think we're all using "innate" to mean three different things.

So I want to build a quiz for the group, working title "what do you actually believe about how kids pick up language?", that sorts us by our answers instead of by the labels we like. Eight to ten questions, each one a concrete little case with two or three options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the real positions. None of us has any linguistics or philosophy training, so keep the wording plain and gloss any technical term you use, and please don't give me a reading list or point me at papers — I just want the instrument. Here are the cases I want used, at least most of them: a made-up miniature language with one clear rule (say, questions get formed by moving a marked word to the front of the sentence, plus a restriction nobody says out loud) and a child who hears only a narrow slice of it; a group of kids who are only ever exposed to a makeshift gesture system with no consistent grammar, who end up speaking something far more regular than what they were given; a kid who picks up a second language flawlessly at nine and another who starts at fifteen and never quite gets it; a system trained on vastly more text than any child hears that still can't reliably tell which sentences of its own language are impossible; a household where every adult sentence is ungrammatical in the same way and the child doesn't copy the error; and something about whether an animal with a huge general-purpose memory and no special language equipment could do it. If you think one of those cases is actually a bad test that doesn't discriminate anything, say so rather than quietly using it. I also want at least one question where the honest answer is "I don't think this question has a fact of the matter", because I suspect that's where I am.

After the quiz, give me the same thing as a tree: each branch labeled with the single claim that splits it from its siblings, and each leaf a named position with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The leaves I care about having: the born-with-grammar-built-in view where the constraints are specific to language; the view that it's domain-general learning over input that's richer than the argument assumes; the middle view where what's built in is the shape of the learner — biases, priors, what counts as a possible guess — rather than the grammar itself; and a deflationary or skeptical one that says the whole argument either can't be tested or just relabels the puzzle. Branch on things like whether the space of possible grammars a child considers is constrained at all, whether any such constraint is language-specific, whether "innate" means genetically specified versus merely not learned from what was heard versus trivially true of every capacity, and whether the argument explains anything or just restates it. Where two leaves can't actually be separated by the questions, flag it instead of pretending, and give me a tie-breaker question for the ones that matter. And where a combination of answers corresponds to no view anybody holds, mark it as empty and take a guess at why — incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's bothered.
