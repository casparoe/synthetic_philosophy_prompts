Here's the question as it was set.

**Question 4 (20 marks)**

Calliope is a clay statue, made on Monday by a sculptor out of a single lump of clay, Lumpl. Calliope and Lumpl occupy exactly the same region and, at every time at which both exist, have exactly the same parts. Assume that Lumpl existed before Monday and that Calliope did not.

(a) Set out the argument from Leibniz's Law for the conclusion that Calliope is not identical to Lumpl, and say whether the standard ("coincidence") account accepts or rejects that argument. Then say what is supposed to be puzzling or costly about the standard account's own position — what does the coincidence theorist have to talk her way out of? Finally, briefly: the museum's conservator proposes to squash the statue gradually, a millimetre a day over a year (call this the Slow Squash). Does the resulting vagueness about when Calliope stops existing threaten the argument from Leibniz's Law? (8 marks)

(b) The eliminativist says: there is no such thing as Calliope; there are only particles arranged statuewise. Explain how this response avoids the puzzle in (a), and state and briefly assess one cost it incurs. (6 marks)

(c) The constitution theorist says: Lumpl constitutes Calliope. Set out what this view must say about (i) whether constitution is identity, and (ii) whether Calliope and Lumpl have the same persistence conditions. If the view says they differ in persistence conditions, what grounds the difference, given that they share all their parts and all their matter? (6 marks)

And here's the answer.

**Answer 4**

(a) Leibniz's Law says that if two things have all the same properties, they are identical. Calliope and Lumpl have all the same occurrent properties: same matter, same mass, same shape, same location, and (given the way the case is set up) the same parts and the same history. So Leibniz's Law pushes hard towards identity. The only difference anyone has managed to point to is modal: Lumpl could survive being rolled into a ball, Calliope could not; Calliope could survive having a finger replaced, Lumpl could not. But modal properties are not genuine properties of a thing, and Leibniz's Law was never meant to range over them, so the standard account is in the clear. On the standard account the statue and the lump do differ, but they differ only modally, and that is a difference Leibniz's Law can live with.

What the coincidence theorist then has to explain is how two things can be in the same place at the same time. That is puzzling because we normally think one region of space holds one object, and because it seems to give you something for nothing: the museum has one insured item but two objects, and could in principle charge admission per object. The real cost is explaining how two objects can differ in their modal properties while sharing all their parts.

On the Slow Squash: this doesn't cause trouble. The argument from Leibniz's Law only needs that there be some possible change — an ordinary quick squash — which the clay survives and the statue does not. If the slow case is vague, that just means one instance of the argument is vague; the quick case isn't, and the argument goes through there.

(b) The eliminativist agrees that there is only one thing in the region, the particles arranged statuewise, and so avoids having to say that two objects coincide. The trouble is that once you start denying composition there is no non-arbitrary place to stop. Statues go, but then so do tables, chairs, trees, and you and I: the eliminativist ends up committed to there being nothing but simples. A second cost is that she has to paraphrase every sentence about statues into a sentence about particles, and it isn't obvious that the paraphrases are available for sentences like "Calliope was admired by millions".

(c) The constitution theorist's idea is that constitution is contingent identity: Lumpl and Calliope are one and the same thing, but the identity between them holds only contingently, since there are circumstances in which the clay exists without constituting a statue. That is what lets the view have it both ways — one object, but two sets of persistence conditions, depending on whether we describe the change as something the lump survives or something the statue survives. So the answer to (ii) is that Calliope does share Lumpl's persistence conditions — after all, she has no parts beyond the clay's — but the conditions get stated twice over, once under the sortal "statue" and once under "lump". The difference is grounded in the fact that "statue" and "lump" are different sortals with different criteria of identity, and sortal-relativity of this kind is something we accept elsewhere anyway: a person and her body are the same thing under different descriptions.

On balance I think the constitution view is the most attractive of the three, because it gives you everything the coincidence theorist wanted without the double counting.
