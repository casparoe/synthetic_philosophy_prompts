I'm auditing a graduate seminar on space and symmetry this term — I'm retired, thirty-odd years as an analytical chemist doing chiral separations for a pharma company, and since I stopped work I've been sitting in on philosophy classes, which is turning out to be a lot harder than I expected. The professor has the auditors grade a couple of anonymised practice answers before each session so there's something to argue about, and this week the topic was whether left and right are real. I've got one answer I genuinely can't make up my mind about, and I'd rather not be taken in by the prose. The question was:

"Is the distinction between left and right a real distinction in nature, or is it merely conventional or a matter of relations among things? In your answer you should discuss Kant's argument from incongruent counterparts, the case of chirality in chemistry, and whether the discovery of parity violation in the weak interaction settles any part of the question."

and the answer I've been given is:

"Start with what is not in dispute. A left hand and a right hand differ, and the difference is not in the parts: every internal distance, angle and relation among the parts of one is matched exactly in the other. Rotate and translate as you like, the two cannot be brought into coincidence. Kant's use of this example was to argue against the Leibnizian who holds that space is nothing but the system of relations among bodies: if that were so, two objects agreeing in all intrinsic and relational respects would be the same object, and plainly they are not. So the ground of the difference must lie outside the object, in its relation to space as a whole, and space therefore has a reality of its own that cannot be reduced to relations among the things in it. Kant's positive conclusion at this point was that space is an absolute container existing independently of the bodies in it; he gave this up in the Critical period, where space became a form of our intuition instead of something in the world, so the argument is best read as a pre-Critical relic and I set it aside below.

Chemistry is the same phenomenon with consequences attached. Enantiomers have identical scalar properties and differ in how they rotate plane-polarised light and in how they behave in a chiral environment; the biological macromolecules are homochiral, and the thalidomide case is the grim illustration. The point for us is that handedness is an intrinsic property of an object rather than something we project onto it: a chiral molecule is left- or right-handed in virtue of its own geometry, and no rotation changes that. Nothing about us enters into it.

The hard question has always been the other one. Grant that the two forms differ intrinsically: what makes one of them the left-handed one rather than the right-handed one? Pointing at my own hand and saying 'this one' works well enough in a lecture theatre but doesn't answer anything, and the old puzzle about radioing instructions to a distant civilisation makes the difficulty vivid — no list of intrinsic properties tells them which way round we mean, and no purely relational description does either, since the relations come out the same on both sides.

Physics closed that gap. The weak interaction violates parity: in the classic cobalt-60 experiment the decay electrons come off preferentially in one direction relative to the nuclear spin, which is a lawlike asymmetry between a process and its mirror image. (Electromagnetic and strong processes are asymmetric in the same way; it is only in weak decays that the asymmetry is large enough to measure conveniently.) So there is a physically privileged orientation, and we can specify it: tell the aliens to orient a nucleus with its spin pointing 'up' along their magnetic field, and tell them which end the electrons come out of. Since the laws single out a unique handedness, left and right are not conventional but real — and Kant's claim that the difference can only be exhibited in intuition and never captured in concepts is simply refuted, because anything a law can single out, a description can state."

What I'd like is two things. First, a mark: the question was worth 20, and the professor's rubric is only three lines — does the answer state Kant's argument and its conclusion correctly; does it seperate the puzzle about labelled directions from the plain geometric fact that some objects come in non-superimposable mirror pairs; does it get right what the parity-violating experiments can and cannot do for the philosophical question. Second, and more useful to me, the margin comments you'd actually write on the paper: specific, tied to particular sentences or claims, saying what's right, what's wrong and why, and what a revised version would have to do differently. Please don't give me a general impression — I can already see it reads well, that's the problem. I want the point-by-point. Also, one thing I'm bad at spotting: if a claim in there is perfectly fine as far as it goes but doesn't actually do the work the conclusion needs it to do, I'd like that flagged explicitly, since that's the kind of thing I miss every time. Don't go hunting for errors that aren't there — I have to defend whatever mark I give in class on Thursday and someone will push back — but don't be generous for the sake of it either. For context: the answers were written under exam conditions, so I'm not expecting anyone to name-drop the literature or cite papers, and the chemistry I can check myself; it's the Kant and the inferential bits where I'm shakiest.
