You are a professional political philosopher who works on religion and the state, with genuine command of the comparative institutional detail in England, Germany, France, and the United States. Please think carefully and take your time with this: before you write anything I will read, do a private brainstorming pass in which you list every candidate tension you can find among the commitments below, mark the ones you intend to reject as merely apparent, and note where you suspect I have smuggled in a principle I never stated. Then give me the audit proper. I would rather have a short, discriminating answer than a long hedged one.

Why I am asking now: I am an American who moved to Germany about two years ago for work, married to a German, with a daughter who started school here last autumn. Registering my residence turned out to have consequences I did not anticipate: my American Lutheran baptism had been carried across, I was on the register of the local Landeskirche, and I have now paid the church tax twice. Since then I have been asked to write a short discussion paper for a parents' and residents' group here on whether the state should be in the religion business at all, and I have discovered that I cannot tell whether what I believe is a position or just a stack of local reflexes. My former colleagues in the United States, my wife's family in England, and a close friend in Paris have each told me I am being inconsistent, and each of them locates the inconsistency somewhere different. I want to know who is right, if any of them is.

Here is where I think I have landed. I have written it out in the order I would defend it, not in order of confidence.

1. The only thing that can make public money reaching a religious body legitimate is consent: state funds may flow to religion only insofar as they track the choices of identifiable individuals, and never because the state has judged that this community or this religion is the sort of thing that deserves support. This is the commitment I am least willing to move.

2. I have also started applying a test of my own, which I call the symmetric-exit test: an arrangement is acceptable only if getting out of it costs an individual no more than getting into it did. I like it because it seems to get the verdicts right in cases I already had views about — I am relaxed about tax-deductible donations to congregations in the United States, and uneasy about any arrangement where membership is the default and withdrawal is the thing you have to go and do.

3. On that basis I defend the German church tax, which surprised me. The state collects a surcharge on income tax and passes it to the religious communities that hold public-law corporation status; only members pay; and anyone can leave by declaring their withdrawal before a government office, with what I am told is a modest fee in most Länder. My colleagues here think this is a bizarre medieval survival. I think it may be the least objectionable of the four systems I am comparing, precisely because the money tracks membership rather than state favoritism.

4. On the same basis I oppose school vouchers and tax-credit scholarship schemes in the United States, including the well-regulated versions where the religious school is academically unremarkable and does not discriminate in admissions. My reason is that I do not think the state should pay for the formation of religious commitment in children who have not chosen it.

5. I think the Church of England should be disestablished — bishops in the second chamber, the monarch as supreme governor, the whole apparatus. I hold this on principle, not on the strength of any bad consequences I can point to.

6. But something I was told recently has stuck to me and I do not want to surrender it. I am given to understand that the incumbent of a Church of England parish is legally obliged to marry, baptize, and bury the people of the parish whether or not they believe anything in particular or ever attend, and that this is one of the few genuinely universal, non-judgmental civic services left — something no voluntary congregation has any reason to offer to strangers. I find myself valuing that a great deal, and I notice it is a feature of the thing I just said should be abolished.

7. I think the French settlement goes too far. I do not accept that a schoolgirl should have to choose between her religion and her schooling, and I am not persuaded that a parent or a teacher must leave their religion at the school gate.

8. I am untroubled by the state paying for the upkeep of old church buildings — the French state maintaining the pre-1905 cathedrals it owns, conservation grants from the Länder for parish churches — because I think of it as preservation rather than as funding religion. For the same sort of reason I am untroubled by state-funded theology faculties at German universities, even though those faculties exist in part to train the people who run the institutions in items 3 and 4.

What I want from you:

Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and how badly, ordered by severity rather than listed as though they were all equal. For each real tension, say exactly which commitment has to give and what the cheapest available revision costs me — I mean cheapest in terms of what else I would have to abandon, not cheapest in terms of how much I would personally mind. Include at least one apparent tension that dissolves on inspection and explain why it looked like one. Tell me if any of my commitments is doing no real work, is too vague to generate verdicts, or is really two commitments sharing a sentence. Tell me whether the symmetric-exit test is the right test, or whether it is measuring the wrong thing and I should replace it with something else. Note where I have simply absorbed a local intuition from two years of living here and mistaken it for an argument.

Do not give me a lecture on the history of disestablishment or the 1905 settlement; I have read enough of that and it is not what I am asking for. Do not soften the result by telling me that everything is in some tension with everything, and do not close by asking which commitment I would prefer to give up — I do not know, and the point of asking you is to be told. If the honest answer is that I have to drop one of items 3 through 6, say which, and say what I lose.
