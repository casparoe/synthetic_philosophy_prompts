Some background on why I am writing this. I am a novelist, and my last three books are set in a river port during the decades when sail gave way to steam. In the third book a character named Aldous Reever spends twenty years trying to get a canal cut around a stretch of shoals; the canal is surveyed, funded, half-dug, and then abandoned when the railway comes through on the other bank. Readers write to me constantly about Reever. Half of them say the canal never had a chance and that Reever was a fool not to see it; the other half say he was robbed by bad luck and a single vote on a committee. What interests me is that both readings feel true and neither can be settled, and I have become suspicious that the disagreement is not really about Reever at all. It is about how much of what happened had to happen.

The essay — working title "The Corridor and the Door" — runs like this.

1. Historical fiction is accused of two opposite faults. The first is fatalism from the back: because we know how it turned out, we write the past as a corridor with one door at the end, and every scene feels like a step toward the door that actually opened. The second is frivolity: the counterfactual novel, in which a different committee vote produces a different century, and which treats history as a set of doors that were all equally ajar. I want to argue that both faults come from the same mistake, which is testing contingency with single-decision counterfactuals.

2. My proposed test. Do not ask what would have happened if one decision had gone the other way. Ask what would have happened if the whole route had been closed: suppose Reever fails, and the canal's other advocates fail, and the canal's advocates in the next generation fail too, and suppose every alternative means of getting the same result — a deeper channel, a bigger warehouse fleet, a different port altogether — is also blocked. Call this the obstruction test. If the outcome still arrives by some route we did not think of, I say the outcome is shadowed: the thing that happened was, in a broad sense, going to happen, though not by the path it took. If the outcome fails to arrive once the corridor is closed, I say it is locked: it depended on the actual sequence, and the counterfactual is a genuine fork and not a detour.

3. A consequence I rather like. The obstruction test suggests that most arguments about founders and first movers are arguments about the wrong thing. Which person or firm or town did the thing first looks path dependent in the strongest sense; whether the thing got done at all is very often shadowed. The identity of the agent is locked, the arrival of the outcome is not, and people who care about credit and blame spend all their energy on the first and then talk as if they had shown something about the second.

4. A worry about my own craft. Narrative needs decision points, so a novelist is under constant structural pressure to convert drift into choice, and to put a face on a process that mostly had no face. I call this the scene bias, and I claim it is not fixable by being more disciplined, because it is a property of the form. What I try instead is what I call the scene of no decision: a scene in which nobody chooses anything and the corridor nevertheless gets narrower. I want to claim that this is the only honest way for fiction to represent a locked outcome.

5. The part I am least sure of, which I think of as retrospective collapse. Whether an outcome was shadowed seems to be settled partly by what happens later: a branch that is never reopened stops counting as a branch, and after enough time the corridor looks like the only corridor there ever was. But if that is right, then the question "was it locked in?" has no answer from inside the moment it is asked, and the answer we eventually give is itself one more effect of the path that was taken. Contingency, on this view, is not a property of events but a property of events plus the standpoint from which they are later described. I do not want this to collapse into the boring conclusion that we can never know, but I cannot currently see how to stop it doing so.

What I would like from you:

- Tell me whether the obstruction test holds together, and in particular whether it secretly assumes what it is supposed to establish. To close a corridor I have to know what would count as an alternative route to the same outcome, and that seems to require me to already know how fine-grained my description of the outcome is. If the test is hostage to the level of description, say so plainly and tell me how much of the essay dies with it.
- Tell me whether the shadowed-versus-locked distinction is doing real work or is just a relabeling of something philosophers of history already have a settled vocabulary for. If it is old hat, I would rather cut it than look naive.
- Give me your three most damaging objections in descending order of severity, and for the most damaging one, sketch the reply you would make if you were me. I intend to answer my critics in the published version, so I need the objection stated at full strength, not softened.
- Tell me honestly whether section 5 is a genuine insight or a confusion dressed up as one, and what would have to be true for it to be the former.
- Finally, one concrete revision that would most raise the value of the essay, plus a note on any place where I use an ordinary word in a way that fights its normal meaning.
