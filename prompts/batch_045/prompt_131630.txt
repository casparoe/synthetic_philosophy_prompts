I'm the editor-in-chief of my school's student paper, a print issue every other Friday plus three or four posts a week, about thirty students on staff, most of them sophomores and juniors who joined to write about the basketball team. Over the last year we have had three near-misses: we almost published a classmate's direct message without telling her, we ran a photo of a student crying in the hallway, and we published a piece built almost entirely around the sentence the student body is outraged, which was supported by about two hundred signatures out of eighteen hundred students and which, on reflection, probably did more to create the outrage than to report it. So the five of us on the editorial board are trying to write ourselves a one-page laminated card: a short set of questions, in order, that whoever is running the Sunday night meeting can work through in under fifteen minutes without having taken a philosophy class, with a clear statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, at least two hard stops where the answer is no or the piece goes to a faculty adviser, and at least one point that we mark as a genuine value call requiring a vote rather than a test. Please don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations: I don't have university library access and I can't check anything, so I need the substance of the ideas, not pointers to where they live.

The ideas I want operationalized are the ones about the press and the public from the Danish writer of the 1840s who attacked the satirical weekly that hounded him and wrote a book about his own age's tendency to flatten everything. As I understand him, his complaint is not that newspapers lie but that a periodical press manufactures an entity, the public, which is not a group of anyone in particular: it never assembles, it cannot answer, it has no situation and no risk, and anyone can shelter inside it and say things they would never say to a face. Related to this is his notion of leveling, the way the abstract collective turns a named person into a specimen or an instance, and his insistence that the ethically real relation is one person speaking to one other person, a relation that has an addressee and can be answered. There is also his stark claim in a later devotional book that the crowd is untruth, and the awkward fact that he himself published under invented names, which makes me think the procedure cannot simply be publish only what is signed and addressed: it has to say when withholding or inventing an author's name is itself the leveling move, and when it isn't.

For the part that I think we can't get from books: I want a test for something I've started calling ventriloquized consensus, the case where a piece attributes a view to the school or the student body when that view is really held by a handful of people plus our own headline, and the story itself is what makes the collective look like it has an opinion. I also want a one-reader version of the addressability idea: would this piece still be worth running if exactly one person we could name read it, and would we still write it the same way? Please build the card with those worked in, then run the whole procedure on three cases: (1) naming a sophomore who was recorded making a racist remark in the cafeteria, in a story that will be published after the clip has already circulated; (2) an unsigned op-ed from three juniors criticizing a particular teacher's grading by name; (3) a news story about a planned walkout framed around the claim that the school is angry, based on a petition with a few hundred signatures. At least one of these should come out with an answer my editorial board will not like, and I'd rather you tell me which and why than soften it. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: whether the reporting is true, whether we are legally allowed to publish, whether the audience will in fact read us as a crowd, and anything else you think a fifteen-minute checklist will systematically get wrong.
