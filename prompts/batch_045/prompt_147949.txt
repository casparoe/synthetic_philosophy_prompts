I'm a family-medicine resident and I've been chewing on this for months — I was arguing with two co-residents after a lecture on overdiagnosis and we definitely did not resolve it, and a friend of mine who did a philosophy degree says there's a whole literature on it (naturalism versus normativism about disease, I think?), but everything I could find online just fights about definitions and never talks about what I actually do all day. So here's the practice I want you to reconstruct the commitments of. Most of what I diagnose isn't something broken. It's a number that crossed a line. Hypertension, prediabetes, osteopenia, subclinical hypothyroidism: the patient feels fine, no mechanism has failed, and the entire content of the diagnosis is that a continuous variable landed on the far side of a cut-point that some committee chose. And the cut-points move around and disagree with each other: the WHO still says hypertension is 140/90 or higher on two separate days, the 2017 US guideline puts stage 1 at 130/80, and under that change a big slice of my panel acquired a disease overnight without anything happening inside them. It gets weirder. We give people the label and then, if their ten-year cardiovascular risk is low, we recommend salt, exercise, and no medication at all — so the disease label isn't what's driving the treatment decision. We also have this growing family of "pre-diseases" (prehypertension, prediabetes, mild cognitive impairment) that aren't diseases but still get us to schedule follow-ups and order tests. And as far as I can tell, the lab reference ranges we compare patients against are usually just the middle 95% of whatever sample the lab drew from, in a population that's already been screened and treated for the very thing being measured — so the supposedly natural baseline is contaminated by prior practice.

My friend's theory is that in this part of medicine "disease" has stopped referring to anything biological at all and just means: a state where the expected benefit of intervening beats the expected harm at acceptable cost, and where there's a billing code that lets the system act. I push back because the thresholds really do track outcome data, and no one would accept a threshold set by a show of hands or by how much patients complain — so it can't be pure values all the way down. But it also can't be straight biology, since the same body is diseased in one country and fine in another. So: what concept of disease does this way of practicing actually presuppose, and which rival concepts does it quietly rule out? Specifically, can the naturalist side (disease as statistically subnormal functioning for a member of the species) survive here, given that it has to pick a reference class and then pick how far out in the distribution to put the line — my friend calls this the reference-class-plus-dispersion problem — and given that the obvious reference class ("untreated people of this age") isn't even available anymore? Does the hybrid view (dysfunction plus harm) do any better, when nothing in a 132/82 patient seems to be malfunctioning? Is the practice strictly committed to some kind of normativism, or is it underdetermined, so that several theories would license it? One co-resident suggested "disease" might just be a vague predicate and the thresholds are precisifications of something fuzzy, which sounded like a dodge to me at the time but maybe it isn't. And finally: if we replaced the binary with treating on graded absolute risk and dropped the disease labels for these conditions entirely, what in the clinic would actually have to change — the sick role, insurance coverage, quality metrics, the way I talk to patients on a Tuesday afternoon? Please write your answer as one continuous piece of prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, the way you'd write a long email to a friend.
