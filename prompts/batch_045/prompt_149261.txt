I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background. A few of us from work go to a reading group run by someone on staff who has a philosophy degree, and our last session was on the objection that utilitarianism fails to take the separateness of persons seriously — the complaint, as I took it, that summing happiness across people treats the boundaries between persons as no more morally important than the boundaries between the years of a single life, so that one person's loss is automatically made good by another person's gain. (I gather this is originally Rawls's, though all we were given was a two-page handout.) One of my colleagues, the most systematic person I know, thinks the whole objection is a bookkeeping superstition, and his argument is an analogy: he says interpersonal aggregation is the same operation as prudence. Deciding to skip dessert for twenty years in order to avoid a heart attack at sixty is maximizing a single quantity spread over time, and so is allocating a fixed budget across a population; the only difference is whether the time-slices happen to be glued together by memory, habit, and continuity of character. And the gluing, he says, is a matter of degree — I am less like my fifteen-year-old self than I am like my wife — so drawing a bright moral line at the edge of a person is like drawing one at midnight. This is not idle for us: the ranking service my team maintains optimizes total predicted satisfaction across all users, and every proposal to add a side constraint, say that no individual user be left worse off than under the previous version, gets waved away as sentiment dressed up as engineering.

I tried to break the analogy with a case of my own and it did not do what I expected. Imagine a continuity dial: a neural link between two people that can be turned up continuously, from no access to each other's memories at all, through partial sharing, to full sharing, and then past that into what we would ordinarily just call being one person. I argued that his view predicts the legitimacy of trading one of them off against the other should ramp smoothly as the dial goes up, and that this is absurd — at zero it is plainly sacrificing one person to another, and there is no setting at which it turns into mere imprudence, the way my skipping dessert is. He agreed that this is the prediction and said it is the right one: the dial merely exposes what is already true, and talk of rights and of compensation is useful shorthand for the high-connectedness cases. I cannot tell whether that is a reductio of his position or a sign that I have misunderstood what the analogy was supposed to deliver in the first place.

So I would like an audit of the base analogy rather than a verdict on utilitarianism. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — saying what plays the role of the time-slices, of the lifetime utility function, of prudence, of compensation, of the decider, and of the younger self's grievance — and then separate the disanalogies that actually tell against the conclusion he wants from the ones that are cosmetic. Three things I especially want addressed. First, whether the fact that the decider and the cost-bearer coincide within a life but not across lives is doing hidden work, since his formalism seems to me to have no slot in which to record it. Second, whether my continuity dial is a counterexample to the analogy or simply the analogy working as designed, which I take to turn on whether the thing the analogy is meant to establish — that aggregation is permissible — is even the sort of thing that can come in degrees. Third, what substantive claim about personal identity the analogy needs in order to get off the ground, and whether someone who grants that metaphysics but withholds the normative step is left with any reason at all to accept the conclusion. He has also tried a second analogy on me, a head of household allocating a budget, which I dislike but cannot say why; if there is a better analogy for what interpersonal aggregation is actually doing, one that makes visible again the moral difference his first one erases, I would like to see it and to hear what it gets right that his does not. Please answer in American English, assume I am comfortable with optimization and probability but not with moral philosophy terminology, and define any term of art you need.
