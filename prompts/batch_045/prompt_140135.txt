so i got into an argument in a group chat after a meetup last weekend and it's been eating at me ever since. context: i've been going back and forth on the special composition question — van Inwagen-style nihilism on one end, universalism (any arbitrary bunch of things composes something) on the other, and the various restricted or brute answers in between — and i keep flip-flopping between them in a way that makes me think the question is being posed wrong. the thing that finally clicked for me is that everyone seems to be looking for a feature of the *candidate parts* that settles it, when maybe what settles it is something about the agent doing the carving.

here's the version i want defended properly. take a roughly predictivist criterion: some things compose an object just in case there is a coarse-grained way of describing their joint microstate that is (1) much shorter than the full microphysical description, (2) at least as good as any other tractably computable description of comparable length at predicting their future states, and (3) still wins on usefulness when you intervene on the surroundings rather than just letting things run. on this sort of view there is obviously something where my desk is, and obviously nothing composed of my nose and the Eiffel Tower, since no compression of the pair beats treating them seperately — so it dodges nihilism and universalism without needing any special life clause or a brute list. but the catch, and this is the part i want argued *for* rather than apologized for, is that tractably computable and predictively useful are indexed to some agent's computational limits and interests, so composition comes out interest-relative: relative to a being with different hardware or different things it wants to predict, different things compose, and a lot of the gerrymandered objects common sense denies are perfectly real relative to some such being.

please argue for that position — give me the strongest version of the interest-relative predictivist answer to the special composition question, and defend its relativity as a feature rather than treating it as a reductio. i especially want you to bite the bullet on the problem of the many (the cloud case, the thousands of nearly-coincident table candidates differing by a molecule) and say something non-obvious about why we shouldn't be bothered that the view also lets in some objects our intuitions hate. don't survey the standard options and shrug; commit to this one. and i'd rather you didn't lean on citations at all — i want the argument, not a reading list.
