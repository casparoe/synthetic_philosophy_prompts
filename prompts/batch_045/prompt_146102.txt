I sit on the model-risk committee of a regional bank, and before that on a state commission that reviewed pretrial risk instruments. Over about a dozen deployments I have voted on which fairness requirement to impose, and my votes do not line up with any single criterion. I want to know whether there is a principle behind them, or whether I have simply been inconsistent.

I take the following as background and do not need it re-derived: when two groups differ in their base rates, a risk score cannot simultaneously be calibrated and equal in its false-positive and false-negative rates; and a parity constraint imposed on a coarse partition will generally fail on some finer partition of the same population. My question is not what the conflict is but which requirement to enforce where, and what to give up.

My verdicts, in each case on the decision I actually voted for:

1. A hiring screen that selects candidates for interview, with different qualification rates across groups. We required equal true-positive rates: qualified candidates equally likely to be shortlisted whichever group they belong to. Right.

2. A fraud-investigation queue, where being flagged triggers a frozen account and an interview, again with different base rates. We required equal false-positive rates: innocent customers equally likely to be flagged. Right — even though this is the mirror image of case 1 and the mathematics is identical.

3. A sepsis early-warning score in a hospital, shown to nurses as a number from one to ten with different guidance attached to each level. We required calibration and explicitly declined to force error-rate parity. Right.

4. A pretrial instrument trained on arrest as its target, in a city where one district is policed far more heavily than the rest. We declined to enforce any statistical requirement on it, on the grounds that the target was itself the defect — and I stand by that even though the tool's error rates happened to be equal across groups.

5. The same pretrial setting, but with failure-to-appear as the target. Here we did require equal false-positive rates on detention, and I think that was right even though it means that detained defendants in the two groups differ in average risk.

6. A vendor model for our credit line that satisfied equal false-negative rates, and which we rejected anyway: for rejected applicants in one group, the only changes that would have flipped the decision were things like relocating to a different metro area, while in the other group they were things like documenting income the applicant already had. Same error rates, very different feasible routes to a different answer. I think we were right, and I do not know how to state this in the vocabulary of statistical criteria.

7. We enforced equal false-positive rates on a coarse partition of four groups, pre-registered before the model was fit. A later analysis found a much higher false-positive rate within one narrow subgroup. I voted to leave the model alone; a colleague voted to refit against that subgroup. I think I was right — and I also think I would have voted with her had that subgroup been on the pre-registered list. This pair is the one I cannot defend.

8. Version two of our credit model reversed the decision for a nontrivial share of customers who had done nothing differently, and it reversed them unevenly across groups. I voted to block rollout until that was reduced, although version two dominated version one on every static measure we track, including error-rate parity. My committee thought I was being precious. I want to know whether equalizing that kind of churn across groups is a fairness requirement at all, or just good operations.

My own tentative gloss has been: equalize whichever error imposes a burden on the person who is subject to it, and fall back on calibration only when the score is an input to someone's judgment rather than a gate. But that says nothing about cases 6, 7, or 8, and I suspect it handles 1 and 2 only by luck.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of candidates, that generates verdicts 1 through 8? Please state it precisely enough to apply, note which case motivates each clause, give its verdicts on cases I have not listed, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

Two further things. First, do requirements of the kind in 6 and 8 make the standard conflict worse — that is, is there a reason to think no model can satisfy calibration, error-rate parity, recourse parity, and cross-version stability at once, and if so which of the four should yield first, and why that one? Second, the case I keep going back and forth on: rather than choosing which parity to enforce, we could randomize. Applicants in a band around the threshold would get a weighted coin flip, with weights set per group so that both error parities hold in expectation, at some cost in accuracy. I cannot tell whether this is a cheat — it makes each individual's outcome partly a lottery — or whether it is the honest reply to the fact that our criteria jointly overdetermine what we are asking for. What does the principle say?
