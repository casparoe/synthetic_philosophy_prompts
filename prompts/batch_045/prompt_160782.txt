I've been poking at Indian philosophy on my own for about a year — no Sanskrit, no classes, just stuff I run into online plus a friend from work who grew up around some of this — and the thing I keep circling back to is the pramana business: the lists of "means of knowledge" that the Nyaya school argues about (perception, inference, comparison from a description, testimony, with other schools wanting to add postulation-from-a-puzzle and cognizing-an-absence, and the materialists wanting to allow only perception). What I want isn't a tour of who held which list. I want the spec first. Concretely: I want a theory I can actually use to audit my own beliefs — to sort the things I think I know into ones I should trust and ones I shouldn't, including things I got off the internet, out of books whose authors I can't check up on, and off instruments. Write me the desiderata such a theory has to meet, with the case or worry behind each one.

Cases I think it has to get right: the rope taken for a snake; a dream that feels exactly like perception while you're in it; someone who guesses and happens to be right; remembering where you left your keys; noticing that there is no pot on the floor; recognizing an animal you've never seen because someone told you it looks like a cow; concluding from "he's fat and never eats during the day" that he eats at night; being told the truth by someone who's generally a liar; being told something false by someone who's generally reliable; knowing that you're in pain or that you're annoyed; a thermometer reading, a calculator output, a photograph, an answer from a chatbot (is the instrument the source, or is it testimony from whoever built it, or an inference from a display?); a sign or a Wikipedia article with no identifiable author to vouch for it; and two-step cases where you learn X from someone and then work out Y yourself — what's the source of Y, and is it one pramana or two?

Things I'd want the list to address, and you should tell me which of these are real requirements and which are just one camp's taste: a principled way of deciding when something is a *distinct* pramana versus a special case of another one (otherwise the list is arbitrary and can grow forever); a reason to stop at the number you stop at; not over-generating into dreams, lucky guesses and confident mistakes; not under-generating so that absence-cognition or analogy get left out; whether the pramana is supposed to be a causal source or more like a justification you could cite; whether knowledge from a pramana has to be *new* — I half-remember a Sanskrit term, something like anadhigata? — and if so what happens to re-checking something you already believe, since re-checking strikes me as one of the most useful things a source can do for you; whether the theory needs the distinction between the source and the knowledge that results from it; whether it needs a story about error that can live with the rope-snake case; and whether "it should lead to successful action when you act on it" is a genuine requirement or a disguised prejudice. My hunch is that the novelty requirement and the confirm-what-you-already-believe one can't both survive — tell me if I'm right, and flag any other pair on the list you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable, because "the desiderata can't all be had at once" is itself the kind of answer I'd find useful. Then say which single requirement kills the most candidate lists. A short scorecard at the end — perception-only, the four, six-or-more, and "whatever process reliably produces true belief" — is fine, but keep most of the thinking in the criteria: that's the part I can't do myself. Please don't just send me to texts; I can't read them anyway.
