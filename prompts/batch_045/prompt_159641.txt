Six months ago I joined the volunteer committee that allocates our town's small arts-grant budget, and Tolstoy's aesthetics has suddenly become a practical problem for me. We are deciding whether to renew funding for a sound installation in the library atrium: a local artist built it out of a model trained on field recordings made around town, and it produces ambient music that never repeats and shifts with the noise in the room. One member of the committee, a retired philosophy teacher, has circulated a one-page memo arguing that by Tolstoy's own criteria this piece is not merely bad art but not art at all, and that most of what our program funds is counterfeit art:

"Tolstoy's book What Is Art? replaces the usual talk about beauty with a single deflationary test. Art is not beauty and it is not self-expression; it is a human activity in which someone who has experienced a feeling calls it up again and transmits it to others through external signs — words, sounds, colors, shapes — and actually infects them with it. That success, which he calls infection, is the whole test: if a work infects, it is art and it is good art, and if it does not, then no amount of beauty, craft, or reputation saves it. Because infection is the whole test, real art has to be immediately intelligible to ordinary people — anything that requires specialized training, a program note, or the right education to appreciate is counterfeit by that very fact, since a plain person cannot catch a feeling from it. This is why Tolstoy dismissed most of the high art of his own day, Shakespeare and Wagner and the symbolists included: it is borrowing, or imitation, or mere effectfulness and diversion, made by professionals for a trained audience rather than by someone with a feeling to convey. Nor is there any separate moral test to worry about; the moral part is just the observation that the feelings worth transmitting are the ones Christianity teaches. And because infection requires a person who has undergone a feeling and means to pass it on, a machine cannot be an artist: it has no feeling and no intention, so an algorithmically generated piece is at best an imitation of art, and we would be spending public money on a counterfeit."

I have read a couple of encyclopedia entries on Tolstoy's views and I cannot tell how much of this is Tolstoy and how much is my colleague. Please go through the memo claim by claim and tell me what someone who has actually worked through What Is Art? would accept, what is a fair compression for a one-page memo, and what is a misreading — and for each misreading, what exactly goes wrong: a technical term taken in its everyday sense, a necessary condition promoted to a sufficient one, a claim about good art attributed to art in general, a standard objection presented as a refutation, and so on. Where he is right, say so plainly; I do not want a brief for our budget, I want to know what to concede in the meeting. My own suspicion is that he is overstating the infection point, but I am not confident.

Two things I would like you to go past the memo on.

First, a case from last year's cycle that his argument does not obviously handle. We funded a short piece for community chorus — a setting of a local text that sends up the commuters who have moved into town lately. It was genuinely infectious: the room caught the same feeling, people left humming it, and there was no doubt that a shared feeling had been transmitted from performer to audience. But it seemed to me to do the opposite of what Tolstoy wants art to do, since it binds the audience together by way of good-humored contempt for everyone outside the room. Does Tolstoy's apparatus actually have the resources to condemn this, or does the uniting part of his view do all the work while infection does none? I want to know whether this is a genuine counterexample to Tolstoy, or just a case where my colleague has quietly dropped the second half of the criterion.

Second, is the algorithmic installation really excluded? Tolstoy's definition does seem to require someone who has had a feeling and deliberately transmits it. Does that entail that a work with no single such person behind it cannot be art, or is there a reading on which the artist stands to the model the way a composer stands to an instrument or to a body of inherited folk material? And even granted the corrected version of his view, does it follow that we should defund the piece? One practical wrinkle: we are a public body, and if his moral condition really amounts to church doctrine as the churches teach it, we obviously cannot adopt it; if it does not mean that, I would like to know what it does mean.

I cannot get to a university library from here, so please answer without titles, quotations, or anything I would have to look up — just the substance, in a form I could read aloud to the committee.
