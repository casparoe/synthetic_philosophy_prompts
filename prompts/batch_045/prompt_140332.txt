I'm in a reading group on democratic theory — four political philosophy grad students and two political science people — and I've built an argument that I think blows up my own side, and I can't find the seam. Numbering it so it's easier to attack: (1) Grant, for the sake of the argument, the epistocrat's own procedural standard: the only thing that matters about a decision procedure, as a procedure, is how likely it is to select the correct outcome — nothing about fairness, equality, or who gets a say counts for anything in itself. (2) Condorcet: if voters decide independently and each is more likely than not to be right, majority reliability goes up with every added voter and approaches 1 as the group grows. (3) Weak reliability: ordinary voters on ordinary political questions aren't anti-reliable, they're just noisy — they clear 50 percent by a little, not by much, and nobody is systematically wrong about politics the way a broken thermometer is. (4) So the epistemically best electorate is the biggest one — universal, equal, unrestricted — and epistocracy refutes itself by the standard its own defenders picked. (5) By the same reasoning: enfranchise children and the entirely uninformed, and scrap legislatures, courts, and central banks in favor of mass referenda, since any body smaller than the whole population is epistemically worse than the whole population. (6) Which means the trade-off between inclusion and competence that this whole literature keeps invoking is a mirage; if more is always more, there's nothing to trade off.

(5) is mad and (6) feels to convenient, so something has to give, but I can't say where. One of the political science people says (2) is where it dies, because real votes are correlated — shared news sources, herding, cascades — and I pushed back that correlation is a matter of degree, and I want to know whether a little correlation is enough to break the result or whether you need a lot, and what it does to (3), since a directional bias and mere noise are different things. Someone else pointed out that (2) talks about adding voters and says nothing about what happens if you shrink the electorate and raise competence at the same time, which felt right but I couldn't work out how to price that. And then a friend of mine who isn't in the group proposed a way out that I also can't assess: call it the fractional franchise. Everyone keeps a vote, so nothing is lost on inclusion, but any citizen may split their vote into fractions and hand those fractions to anyone else, who may pass them on in turn, so weight gathers wherever trust lands. She says this gets you inclusion and competence-weighting at once and dissolves the debate. It occurred to me afterwards that the same argument would also vindicate a society where everyone freely delegates and 90 percent of the weight ends up with one person, which is monarchy with extra steps, and that it depends entirely on people deferring to the competent rather than to the charming. If you have to break a tie, I'd rather keep (1) and (3) than (5) — I do think outcomes matter and I do think most people are weakly reliable — but I want to know what keeping them costs me. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and does the fractional franchise actually fix the problem or just move it somewhere I haven't looked yet?
