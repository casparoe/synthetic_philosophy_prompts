I've got a grad reading group on signaling this term — mostly philosophy students plus two brave biologists — and I sketched the following as a reductio of the handicap principle. I definately don't believe the conclusion: the theory obviously does explain something about peacocks' tails, stotting, and the rest. But I can't find the bad step, and it's bugging me.

Set up some terms. A signal's differential cost is the extra cost a worse-quality sender pays, compared with a better-quality one, for producing the same signal. And call a signal recycled if the sender gets part of the expenditure back later through the receiver's response: the wage premium that repays the cost of the credential, the mating success that repays the tail, the extra custom that repays the advert.

(1) What makes a signal honest, on the handicap story, is that a lower-quality sender gains nothing by mimicking a higher-quality one: the extra cost of the mimicked signal outweighs what the mimic would gain by being taken for the better type. Honesty is bought by cost, and only by cost.

(2) So what does the work is the difference in cost between types at a given signal level, not the level of cost itself. Nothing in that reasoning changes if you add or subtract the same quantity of cost from every sender.

(3) Differences are cheap. Pick any tiny margin you like. There is a possible signal whose cost to a worse sender exceeds its cost to a better one by exactly that margin, and a receiver response small enough that the margin suffices to deter mimicking. So, in principle, an entirely honest signal can cost next to nothing.

(4) And you can cheapen any signal you already have. Take a working costly signal and add a recycling step: the receiver hands back a fixed benefit to everyone who produces it. The honest sender and the mimic both collect the rebate, so the gap between them — the only thing that matters, by (2) — is exactly what it was, and the separation survives. Now scale the rebate up. The net cost falls to zero, and then below zero, with honesty intact throughout.

(5) So the level of cost does no work. Honesty is cheap to arrange, no particular amount of waste is predicted, and since a differential can be manufactured at any scale, the theory is equally comfortable with honest signals that are free and with honest signals that are ruinous.

(6) But then the handicap principle has no content. It was introduced to explain why signals are extravagant rather than cheap, it earns its keep by predicting real costs that people then go and measure, and a story that fits costless signals as comfortably as costly ones fits anything.

I want to keep (6) — I think the theory predicts waste in some sense — and I'm willing to give up (1) under pressure, since it always was more slogan than claim. What I want is the diagnosis and the bill. Which step do you reject? If it's (4), what exactly does recycling do to the receiver's side of the interaction that I've illegitimately held fixed, and is there a bound on the rebate I'm ignoring? If it's (3), why can't a tiny differential be enough — is the problem that cost functions aren't free parameters, or that thin margins aren't stable once noise, drift, and mutant senders are in the picture? If it's (2), give me the notion of cost on which the level matters and not merely the difference. And if the honest answer is to swallow (5) and reinterpret waste as waste relative to some counterfactual — full information, the cheapest signal that would do the job, a world without the signal — then tell me which counterfactual, and what an experiment would have to show to tell the readings apart.
