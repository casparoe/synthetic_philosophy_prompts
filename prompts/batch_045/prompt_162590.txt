hey — assume you're a professional philosopher who also happens to have run a lot of classroom games (seminars, workshops, the kind of thing where half the room is checked out by minute 20). Please brainstorm two or three candidate mechanics first, kill the ones that don't survive contact with the arithmetic, and then write up the one that does. Show me the algebra explicitly — I want to check it before I run it on real students.

The setting: second-year undergrad course I teach called "Chance and Reason", about 20 students, mix of philosophy and econ majors, one 75-minute slot. They've done basic Bayes and expected value and nothing at all on self-locating belief. The room has movable chairs and a projector, no laptops, and I've got a coin and my phone. No real money changing hands — points on a ledger, and the winning team gets something stupid.

The thing I want them to feel is the Sleeping Beauty setup: fair coin, woken once if heads and twice if tails, memory erased between wakings, and on waking she's asked for her credence in heads — and the genuinely uncomfortable fact that smart people divide into 1/2 and 1/3 and neither side budges. Constraints: they shouldn't hear the name Sleeping Beauty, or the words halfer and thirder, until the last ten minutes; no lecture up front; the idea has to arrive through what the rules make them do, not through anything I say.

Three mechanics I've been toying with, which you can build on, combine, or throw out if you've got better:

1. A hive-mind version, where a team of students is *one* person with *one* bank account, and a "waking" is implemented by the experimenter tapping shoulders while everyone's eyes are shut and music is playing. Woken members write a number on a slip without opening their eyes or talking, and crucially nobody knows how many of their own wake-ups are happening right now. Reports are scored with a quadratic (Brier-style) rule so honest reporting is optimal, then pooled into the team account.
2. A "captain" variant: in each trial exactly one of the wake-ups, drawn at random from among them, is designated the captain, and only the captain's slip is scored. My guess is that being told you're the captain washes out the multiplicity somehow and the number jumps — work out where it lands.
3. A round where I stop asking them for the answer and instead ask them to *build* the protocol: teams are secretly handed a target number (1/2 or 1/3) and have to design a waking-and-scoring procedure in which that number is the optimal report. Then the teams swap procedures and have to work out which number the other side was assigned. The horrible possibility I want them to walk into is that both teams succeed.

I also have a hunch about diagnosis, which you should test rather than humour: that the whole dispute turns on whether the *trial* or the *awakening* is the unit the credence is indexed to, and that you can flip the right answer without changing anyone's evidence at all — e.g. by taking the same slips from the same trials and summing them per trial versus averaging them per trial. If your mechanics don't actually support that reading, say so and give me the one they do support.

What I need in the write-up: rules tight enough to run cold, materials, minute-by-minute timeline, the exact payoff scheme (no money), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the disagreement.
