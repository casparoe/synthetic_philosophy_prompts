Write a single page — roughly 800 to 900 words, to be read in one sitting — from a fictional industrial-supply catalogue for municipal furniture and infrastructure, in the deadpan house style of a procurement catalogue: running header, item name and model number, a short "Application" paragraph, a spec block with material / finish / lead time, and a footnote. The conceit is that the catalogue is issued in a jurisdiction that has not merely accepted the view that artifacts have politics but has bureaucratized it. Every item is warranted to produce a specified social effect — exclusion, dispersal, dispersal-with-surveillance, deference to authority, participation — and that effect is listed on the same line as the gauge of steel and the delivery time, as though it were a lumen rating.

Include at least these five items, and add one or two of your own if they earn their place:

1. A three-position armrest set for a transit bench, listed as a night-use deterrent.
2. A parkway overpass with a specified vertical clearance, sold as "standard geometry."
3. An automated public convenience or fare gate whose timed cycle enforces a norm — a machine that, in the catalogue's own words, settles a question so that no attendant has to decide it.
4. Two alternative supply modules offered side by side: a large centralized plant and a kit of small distributed cells. Their spec blocks should list required social arrangements — continuous custody, exclusion zones, staffing levels, ownership assumptions — in the accessories column, as if a guard force were a bolt-on option.
5. One item carrying the certification body's highest rating for participation, neutrality, non-exclusion, and freedom from surveillance.

The comedy has to come from the content, not from wordplay. Each item should exaggerate a real feature of the claim that artifacts have politics, and get that feature right: the way a physical dimension can do distributive work silently, permanently, and with a paper trail that mentions no one; the claim that some technologies are political in a stronger sense because they are only compatible with certain social arrangements; the idea that designers delegate a moral or political decision to an object so that it appears to enforce itself; the corollary that a designer can then be credited — or blamed — for effects that are actually produced by the surrounding regime; and the institutional habit of treating a public vote as a design input.

The page should arrive at a reductio without ever announcing one. The perfectly certified item in (5) should be an object no one can use for anything: value-engineered into full compliance — no exclusion, no surveillance, everyone consulted — and thereby reduced to something unusable, or usable only as a monument to the procedure that produced it. And the effects should be warranted, somewhere in the small print, only under "Standard Social Conditions" as defined in a long appendix nobody reads. A reader should feel, without being told, that the politics keeps sliding off the object and into the institution, and then back onto the object again.

Two constraints on tone and fairness. First, no winking: the voice is that of a competent, slightly tired procurement officer trying to be helpful, and nowhere does the text admit that any of this is strange; the irony must remain legible to a reader with no philosophy background and must stay deadpan for a reader with one. Second, the satire has to be fair: at least one item should be a case where the political reading is genuinely correct — where the object really does, in fact, sort people — so that the piece does not collapse into the opposing slogan that objects are neutral after all. Someone sympathetic to the claim that artifacts have politics should wince in recognition rather than feel parodied by a strawman.

Invented apparatus only — an effects index, a certification body, appendix references, model numbers. No real persons, no citations, no allusions that depend on knowing who argued what, and no jokes whose payload is a pun or a current event. Format it as a genuine catalogue page, including a small "How to Order" box.
