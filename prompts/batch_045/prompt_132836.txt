At the hospital network where I work there's a policy I have to vote on every two years (I'm on the committee that re-signs it, which is why im asking). Everyone over 40 gets their ten-year risk of a first heart attack computed by the model that's built into the electronic health record, anyone who comes out above 15 percent gets offered statins and a year of nurse follow-up, and anyone below gets a leaflet. The model outputs one number and that number decides it. That's the whole rule.

Here's what's been bugging me for two years. My colleagues are perfectly happy to move someone across the line by adding information: a calcium scan, a polygenic score, a detailed family history, sometimes a variable I'd never heard of. And they're equally happy to move someone back under it, or to refuse to compute it at all with certain inputs, because they think that variable isn't "really about" the patient: postcode, ethnicity, sometimes sex, sometimes how long someone's been in the country. Nothing about the patient changes. Their risk goes up or down depending on how much we're willing to know and which bits of what we know we're willing to feed in. Then we hand them the number as if it were a measurement of them, like a blood pressure.

Second thing, same meetings. The doctors say constantly that guidelines are for populations and they treat individual patients, and they say it as a criticism of the policy. But they also use the score to turn people down, and we audit any clinic that enrolls too many patients from below the threshold, because we can only afford about four thousand a year. So the number is simultaneously too crude to treat a person by and good enough to refuse them with.

I don't know the philosophy vocabulary for this, so bear with me. What I want from you is: what does a policy like this presuppose about what a risk number for one particular person even is? Does it presuppose that there's some single correct answer for this patient that we're approximating more or less well, or can the whole practice be run on the view that the number is just the rate in whatever group you decide to count them in, with no group being the right one? If it's the second, is our policy incoherent, or is it fine as long as we're honest that we're picking the group for reasons that aren't medical at all — cost, fairness, what we're willing to be seen using? And does the fixed budget change the question? Maybe all we really need is a ranking, and it doesn't matter whether the numbers are comparable between people as long as they order them right, in which case half our arguments about which variables belong in the model are arguments about the wrong thing.

Then: which views does the practice quietly rule out, and where is it underdetermined — where would several quite different theories all license exactly what we do? And tell me what the policy would have to look like if we gave up the idea that there's any fact of the matter about an individual's risk. Would the threshold survive, would the audits survive, and what would we say to a patient who asks why they got the leaflet?

You won't be able to look anything up, so don't cite studies or tell me who to read — I want the argument worked out on our actual case. And please write it as continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs the way you'd write me an email.
