A familiar institutional device: an authority announces in advance that some check will occur while withholding the date, so that the check cannot be anticipated — announced-but-undated fire drills, random workplace drug testing, simulated phishing campaigns, unannounced teaching observations. I want to know what such a device presupposes about surprise and about knowledge, and I want to approach it through two versions of the device that a single university might run, because they seem to me to pull in opposite directions.

Scheme A, the integrity spot-check. On the first day of a ten-week term the registrar posts that there will be exactly one mandatory in-person integrity check for the online degree programme that term. The posted rules add two further clauses. First, the check counts as valid only if, at 6 p.m. on the preceding evening, no student could have known that it would fall on the following day. Second, if the office finds that students had in fact worked out the date — a shared spreadsheet, a long message-board thread — the check is void: it is cancelled, a fresh date is drawn by lottery, and the term continues under the original announcement.

Scheme B, the classroom observation. The dean announces that each instructor will receive one unannounced teaching observation per term. Here the rules say the reverse about being worked out: an observation goes ahead even if the instructor has deduced the date correctly. The dean's written rationale is that the point of the scheme is that instructors prepare every session, and a correctly deduced observation secures that just as well. The only formal requirements are that the date be drawn by lottery and that nobody in the office tell the instructor the result of the draw.

For the record, the argument the students run in Scheme A. Suppose the check has not occurred by the evening before the last day of term. Then, since the announcement guarantees exactly one check, it must fall on the last day; so at that point the students could know it in advance; so by the rules it cannot fall on the last day. But with the last day eliminated the same reasoning applies to the second-to-last day, and so on back to the first; so no valid check can occur at all — after which a check on, say, the Wednesday of week four surprises them completely.

Please reconstruct, as explicitly as you can, the implicit theory each scheme rests on, and address the following.

1. What must the drafters of Scheme A believe about surprise and about knowledge? Their rule presupposes that the students' argument fails somewhere. Where, if anywhere, does the practice implicitly locate the failure — and does the practice need a diagnosis of the failure in order to be workable at all? Does it strictly presuppose that a valid check is possible, or could the scheme still do its job if the announcement were somehow inconsistent?

2. What else does the void-if-worked-out clause commit them to? It requires the office to distinguish a student who knew the date from one who guessed it correctly and posted it. What notion of knowledge is the policy using, and does it need something like an anti-luck condition, on which a true guess does not amount to knowledge? And what is the could in could have known doing: is it about the evidence available to the students, about what follows from the announcement, or about what is knowable given what they already know? Does the rule presuppose that knowledge is closed under known implication, and what happens to the rule if it is?

3. Do the same for Scheme B. What does it strictly presuppose — something thinner, presumably? Is its unannounced an epistemic condition at all, or merely a procedural one, a matter of not having been officially told? Which of the two schemes, if either, is a surprise-inspection scheme in more than name?

4. Are the two schemes consistent with one another? Can one institution rationally cancel a check because it was deduced and run an observation because it was deduced, or does one of them rest on a mistake about what it is doing? Distinguish what each strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where either scheme is underdetermined — that is, where two or more mutually incompatible accounts of the paradox would license exactly the same rule.

5. Suppose the university amended Scheme A to a probabilistic condition: a check is valid if no student's credence, at 6 p.m. the evening before, that it would fall the next day exceeded 20 per cent. Would that make the scheme more or less workable than the knowledge version? Would it collapse the distinction between A and B, and what would it cost the university in terms of what it has to be able to verify?

Throughout, keep separate what is settled from what is contested. I take the structure of the backward-induction argument and the absence of any agreed resolution of it to be settled matters of logic and of intellectual history. Where you offer a diagnosis of the paradox, or a claim about closure, anti-luck conditions, or the relation between surprise and high credence, mark it as one contested position among others rather than as the consensus, and say in a sentence what the main rival position is.
