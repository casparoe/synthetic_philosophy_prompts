Last weekend I was at a friends birthday thing and we killed the better part of an hour coming up with dumber and dumber versions of the happy birthday song — change one word, change the tempo, sing it as a question, do it as a football chant, whatever. What struck me afterwards was how uneven it felt: some changes were instantly funny and some were just nothing, and the ones that felt like nothing were usually the ones where we were only turning a dial we had already found, while the good ones seemed to invent a new dial. That is more or less Hofstadter's picture in his essay on variations on a theme — concepts have sliders or knobs, creativity is mostly about which knobs you notice exist and how far you push them, not about ex nihilo invention — and I want a session where students feel that in their bones instead of being able to recite it.

Concretely: I teach a mixed undergraduate seminar called something like Creativity, Concepts and Computation — about 18 students, mostly CS and philosophy with a few fine-art people, one 90-minute block, no budget, no instruments, a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard. Design a runnable game in which they generate and judge variations on a seed theme. I have been going back and forth between three seeds — a tiny eight-note melody I made up, a four-line verse, and the wedding toast treated as a little social script — so pick one (or propose something better) and justify it given that these students cannot read music and will not want to perform. Two things I care about. First, the rules should make them run into, before anyone names it, the gap between turning a knob and discovering one: turning a known knob forever produces that flat endless-remix feeling, and a genuinely new knob retrospectively changes what the theme was, so the theme cannot have been a fixed essence with fixed dimensions sitting there in advance. Second, there is a thing I keep half-noticing in sessions like this that I want built into the scoring and then actually tested rather than assumed: the first person to find a knob seems to own it, later variations on the same knob read as derivative even when the setting is genuinely new, and so what a variation is worth looks like a function of where it sits in the order of discovery rather than of what it is. I would like the payoff scheme (points, poker chips, sticky dots, nothing real) to make that visible as data — say two seperate axes they vote on, recognisable-as-a-variation-of-the-theme and surprising, plotted on the board so the dead zones and the sweet spot and the decay over discovery order all show up as a picture — plus, if it fits, a short reverse round where a group is handed another group's set of variations and has to reconstruct the knob-set, and finds that more than one fits.

Give me: the rules and materials, any roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the exact facilitator script for framing it up front without leaking the framework — ideally they coin their own word for knob and only get Hofstadter's in the debrief. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto specific parts of the model, an honest list of what will predictably go wrong when I run it (the class clown who derails it, the subgroup that converges on one joke, someone who produces something that is just a different thing entirely, voting getting gamed, the quiet students who hate generating things out loud) with how to handle each, and finally a frank note on what the activity cannot deliver. My worry is that 90 minutes with an invented theme cannot reproduce whatever it is that makes a variation actually good rather than merely novel, and that the knob model may itself be silent on that — so tell me what I will have to say out loud myself, and if the whole thing does not fit in 90 minutes, cut something and tell me what and why.
