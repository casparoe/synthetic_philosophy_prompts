ok so I got voluntold onto our college's committee for rewriting the AI-in-the-classroom policy, and because I'm the only philosopher on it (rest of the room: a dean, a chemist, a reference librarian, two undergrads) I've somehow become the "what even is a source" person. We report in three weeks and I've got maybe ten minutes in the meeting. I want to bring the Nyāya epistemology stuff I teach to bear on it, but I need to know what it actually commits us to.

The bits I have in mind: śabda (testimony) as its own pramāṇa rather than a species of anumāna (inference) — the payoff being that when you learn something from a speaker you are not running an argument from "this person says so" plus some observed track record to the conclusion; you're taking the word of an āpta, someone who knows and says what they know. Plus the Nyāya line that a cognition's validity isn't self-certifying but gets established from outside, roughly by whether acting on it succeeds. And the related question about whether two different pramāṇas can deliver the very same fact or whether each has its own proper patch.

Here's where I think the weeds are. Our committee has started calling model output "orphan assertions": strings with the whole surface profile of testimony — assertoric, fluent, apparently informed, apt to be true — but with nobody behind them who knows anything or intends anything, so there's no speaker whose reliability you could be leaning on. Which means, if I've got the framework right, they aren't śabda at all and the framework gives you no third slot: either you've quietly demoted them to inference off a track record, or you've got a source type the theory has no room for. Then it gets messier: (a) the "grounded" case, where the system hands back a sentence plus a link to a document with a real author, (b) the laundered case, where a student lightly rewords the output and signs their name to it, and (c) the calibration problem, that the same system is fine for boilerplate emails, shaky on the history of the Mughal court, and hopeless on case law, so "is it reliable?" has no global yes-or-no, and anyway the check-by-successful-action move only bites where feedback is cheap and fast.

Please give me the most important implications of this package for how we should actually draft the thing — in particular whether it tells us anything about the difference between requiring disclosure and requiring verification, and whether it commits us to something the dean will hate. No reading lists or citations, I can only really use reasoning I can say out loud in the room, and definitely keep the Sanskrit light enough that a chemist and two nineteen-year-olds follow it.
