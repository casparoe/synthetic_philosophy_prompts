I have been chewing on this since a comment thread on a blog I read, and I cannot find the exit.

The target is the Marxist theory of exploitation. I will state it in the version I think is defensible, and then run an argument that seems to show it proves far too much.

The theory, as I will grant it:

(1) Exploitation is the appropriation of unpaid labor. The capitalist pays the worker the value of her labor-power — roughly, what it costs to produce and reproduce a worker of that kind — and receives in exchange the use of that labor-power for the full working day. The excess of labor performed over labor paid for is surplus value. Nothing is stolen in the sense of a broken contract; the wrong, if there is one, is that labor is extracted without an equivalent anyway.

(2) The surplus value a given capitalist *realizes* need not equal the surplus value her own workers *produced*. Capitals compete, profit rates tend to equalize, and commodities exchange at prices of production rather than at values. A capital-intensive firm realizes more surplus value than its own workers created; a labor-intensive firm realizes less. Both claims, (1) and (2), are things I want to keep, and as far as I can tell Marxists grant both.

The argument I cannot escape:

(3) Consider the limiting case of (2): a nearly fully automated firm. The machines were designed and built elsewhere, by other people, at other firms. Its own production workers are a rounding error. The new value its workers add is close to zero, and yet it earns the average rate of profit on its capital. So essentially all of the surplus value it realizes was produced by workers employed by other capitals, and reaches it through the price system.

(4) If that is coherent for one firm, nothing in the account ties any capitalist's profit to her own workers. And symmetrically, nothing ties any worker's being exploited to her own employer or her own wage bargain. Exploitation, on this account, is a relation between classes, mediated by market prices.

(5) But then the story about the point of production is scaffolding. What does the real work in (1) — the working day, the wage contract, the extraction of surplus labor inside the factory — can be removed without loss. All that has to be true is: the producing class produces more than it gets back, and the non-producing class gets the difference.

(6) And that is true in any society that sets aside a surplus. A hunter-gatherer band that feeds its children and elders. A democratically planned economy whose producers collectively and knowingly vote to invest a fifth of output in growth, or in hospitals, or in a buffer against famine. In all of them, the producers produce more than they individually receive back.

(7) So the Marxist claim is either a truism that holds of every society that has ever existed or could exist — in which case it cannot indict capitalism in particular — or it is really only a complaint about the structure of ownership and the coercion built into it, which is a claim about property and force, not about labor and value.

(8) If it is the latter, the entire apparatus of value theory is idle machinery. One could have run the whole critique with nothing but a theory of property, coercion, and bargaining power, and every step from (1) onward was unnecessary.

(9) Either way, exploitation theory cannot do the thing it was built to do: ground a specific indictment of capitalism at the point of production.

I do not accept (9). But I also do not want to end up saying that a planned economy whose producers voted to invest their own surplus is exploitative in the same sense that capitalism is, and I would rather give up almost any other step than give up (1) or (2).

So: which step do you reject?
