I teach a 90-minute seminar session on cooperation and rationality for a mixed advanced-undergraduate group: about eighteen students, mostly philosophy and computer science, all of whom have already worked through Nash equilibrium, the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and the backward-induction argument for defection in a finitely repeated game. I want one in-class game that makes them feel the following idea, without my naming it in advance.

The idea is this. Two anonymous players are about to play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma: no repetition, no communication, no enforcement, no reputation, no shadow of the future. But instead of each choosing an action, each submits a program that receives the other's source code as input and outputs an action, and the two programs are then run against each other. In this second-order game something becomes available that was not available before: a pair of programs each of which says, in effect, if my opponent's source text is identical to mine then cooperate, otherwise defect. Both cooperate. Neither can be profitably rewritten unilaterally, given the other, even though defection was the unique rational action in the underlying game. Nothing here depends on altruism, repetition, or an enforcer: the whole effect rests on one program's being able to recognise the other well enough to trust it. Most of what is interesting about the idea lies in how fragile that recognition is.

Design the game for this group, in which the students supply the programs, find this move themselves, and then lose it. I want them to end the session having felt both how cooperation becomes available at all and how narrow the conditions are under which it survives. Please give me:

- Full rules, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline, including how to group eighteen people and how submissions are sealed, anonymised, and paired. Assume I do not want to spend the session debugging infrastructure: either everything is resolvable in the room by hand or on a projector, or whatever software you propose can be set up in fifteen minutes and has a hand-run fallback.
- A submission format usable by students who cannot program: either a minimal instruction language with a handful of primitives, or a fixed natural-language template, together with how I translate a submission into something executable and how I keep each one short enough to adjudicate live.
- Four rounds of escalating perturbation, with what you expect to happen in each and why: plain source exchange; source passed through an obfuscating compiler that renames variables, reorders independent lines, and inserts a no-op, breaking naive string comparison without changing behaviour; no source at all, only a behavioural fingerprint consisting of each program's output against three fixed probe programs, so that trust has to be inferred from conduct; and finally execution noise, in which there is a fixed chance that a program's output is flipped or that one character of its source is altered before the run.
- A small number of planted submissions that guarantee the failure modes occur even if the class happens to be homogeneous, and a way of planting them that does not give the game away.
- A payoff matrix with actual numbers, satisfying T > R > P > S and 2R > T + S, chosen so that the pull towards defection is genuinely felt and so that the always-defect submission can plausibly finish top of the individual leaderboard.
- Small stakes that are not money, an individual score, and a visible running group total.
- A debrief, after which and only after which the idea gets its name, mapping particular moments in the play onto particular features of it: why this is not the folk theorem again, given that there is no future to be shaded; the difference between cooperating with a copy of yourself and cooperating with anyone who would cooperate with you, and why the second collapses into a regress or a simulation that never returns; why the mutually cooperative profile is one equilibrium among many rather than the solution, so that an equilibrium selection problem remains; and why the availability of a mutually cooperative outcome here settles nothing about what an individual agent has reason to do. Say how to handle the student who points out, correctly, that the program which always defected won on points.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, specifically: near-identical copy-detectors that fail to recognise each other, out-of-band collusion, underspecified or crashing submissions, the unconditional cooperator who flattens the comparison, the student who defects while presenting themselves as having submitted a cooperator, and whatever else you anticipate.
- An honest note on which feature of the idea the game cannot stage, and which I will therefore have to state in so many words.
