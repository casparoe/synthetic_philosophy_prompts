Me and my mate had a huge arguement last night about whether Hume's case against miracles is a real argument or just a machine for making sure no testimony can ever win. His line was that laws of nature are only summaries of uniform experience, so that experience will always outweigh what some people say they saw. I said fine, but if laws are only summaries, then every exception you accept eats into the uniformity that's doing all the work, and the bit where you ask whose falsehood would be more miraculous can be quietly retuned forever so the answer is always the witness. I definately didn't win it, and I reckon the whole thing works better as a bit of writing than as a debate.

So: write me a deadpan onboarding memo, about 550 words, from a senior assessor at a government Office for the Assessment of Prodigies to newly recruited claims assessors, explaining office procedure. Make the procedure a straight-faced application of Hume's way of handling miracle claims, and build it around a few things I want in there: the office maintains a Uniformity Reserve, the stock of unbroken experience that gives its rejections their force, and every prodigy it certifies is debited against it, so that after a run of successful claims the office has to stage an Emergency Restoration of Uniform Experience, retroactively reclassifying previously certified healings as unusual but lawful or as insufficiently witnessed; it grades witnesses on a Retrospective Barbarism Index, by century, latitude, and how much the witnesses stood to gain from being thought holy; it applies an Offset Rule under which rival traditions' prodigies cancel one another out, including a standing power to declare two sects that agree about nothing else to be a single religion for offset purposes; and it has a rule for the awkward cases where the documentation is so good that its falsehood would be more miraculous than the event itself, namely that senior staff must produce an ever more elaborate account of how the testimony came about (mass suggestion, pious forgery, herd delusion) without ever examining the event.

The jokes have to come from the actual machinery of the argument: laws as summaries of experience, the balance between whose falsehood would be more miraculous, the presumption against reports that flourish among ignorant and barbarous peoples, rival faiths knocking each other out. Don't make it about Hume the man and don't make it about religion being daft: someone who thinks Hume basically got it right should read it and think it fair rather than a strawman, and someone who's never heard of Hume should still get the joke. Write it as one continuous block of prose, like a long email, no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and in British English.
