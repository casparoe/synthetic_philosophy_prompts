I teach a third-year seminar on philosophy of action and social ontology — 17 students this term, and my department just moved us to 80-minute blocks, so I'm still getting used to having more time than I know what to do with. We've read a bit of Bratman and a bit of Gilbert, and the same thing keeps happening: everyone nods along when we say that a joint action isn't just several people each doing something that happens to add up, and then they go straight back to drawing it that way the moment we hit a hard case. Two years ago I tried the demo where two students carry a table out of the room and I ask who did what; it was useless, everyone just said "we did it" and looked at me like I was wasting their time. Separately, I was talking to a colleague down in psychology who works on joint attention in toddlers, and she made the offhand remark that very young kids seem to coordinate on things they can't possibly have any overall conception of — they're tracking a local cue, not a shared goal — and I haven't been able to stop chewing on that.

So I want a real activity this time: rules, roles, small stakes, where they run into the problem before it has a name. Constraints: no real money (points toward a coffee voucher are fine), no more than about 50 minutes of actual play so I keep 30 for debrief, and nothing needing prep beyond envelopes, index cards, a stopwatch and a whiteboard. I don't want to say "shared intention," "we-intention," or "joint commitment" out loud until the debrief.

The contrast I want them to feel has a few faces, and I'd like the rounds arranged so they hit them in this order, more or less. First: a case where nobody in the group has the whole plan — each person only gets their own fragment and only gets local feedback about whether they're on track — and yet they still succeed, and they still get quietly annoyed at anyone who slacks, and afterwards they still describe it as something *we* did. Second: a case where the group succeeds smoothly and everyone is fully informed and fully cooperative, except that one member was privately instructed to be a pure mercenary — they don't care about the group at all, they're only maximising their own points, they'd walk if a better offer came along — and nobody else knows this. Third: a case where the group has to choose what to do by majority vote, and someone who voted against then has to carry out the winning option; afterwards "we did it" feels wrong in their mouth, but from outside it's obvious that the group did it. My own suspicion, which I'd rather the activity test than assume, is that the "we" isn't really in anyone's head at all — it's in the structure: roles, procedures, who can call things off, who can be rebuked and by whom. But I want at least one round set up so that this suspicion could lose, and lose visibly, because the students should be arguing with each other and not just with me.

What I need from you is the activity itself, and I'd like it in these sections, roughly this long:

**Setup and materials** — about 150 words: what I prepare, what each envelope contains, how groups are formed.
**The rounds** — about 600 words: three rounds with concrete rules, the secret role cards, and a payoff scheme that makes the relevant choice bite without money.
**Minute-by-minute** — about 150 words: a timeline for the 80 minutes including what I'm doing while they play.
**Debrief** — about 450 words: the order in which I reveal what, which moment in the play maps onto which theoretical option, and the questions that get them to say the distinction themselves before I name it.
**What will go wrong** — about 250 words: the predictable failures and how to handle them (someone working out that there are secret cards, a group finishing early, someone refusing a role, the vote landing on the one person who can't do the task).
**What the activity can't show** — about 200 words: the part of the theory that has to come from me in lecture form, especially anything about the normativity of joint action that a coffee voucher can't buy.

**Settled vs contested** — about 200 words: in your notes to me, seperate what's close to consensus among people who work on this from what's a live fight, and flag anywhere your design quietly rigs the intuition toward one theory. Please don't write the debrief as though my "structure not heads" hunch were the received view — if it's a minority position, say so and say what the majority would make of the same rounds.
