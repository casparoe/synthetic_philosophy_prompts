Hey — I'm working on a short paper arguing that the question "is brain size morally relevant?" is underspecified until you say which neural surrogate you actually care about, and I need a case that pries the surrogates apart. Can you build me one to order?

The shape: two subjects whose moral status we have to weigh against each other — who gets the last dose of analgesia, who to treat first, whose hour of suffering counts for more, your pick — set up so that three views deliver three different verdicts:

- a volume view (moral status tracks brain size, bigger is more) has to say subject L counts for more than subject S;
- a total-machinery view (what matters is how much neural equipment there is — neuron count, integration capacity — not how much space it occupies) has to say L and S are exactly tied;
- and a hedonist who says nothing about brains at all, only that what matters is the total valence experienced summed over conscious moments, has to say S counts for more than L.

Strictly greater, exactly equal, strictly less, all out of one case.

The bit I think is hard, and the reason I'm asking you rather than doing it myself: that third verdict has to come out inverted — smaller subject, more moral weight per unit of clock time — without the small-brained subject being smarter, more self-aware, or better at anything. Hold the behavioural and cognitive profile fixed: same repertoire, same pain behaviour, same life stretching ahead; whatever differs has to be neural, not something you could read off what the subjects do. Two levers you're welcome to use if they help, and I'll grant you both as premises: to get the tie you probably want the same neuron count packed into different volumes (bird-style dense packing versus mammal-style), and for the inversion, if a more compact brain runs on shorter conduction delays and finer temporal resolution, then per clock-minute it may pack in more distinguishable conscious moments, so a view that sums valence over moments weights its minutes more heavily even though it never mentions size. Use them or find your own; I dont mind, so long as the verdict falls out of the view plus the facts of the case instead of being written into the case.

Realism constraints: something a clinician, a vet, or an animal ethics committee could genuinely face — no sci-fi, no mad scientists. Real conditions are fine to lean on (people living with half a cortex, or with badly hydrocephalic brains, who still test in the normal range), and grant yourself whatever empirical premise you need there; I dont want citations. One paragraph for the scenario.

Then the part I actually need: for each of the three views, show the reasoning that gets it to its verdict, and be explicit about which single feature of the case is doing the work. Tell me what collapses if I remove that feature — my guess is the third verdict evaporates the moment I compare by subjective moments rather than clock minutes, but tell me whether that's right. And tell me how the defender of whichever view comes out looking worst would attack the case: what exactly would they say is rigged?

Last thing: flag anywhere the design cheats, i.e. anywhere you've stipulated a verdict instead of deriving it, and give me a confidence level for each of the three derivations plus the one point you're least sure of.
