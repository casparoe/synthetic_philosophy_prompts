I am giving a paper in the work-in-progress seminar of a mainstream analytic epistemology department in three weeks, and I want the question period mapped in advance: a prep sheet, not a defense of the view.

The view I am defending. Hinge epistemology begins from the observation that some propositions — that I have hands, that the earth long predates me, that other people have minds — are not held on the basis of evidence, are not rationally assessable, and are not open to revision in the way ordinary empirical beliefs are; the usual explanation is that they are not beliefs at all but something more like rules, or the fixed framework within which reasons operate. I defend a different account, which I call the default-deflection account. What makes a proposition a hinge, on my account, is a social fact about a community: there is an established practice such that when p is questioned, the default response is to treat the question as a symptom of something wrong with the questioner — ignorance, confusion, bad faith, too much philosophy — rather than as a reason to go and check whether p. Three consequences I argue for explicitly and will not give up: hinges are local, so the same proposition can be a hinge for a surgical team and a live empirical question for a historian; hinge status can be lost without anyone changing their mind about the content, when the practice of deflecting dissolves; and hinges can be false, since a practice of deflecting can be misplaced and nothing in the account requires that deflection track the truth. The talk is forty-five minutes, so replies have to be deliverable in about two minutes.

The audience. Two internalist evidentialists who think of knowledge as requiring reflectively accessible reasons and who will suspect that I have replaced a normative question with a sociological one. One social epistemologist whose own work is on expert deference and testimony, and who will want to know whether my hinges are just her credibility judgments under a different name. One formal epistemologist who will ask what deflection is in Bayesian terms — a prior of one, or a refusal to conditionalize on evidence one has — and whether the account still predicts the immunity to revision that was supposed to be the datum. One senior Wittgenstein scholar, sympathetic but fussy, who holds that the late remarks are grammatical and therapeutic rather than empirical, and that calling a hinge false is exactly the move they were written to dissolve. A handful of graduate students who ask the dangerous naive question. Nobody in the room cares about skepticism, brains in vats, or Gettier; do not give me the objections I would get from an undergraduate audience or from the Internet, and give me no readings, citations, or bibliography — I know the literature.

Give me exactly five numbered items, ordered by how much damage each would do weighted by how likely it is to come, one per constituency above, with the fifth being the question I am hoping nobody asks. Each item must be at most two sentences: the first names who raises it and gives the objection as that person would phrase it, the second gives my two-minute reply and the point at which the reply is softest. If any objection should be conceded rather than answered, mark that in the item. No introduction and no conclusion.
