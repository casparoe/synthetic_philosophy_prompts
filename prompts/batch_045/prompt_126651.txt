I should be grateful for your help with a difficulty that is partly scholarly and partly, I am afraid, departmental.

Our curriculum committee is reconsidering the required sequence in the history of philosophy, and a colleague — a Nietzschean of some standing, and a formidable presence in committee — has circulated a position paper in which she grounds her proposal in Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, *Vom Nutzen und Nachteil der Historie für das Leben* (1874). I work in epistemology and philosophy of mind and have never taught the essay. I cannot tell how much of her exposition is a fair rendering of it and how much is the sort of smoothing a gifted expositor permits herself, and I would not wish either to raise or to withdraw an objection on the strength of a hunch. I reproduce the central passage of her paper as she wrote it:

> Nietzsche's question is what history is for, and his answer is that it is for life. History is not a self-sufficient science, to be pursued for its own sake; it is an instrument of the health of an individual, a culture, or a people. A living thing can take up and transform only so much of the past — he calls this capacity plastic power — and on his diagnosis modern culture has vastly more historical knowledge than it can assimilate. The result is a historical fever in which we know more and more about the past and are less and less able to act, to create, or to forget. Forgetting, which we treat as a defect, is on his account a condition of anything's being done at all: the beast lives within the narrowest horizon, remembers nothing, and is for that reason comparatively contented, whereas a human being who remembered everything and could forget nothing would be paralyzed by the past and unable to act.
>
> Within this framework he distinguishes three kinds of history, each answering to a different need and each liable to its own characteristic degeneration. Monumental history answers to the need of the agent, the ambitious and creative person. It lifts the great moments, works, and deeds out of the past and sets them up as evidence that greatness was once possible and may therefore be possible again. Its degeneration is that it becomes a gallery of effects severed from their causes, invites a hollow imitation of what cannot in fact be repeated, and can be turned against the living, as though the age of greatness were over and nothing remained but admiration. Antiquarian history answers to the need of the pious and the conserving. It is the loving, reverent attachment to the past out of which one has grown — the household, the city, the religion, the discipline — and the desire to hand it on intact. Its degeneration is that it loses all sense of proportion, treats everything inherited as equally venerable, and stiffens into a collector's mania hostile to everything new. Critical history answers to the need of the sufferer who must be released from the past. Here Nietzsche means the properly scholarly and scientific treatment of the past: the disinterested, evidence-bound examination of what really happened, as against the edifying legend of the monumental and the pious fiction of the antiquarian. Its danger is that it is difficult to condemn the past without condemning the present that descends from it, and that the past one wins by such criticism is at best a second nature, usually weaker than the first.
>
> The moral, then, is not that we should have less history but that we should have the right mixture. Each of the three has its season and its claim, and the healthy culture holds them in balance: enough monumental history to give it the courage to act, enough antiquarian history to give it identity, enough critical history to keep the other two honest. What ails us is not history as such but a wrong proportion — we have inherited an immense store of reverent and edifying history and have far too little of the disinterested scholarship that would let us see the past as it really was. And since history is justified only by what it does for life, it must finally be bounded by its opposite: by forgetting, by the narrow horizon, and above all by the perspective of art and religion, which turns the eye away from becoming and lets it rest in what is eternal. Nietzsche calls this the unhistorical, and it is only from within it that the study of the past can serve life rather than merely oppress it.

She then applies the account to the curriculum. The sequence should be reorganized into three strands corresponding to the three kinds: great books read as exemplars of what philosophical thought has achieved and as an incitement to it; a strand that transmits the discipline's inheritance and its sense of its own identity; and a strand of contextual, source-based, demythologizing scholarship. The third, she argues, must be the organizing strand and must grow at the expense of the first two, because on Nietzsche's own account it is the only one of the three that is genuinely scholarly — the only one that treats the past as an object of knowledge rather than as an inspiration to imitate or a heritage to venerate. Hence, she says, the survey should be taught so that students encounter past philosophers as they really were, stripped of the legend that edifies and the piety that preserves. Finally, she proposes a fourth, non-historical strand, in which canonical texts would be read outside their historical settings, for their beauty and for their claim to a significance that does not pass away; this, she says, is Nietzsche's unhistorical element, and it is what finally licenses the other strands and sets their limit.

Two things make me uneasy, although I cannot say how much weight they bear. First, her use of the word critical looks to me like its ordinary academic sense — the sense in which a critical edition or a critical historian is simply a careful, evidence-bound one — and I doubt that is what Nietzsche means by the third kind. Second, the claim that the remedy is a matter of getting the mixture right, so that our disease is a deficit of the critical kind, strikes me as suspiciously tidy; but I genuinely do not know whether the essay prescribes a balance among the three, or whether the three are tied to different kinds of person and different needs rather than being ingredients in a mixture, or whether the real remedy lies elsewhere in the text altogether. I also have an indistinct impression that the essay's closing chapters invoke more than one countervailing power rather than the single one she appeals to, but I may be misremembering something I once overheard.

Could you go through the passage in order and tell me: what a careful reader of the essay would accept as accurate; what is a simplification a teacher might reasonably allow herself, and why it is a simplification; and what is subtly wrong, together with the direction in which it distorts what Nietzsche claims. Then tell me which of her curricular conclusions survive the correction and which rest on the misreading. In particular I want to know whether the third kind, as Nietzsche understands it, is the sort of thing a curriculum could institutionalize as a standing strand at all, and whether the fourth strand she proposes is really the sort of thing the essay envisages, or whether it rests on a mistake about what the unhistorical and the suprahistorical are. If you think the threefold typology cannot bear the pedagogical use she puts it to — because the three kinds are stances toward the past rather than genres of teaching, or for some other reason — say so plainly, and say what her proposal would look like if it were rewritten to fit the text.
