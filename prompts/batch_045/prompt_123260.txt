I teach a final-year seminar on responsibility and autonomous systems, and next week we do the responsibility gap literature — the epistemic sort, the blame-or-control sort, and the retribution gap, plus the one I keep adding to the list myself: an answerability gap, where what the injured party is owed is an account of what happened and why, and nobody is in a position to give it. I want a short story to hand out cold and then argue about, maybe 800–900 words, British English please.

Setting is yours — automated port, care home, warehouse, road freight — but two constraints. First, there definately has to be no hidden culprit: nobody cut a corner, ignored a warning, or was negligent in a way we only learn later, and no last-minute reveal that a human was really behind it after all. The gap has to be a genuine one or the story teaches the opposite of what I want. Second, I want the story to contain an institution I've been toying with in my notes and haven't published on: a salaried, trained Explainership, a person whose statutory job after any incident involving an autonomous system is to reconstruct what happened, meet whoever was harmed, give them the fullest account anyone can put together, and make the apology the system cannot make — with it written into the role that this involves no attribution of blame and is not a finding of fault. The fiction should let a reader feel whether that closes the answerability gap, or whether it's only a costume that leaves the actual thing unanswered.

Keep it deadpan rather than melodramatic: the ache should come from the reader seeing exactly which gap is still open, not from the injury itself being described in detail. And don't let the explainer turn out to be cynical, corrupt, or secretly covering something up — the case I care about is the one where everyone does their job properly and it still isn't enough. Or is enough. That's the seminar.
