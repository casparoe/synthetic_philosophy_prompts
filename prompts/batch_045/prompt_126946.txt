Went down a rabbit hole at lunch and now I can't find the exit.

Setup: an eccentric billionaire slides a vial across the table. You get a million dollars right now if, at this very moment, you intend to drink it at midnight. The stuff just makes you miserably sick for a day, no lasting damage. The money lands in your account whether or not the job ever runs — no observer, no test, no penalty for skipping. Everyone seems to agree you can't simply decide to intend it and pick up the check. Here's my argument for why, and I'm pretty sure it proves too much:

(1) Intending to do X requires believing that you will do X. A plan you're certain you'll never carry out isn't an intention, it's a daydream.
(2) In the vial case, by midnight the moneys already in the bank and drinking is pure cost with zero upside, so you know for a fact you won't drink.
(3) So you can't intend to drink it, and the offer is unwinnable no matter how badly you want the cash. That part feels right to me.
(4) But (1) doesn't care *why* you believe you won't do X. Weak will, fear, the reason evaporating — it's all just "I won't," and on (1) it blocks intending in exactly the same way.
(5) So the person who says "I've signed up for the marathon and told everyone, and I also know from ten years of evidence about myself that I'll quit at mile 18" can't intend to run it. The more honest and better-informed you are about your own patterns, the less able you are to intend. That seems backwards.
(6) Same for the guy who knows he'll hit snooze, the drinker who knows she'll have one tonight, anyone intending to keep a costly promise nobody can enforce. On (4) none of them have intentions at all. But then nobody ever acts against their intentions, so akrasia is impossible and "I meant to but didn't" is always false.
(7) Akrasia is obviously possible. So (1) is false, and intention isn't a prediction about yourself at all.

My friend pushes back with a variant: suppose that instead of willing yourself to drink, you could swallow a pill at noon that mechanically makes you drink at midnight, no willpower involved. Now (2) fails, you satisfy (1), you form the intention, you collect the money. She says this shows the puzzle was never about intending — it's about whether you can rig the hardware, and the original case is only weird because forming the intention is the very thing that destroys your reason to act. I half buy that, but I can't tell which step it kills, and I don't love the implication that "I intend to run it" and "I intend to drink" differ only in whether I can rig myself.

I'd rather keep (2) and (7) then (1). Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? And if (1) survives in some weakened form, what's actually left of it?
