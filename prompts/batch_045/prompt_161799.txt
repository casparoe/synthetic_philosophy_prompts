I am a novelist, three drafts into a book that I am afraid has quietly become a tract, and I need a critic's reading of the story I keep circling back to: Ursula K. Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas. My own novel is set in a city whose continuance depends on a comparable bargain, but I have given the bargain a feature Le Guin's does not have. In my city the terms are administered by a council that can satisfy them in more than one way: it may keep one child in the condition Le Guin describes, or a hundred children each in a condition roughly a hundredth as bad, or ten thousand in a condition ten-thousandth as bad. The quantity to be discharged is fixed; only its distribution among subjects is up to the council, and my characters argue about which arrangement to choose. A rival faction argues for something else entirely: that the city should never have been founded, or should be allowed to dwindle, because a world without the arrangement is better than a world containing it. I want those arguments to be load-bearing, and I want them to be right about what they are committing my characters to.

So I would like a reading of Omelas through population axiology, and I want the reading to be honest in both directions. Tell me what axiology the story actually presupposes, and in particular whether it is an aggregation parable at all: its structure is, on the face of it, conditional rather than maximizing, since the citizens are told that the city's happiness depends on the child rather than asked to compare totals, and since the responses the story records are acquiescence, on the one hand, and departure, on the other, neither of which is a piece of maximizing. Set that against the total, average, and critical-level views and say which of them, if any, the story's arrangement is really a test of, and what each would recommend about it.

Then tell me honestly whether the repugnant conclusion reaches my case. My suspicion is that it may not: the repugnant conclusion is driven by the creation of lives, whereas both Le Guin's city and mine hold the population fixed and vary only the distribution of harm among the same people. If that suspicion is right, say what the genuinely analogous result is within a fixed population — whether the total view's indifference to distribution generates a structurally similar repugnance when the trade is one severe deprivation against many mild ones, and whether the average view or a threshold view escapes it or merely relocates it. And say which of my two disputes, the council's or the dissolution faction's, is the one that actually engages population axiology properly, and why.

I also want to know where the story resists the reading, because I would rather cut material than distort it: the child's singularity may be doing work that no arithmetic preserves, and the story pointedly refuses to tell us whether those who leave help anyone, whether the city falls, or whether there is anywhere they go. Finally, tell me what the story adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not — I take it to be something about knowledge, complicity, and a form of refusal that is neither rescue nor optimization — and tell me what a novelist who borrows its structure would most likely get wrong. Point me, along the way, to the moments in the story that carry the argument, so that my characters can be made to argue about those and not about a paraphrase of them.
