Consider a pattern that runs through the institutional design of most constitutional democracies, and most sharply through that of the United States.

The officials who *make* law are rotated. Presidents are limited to two terms in many presidential systems; legislators in many American states are limited to a fixed number of terms; and even where no legal limit exists, "career politician" functions as an insult, and challengers campaign on the claim that long service in office is itself disqualifying. The officials who *say what the law means* hold office for life during good behaviour and are deliberately insulated from the electorate. The officials who *administer* it — the permanent civil service — cannot be dismissed at the pleasure of an incoming government and are expected to serve successive administrations of opposing parties. And the citizens who decide *what actually happened* in a criminal case are chosen by lot from the electoral roll, questioned and struck until both sides accept them, given no training, required in many jurisdictions to agree unanimously, and asked to give no reasons for what they decide; the jury then dissolves.

The pattern is not obviously consistent with itself. We treat long tenure as a corruption of the office when the office is legislation and as a safeguard of the office when it is adjudication. We exclude legal expertise from the jury room and require it on the bench. We select the finders of fact by lottery while giving the interpreters of law permanent tenure, even though both exercises are said to be done in the name of the people. And term limits and life tenure are each defended as anti-corruption measures — the first by preventing officeholders from becoming attached to office, the second by preventing them from becoming dependent on anyone who could remove them.

I would like the implicit theory reconstructed. What would have to be true about the difference between legislating, judging, administering and finding facts for this allocation of tenure to be the right one? Along which dimension is the gradient supposed to run: how far the task expresses a will rather than tracks a truth; how far it requires judgment that only long practice produces; how predictable and identifiable its holders are, and therefore how easy to capture or bribe; how far its outputs need to be stable across time; or how far it allocates divisible advantages that someone could sell? Please distinguish what the pattern strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined — where several rival theories would have produced exactly this arrangement, so that the arrangement is evidence for none of them.

Then test two things against whatever theory you reconstruct.

First, argue the counterfactual. If the anti-capture rationale were really doing the work, a predictable, long-serving, individually identifiable judge looks like a far better target for influence than an anonymous body drawn by lot and dissolved the same week; the rationale therefore seems to point towards rotating judges and professionalising legislators, the precise inverse of what we have. Does the practice have a resource for answering this, or does it show that judicial tenure rests on an entirely different rationale, so that the pattern is really two unrelated designs laid side by side?

Second, consider a package that splits the difference in an unfamiliar way. Call it the insulated legislature: representatives chosen by lot from the citizenry, serving a single non-renewable six-year term, paid a generous pension thereafter, forbidden any post-office employment in a regulated industry, and assisted by a permanent professional staff; alongside it, judges elected to short renewable terms in low-salience non-partisan elections. Which half of which reconstructed theory would endorse each half of this package? Where would the package be more defensible than the status quo on a theory's own terms, and where would it break?

Finally: does the fact that juries already work in something like this way — amateurs, chosen by lot, no reasons given, then gone — count as evidence that the sortition half is workable, or does the jury's narrow remit and its total lack of agenda-setting power make it no precedent at all?
