I'm a PhD student in philosophy — I work on agency and moral responsibility — and for the past year I've sat as the graduate member of our university's behavioural research ethics board. Tomorrow we have to decide on a proposal that is, as far as I can tell, the first genuinely interesting free-will study to come in front of us, and I've been asked to draft the memo that will carry the recommendation, so I want to get it right rather than fall back on boilerplate. The study is called "Can You Stop Yourself? Real-Time Detection of Movement Preparation and the Limits of Conscious Veto," and it's run by a cognitive neuroscientist in psychology with a philosopher as co-PI. Participants wear EEG and make self-paced button presses while watching a rotating clock hand, reporting where the hand was when they first felt the urge to move — the standard Libet arrangement, in which the slow negative buildup over motor areas that precedes movement is said to begin a few hundred milliseconds before the reported urge. The new part is the veto manipulation: in some blocks a classifier trained on the participant's own preparatory activity triggers a tone when it detects the buildup, and the participant has been instructed to cancel the movement on a subset of trials when they hear it; critically, some of those tones are yoked shams fired at matched latencies, so the team can ask whether cancellation tracks genuine detection of one's own preparatory activity or is just a reaction to an external cue. There is also a cueless condition in which participants simply try to abort as late as they can, which the PI describes as measuring a "point of no return" for self-cancellation. The PI's framing is that Libet himself stopped short of the determinist conclusion and appealed to precisely this sort of conscious veto, but that the existing attempts to test the veto give subjects an external prompt they would never have had outside the lab, and so don't really settle whether the veto is anything more than a response to a signal. Our neurosurgeon member thinks the underlying finding is settled and the study is methodologically straightforward; another member, a philosopher of action, thinks the design is conceptually confused until the PI says what would count as a successful veto and what would count against one.

The second arm of the study is what I actually expect to be contested, and it's why I'm writing to you. After the EEG session participants are randomized into one of two debriefing scripts. The neutral one describes the study as being about the timing of voluntary movement and attention. The "provocative" one tells participants that their brain activity predicted their choices before they were aware of intending them, and that this is widely taken as evidence that conscious intentions do not cause actions — a claim I take to be at best contested, since the same data are compatible with the buildup reflecting something other than a decision made on the participant's behalf. Participants then complete a third-party punishment task, a trust/reciprocity game, and a moral-responsibility attribution battery, and the team wants to know whether the script shifts behaviour. My worry is twofold: the script asserts as established something many people on our own board would deny, and the literature on whether induced disbelief in free will moves pro-social behaviour in one direction or the other is itself unsettled, with replications going both ways, so we cannot even say what magnitude or direction of risk we are licensing. There are also the ordinary complications: the pool is undergraduates earning course credit, the EEG lab is shared with a clinical sleep group so there is a booking window, and the philosophy co-PI has told us in writing that a balanced debriefing would "deflate the manipulation" and make the paper unpublishable in the venue she has in mind.

What I need from you is the board's decision memo — a real document, not a discussion of the issues. It should come down on one side: reject, approve, or approve with conditions, and if conditions, they should be concrete and checkable with a date for resubmission and a named person responsible for verifying each one. I'd like it to take an explicit position on at least four things: whether the provocative debriefing script may be used at all and in what wording; whether the yoked sham tones are permissible given that participants have consented to "a study of the timing of voluntary movement"; whether we should require the cueless late-abort condition as the only condition that speaks to the philosophical question; and what restoration we owe participants afterwards — I've been toying with requiring a one-week follow-up that re-measures the responsibility attributions and includes a corrective script, but I can't tell whether that is proportionate or merely theatrical. I also want the memo to give the losing side its due rather than bury it: if you recommend approving with conditions, give the strongest version of the case for rejecting outright — roughly, that deliberately inducing a possibly false belief about one's own agency in a captive credit-earning pool is not a harm that a consent form can cure — and say why it fails; if you recommend rejecting or requiring redesign, give the strongest version of the case that this is exactly the kind of study boards exist to permit, that the uptake of neuroscience is a legitimate object of study, and that a board which insists subjects only ever be told what the board takes to be consensus is overreaching. I've drafted conditions myself and keep going back and forth, so I'd rather have your verdict and reasoning in a form I could circulate than a survey of the options.
