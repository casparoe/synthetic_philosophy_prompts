I am a novelist at work on a book about the ways people misjudge their own futures, and I need one illustrative case built to a specification rather than borrowed from the literature.

Let me fix the terms as I am using them. To reason from *the inside view* about a project is to attend to its particulars: the shape of the plan, the obstacles one can presently see, the progress made so far, and the estimate that falls out of putting those together. To reason from *the outside view* is to set the particulars aside and treat the project as a sample drawn from a broader class of projects of its kind, asking what the outcomes in that class have typically been. Epistemic modesty, in my usage, is the position that wherever the two disagree one should generally defer to the outside view, on the ground that one's appreciation of one's own particulars is exactly the place where one is most reliably overconfident.

Please construct a concrete example, drawn from the working life of a novelist, that meets every one of the following conditions simultaneously.

1. The two views yield estimates of some quantity the novelist cares about — whether a manuscript will be finished by a contractual deadline, whether a book will reach an audience, whether a structural problem will be solved by revision — that differ enough to change what she would rationally decide to do next.
2. The outside view's estimate is not unique: it depends on which reference class the project is sorted into, and there are at least two reference classes she could defensibly choose which point toward different estimates. Each class is broad enough to carry some statistical weight and narrow enough to be genuinely informative, so neither is obviously gerrymandered.
3. The novelist also holds case-specific evidence that no reference class can register — something she has learned about this manuscript, her own working history, or her present circumstances that bears directly on the outcome and that she has good independent reason to trust. Discarding it entirely, as a strict deference to base rates would require, looks like a loss rather than a gain in accuracy.
4. The choice of reference class in (2) cannot itself be settled by stepping outside. To ask how projects like this one have usually been classified is only to pose the same question one level up, and that question in turn invites a classification of its own; the vantage point from which the outside view is supposed to be taken recedes as one approaches it. I have been calling this the vanishing vantage point.

Then, in a single closing paragraph, say what you take the upshot to be. Does the vanishing vantage point show that the outside view is idle, that it is serviceable only as a corrective to enthusiasm rather than as an arbiter of what to believe, that epistemic modesty must be applied to the selection of reference classes rather than to the estimates they produce, or something else again? Argue for the conclusion you favor.

Two constraints on the answer. Keep it legible to an intelligent reader with no background in the relevant research: no citations, no studies, no named schools or authorities, and no technical formalism beyond what you define as you go. And keep the example genuinely particular — a novelist with a real decision in front of her, not a schematic stand-in — since it is the concreteness that will carry the chapter.
