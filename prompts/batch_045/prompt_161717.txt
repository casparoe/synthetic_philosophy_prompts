I TA a discrete math course and we did a couple of sessions on induction and self-reference, and since then my roommate (math major) and I have been going back and forth about the surprise exam / unexpected hanging setup. I have pretty firm gut verdicts on a bunch of variants and I can't find one principle that respects all of them, so I want to see what you think the pattern actually is.

Here are my verdicts. (1) One-day version: teacher says after class Friday "there will be an exam tomorrow and you won't know it's tomorrow until tomorrow morning" — no surprise possible, the announcement is just false, nothing interesting here. (2) Classic five-day version, exam actually on Wednesday — genuine surprise, announcement vindicated; the backward-elimination argument the students run is bad, and the prisoner in the hanging version really is surprised even though his reasoning was fine-looking. (3) Same five-day version, exam on Friday — I want to say this is cheating, not a surprise, they knew Thursday night. But this is the pair I keep tripping over: if the elimination argument is broken, as I say in (2), then they can't deduce Friday either, so Friday should be just as good as Wednesday, and I don't believe that. (4) Two-day version (exam Monday or Tuesday, unpredictably) — I feel uneasy in a way I don't with the five-day version, like it's broken or at least degenerate, even though structurally it's the same setup with fewer days; and I also don't think there's some magic threshold number of days, so my unease here should be unjustified, which bugs me. (5) Escape-hatch version: "there'll be one exam next week unless I cancel it, and it'll be on a day you can't predict" — I feel adding the cancellation clause makes it *more* satisfiable, and that even Friday is fine now, since on Thursday night you can't rule out a cancellation. (6) Over-informative version: "the exam is on Friday and you won't be able to deduce that from what I just told you" — unsatisfiable, but in a way that feels different from (1): it's self-undermining rather than merely useless, more like the liar sentence than like a false prediction. (7) Forgotten-announcement version: announced in week 1 for week 6, everyone has forgotten by week 6, exam lands Wednesday — they're startled, but I don't count it; whatever "surprise" means here, the announcement has to still be part of their evidence when the day comes.

The best thing I've come up with myself is something like: a day counts as a surprise iff, at the start of that day, at least two days remain in the announcement's window (so Friday in a five-day week fails, Wednesday and Monday pass, the one-day case fails automatically). But that rule says nothing about whether the students could *deduce* the day, which is what makes me nervous in (4), and it gets (5) wrong by my own lights, since under it Friday still has only one day left in the window. Can you take that rule, test it case by case against my seven verdicts, and either repair it into something precise or replace it with a better one — clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause? Then tell me what the result says about two cases I haven't settled: the teacher announces the five-day version on Sunday and, on Tuesday night with no exam yet, repeats the announcement word for word (does the window effectively restart, and is Wednesday still a surprise?); and a version where the teacher announces a surprise exam for a five-day week and adds "and it's not Monday." Please also be straight about which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate and which one you'd overturn — I'd rather have a definite reading of what "surprise" requires here (psychological? not-deducible-from-your-evidence? not-deducible-from-the-announcement-alone?) with reasons, than a survey of options, and I'd like you to flag the parts where you're confident versus the parts where you're basically making a call.
