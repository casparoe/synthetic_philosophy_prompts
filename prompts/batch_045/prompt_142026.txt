Hey, I need a proposal for a one-semester independent study project (so: one student, one supervisor, about four months, ~6000 words of output at the end, and I have to fit it around two other courses). Please write it in American English, and keep it in the register of something I'd actually hand in — not a journal abstract.

The topic is Jain anekāntavāda, and I have a half-formed idea I keep going in circles on. Turn it into something workable.

Here's where I'm stuck. Everyone summarizes the view as something like "reality is many-sided, so every claim is true only from some standpoint." But I think two separate claims are being run together, and the whole thing hinges on keeping them apart:

- Rel: any affirmative claim about a thing is true only relative to a standpoint (a naya, or whatever you want to call the index).
- Cov: for any two claims that look like they conflict, there is some pair of standpoints from which both come out true.

Rel obviously doesn't give you Cov: you could have claims that clash from every standpoint you like, or standpoints that can't be occupied together. And Cov doesn't seem to need any of the fancy machinery — it just needs standpoints to be plentiful and combinable. What I actually want to know is whether the sevenfold scheme of conditional predication (the syād asti / syād nāsti / both / inexpressible pattern plus the three combinations with inexpressible) is best read as following from something like Cov, or as a separate thesis about assertion — i.e., about how you ought to hedge what you say, rather than about how truth behaves.

The second thing, which I think is really where the paper is, is that fourth mode, the inexpressible one. The readings I keep running into treat it as a third truth value, or as a gap, or as a claim about the limits of language. I've been toying with a fourth option I'm calling the 'silence reading': the mode isn't about the object at all, it's a norm — from certain standpoints the correct move is to decline to answer, so the mode is an instruction rather than a description. If that's right, the reason to bother listing all seven combinations instead of stopping at three is that the list is partly a manual for how to talk, and I want to know whether that reading has to collapse back into the gap reading or whether it makes different predictions somewhere.

To keep it honest, here's the case I want everything tested against: two people stand on the same hillside a year after a wildfire. One says the watershed has recovered; the other says it has not. They have exactly the same data, neither is ignorant of anything the other knows, they agree on every prediction and every policy recommendation, and they'd switch words happily if you paid them. That feels like a merely verbal disagreement, or a clash about which property the word picks out — not a standpoint disagreement in any interesting sense. If many-sidedness can't tell that case apart from a real one, Cov is trivially true and who cares. So the proposal needs a working criterion for when a difference in standpoint is genuine, and that criterion can't just be "the two speakers disagree."
