I'm a third-year in the philosophy department, and this term I'm leading one of the weekly discussion sections attached to our introductory Philosophy of Action course: fifty minutes, fourteen students, almost all of them non-majors fulfilling a distribution requirement (heavily pre-med and engineering), who have done one short classical compatibilist reading and one contemporary incompatibilist one and arrived at section convinced that the compatibilist simply changed the subject. Next week's session is on the varieties of free will worth wanting — that is, on getting them to see that the question whether we have free will is not one question but a cluster of questions about different grades of agency, and that which grade you actually need depends on what you wanted free will for in the first place. I don't need the concept explained to me; I need a plan for getting fourteen skeptics to see the cluster as a cluster. I've been sketching the session around four grades, which I've been labeling uptake (the agent's effective will genuinely tracks her reasons, so that a change in the reasons would have produced a change in what she does), endorsement (the effective will is not one she is alienated from at a higher level, so she isn't being dragged through a conflict she dissociates from), provenance (the way she came to have the will she has is not itself someone else's engineering project, even a benign one), and answerability (she is the sort of thing toward which resentment, gratitude, and demands for justification are apt responses). What I can't settle is the order: does answerability have to come first, as the thing that makes the question matter at all, or last, as the payoff the other three grades were quietly being assembled to support? And which of the four, introduced before its predecessors, will just be heard as one more definition of free will that happens to favor whichever side I like?

The other thing I want your judgment on is the carrying example. Last week at dinner I argued with two friends — an engineering major who insisted that if your choice could in principle be predicted from the state of your brain a second beforehand the question is settled, and a classics major who said the whole debate is a leftover scruple about guilt — and in the course of it I found myself inventing a case that I think might do more work than the familiar ones in which a team of neuroscientists designs an entire psychology in advance. Mine is deliberately quiet: a woman starts using a wellness app to track her sleep, and through ambient feedback and nudges that no one designed for this purpose her preferences shift over roughly eight months. By the end she is fully reasons-responsive, her higher-order attitudes have traveled with the changes, she would defend the new desires wholeheartedly, and there is no single moment of intervention, no intention to produce the outcome, and no one who could have said in advance where it would land. I have deliberately built in the absence of a manipulator and of any discrete intervention, and the presence of full uptake and full endorsement, and I have deliberately left out the dramatic gap between surface reasoning and actual motivation that I take the standard manipulation cases to supply. Is that the right set of features, or does removing the intentional manipulator remove the very intuition the case is supposed to elicit? What must a case like this have, and what must it lack, to pry the four grades apart rather than making everything collapse together?

Then there are the misconceptions, and I'd like them mapped onto the sequence rather than just listed. I'm fairly confident about four that will show up: that determinism is the crux and everything else is a sideshow; that the compatibilist grades amount to lowering the bar; that if a manipulated agent isn't free and a manipulated agent is in the relevant respects just like an ordinary determined agent, then parity finishes the job; and that randomness at the moment of choice is the thing that would rescue us. I also expect the prediction line from my engineering friend and possibly the cultural-inheritance line from my classics friend. For each: at what point in the sequence should I head it off, and what has to be on the board already before the defusal can even be stated? I don't want to answer objections before the students can feel the pull of the distinction doing the work. Finally, give me one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that would show me whether they have actually distinguished the grades rather than memorized four terms, and tell me what I should openly refuse to get into at this level — the hard incompatibilist's positive program, the structure of the standard anti-alternative-possibilities cases, the physics — and how to say so without its sounding like I'm hiding something. Please write the plan in American English.
