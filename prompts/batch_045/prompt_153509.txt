I work on the treatment side of a drug court, and there is a pattern in the programs we send people to that I have never been able to make philosophical sense of.

The pattern has five parts, as I observe it:

A. Every institution involved classifies addiction as a disease. The court sentences to treatment rather than to punishment; the insurer pays for it as a medical condition; the program's own materials present the drug-taking as compulsive.

B. The family is coached in the opposite emotional direction: to stop being angry, to detach lovingly, to treat the lying and the stealing as symptoms of the condition rather than as things the person did to them.

C. The patient is required to do something that looks like the exact reverse of A and B: write out an inventory of the harms he caused, name them to the people he harmed, apologize, and make amends.

D. Remorse is treated as a deliverable. Failure to show it counts as failure of treatment and can bring a sanction from the judge — so the program seems to treat guilt as something that can be owed, and its absence as something further blameworthy.

E. No such demand is made of people with advanced dementia, or of small children, even though both undermine control at least as thoroughly.

Call the core of it the split-stance requirement: the program requires the patient to occupy toward himself, at precisely the moment it asks everyone around him to stop occupying toward him, the stance of someone who is answerable for what he did.

I have been reading in the reactive-attitudes tradition that runs from Strawson, and I want to know what this package of practices actually commits us to. Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of responsibility. Is the package coherent only if the reactive attitudes are in part constitutive of what the harm is — guilt, remorse, resentment, and amends not merely responses to a responsibility the agent antecedently had, but part of what makes up the relationship the harm damaged? Or is there a thinner reading on which the inventory and the amends are reparative technology and the disease classification does all the real work?

2. Separate what is strictly presupposed from what merely fits. Does classifying addiction as a disease strictly commit the program to exempting the addict — to holding that he is not a fit object of resentment? Or can a view that puts the reactive attitudes at the center hold that the disease claim is a reason to withdraw or temper the attitudes of others while continuing to demand the self-directed ones?

3. Item D. If the reactive attitudes are not directly under the will, what is the program actually demanding — an avowal, an acknowledgment, with remorse as a hoped-for by-product? And does the answer change what ought to count as a successful step?

4. Item E. What is the practice implicitly relying on to draw its line: the bare capacity to occupy the answerable stance at all, the repairability of the relationship, or something less principled such as whether the demand will work? Say which, and whether the practice is entitled to the line it draws.

5. Underdetermination. Could a desert- or quality-of-will-based view license the same package? Could a purely forward-looking consequentialist one? Which parts of the practice are diagnostic between these readings, and which would almost any view allow?

6. Flag any pair of parts that presuppose inconsistent things. I would rather be told the practice is incoherent than given a tidy story for it.

7. What would have to change. Suppose we dropped C and D and substituted what I will call repair without guilt — a purely forward-looking schedule of restitution, no finding of fault, no apology, no remorse criterion. What would be lost, on the answerable-stance reading, and what, if anything, gained? And the reverse experiment: if B were reversed and relatives were told to keep their resentment and to demand answers, what would break? Does the withdrawal of the reactive attitudes have a shelf life, or does it corrode in a way the program never accounts for?
