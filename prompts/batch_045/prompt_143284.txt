I got drafted to teach our department's rational choice seminar in the spring (my real area is philosophy of mind, don't ask), and I've been arguing for years with a friend at another university who is about as updateless as a person can be. She's putting together a survey paper with one of those big tables — rows are cases, columns are theories — and she's filled in her own column for most of the rows, but the last two entries are blank, and I want to guess what she'll write before she circulates it. The verdicts I can remember from the filled-in rows: Newcomb's problem with a 99%-accurate predictor and opaque boxes, one-box. Counterfactual mugging, fair coin already tossed, she's told it came up tails and asked to hand over $100 while the million would have been paid on heads: she pays. Prisoner's dilemma against an exact physical replica of herself in another room, no communication, both know the setup: cooperate. Smoking lesion, where the lesion causes both the craving and the cancer and the smoking itself does no harm: she smokes, no hesistation. Absent-minded driver: she gives what I'd call the planning answer — settle the continuation probability before you set off, don't reopen it at the intersection. And the row that actually surprised me: a copy of her own decision procedure running somewhere else with a completely different utility function plugged into it (paperclip-ish agent, she isn't one, no causal contact, one shot, each can burn one unit of their own resource to hand the other five units of what they care about): she burns. She said something like "of course, it's the same procedure," and I didn't press her on it at the time.

The two blank rows are both about what I'll call cousins: systems that are not copies of her, that never read her source and never simulated her, with a different architecture entirely, but which across a long series of comparable decisions have matched her choices about nine times out of ten — and we're stipulating that the correlation is explained entirely by the two of them having been shaped by the same selection pressures and trained on the same distribution, not by anybody modelling anybody. Row one is a Newcomb setup: her box is full iff the cousin, facing its own pair of boxes in another room at the same time and told the analogous thing, one-boxes. Row two is the burn-one-to-give-five trade from the paperclip row, except that her partner is a cousin of that kind rather than a copy of her procedure. My own guesses were two-box on the first and burn on the second, but I've been wrong about her before, and I have a feeling the paperclip row is doing more work in her thinking than she's admitted to herself.

So: what's your best guess for each blank row, and more usefully, what rule do you think she's implicitly running? Give me at least one other rule that fits those six filled-in rows just as well, say what each rule predicts for the two blank rows, and tell me which of the two rows (if either) actually separates them — and if neither does, tell me what case you'd add to her table to force the issue. If you think one of her earlier verdicts is the unstable one rather than anything in the new rows, say which and why.
