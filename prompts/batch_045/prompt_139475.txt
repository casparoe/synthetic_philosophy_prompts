Ive been going back and forth with a friend who does medical statistics about whether there's one correct probability for a one-off event, or whether the reference class you quietly pick is doing most of the work. She says there's always a right one: take the narrowest class you still have decent numbers for, and that's the number. I want a case to bring to our next argument.

Build me one ordinary, one-off decision — a doctor, a parole board, a small landlord, whatever — with no lotteries, no dice, no imaginary repeated trials, no sci fi. Two reference classes should both feel like the natural one to use, and they should give numbers far enough apart that they recommend different decisions. Three constraints matter to me:

1. Neither class contains the other; they cross-cut, so her narrowest-class rule has nothing to grab. And taking the intersection isn't allowed to rescue things — it should either be too tiny to give a stable frequency (like four or five people) or it should swing around depending on which extra detail you add next.
2. Nobody in the case should be being sloppy or spinning the numbers. Both classes come from genuine statistics a careful person would defend, and both are ones you'd pick before seeing which answer you liked.
3. It shouldn't feel like the disagreement is just ignorance that better data would dissolve. I want it to feel like even someone with every recorded fact about the individual would still have to choose.

Then state the two classes and their numbers, show the decision each one pushes toward, and be straight with me about which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the case if you delete that feature. Last thing: tell me what my friend will say to wriggle out of it, because she will.
