I'm a software engineer — about a decade in ML infrastructure, mostly on the platform side — with no formal philosophy training, and I'm applying for a small six-month, one-day-a-week research grant that my employer funds for projects sitting somewhere between engineering practice and something more reflective. I need a two-page proposal for it, in the area of machine ethics: specifically, what it would mean to implement ethical reasoning in a system rather than merely filter or constrain its outputs.

The half-formed idea I want turned into something fundable came out of an incident at work. A system we ship produced a fluent, well-structured justification for refusing a user request, and when we dug in, the stated rationale was not what had actually driven the behaviour — it was a post hoc reconstruction, close enough to pass review and wrong about its own causes. My team split into two camps, and I think both are wrong-footed: one says the fix is to make the normative rules explicit so they can be audited; the other says we should stop hand-authoring rules and learn the behaviour from human feedback. It seems to me both sides assume that the deliverable of machine ethics is a function from situations to actions, and that the only argument is about how to build that function. But in every safety-critical system I've worked on, the load-bearing component is not the decision procedure; it's the escalation path — the machinery that notices it is out of its depth and hands off to a human. So the question I want to pursue is whether a machine's competence at knowing when it does not know can count as a form of moral knowledge, or whether it is only well-calibrated ignorance wearing a moral label. And a second, related question: when we demand that a moral machine give reasons, do we require those reasons to be the actual causal producers of its action, or is it enough that they be a reliable public certificate that some independent checker could in principle verify? My hunch is that those two demands come apart, that the debate quietly runs them together, and that which one you should want depends on whether your real concern is safety or blame — a distinction that maps, roughly, onto whether you are trying to prevent bad outcomes or to decide who is answerable for them.

What I need in the proposal: the question stated sharply enough that it has a determinate answer within six months at one day a week; why the standard framing — hand-coded rules versus learned behaviour, and the stock hypothetical test cases used to compare them — leaves that question open; the method, month by month, given that I can write code and run small experiments but cannot do anything involving human subjects; the two or three possible conclusions and what would actually decide between them; a concrete deliverable that is not merely a paper (I was imagining some sort of evaluation harness, but tell me if that is the wrong artifact); and an honest closing section on what would make the project fail, including the possibility that the whole idea collapses into something already settled under another name. Please include no bibliography and no citations — the committee explicitly wants the plan rather than the reading, and I will add references myself. Assume the reviewers are half philosophers and half engineers, each knowing nothing of the other's field, so avoid both sides' shorthand. Begin the response with the proposal itself: no preface, no note about what you are about to write. The first sentence should state the question and the angle on it in one breath, and everything after that should be the proposal.
