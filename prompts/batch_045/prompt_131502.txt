I got pulled into an argument in a comment thread about the block universe and I can no longer tell whether I have been handed a proof or a pun.

The setup is the familiar Rietdijk–Putnam one. You and I walk past each other on a sidewalk, moving in opposite directions. Because simultaneity is frame-relative, the hypersurface each of us calls "now" cuts an event a couple of million light-years away — say the launch of an invasion fleet in Andromeda — into different temporal positions, days apart. If my "now" already contains the launch, then the launch cannot be unreal for you merely because your slice of spacetime places it in your future; reality, we are told, is not a matter of which coordinates you happen to be using.

The person I was arguing with (call him H.) was not content with that modest conclusion. He offered what he called the zigzag or ladder version, and I have reconstructed it as carefully and as charitably as I can:

(1) What is real cannot depend on a choice of coordinates. If an event is real for one inertial observer, no other inertial observer may correctly describe it as unreal.

(2) For any two spacelike-separated events, there is an inertial frame in which they are simultaneous.

(3) If two events are simultaneous in some frame, they are co-real: they are both there, together, in one and the same reality.

(4) Co-reality is transitive. If A is co-real with B and B is co-real with C, then A is co-real with C.

(5) For any two events anywhere in Minkowski spacetime — not merely spacelike-separated ones — you can get from the first to the second by a finite chain of spacelike hops, each hop lying outside the light cone of the one before it. (This is the zigzag. Take two events a second apart on my worldline; an event a hundred light-years off to the side is spacelike separated from both of them. I checked the construction and it seems to hold generally, for timelike and lightlike pairs as well.)

(6) So every event is co-real with every other event. All of them are there, together.

(7) But (6), read H.'s way, is not the block-universe claim that every event exists tenselessly. It is stronger: it says that all events share a single present — that Caesar at the Rubicon, the launch of the fleet, and my typing this sentence are all going on at once, in one all-inclusive now.

(8) That I cannot accept, and I do not think H. can either. If all events are co-present, then there is no invariant distinction between timelike, lightlike, and spacelike separation, no light-cone structure, and therefore no special relativity — which was a premise of the argument. It also makes nonsense of my typing this before reading your reply. So (1) through (6) cannot all be true as H. reads them.

Something has to go, and I cannot see what. My own ranking of what to keep: I would most like to keep (1) and (5). I am willing to give up the claim that my own present is metaphysically privileged, since I already half accept eternalism. What I do not want to give up is special relativity, and I do not want to give up that there is an objective difference between my writing this and your reading it.

So, three things, in order of how much I want them:

First: which step do you reject? Name it, and say precisely why it looked innocent — what made it plausible enough to pass unremarked inside the original two-observers-on-a-sidewalk argument, where it really does seem to do work.

Second: price the alternatives. For each other step that someone might reject instead, what does rejecting it cost? In particular, what happens if (3) is kept and (4) is dropped, versus (3) dropped and (4) kept — does either combination still get you to eternalism, and does either collapse back into the absurdity in (8)? And is there a reading of "real", "exists", or "is present" on which (1) through (6) all come out true but (7) does not follow, and if so is that reading still doing the work a block-universe partisan needs it to do?

Third: H. has a fallback I could not answer. He says the all-inclusive now in (7) is not a physical frame at all but a metaphysical one, and that "present" in (7) is being used tenselessly, so there is no conflict with special relativity and no threat to the difference between my writing and your reading. Is that a stable position or a retreat, and what does it cost him? I would rather be told that the fallback works than be handed a cheap win.

Please end your reply with a single final line in exactly this form: a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by the number of the step you reject and a clause of no more than ten words naming the flaw — for example, "Verdict: step (4) — quantifier shift from pairwise linkage to one all-inclusive present."
