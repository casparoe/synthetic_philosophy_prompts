Here is my material. I write literary fiction, and the novel I am finishing has a two-timeline structure. In both timelines the same man, at the same hour, on the same road, in the same state of mild intoxication, makes the same decision to drive home rather than call a cab. In one timeline a child steps out from between two parked cars and dies; in the other the road is empty and he wakes at home with a hangover. I have tried to make the timelines agree in everything that is up to him — his reasons, his evidence, his degree of impairment, his willingness to run the risk, even the few seconds in which he hesitates — so that what varies is only what the world happened to put in front of him. Readers and my writing group respond as you would predict: in one timeline they call him reckless, wicked, unforgivable; in the other, careless, lucky, recognizably human. I did not expect to be troubled by this, since it is only a matter of craft, but I find that I am.

A philosopher in my group said that my book is a long illustration of what the literature calls resultant moral luck, and that the way to see it clearly is by analogy to gambling, with the law's distinction between attempt and completion as a second version of the same thought. His version runs roughly like this: a person's choice is a wager placed under uncertainty; the outcome is the fall of the cards; blame is the settling of accounts afterwards. On that picture, he says, two people who place the same bet have done the same thing, and it is irrational to treat the one whose bet lost as the worse person; the law half-knows this, which is why it punishes attempts less than completed crimes, but it is still in the grip of the confusion. He draws a large conclusion from it: blame is not a verdict on the person at all but a settlement mechanism, a forward-looking price attached to risk-creation, and so we should stop pretending that our blame practices track desert and reorganize them instead — replacing backward-looking blame with something like ex ante auditing of decisions under uncertainty, or strict liability for the risks one imposes.

Three things make me doubt that the analogy is bearing this weight.

First, a worry about my own device rather than about his argument. I have built the two timelines so that everything up to him is identical and only the outcome differs. But it occurs to me that if the outcomes differ, then something upstream must have differed too, and perhaps the stipulation that the agent and his deliberations are the same in both timelines is less coherent than it looks — perhaps I am relying on a branching-worlds picture that the moral case does not really allow. I would like to know whether the analogy depends on that stipulation, and whether the stipulation can be made good.

Second, something I have started calling salience luck, since I have not found it named in the literature I have dipped into. I find that I can move a reader's blame a very long way without changing the outcome at all, merely by changing what I show: a chapter from his childhood, a chapter written from the dead child's point of view, a scene of him buying the drinks, a scene of the empty road from the timeline where nothing happens. Holding the act and the result fixed, I can make the same reader condemn him or forgive him, and I cannot see that this is the same phenomenon as the resultant luck the analogy is built to explain. Does the gambling analogy have any slot for this? Is it genuinely a further kind of luck, or is it reducible to one of the standard four — resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, causal — or is it not luck at all but something about the blamer?

Third, and what I most want your view on: it seems to me the analogy might pull against my friend rather than for him. A gambler who knowingly accepts variance is normally held to the actual result; he does not get to keep the winnings and disown the losses, and the whole point of a wager is that you have agreed in advance to be judged by how it came out. Read that way, the analogy licenses settling up at the outcome rather than abolishing outcome-sensitivity, and it is the attempt/completion half of the analogy that pulls in the other direction. So which way does the source domain actually push, and is my friend using one half of the analogy and discarding the other?
