Hi — I'm chief of staff (and by default the office ethics person, because nobody else will take it) in a mid-size city mayor's office, and I need something I can actually run, not something to think about.

The situation: two or three times a week something hits my desk where the options are (i) do something the mayor and I both think is morally wrong, or (ii) let something bad happen that the city exists to prevent. Sometimes the wrong thing is legally shaky, sometimes it's completely legal and that's the problem. Legal review is a separate step and we have corp counsel for it, so please don't spend the procedure on "is it lawful" — I need whatever comes after the lawyers say yes or shrug.

I've read a bit of the philosophy on this (dirty hands) and it's maddening in practice: lots about tragedy, moral residue, guilt, and whether the politician who does these things is the one we should want in office — and almost nothing about how to tell whether you're actually in a dirty-hands case or just telling yourself you are so you can sleep at night. I think that distinction is doing all the work, so build the procedure around it. Here are the things I keep wanting to test and can't quite operationalise:

- Is the wrong load-bearing? Does the action still produce the good outcome if you take the wrong-making feature out of it, maybe at some cost in speed or money? If the wrong bit is just the cheapest or fastest route to a result you could get otherwise, then as far as I'm concerned it isn't dirty hands, it's a bad trade with nicer packaging. (I invented "load-bearing wrong" as a label — use it if it helps, swap it for something better if you have one.)
- Does the wrong survive delegation? Half the time the act happens three layers down and nobody quite ordered it, or the mayor "doesn't want to know the details." My instinct is that you can't launder it by pushing it down the chain, and that the dirt actually attaches to whoever set up the situation where someone lower down had to do it. I want that as a step, not a vibe.
- Are we really just in lesser-evil territory? Plenty of what comes through here isn't "wrong but necessary," it's "imposes real costs on people and nobody's rights are violated." I want the procedure to say so quickly and let us move on, instead of us agonising over everything.
- The acknowledgement condition. My sense from what I've read is that any permission here is tied to the agent being willing to own the act in public afterwards and take whatever comes — being judged, being punished, resigning, carrying the guilt rather than treating it as washed away. But most of the options that reach my desk only work if nobody finds out. That seems to mean most of them can't qualify at all, and I want the procedure to say that in so many words rather than leaving it to my judgement every single time.
- Frequency. This is roughly the fourth emergency procurement / back-channel deal / shortcut this year and I've noticed we've stopped noticing. Is there a version of this test that catches habituation — the slide into "well, we did it last time"? If it can be proceduralised, put it in. If it genuinely can't, say that instead of faking a step.

What I want back:

1. A decision procedure I can run in about 45 minutes on a real proposal: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what counts as a non-answer (i.e. the kind of thing my office writes that sounds like an answer and isn't), hard stops where the answer is no, and explicit hand-off points where the question is a values call the mayor has to make rather than me. Mark clearly which steps are real tests and which are judgement calls dressed up as procedure — I'd rather know than be reassured.
2. Who signs off at each stage, given that I have about an hour, the mayor has ten minutes, and anything touching council has to survive a hostile oversight hearing six months later where someone reads our emails out loud.
3. Run it on three cases, showing the actual path through the steps:
 (a) A fast-moving wildfire. The fire chief wants standing authority to demolish two unoccupied houses without the notice our ordinance requires, to cut a firebreak that will probably save a block. Legally dubious, time pressure is real.
 (b) A housing deal. In exchange for a much larger affordable-housing set-aside than we could get any other way, we steer a seperate no-bid garage contract to the same developer and let the public believe the garage went to them on merit. Nobody's rights are violated exactly, but a process we're supposed to be guarding is being rigged on purpose, and elected colleagues are being lied to by omission.
 (c) An undercover policing operation that requires the officer to participate in a crime to hold cover, which will genuinely prevent serious harm to identifiable people, and which cannot work unless the city can deny any knowledge of it afterwards. This is the one where I think the procedure is going to hand me an answer I don't like. I'd much rather see it come out that way in writing than have you smooth the edges because the stakes in the case feel high.
4. A short, honest closing section on where this thing will misfire: what it will wrongly clear, what it will wrongly block, and the kinds of case where it's basically a rubber stamp for whatever we already wanted to do.

Two more constraints. Don't give me a reading list, citations, or a summary of who argued what — I can't chase references and it wouldn't help me decide by 3pm. And design it for someone who is tired, recieves most of these proposals from people more senior than them, wants to keep their job, and will therefore rationalise hard: if there's a way to build an anti-rationalisation check into the ordering of the questions (or into who has to answer them), do that.
