I'm drafting the moral-psychology section of a report for my professional association, which is rewriting its disciplinary procedure from scratch, and I want a consistency check on the position before I defend it in front of colleagues who will be looking for the weak joint. Here is the package, as eight commitments.

(a) The guilt/shame distinction is a distinction of object: guilt takes acts and omissions as its object, shame takes the self. Because of this, guilt is proportionate and evidence-responsive, whereas shame is epistemically unruly — it spreads from what was done to who did it, resists excusing conditions, and fires independently of whether the act was in fact wrong.

(b) Guilt should carry the great majority of the weight in an institution like ours, and the balance of our practices should be shifted toward guilt responses and away from shame responses.

(c) But guilt is dischargeable: confession, repair and accepted penalty close the ledger. A guilt-saturated culture therefore drifts toward a bookkeeping morality in which every wrong is settleable. Something is lost there — some wrongs should leave a residue that survives full repair, and marking that residue is the one job shame does that guilt cannot do. So a guilt-only culture is not an option.

(d) Shame's pathologies — concealment, withdrawal, counter-attack — are contingent, not intrinsic: they are effects of shame being administered as an identity verdict with no route back. Reform the administration and you keep the force without the damage. Make the censure public, specific to what was done, bounded in time by an expiry rule (after a fixed period the matter may not be raised against the person in any of our forums), and attached to a guaranteed route to reinstatement. Call this bounded censure; I mean it as a reformed version of shame, not a substitute for it.

(e) A culture can in fact choose its emotional economy, because the guilt/shame balance is held in place by practices rather than by fixed affect. The lever is the remedy script the institution makes salient: offer discharge-through-repair and you invite guilt, offer exposure-and-exclusion and you invite shame. So design should work on remedy scripts and should not try to train feelings directly.

(f) Guilt is not fear of a punisher. It presupposes that the agent endorses the standard she is accused of violating; someone who feels the pang while rejecting the standard is not feeling guilt at all.

(g) A case verdict I want to keep: members whose professional standing was built on a system that excluded others — who neither chose that exclusion nor could have prevented it — should be shamed rather than left to feel guilt, and it is decent for the association to press this on them publicly.

(h) The empirical literature that makes shame look bad — shame-proneness correlated with concealment, aggression and failure to repair — mostly operationalizes shame as global self-attack and guilt as regret about a specific behavior, so it cannot adjudicate between guilt and shame as (a) draws the distinction.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Go through the set and separate the pairs or triples that are genuinely in tension from the ones that only look as if they are, then rank the genuine ones by severity. For each: which commitments are involved, exactly what one of them commits me to that another forbids, which one would have to give, and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. Please say explicitly where a tension is merely apparent and why it dissolves, and tell me if any commitment in the list is doing no real work. I would rather have three tensions honestly ranked than eight flagged as equally bad.

Then: if I can make only one revision, which single revision — dropping, weakening, or refining one commitment — cleans up the most, and what would it leave me unable to say? Argue from the claims as I have stated them; I have no library access this week, so please do not cite, recommend readings, or appeal to what some author argued. A table whose rows are ordered by severity is the most useful form for the first part, followed by a short paragraph on the cheapest single revision. End with one machine-readable line: \answer{X}, where X is the label of the single commitment you think I should revise or drop, using none only if you judge the set to be consistent as it stands.
