I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I'm assembling the session of our graduate seminar on spacetime theories in which I have to present the hole argument and then, as everyone does, gesture at Leibniz. My difficulty is that the gesture normally comes in two incompatible forms: the argument is announced as a vindication of relationism over substantivalism, and a quarter of an hour later it is announced as having little to do with that dispute at all. I want to put the question to Leibniz directly, and I want a committed answer I can hand to students rather than a menu of options.

Vocabulary first, so that we are describing the same things. By substantivalism about spacetime I mean that the points of the manifold are individuals existing independently of the material and metric fields. By haecceitism I mean that which point is which is a brute, non-qualitative matter, so that two assignments of field-values to points can differ even when they are qualitatively indistinguishable. The argument: take a model of general relativity with a global time slice, choose a bounded region lying to the future of it, and apply a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside that region and non-trivial inside it; dragging the fields along produces a second model that is also a solution of the field equations and that agrees with the first everywhere up to the time slice. So if the points are individuals in the haecceitist sense, the theory together with the complete state of the universe up to that slice fails to determine what occurs in the region. The morals on offer are: relationism; a sophisticated substantivalism on which points exist but diffeomorphism-related models are two descriptions of a single world; or the abandonment of determinism as a constraint on an acceptable theory.

Three things I want. First: which of these would Leibniz take, and which of his principles forces the choice? My own suspicion, which I would like you either to break or to put on firmer footing, is that his case against absolute space in the correspondence with Clarke is best read as a case against haecceitism rather than against the existence of spatial entities as such — that the argument about a universe displaced by some distance being indiscernible from the original is an argument about the non-primitivity of location, not about whether location is a thing. If that is right, then Leibniz is in contemporary terms closer to the sophisticated substantivalist than to the relationist, and relationists have been enlisting him on a misreading. But I cannot reconcile that with his insistence that space is not an entity at all, only an order among coexistents; so tell me whether the tension is genuine and where you think he would resolve it.

Second: given his principle of sufficient reason, would he treat the failure of determinism as a reductio in its own right, and would that give him a route to the anti-haecceitist conclusion that does not rest on verificationist scruples about indistinguishable possibilities? I would rather the answer not lean on the thought that we could not tell the two models apart. I want to know whether, for him, the defect is that there would be no reason for one assignment rather than the other.

Third, and this is the part I have been arguing over with my supervisor: consider an escape I will call occupancy substantivalism. Points exist as individuals and are not mere relations among material things, but a point exists only if something happens there. Since there are no unoccupied points, there is no region within which the fields can be freely reassigned, and the argument never gets off the ground. Would Leibniz accept this as a way of keeping a substantivalist ontology while escaping his own displacement argument? And does the view survive contact with general relativity, where a vacuum region still carries metric structure and gravitational waves propagate through it — that is, does occupation collapse into plain substantivalism if possessing metric properties counts as being occupied, and into the sophisticated variant if it does not? Say which collapse you think is more damaging and why.

Commit to one reading: the moral Leibniz would endorse, the principle from which he would derive it, and whether the hole argument would have struck him as genuinely new rather than as his own displacement argument made local, dynamical, and recast as a problem about determinism within a theory. Tell me plainly if you think his texts underdetermine any part of this, and name the single premise your answer leans on hardest — but give me the position you would actually defend in the seminar, argued rather than surveyed.
