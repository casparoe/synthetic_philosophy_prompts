I'm in a final-year seminar on population ethics, and my essay is due at the end of next week. My supervisor's written feedback on the draft was that the second section is undermotivated: she thinks I move from the four-cell asymmetry to my own principle too quickly, and that if I don't fix it the last third of the paper has nothing to stand on. A friend in the seminar went further and said that once my view is stated properly it just turns into the deprivationist account of the harm of death and there is nothing new in it at all. Before I decide whether to rewrite the middle section or cut it entirely, I would like a second opinion on the substance. I am not asking for references or reading suggestions; I can chase those up in the library catalogue myself. What I need is someone to argue with.

The argument as it currently stands runs like this. First, the four-cell asymmetry as it was presented in the seminar: a person exists and experiences pleasure, which is good; a person exists and experiences pain, which is bad; the person never exists and so there is no pain, which is good even though there is nobody for whom it is good; the person never exists and so there is no pleasure, which is not bad unless somebody is thereby deprived. The two comparative morals we were asked to draw are that creating someone who will suffer is worse than not creating them, while creating someone who will be happy is not better than not creating them. Second, my proposed explanation, which I call the Fixed-Bearer Requirement: an axiological comparison between two possible outcomes can be non-indifferent only if some individual exists in both outcomes as the bearer of whatever is being compared, with one exception class of bearer-independent bads, of which pain is the paradigm. On this reading the asymmetry is not a primitive fact about pleasure and pain but a consequence of which comparisons have a subject: the never-existing outcome contains no bearer, so it cannot be worse for lacking pleasure, but it can be better for lacking pain, because pain is bad whether or not anyone is there to have it. Third, the cost: the requirement seems to travel beyond procreation. If the absence of future pleasure is not bad for someone who does not exist at the relevant time, then being killed is not bad for the victim apart from the process of dying, which is the Epicurean conclusion most of us reject; likewise, the extinction of all sentient life would not be bad, and a universe containing one happy person would be no better than an empty one. Fourth, my fix, which I call the Life-Anchor Principle: an individual can anchor axiological comparisons across times only if they have a life, that is, only if they exist at some time; merely possible individuals have no life, so the outcome in which they never exist is not worse for them and carries no impersonal deficit either. Death is therefore disanalogous to non-conception, because the person who dies has a life from which the remaining goods are subtracted, whereas the never-conceived person has nothing to subtract from. Fifth, the payoff I want to claim: this blocks the familiar move from the asymmetry to the conclusion that coming into existence is always a net harm, since once someone exists their pleasures and pains carry their ordinary symmetric权重 — sorry, their ordinary symmetric weight — and their life can be good on balance; the asymmetry then says only that their never existing was not worse. The result is that there is no reason to create happy people, without the further result that every actual life is bad. Sixth, the objections I can already see: the Life-Anchor looks reverse-engineered from the conclusion I want; it makes the badness of my death depend on whether I actually ever exist, so that in the nearest world in which I was never conceived there is no comparable loss, which smells like a form of actualist moral luck; and if the asymmetry is really a thesis about reasons for and against procreating rather than about value, my whole derivation may be aimed at the wrong target.
