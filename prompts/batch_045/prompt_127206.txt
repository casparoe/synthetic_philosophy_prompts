After thirty-odd years in experimental physics — most of it spent on instrumentation, and a surprising amount of it on the question of how you are entitled to trust a device whose readings you can only check against other devices — I retired and, rather late in the day, started sitting in on philosophy seminars at my old university. I have now been asked to set one of the questions for the final examination of a small upper-level course on scepticism and common sense, the students being mostly final-year undergraduates with a scattering of graduate auditors and a good deal of science and mathematics between them. I have been assigned Moore's essay on the proof of an external world, and I would be grateful for help constructing the question rather than for help understanding it, though I am aware the two come apart. What I want is a single ninety-minute essay question that cannot be answered by producing the famous business with the two hands, declaring that Moore must be begging the question, and stopping; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words; and a marking scheme out of twenty marks built around five or six must-hit points, with the marks for each and, for each point, one line on the way candidates characteristically get it wrong. It would also help me to have a short closing note on what distinguishes a first-class answer from an upper-second one: my impression is that the upper-second answer has all the vocabulary and no crux, and I want the scheme to penalise that specifically rather than merely rewarding the crux when it happens to appear. I should add two constraints. The question must be answerable from Moore's essay itself, from the Cartesian dreaming or demon arguments the class has already read, and from whatever case is built into the question — I do not want an answer to depend on locating the specialist secondary literature, and nothing in the scheme should require a citation. And I would like the question to force a distinction I think my students will otherwise slide past: between the premises being true, Moore's knowing them, Moore's being able to prove them, and the argument's being the sort of thing that could give him a standing he did not previously have. Moore's own remarks about what a good proof requires — premises that differ from the conclusion, premises that are known, a conclusion that follows — are the obvious place to hang this, and I would like the scheme to reward noticing that the interesting pressure falls on the second of those three rather than the third.

The reason I am writing rather than doing it myself is that I have a case I want built into the question, drawn partly from an analogy I cannot shake from my old field, and I do not trust myself to know whether the analogy illuminates or misleads. The analogy is this: in the laboratory we trusted our instruments before we had any verification of them that did not itself presuppose other instruments, and the calibration chain famously terminates in standards that are compared with one another rather than with something outside the practice; nobody in my building ever concluded from this that measurement was impossible, and the move always struck me as a kind of bootstrapping that is nonetheless not vicious. Moore seems to me to be doing something structurally similar when he insists that he knows some things better than he knows the premises of any argument to the contrary, and I want students to say whether that is the right description of his procedure, a flattering one, or a category mistake — because it may be that a claim about what one is entitled to assume is a different animal from a claim about what one knows, and if so the analogy limps. The case, which I would be happy for you to improve on or replace, is this. Suppose Anna has been inside a computer simulation for her entire existence, with no memory of an entry and no discontinuity in her experience; the world she inhabits contains a body, hands, tables, other people, and a physics of its own; and the simulation is run on a machine which is itself a material object in the wider world. Two features matter to me: her hands in the simulated world are made of simulated matter, so that Moore's conclusion that there are external objects, understood as objects external to a mind and in space, comes out true even on the sceptical hypothesis, from which I infer that the proof cannot be aimed at ruling that hypothesis out — the target must be the claim that nobody can know. And I would like a second variant in which Anna's experiences are fed to her directly, with no hand-shaped objects anywhere in the simulated world and her evidence subjectively identical to the first case, so that students have to say whether the truth of the premise is doing any work in Moore's argument or whether it is the epistemic status that is doing all of it. If you think a better pair of cases would make the same distinction bite harder, by all means substitute them, but please keep the distinction itself at the centre, since for me that is the whole point of the exercise.
