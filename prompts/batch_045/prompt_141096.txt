For eleven years I have written the narrative that goes at the front of a serious-incident report in a large hospital trust: the account of how this happened, starting from the earliest deviation and running forward, link by link, until it reaches the harm. The form teaches you to write as though the earliest link is where the causal weight sits. Last March my mother died on one of our own wards of a hospital-acquired infection, and the chain in her report begins with a single missed hand-hygiene moment by an agency nurse nine hours into a double shift. The report then says, two paragraphs later, that given her condition the outcome was highly likely regardless of that moment. I have not stopped thinking about the distance between those two claims, and I have started to doubt that the form I have spent my career filling in can express the truth about either one.

I am putting together a short collection of stories for people who do this kind of work — investigators, auditors, safety reviewers, insurance assessors — and I want the opening piece to dramatise, without ever naming them, two things that get conflated in every report I have ever read. Call them trajectory-sensitivity and basin-sensitivity. A small event can be trajectory-sensitive: perturb it and the route to the end state is unrecognisable — different people bear the cost, different things get destroyed, the timing and the texture of everything changes — while the end state itself was always going to arrive, because the system was heading there anyway and only the path was up for grabs. Or a small event can be basin-sensitive: perturb it and the system tips into a different outcome altogether. When a report says that if that link had held the patient would be alive, it is quietly running a minimal-rewrite counterfactual, one fact changed and everything downstream held frozen, which is precisely the kind of counterfactual a system with sensitive dependence refuses to honour. And the question has a second-order twist I cannot get out of my head: the report I write is itself one more small event inside the same system, and settling the question changes what happens next.

Please write the story. Before drafting, think it through properly in your own working: sketch two or three candidate settings, decide for each which of the two sensitivities it actually exhibits and which counterfactual your reader will be tempted to run, and pick the one where the reader is pulled hardest toward both verdicts at once and cannot settle between them. Then give me nothing but the finished piece — no preamble, no note on your process, no afterword.

Constraints on the piece:

1. Exactly five numbered scenes, each with a short slug title, each between 120 and 180 words. Nothing before scene one and nothing after scene five.
2. Set it in a concrete, mundane, contemporary world of work or infrastructure — a district heating network, a ferry timetable, a payroll migration, a seed-bank freezer, a school admissions database. No kingdoms, no horseshoe nails, no butterflies and tornadoes, no battlefields, no philosopher in a study.
3. The triggering event must be genuinely trivial and morally blank: a transposed digit, a swapped crate, a phone call nobody picked up. Not a sin, not a crime, not malice.
4. Nobody in the story explains anything. No character names the concepts, and no character says the words chaos, attractor, contingency, counterfactual, causal chain, or sensitivity. The distinction has to be carried entirely by what happens.
5. One scene must dwell on a real, irreversible cost — a person harmed, a thing lost — that exists only because of the particular route taken, even though the destination was, in the relevant sense, going to be reached either way. The reader must feel that this cost matters while also seeing that it changed nothing about where things ended up.
6. The final scene must leave the verdict genuinely open, must make visible that the investigation being written is itself another small event feeding back into the same system, and must end on a line that someone in my job would have to sign their name to.

Write it as someone who is both a professional philosopher of causation and historical method and a working short-fiction writer, and note that the piece will be read aloud, without introduction, to a room of about forty incident reviewers who are tired and slightly defensive. Aim for something that survives being heard rather than studied.
