I've been in a small reading group working through the mid-century literature on whether dreaming is something that happens to you while you sleep — Malcolm's Wittgensteinian line, on which the concept of a dream is fixed by the waking report and there is no inner episode for the report to be about, and Dennett's later "cassette" picture, on which the narrative is loaded into memory at the moment of waking rather than played to an inner audience overnight. One member of the group has compressed all of this into an argument that I find myself unable to fault line by line, whose first conclusion I am half attracted to, but which plainly keeps going past the point where I can follow it. I don't want to just wave my hands and say it proves too much; I want to know which step carries the weight and what I owe if I give that step up.

Here it is as he gave it, with my own numbering. (1) The camera picture is a dead end: a film is only an experience if someone watches it, and an inner watcher is a homunculus, so a dream cannot be an inner film playing to someone while I lie there asleep. (2) Everything anyone has to go on, including me in the first person, is the waking report and the impressions it draws on — what I can say, gesture, draw, or recognize when asked on waking. There is no other route to the *content* of the purported episode; the electrophysiology records that something was going on, but not what it was like. (3) If the only thing that settles what I dreamed is a later state, then there is nothing to the episode beyond what that later state supplies: a supposed further fact that could never, even in principle, make a difference to anything I or anyone else could later think, say, recognize, or do is not a fact at all. (4) So the temporal order, duration, and sensory specificity of a dream are properties of the process that produces the report at waking, not of anything spread out across the night; the dream is not a memory of an experience but an improvisation that gets filed as one. (5) Nothing in (1)–(4) mentions sleep. They are claims about any putative episode whose only access is a later state: an episode is the experience it is only if it is subsequently available as that experience. (6) Hence: (a) the patient who follows commands during surgery and then has total retrograde amnesia for the interval had no experience during it; (b) the driver who steers twenty miles of familiar road and later recalls none of it neither saw nor felt anything while doing so; (c) the patient whose seizure is followed by hippocampal damage that makes the ictal period permanently unretrievable felt nothing during the seizure; (d) pre-linguistic infants and non-human animals, who produce no first-person avowal and no retrospective report, have no experiences at all. (7) (6) is not something I can accept — (a), (c), and (d) especially — so at least one of (1)–(5) has to go.

I'd like to keep (2) and (4): I am fairly convinced that dream reports are partly confabulated, that their apparent order is not to be trusted, and that we should resist the picture of a film reel unspooling in the dark. I'd also like to keep the verdicts in (7). Which step should I give up, and why did it look innocuous enough to pass me by? Please give me more than one option: for each candidate rejection, say what it commits me to elsewhere — in particular whether it forces me to admit a kind of backwards dependence, where later memory loss retroactively erases an experience, or instead forces me to say what the "contemporaneous traces" in (a)–(c) do that the dreamer's sleep-time activity doesn't. And please tell me what your diagnosis predicts about two cases I keep changing my mind on. First: a lucid dreamer who, during polysomnographically verified REM, executes a pre-arranged sequence of eye movements and then on waking reports both the signal and the dream scene it was embedded in — is that contemporaneous difference-making, later access, or neither, and does it tell against (3) or only against (4)? Second: suppose a future device decoded sleep-time activity into a running diary whose entries matched the waking report only loosely and were sometimes flatly contradicted by it — would that device change any verdict in the argument, or does it just reveal that the disagreement was never about reportability in the first place? Please answer in American English.
