**Liberty Upsets Patterns — But Whose Surplus Is It?**

*1. What the argument is, and what I think it actually proves.* Nozick is attacking what he calls patterned or end-state principles of justice: the view that a distribution is just only if it matches some pattern the theorist likes — equality, distribution by need, by moral merit, by total happiness. His own alternative is the entitlement theory, which is historical rather than patterned. It has three parts: justice in acquisition (how unowned things first come to be held, limited by something like a Lockean proviso requiring that others not be made worse off), justice in transfer (whatever arises from a just situation through voluntary exchange or gift is itself just), and rectification (repairing past violations). Whether a distribution is just depends entirely on how it came about, not on what shape it has.

The Chamberlain example is supposed to make this vivid. Start from a distribution D1 that the patterned theorist regards as perfectly just — say, strict equality. Chamberlain signs a deal in which he keeps an extra quarter from every home-game ticket. Over a season, a million people voluntarily pay that quarter, each of them happy to, in order to watch him play. The result is D2, in which Chamberlain holds a very large sum that nobody else holds. Nobody was forced, nobody was defrauded, D1 was just by hypothesis, and every step was a voluntary transfer. So by the transfer principle, D2 is just. But D2 wrecks the pattern. The only way to preserve the pattern is to forbid people from doing things with money they are entitled to hold, with people who are entitled to accept it. So freedom disrupts any pattern you care to name: you can have the pattern, or you can have liberty, but not both.

My claim is that this is a dilemma handed to the patterned theorist, not a proof of the entitlement theory. To get from the setup to the conclusion you need the transfer principle itself, plus a clean history of acquisition, plus rectification having been done. The patterned theorist can simply accept the cost: yes, maintaining the pattern requires banning consensual transfers, that is a real loss, and I judge it smaller than the loss from letting fortunes accumulate in ways nobody chose. Nothing in the example shows that this trade is a bad one; it only shows that there is a trade. What's more, the example is rigged in a way that smuggles in a lot of the conclusion: D1 is stipulated to have a spotless history, which quietly excludes the two objections that do most of the real work in actual politics — that initial acquisition was often conquest or enclosure, and that historic injustices were never rectified. Once you notice that, the argument looks less like a refutation of patterned principles and more like an argument that patterned principles have running costs.

*2. Coordination rent: the objection I want to develop.* Here is my own variant. A million people each voluntarily send one dollar to a livestreamer. Now stipulate that the reason each sends it is not the recipient's rare skill or scarce product: each person wants to be where the crowd is. The thing they are buying is worth nothing to anyone if a thousand people buy it, and worth a great deal if a million do. Its value is constituted by the fact that the others are buying it too. Every transfer is voluntary, every payer benefits by their own lights, and so the entitlement theory must say that the resulting fortune is just, full stop.

But the fortune here is not attributable to the recipient in the way Chamberlain's surplus is attributable to Chamberlain. It is a million people's coordination precipitating onto a single focal point. Call it coordination rent. Entitlement theory is purely procedural and historical; it asks only whether the steps were voluntary, and so it has no vocabulary for asking who created this value. It therefore has to bless coordination rent wholesale, and I think that is a cost it should not be able to shrug off.

I can hear the reply already: for ordinary goods this proves nothing, because the price of everything depends on other people's preferences. But I think that is disanalogous in a way that matters. Bread nourishes you whether or not the crowd wants it; a coordination good does not exist at all without the crowd. That is a difference in the source of the value, not just in its size. The harder version of the reply is that the two cases are a continuum rather than a dichotomy: almost every good has some network or positional component — a language, a phone, a credential, a fashionable coat — so a principle saying you may keep what you attract minus the share created by the crowd's coordination threatens to be either vacuous, because everything is partly socially created and therefore nothing is fully yours, or unadministrable, because nobody can decompose a surplus into its true sources. My tentative answer is that the principle is scalar and only bites where the crowd-constituted share is both large and identifiable. I am not confident, and I suspect this is the weakest part of the essay.

*3. Why the example works on us.* Finally, a point about rhetoric rather than logic. The example's pull comes from two features: the quarter is trivial, and Chamberlain is genuinely superb. Change either one and our confidence in D2 wobbles. If the payment were large enough to narrow the payer's own future options, or if the payers were paying because of a false belief the recipient had cultivated, we would be far less sure that D2 is just — even though the steps are still voluntary in the minimal procedural sense and the argument's logic is untouched. That suggests our judgment was tracking something the argument leaves implicit: a much thicker ideal of a competent, informed, self-governing choice. If I'm right, the argument's persuasive force depends on quietly assuming a demanding account of voluntariness. Make that account explicit and you have to defend it; and once you defend it, you have conceded that voluntary is a substantive moral notion rather than a procedural one, which is precisely what the entitlement theory needed it not to be.
