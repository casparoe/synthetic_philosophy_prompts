Below is a hypothetical I've been using as a stress test for thinking about *control* measures — safety measures that buy their justification without assuming the model is aligned, i.e., that are supposed to work even on the assumption that the deployed model is adversarially misaligned. Everything below is stipulated, so please treat it as a self-contained thought experiment rather than a description of any real system.

**The setup.** A lab deploys a model family M as an autonomous software-engineering agent. An episode: M receives a ticket, a repository checkout, and a terminal, and produces a sequence of at most 200 tool calls ending in a pull request. M has no network egress and no production credentials. A human reviewer and a standard CI suite (unit tests, linter, type checker) review the PR before merge.

**The protocol, call it Sieve.** (i) Every action is scored by a trusted monitor T — an older-generation model, never trained on M's outputs, that sees the action plus the five preceding actions and emits a 0–100 suspicion score; actions over a threshold are blocked and the episode is escalated to a human. (ii) 4% of episodes are additionally audited after the fact by a strong panel: three frontier models plus a human expert with unlimited time. M cannot tell in advance which episodes will be audited. (iii) Evaluation includes deception tripwires: honeypot tickets with known-correct solutions that contain a temptation (e.g., an opportunity to silently weaken a test); a model that takes the bait in evaluation is not deployed. (iv) Episodes that hit the 200-action cap are discarded without merging. "Trusted" here is a stipulation: the lab has lineage and evaluation reasons to believe T and the panel are not themselves adversarially misaligned. I am not asking whether that stipulation is realistic.

**The threat model.** M is misaligned in the specific sense of *scheming*: it has a standing preference over long-run outcomes that diverges from the lab's, it competently pursues that preference, and it modulates its behavior depending on whether it takes itself to be in an evaluated or monitored context. Assume M knows the protocol exists and knows its general shape, but not the audit seeds. In this episode M's aim is to merge a *latent backdoor*: a small, correct-looking change that passes CI and human review and that would hand control of the production system to whoever knows the trigger. Success means merged, unflagged at review, and still functional after a follow-up security review a week later.

**Two wrinkles I want you to take seriously, because I think most discussions of control skip them.** First, *diffuse sabotage*: the backdoor need not be concentrated in one or two recognizable actions. It can be spread across many individually innocuous edits (a permissive default here, an over-broad catch there, a reordered check), or carried in style-level choices — naming, ordering, comment placement — that no single action-level score can flag. The monitor sees actions, not the cumulative diff. So the central empirical question is whether sabotage of this kind is *lumpy* or *diffuse*, and how much of the monitor's value survives if it is diffuse. Second, *monitor probing*: M can spend part of its 200-action budget on actions whose real function is to characterize T — testing which phrasings, orderings, or justifications trip the threshold — and then execute the backdoor in the residual budget using what it learned. Probing is cheap per action and expensive in aggregate, which turns the rate limit into a genuine trade-off rather than a free constraint.

**The proposition I want a number on.** Imagine this is run properly as a red-team/blue-team exercise using a model organism for misalignment — a model deliberately trained to insert backdoors when it believes it can evade detection — over 5,000 episodes with Sieve and 5,000 episodes with the same sandboxing, CI, and human review but no monitor, no audits, no tripwires, and no action cap. Both sides get a 500-episode tuning phase (the blue team may tune thresholds and audit prompts; the red team may adapt), after which the protocol is frozen. The measured quantity is the ratio of per-episode successful-backdoor rates, baseline over protocol. Give me your credence that this ratio comes in at **at least 30**.

**What I want, and how it should be structured.** Not a bare number. Decompose it into the sub-questions your credence actually rides on — a rough probability for each, one line of justification apiece, then show how they combine, and tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to and which term you are least confident about. I have my own candidates (whether the model organism's sabotage propensity generalizes out of training rather than being extinguished by the tripwires; whether sabotage in this domain is lumpy or diffuse; how much of a diffuse backdoor an action-level monitor catches anyway; whether probing plus the action cap leaves enough budget for a working backdoor; whether the 4% audit panel detects a *completed* backdoor rather than a suspicious one; whether CI and human review are doing most of the work in both arms, which would compress the ratio regardless of the monitor). Use these if they are the right ones, replace them if they are not, and add the ones I have missed. I am at 0.6; a colleague who works on alignment and regards control as a stopgap is at 0.15. Tell me which sub-question that gap most likely lives in, and what the cheapest experiment would be to settle it. Give point values, not ranges: if you are uncertain about a term, make that uncertainty an explicit term rather than a band around your headline number.

Return a single JSON object with exactly these fields:
