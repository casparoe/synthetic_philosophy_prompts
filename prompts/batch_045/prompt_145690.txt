Hi — I need a document rather than an essay, and it's for two people I know who've been going in circles for about a year on the inefficacy objection. Let me name their camps badly but usefully. The expected-difference camp ("ED"): whatever duty you have about your driving, your beef, your vote is settled by the expected difference your act makes; the probability is tiny but not zero, the stakes are enormous, the expected value is what it is, and "it makes no difference" is just sloppy talk for "you'll never see the difference." The participation / fair-share camp ("P"): what's doing the work is that you're joining in a practice that collectively harms, and taking the benefit while others restrain themselves; the causal difference made by your particular act is neither necessary nor sufficient for the duty, and ED gets the shape of the reason wrong even when it lands on the right verdict.

What I want is the joint statement they could both actually sign, and I want it in two parts: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with columns for the ED wording, the P wording, whether the disagreement is merely verbal, the crux, a case or argument both accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance.

Please build it around these cases, which I made up partly because I got bored of the standard ones:

1. The pallet case. A chicken supplier orders in blocks of ten thousand, and unfilled demand carries over as a residual that's effectively random. So your not buying has about a one-in-ten-thousand chance of shifting the next order by ten thousand birds. Compare a smooth supply chain where each bird forgone maps one-to-one onto the next order. Expected effect is basically the same; the "chunkiness" is different. Does either camp's verdict move, and why or why not? I especially want to know whether a P theorist who leans on "you were part of the causal process that produced this order" can still say that in the 9,999 out of 10,000 worlds where the order would have been placed anyway.

2. Two jurisdictions, same election. In one, a tie leaves the status quo in place. In the other, a tie is broken by a coin flip. In the second one your vote matters more often, but on the occasions it matters it moves less. Is the duty to vote different between them, on each view? This is my main attempt to pry apart "probability of mattering" from "expected size of the difference" as the thing that grounds the duty — tell me if it doesn't pry them apart.

3. The audit. Afterwards you find out — leaked supply records, or a ballot-level audit — that your purchase in fact wasn't the one that tipped the order, or your vote in fact wasn't pivotal. Does either camp owe a verdict on whether that retrospective knowledge changes anything? What would each predict ordinary people say when asked, and does each side treat that reaction as evidence about the normative question or as a predictable mistake?

4. Two "reversed" cases. (a) Someone participates but fully compensates: eats the beef and buys what everyone agrees are genuinely effective offsets. (b) Someone refuses to participate and thereby makes things slightly worse: gives up her car, and the car is bought by someone who drives it twice as much. I expect ED to be fine with (a) and queasy about (b), and P to be the reverse, but I want to know if that's the real fault line or a red herring.
