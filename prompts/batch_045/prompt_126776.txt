I'm on a tiny committee at my professional body — we certify people who write technical assessment reports, sort of like a licensing thing — and we've been going back and forth on a proposal one of our members pitched, which he calls a "currency requirement," copied from aviation. As far as I understand the aviation version: airline crews fly almost everything on autopilot and are often required to by their own procedures, but pilots still have to log a few recent manual takeoffs and landings to stay legal to carry people, and they go into the simulator every few months to have the automation failed on them and hand-fly something ugly. His version for us: members would have to log around ten hours a quarter of work done with no AI assistance at all (no autocomplete either), plus one unannounced "cold check" a year where you're handed something you submitted eight months ago, no tools, and asked to reconstruct the reasoning out loud while someone grades it. We'd still let people use AI freely on actual client work.

What I can't get straight is what we'd be claiming if we did this, because the two halves of the aviation practice seem to pull in opposite directions — you're mandated to depend on the automation and also mandated to periodically not depend on it. So: what does the practice actually presuppose about understanding or skill? Is the point that you need the unaided capacity in reserve to notice when the tool goes wrong, is it that the capacity decays without use and the credential is supposed to certify the person rather than the document, or is it something about the struggle being what builds the understanding in the first place? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with — I have a feeling several quite different theories of understanding would each license some version of it, and I'd like to know where it's genuinely underdetermined.

Two more things are bugging me and I'd like them addressed rather than swept aside. First, the line-drawing: we never required anyone to do unaided mental arithmetic, or navigate without GPS, or write without a spellchecker, or (for that matter) work without colleagues and reference books, and those all got absorbed fine — so is there a principled difference in the AI case, or is this just novelty and professional-status anxiety dressed up? One of our members keeps saying that on the extended-mind / distributed-cognition sort of view there was never an "unaided" state to protect anyway, and I can't tell whether that kills the proposal or is compatible with it for totally different reasons. Second, whether the analogy even transfers: in the cockpit the automation fails loudly — it disconnects, the alarms go off, you're handed the airplane — whereas an LLM hands you something fluent and wrong and nothing announces it, and I suspect that changes which rationale, if any, survives. And if we decided the whole presupposition was mistaken, what would assessment have to look like instead? I keep coming back to unbundling the two things — grade the artifact, and separately grade whether the person could have produced or defended it — and then not knowing whether that second number should go on the credential or stay private. I'm not a philosopher, so feel free to use the technical vocabulary but explain it as you go, and please don't just tell me who to read.
