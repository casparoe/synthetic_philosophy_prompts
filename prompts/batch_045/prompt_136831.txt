Bit of an odd one. Im a phd student in philosophy — I work on the computational theory of mind, mostly at the architecture level (what the representational system has to be like for thought to be the sort of thing that could be a computation), and I've somehow been roped into doing a guest seminar on 1984 for a first-year course on language, thought and ideology. Everyone who teaches that book does the Newspeak-as-Sapir-Whorf lecture and I don't want to: I'd spend the whole hour explaining why strong linguistic determinism is false and never get to the novel. I'd rather read the book through the language of thought hypothesis and see how far that actually gets me.

The bit of the hypothesis I care about: thinking is manipulation of symbols in an inner system with a combinatorial syntax and semantics, that system is not any public language, and — the part I find shakiest — learning a public language can't give you new conceptual primitives, because you'd have to formulate the hypothesis in a system that already had them.

So here's what I want. Read the books treatment of Newspeak against that. In the canteen scene where Syme is delighted about destroying words and explains the whole point of it — narrow the range of thought, make heresy literally unthinkable, last man left will have no words for it — which of three things is he actually claiming: that the inner systems stock of primitives is shrinking, that the public vehicles you use to steady and combine and rehearse a thought are being taken away, or that the procedures by which you arrive at and hold onto a thought are being trained out (crimestop looks to me like a skill and not a lexical gap at all)? And how much of the plot needs the strong first claim as opposed to either of the other two?

Two more things I want handled. First, the people writing Newspeak have to be able to represent exactly the thoughts they are trying to make unrepresentable — the lexicographers need the heresy in order to delete the word for it, and Syme disappears a few chapters after his canteen speech. Is that a real problem for the project or just a personnel issue? Second, the torture room and the final line about loving Big Brother: on the hypothesis, is there a substantive difference between Winston having been changed and Winston having been broken, or does the distinction between what he thinks and what he is now disposed to say collapse — and does the novel give us any way to tell?

Ive been toying with a reading that reconciles things without strong Whorfianism: primitives are innate and can't be deleted, but they can go dormant, and what the Party actually controls is which of them anything in the environment ever triggers — so Newspeak is an attack on the ecology of thought rather than its inventory. Tell me whether that's coherent, and whether its a reading the book supports or just a way of saving my favourite theory.

What I don't want: a paper that reduces the novel to an illustration, or one that quietly bends the hypothesis until it fits, or the observation that its fiction and not an argument. I want to know where each side is stubborn, and if there's something the novel shows about the dependence of thought on public and social scaffolding that you cant get from the standard arguments, say what it is — that's the part I'd actually teach. No need for citations or a reading list.
