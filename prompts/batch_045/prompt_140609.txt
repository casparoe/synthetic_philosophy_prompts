You are a professional philosopher whose research is in moral psychology and the philosophy of the emotions, and who has also advised institutions on the design of accountability practices. What I need is a careful audit, not an introduction to the topic. Do not pad the answer with background on guilt and shame that I already know, and do not flatter the package — if two commitments clash, say so plainly and say which one has to move. Context: I sit on a working group rewriting how our institution — a national professional body with roughly forty thousand members — handles complaints against members: whether findings are published, whether hearings are open, what forms of censure exist, whether repair is required. I have had to write down the assumptions I am relying on, and reading them back I cannot tell whether they add up. Here they are.

1. Guilt is the better moral emotion and shame the worse, and my grounds for saying so are broadly consequentialist: guilt disposes a person toward disclosure, repair and reform, whereas shame disposes toward concealment, withdrawal and, in a minority of cases, lashing out. I intend this as an empirical claim about motivational profiles, and I intend that empirical claim to be what settles the ranking.

2. Emotions, though, are subject to a norm of fittingness and not only to assessment by their effects. An emotion represents something — guilt represents you as having done something wrong; shame represents you as diminished in standing, or as failing to be the kind of person you take yourself to be — and an emotion is defective when that representation is inaccurate. I do not want to give this up: an emotion that reliably produced good conduct by misrepresenting the situation would still be a defective emotion.

3. Part of what makes shame the worse emotion is its globality: it indicts the person where only the act is at issue, and a person is not an apt object of global negative assessment on the basis of one wrong. At the same time, I hold that there is a class of wrongs — call them identity-implicating wrongs, where the wrong was no accident but expressed who the agent then was: the betrayal of a constitutive role or commitment rather than a lapse — in which the self really is what is at issue, and in which an agent who felt guilt and never shame would be missing something morally important. Relatedly: a person who is wholly incapable of shame is a worse person for it.

4. A culture can, to a significant degree, choose its emotional repertoire. Emotional dispositions are carried by practices, institutional scripts and public rituals rather than floating free of them, so a deliberate redesign of those practices can, over a generation or so, shift a population's dispositions away from shame and toward guilt. In our setting: replacing open censure with a structured private procedure — call it the private-censure regime — should over time produce members who feel guilt rather than shame.

5. Emotions are not under direct voluntary control. On a given occasion you cannot simply decide to feel guilt instead of shame, and nobody is rightly blamed for the occurrent feeling itself as opposed to what they do about it.

6. What makes shame fitting are facts about how a person is actually regarded by those whose regard matters to them — call these regard-facts. Regard-facts are not themselves settled by a decision about practices: a community can resolve to conduct itself differently without thereby altering whether its members in fact hold one another in diminished standing, and where the two come apart — call it shame-lag — the older feeling persists.
