I'm assembling the closing packet for a graduate reading group I run on causal inference in economic history — we spent the term on the three-way standoff between institutions, geography, and culture as explanations for why some countries are rich, and on how much of that literature is really an argument about identification rather than about history. For the last session I want something that makes the methodological problem land emotionally before we discuss it, so I'd like a piece of satire rather than another critique.

Write a mock brochure for a fictional development consultancy — call it something like Counterfactual Development Partners, LLP — that sells, to national governments, the thing the literature keeps wishing it had: a properly randomized history. The voice should be glossy management-consultancy prose (mission statement, service tiers with prices, a sidebar on methodology, one testimonial from a satisfied finance minister of an invented country, and a short FAQ of three or four entries), 700–900 words, formatted with headers. Its product line should include randomized assignment of inclusive versus extractive institutions to matched provinces within a single country, a Geographic Relocation package (latitude, continental axis, disease burden, access to navigable water), and a Cultural Re-education module covering trust, patience, and attitudes toward work, sold as an add-on. The reductio has to be that the flagship product requires deliberately imposing extractive institutions on a randomly selected half of a country and leaving them in place for a century or so, and the brochure must present this as routine, humane, and merely technical — deadpan throughout, never winking, never letting anyone say outright that the project is impossible, so that the reader feels the impossibility rather than being told it.

The comedy has to come from getting the causal-inference problems right, not from inventing silly ones. I want the fine print to carry it: a mandatory exclusion-restriction waiver; a spillover surcharge because institutions in treated provinces leak into untreated ones; a persistence surcharge with results delivered in eighty to a hundred and twenty years; an external-validity rider; a fee for changing the unit of analysis mid-engagement; and some invented in-house methodology — a Triangulation Guarantee, say, promising that institutions, geography, and culture will each be certified as the true cause, or a Residual Channel Option for whichever of the three is left over after the others are controlled for. Have the firm quietly rely on the classic move of instrumenting for present-day institutional quality with mortality rates faced by European settlers centuries earlier, and note with pride that this instrument is made of geography, disease environment, and colonial policy — without ever noticing that this undermines the separation it is selling. Include one FAQ answer that replies to a real objection with something that is not an answer, in the spirit of a firm insisting that a large, precisely estimated coefficient is the same thing as a cause. The target is the practice and the style of argument — identification rhetoric, the treatment of history as one usable draw, the slide from a local average treatment effect to a claim about nations — not any named person, living or dead, so please don't build jokes around real individuals. Ideally an economic historian would read it and think the satire is fair, while a smart non-specialist still enjoys it. No footnotes, no citations, and please don't do any research for it.
