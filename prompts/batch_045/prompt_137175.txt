Hey — I'm in a little reading group on philosophy of probability. I'm the token non-philosopher: I work as a data scientist at an insurance company, so pricing, expected loss, calibration, and that whole way of talking is native to me, but formal epistemology definitely isn't. We got stuck on Dutch book arguments, and one of the econ PhD students in the group keeps insisting the whole thing is just no-arbitrage — your credences are a price list, a Dutch book is an arbitrage, the argument says don't publish a price list somebody can arb, end of story. I feel like that can't be all there is to it, but I can't put my finger on what's left over. So instead of going in circles I tried writing down my actual gut reactions to a bunch of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to — or whether I'm just confused about what the argument was ever supposed to show.

Here are the verdicts. (1) Someone who pays $1.10 in total for two bets that between them pay out exactly $1 no matter what happens: clearly criticizable. (2) Same setup but they only overpay by two cents: still criticizable in principle, but honestly I barely care — and I worry that reaction quietly makes me a consequentialist about this in a way I don't want to be. (3) Someone who just declines every bet anyone ever offers them: I really don't want to call that person irrational, but I also don't see how the argument gets any grip on them, which makes me think it needs some extra premise like "you should be willing to bet on anything" that nobody has defended to us. (4) A friend of mine is fairly risk-averse, and his betting odds are way off his actual confidence because money has diminishing returns for him; I don't want to say his *beliefs* are incoherent, and the usual "just use small enough stakes" move feels like it's hiding the problem rather than solving it. (5) A bookie who simply knows more than me offers me a bet I'm happy to take, and I lose: I don't feel rationally criticized at all there, so "you lost money" can't be the content of the criticism — but then what is? (6) The diachronic version: I adopt a plan for how I'll update on some evidence, and the plan is exploitable by someone who offers me one bet before and one after. I do feel this is a real flaw, but a different one from case (1), and I can't say why it's different; and relatedly, when I know the evidence I'm about to get is misleading (spiked drink, evil demon, whatever), just planning to go ahead and update anyway strikes me as nuts, but the setup seems to say that refusing to update is what gets me exploited. (7) A bookie who gets to inspect my betting dispositions first and then picks which bets to offer: if I'm vague or unsettled about something, I get money-pumped, and I genuinely can't tell whether that shows vagueness is irrational or just that the bookie's setup is rigged. (8) A setup where every individual bet looks fine to me but I'm guaranteed to lose money in the limit over an infinite sequence: my gut says criticizable, but I have way less confidence here than in (1).

So: give me at most two or three candidate principles about what a Dutch book argument actually establishes — in particular what the defect is supposed to be *in* (beliefs? preferences? dispositions to act? the price list itself? my friend would say none of these, just the list) — with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, and tell me what each one predicts for cases I haven't listed, e.g. whether this style of argument says anything at all about how confident I should be in something like a mathematical conjecture I can't check, or about whether I'm allowed to be completely certain of anything. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts I'd have to give up to get a clean principle — especially (2) and (3), which I suspect don't fit together — and whether the no-arbitrage line survives those two cases or needs something bolted onto it. Please don't just walk me through the literature; I want the principle and the damage report.
