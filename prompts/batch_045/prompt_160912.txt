I help allocate a modest annual budget on the grants committee of a small foundation, and our last two cycles have been dominated by arguments that I think are really arguments about method rather than about the causes themselves. Everyone on the committee reaches for the familiar three-factor framework — importance, tractability, neglectedness — and then multiplies (or, more often, eyeballs) the three ratings. I have become fairly convinced that this habit is doing real damage at the decision stage, and I have drafted the notes below for an internal memo proposing that we demote the heuristic to a screening device and use something more explicit for the shortlist. Before I inflict it on colleagues who are attached to the framework, I would like a careful critique.

The notes, somewhat condensed:

1. The framework is being asked to answer two different questions: which causes are worth investigating (screening), and how the next unit of money should be spent (allocation). For screening, a crude salience filter is fine and probably healthy. For allocation, the target quantity is the expected marginal value of the next unit of resources at the current funding margin, and there is no reason to think a product of three context-free ratings approximates it. The factors are not independent, they are not dimensionless, and their product is not a quantity anyone is trying to maximize.

2. Neglectedness partly double-counts with diminishing returns. What it is gesturing at is that causes differ in how far along their marginal-returns curve they already sit. But headcount of existing funders is a contaminated proxy for that: existing work may be complementary to mine rather than substitutable; a crowded field may be crowded with ineffective actors, which raises rather than lowers my marginal value; and a cause can receive steady institutional funding that will simply not be there next year.

3. A separate correction — call it a fungibility discount — seems to me to belong in the model explicitly: the value of my dollar depends on what the marginal incumbent would have done with resources I decline to spend. If the counterfactual funder is a government that would have spent the money on something close to neutral, my dollar is nearly as good as it looks; if the counterfactual funder is a sophisticated donor who would have redirected to the next-best cause anyway, my dollar buys much less than it looks. This is a different question from "how many people are working on this."

4. Durability and reversal risk are missing. Some gains are stocks that persist without further outlay (a technology that now exists, changed professional norms, trained personnel who stay in the field); some are flows that must be continually re-bought (recurring delivery of a service); and some are stocks that can be undone (a policy win reversed by a successor administration, a norm that decays). If you discount a cause's benefits with a hazard rate for reversal rather than treating all benefits as permanent, rankings among the top causes can flip — a high-importance, high-tractability cause whose effects must be re-purchased every year can lose to a lower-importance cause whose effects compound.

5. Given how uncertain we are about downstream effects, part of a cause's value is the information that funding it now generates for later allocations. This favors causes with short feedback loops and measurable outcomes independently of their direct value, which means "importance" should be assessed under uncertainty rather than at a point estimate. I am uneasy about this term, though: it seems vulnerable to a regress (should I also value the information about the information?), and it may give an unbounded reason to prefer tiny, fast, legible causes.

6. My proposal: keep the three-factor scoring for generating a longlist, then score the shortlist on an explicit marginal-value ledger with four entries — (i) direct marginal value per dollar along an explicitly modelled returns curve rather than a single number, (ii) a fungibility/counterfactual-redundancy discount, (iii) a durability-adjusted discount rate including a reversal hazard, (iv) an information term. I know the obvious reply: this demands numbers nobody has, and the virtue of the heuristic is that someone can apply it in an afternoon. My answer is that almost all of the money is decided at the shortlist stage, so "the hard case is the important case" is not much of a defense of using the easy tool there.

7. I am also suspicious of my own framing in one respect: it presupposes that causes are separable units of allocation. But causes interact — building capacity in one area can make another area tractable a decade later — and if that is the dominant effect, any per-cause scoring, mine included, is the wrong shape of object.

What I would like from you:

Please treat the notes as a draft argument and give me the strongest critique you can, at around 900–1,100 words, written as if for a reasonably numerate committee that has not thought about this methodologically. I am more interested in being shown where the argument breaks than in being told it is reasonable. In particular: which of the seven points is weakest, and why; whether the fungibility discount in point 3 is genuinely a new consideration or merely neglectedness relabelled; whether the durability point is separable from importance or just importance measured over time; whether the information term in point 5 collapses into a regress or a license for timidity; and what the best defense of the multiplicative heuristic is against my point 1 — including any way in which my "expected marginal value" framing quietly presupposes something the heuristic was designed to avoid presupposing. Please also flag anywhere I have smuggled in a substantive ethical assumption (expected-value maximization, impartiality, a particular view about how to handle uncertainty) rather than derived it. Do not spend words on bibliographies or citations; I want the reasoning, not the literature. Finally, and this matters to me most: end by saying, as plainly as you can, which single consideration would most change your verdict about my proposal, where your own critique is most likely to be wrong, and how confident you are in the ranking of objections you have given.
