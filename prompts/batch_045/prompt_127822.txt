I'm putting together a talk on individual contributions to collective harms and I'd really like a second pair of eyes on the draft before I present it. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on collective action and normative ethics, and take your time: start by brainstorming privately about what is actually load-bearing in what follows — which step the whole thing rests on, and what the person in the back row would say to it — before you write anything down. Be blunt rather than flattering; if it's a retread in a new hat I'd much rather you told me that. Also assume you have no web access and please don't give me a bibliography or citations — I know that literature well enough, it's the argument I'm stuck on.

Here are the notes, its rough and about half-written:

§1. The inefficacy objection in its strongest form. For most of the things I might do — eat the burger, drive to the shop, buy the cheap t-shirt — things would have gone just as badly had I not done it. Not "slightly less badly": identically. There is no threshold my single purchase crosses, and once you run the numbers the expected difference I make is near enough zero. So, says the objector, I have no reason of the relevant outcome-shaped kind to refrain.

§2. The familiar replies all (I want to claim) try to put the outcome-difference back: (a) expected-difference arguments — there's some small chance I'm the marginal unit and the harm at stake is big, so the expected disvalue isn't negligible; (b) group or "share of the total" moves — I'm not a lone individual, I'm a member of the collection whose acts together do the harm; (c) expression- or attitude-based replies — what's wrong with the act is what it expresses or what it shows about what I care about; (d) participation and complicity accounts, the Kutz-style stuff — what matters is what I'm doing together with others, not what I bring about single-handed.

§3. My reframing. The dialectic is mis-set because both sides grant that the normative question is a question about my act's causal profile with respect to the outcome. I want to deny that. Distinguish: (i) does my token act make a difference to how things go? (ii) is my token act one of the acts that together constitute the ongoing practice which is the harm? Claim: the moral weight rides on (ii), and (a) and (b) are really attempts to squeeze (ii)'s verdict out of (i).

§4. The case I want to hang the whole paper on. Call it the fixed-contract case. A supermarket chain sets its meat supply a year ahead; how many animals get killed over the coming year is already fixed by that contract, and the next contract is set from quarterly sales data that my one purchase cannot nudge in any way whatever. Stipulate further that the carcass is already bought: if I don't take it, it goes in the bin or gets sold to someone else on Tuesday. So (a) is dead, because I've stipulated the threshold uncertainty away; (b) is dead too, because the collection of purchases that fixed the contract is already in the past and I'm not part of it. And still I want to say there's a reason not to buy it. My proposal: the significance of the act is that it is an instance of the practice — practices are held up by the density of their instances, and abstaining thins a practice's grip on the people caught in it — and that the instance-to-practice relation here is constitutive, not causal.

§5. The worry I can't shake, and where a friend over dinner said it just collapses into (d). Is the constitutive relation doing any work that (c) and (d) don't already do, or have I only redescribed complicity with extra machinery? And does it over-generate: if constitutive participation is what matters, the inefficacy objection to voting collapses as well, and I can't decide whether that's a bonus or a reductio.

What I want from you: (1) is any step in §§1–4 invalid, or does any premise quietly assume what I say I'm going to establish? Name the step. (2) Is the constitutive move genuinely a distinct position or a notational variant of (c) or (d), and what would have to be added for it to be more than a redescription? (3) The single strongest objection I haven't addressed — not the inefficacy objection in fancier dress, please. (4) A case that breaks the constitutive claim, ideally one I haven't thought of; I'll happily take two. (5) Honest verdict: new, or old news? If old news, which of the four replies does it collapse into and why. (6) If you had to cut this to twenty minutes, what goes?

Take a verdict on each point even where you aren't certain, and don't hedge the whole thing into mush at the end.
