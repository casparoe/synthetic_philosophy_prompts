You are a professional philosopher of perception — someone who has taught the argument from illusion many times and can state disjunctivism's several versions, and their rivals, without reaching for a bibliography. Think carefully and slowly before you write, and produce the deliverables in the order I list them at the end.

Context for what I want. I write long posts for the rationalist forum I've been reading for about a decade, and I'm drafting one on whether disjunctivism ever has to say, in the positive, what relation it thinks perception is. My half-formed suspicion is that the debate has been run almost entirely on two exemplars: the good case and the Cartesian bad case. Every supposedly intermediate case has a stock reply — mirrors and hearing aids are just unusual causal routes, prosthetic and substituted vision still bottoms out in a real object somewhere, and "veridical hallucination" is dismissed as a coincidence that gives the subject nothing. So the interesting cases keep getting absorbed. What I don't think I've seen anyone try is a case that is intermediate along exactly one dimension: hold fixed the phenomenal character of the experience, the truth of its content, the reliability of the method across nearby environments, and the subject's total evidence; vary only whether the object itself enters the causal chain in the ordinary way.

If such a case can be built, I think relational disjunctivism is put on a fork. Either it classifies the case as perception — in which case the relation it requires is thinner than the relational story advertises, since counterfactual dependence or reliable tracking, rather than causal contact, would suffice — or it classifies it as non-perception, in which case the epistemic asymmetry that motivates the view looks lost, because by construction nothing available to the subject has gone missing.

What I want from you is that case, built to order.

Specifications.

1. One paragraph, no more than about 150 words, in an ordinary setting — a kitchen, an airport, a hospital room.
2. Realistic technology only: things that exist or that a reasonable person would expect to exist within ten years. No demons, no evil neuroscientists, no brains in vats, no perfect predictors or simulators, no exotic physics, no science fiction.
3. The variable you isolate is the causal route from object to experience, and nothing else. I want to be able to state, in one sentence each, what is held fixed and what is varied.
4. The scenario description may not contain the verdict. No "of course she isn't really seeing it," no stipulation of what intuition the reader is supposed to have, no tagging of the case as a case of perception or of hallucination. The pressure has to fall out of how the theories work, not out of your prose.
5. The fork must be genuine: whichever way the relational disjunctivist classifies the case, they give up something they need, and you should be able to say what.

Deliverables, in this order.

First, the scenario.

Second, the fork, taken one branch at a time: classify the case as perception, and say precisely what the disjunctivist has to concede and why; then classify it as non-perception, and do the same. If one branch is cheaper than the other, say which and why, and say what it would take to make the other branch credible.

Third, verdicts from two other positions, each stated in the terms that position would accept: the conjunctive common-factor theorist, for whom perception is a state shared with hallucination plus an extrinsic causal or veridicality condition; and the epistemological disjunctivist, who grants the common factor but denies that the good and bad cases confer the same reasons. For each, say what they say about your case and what their bill is — that is, what the case makes them commit to that they might rather not.

Fourth, tell me which structural feature of the case is doing the work. Do this without relying on the phrase "causal contact" as an explanation: taboo it and say what is actually carrying the weight. Is it counterfactual dependence of the experience on the object? Reliability across nearby cases? Something about the subject's access to her own reasons? If the work is being done by something I haven't named, name it and define it.

Fifth, two ablation variants, and an honest report. Variant A: the same machinery, but the rendered image no longer matches anything present — the display shows an object that isn't there. Variant B: the machinery is made fully transparent, so the ordinary causal route is restored. For each, say what changes for each of the three positions and whether the interesting pressure survives. In particular, tell me honestly whether Variant A shows that my case was only the familiar veridical-hallucination case in new clothes, or only an elaborate mirror case with a disjunctivist answer already on the shelf. If it is, say so plainly; I would rather know that than publish a case that dissolves on contact.

Sixth, steelman the strongest reply available to a relational disjunctivist — the one I would get in the comments from the most informed and least concessive critic — and then tell me whether it is a reply or a retreat, and what the crux is between you and them.

Seventh, a short constraints checklist confirming each of my five specifications against your draft, and a one-line verdict on whether the case does the job I described.

Length: roughly 800 to 1,000 words plus the checklist, with headings. No citations and no literature review — I know the terrain and I will fill in references myself. What I need from you is the design and the demonstration that it works.
