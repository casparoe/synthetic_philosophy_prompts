so i've been going to this monthly reading group — mostly ex-EA types and two phil grad students, we did the longtermism material last year and now we're on climate and what we owe future generations — and one guy in it does basically all his reasoning inside a single picture: the atmosphere is a bathtub. emissions are the faucet, the ocean and land sinks are the drain, atmospheric CO2 is the water level, and warming-and-damage is the water getting high enough to slop over the rim onto the floor. he has a whiteboard version he redraws every meeting.

i used to find it clarifying and now i don't trust it, but i can't tell whether i'm objecting to the picture or to something he's quietly importing alongside it. he uses it to license roughly four things: (a) the only thing that matters morally is the level we hand over, so the unit of justice is a cumulative carbon budget and the annual flow is morally inert except through the total — this is also how he shuts down talk about discount rates, on the grounds that the people alive in 2100 are just people; (b) since only the level matters, engineered carbon removal later is a full substitute for not emitting now, a ton out cancels a ton in, so overshoot now and mop up later is fine in principle; (c) the drain isn't ours to fix, so it isn't our problem that natural sinks are currently doing a big chunk of our work for us; and (d) mitigation and adaptation are the same kind of intervention — turn the tap down or mop the floor, whichever is cheaper per unit of water kept off the floor.

can you audit this properly? give me the correspondence table first — what plays the faucet, the drain, the level, the overflow, the person holding the tap, the person standing in the water — then sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually bite and the ones that are cosmetic. the ones i half-suspect myself: a real drain has a fixed capacity and doesn't shrink as the level rises or clog as things warm, whereas sinks weaken and feedbacks can add flow; real damage is a smooth and probably convex function of level rather than a threshold at the rim; water that has slopped out does not climb back in when you pull the plug later, so the picture builds in reversibility; and the tub has one agent at the tap, while in the actual case the people filling and the people getting wet are partly the same overlapping cohorts. but i want your read on which of those matter for which of (a) through (d), what each conclusion is really resting on once the mapping is exposed, and whether there's a better picture — or a repaired bathtub — that keeps whatever is genuinely right in his argument. lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
