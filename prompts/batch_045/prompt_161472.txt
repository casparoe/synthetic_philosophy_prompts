I teach a mixed second-year seminar on rational choice — about twenty students, mostly philosophy majors with some economics majors — and I have one ninety-minute session, a flat room with movable tables, and no assistant. Everyone has seen expected value and basic conditional probability; as far as I know none of them has met the two-envelope situation, and I don't want the name, the standard framing, or any version of the switching argument anywhere in the room until the last fifteen minutes. My budget is about thirty euros, so stakes can be real but must be small: my plan is that play happens in tokens, the class's tokens accumulate in one shared bank, and at the end the bank buys something modest for everyone (or nothing, if the bank is thin). I also want each student to keep a decision sheet — a row per round recording the amount seen, the choice made, and one line giving their reason in their own words — because I would rather have the paradox arrive in their handwriting than in my voice.

What I need from you is a runnable session: the materials and the printed tables or card/dice procedures for generating amounts, a timeline with rough minute marks, and the exact instruction wording (the wording matters — I have to get them to act without telling them what to think). The staging I have in mind, which you should fix, replace, or reorder: a blind round where they must decide without opening anything; a peek round where they write their reason before any discussion; a paired exercise in which two students hold the two envelopes of the same pair, each is privately offered the swap, each finds the reasoning equally compelling, and the transfer turns out to be zero-sum; and then two different generators to compare. In the first, a pair is fixed and then randomised across the two envelopes; in the second, their amount is drawn first and the other envelope is then set to double or half of it by a coin flip, so that the second envelope is causally downstream of theirs. The two should look indistinguishable from the inside — same thing observed, same one-line argument available — yet only one of them rewards switching, and I want the class to feel that difference rather than be told it. I also want peeking to be scored against a control: half the switches in each round decided by a coin, and the other half by the students' own policy, with every round ending in both envelopes being revealed so each student can tally not only what they got but what the alternative policy would have got on that very same sequence. Finally, I want them to discover that with a bounded table of amounts a threshold policy ("switch if I see less than X, otherwise stay") genuinely beats blind switching, and that its whole advantage comes from the top of the range rather than from the halving-and-doubling step — so that when I later stretch the table upward, the advantage drains away without the argument they wrote down having changed at all.

Then the debrief, mapped moment by moment onto what the students actually did, and I want it to include: how to handle the student who puts the airtight-looking one-point-two-five-times algebra on the board in the peek round, in a way that lets the class test it rather than me answering it; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (noise in small samples, students who peek and still feel nothing, the student who refuses to write a reason, the pair of friends who compare amounts mid-round); and an honest account of which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in words — I suspect it is the genuinely one-shot case against an unknown or bottomless generator, where the pull of the argument is strongest and no amount of playing, or of counterfactual scoring, could settle it. What I want them to walk out seeing is that the step from "whatever I see, the other envelope is half or double it" to "so switching is worth a quarter of what I see" is licensed by nothing they can observe in the envelope, that it depends entirely on how the envelopes were filled, and that the only information peeking really gives them is information about the edges of the range.
