I convene a third-year seminar on mental representation and, for the past five years, have held a joint appointment that puts me half-time in a machine-learning group, where I co-supervise two doctoral students working on model-based planning and offline evaluation. I move between two vocabularies every day and my students assume they are irreconcilable; I want to test that assumption with a careful translation exercise, and I care rather more about what the translation fails to carry than about what it carries.

The source text for the exercise is Dennett's ascending taxonomy of kinds of minds: Darwinian creatures, whose behavioural competence is fixed by phylogenetic selection over generations; Skinnerian creatures, whose lifetime learning is shaped by the selective retention of rewarded variation; Popperian creatures, which can let candidate actions fail inside an internal model before they are tried in the world, and so can die in their stead; and Gregorian creatures, which are fitted out with culturally transmitted mind-tools, most importantly public language, that restructure the very space of moves available to them, and are thereby lifted into a further grade rather than merely improved within their kind. Translate these four grades, one at a time and as faithfully as you can, into the vocabulary I actually use in the lab: reward functions and policies, model-free as against model-based reinforcement learning, world models and offline policy evaluation, credit assignment over long horizons, the outer loop of meta-learning and learning-to-learn, pretraining and fine-tuning, curriculum and environment design, sim-to-real transfer, distributional shift and reward hacking. For each grade, tell me which claims map cleanly onto engineering machinery, which survive only if I bend them, and which cannot be expressed in that vocabulary at all. Then give me your verdict on the residue: is what fails to translate a blind spot of the machine-learning vocabulary, a sign that Dennett's grades are doing normative or phenomenological work they are not advertised as doing, or evidence that some of the distinctions are less sharp than the ladder's rhetoric suggests? Please be explicit wherever you are importing an assumption from the machine-learning side rather than translating, and keep the translation honest rather than flattering to either vocabulary.

Two complications should be folded in, because both are places where I suspect the taxonomy itself is under-described rather than merely difficult to render into my terms. First, a problem about order: if the Popperian grade depends on internal rehearsal, and internal rehearsal depends on symbols that are first learned in public exchange — the Vygotskyan thought that private thought is interiorised social speech, and Andy Clark's related suggestion that language is a cognitive technology that reorganises the computational problem rather than merely reporting an already completed solution — then the explanatory order may invert the ladder. On that reading the Gregorian grade has to be substantially in place before genuine Popperian testing is possible, even though the Popperian grade sits lower in the ordering of power, and the neat image of a tower of generate-and-test built up grade upon grade becomes something closer to a loop. I would like to know whether that inversion is fatal to the idea of grades as grades, or whether the ladder can absorb it as a distinction between order of explanation and order of attainment. Second, a fifth grade I have invented for seminar purposes and cannot place: call them curatorial creatures, minds whose principal adaptation is not the acquisition of tools but the maintenance of an informational environment that outlives and outruns any individual — archives, ledgers, case law, standing procedures for error-correction and for the recording of error — so that what is transmitted is not a stock of devices but a maintained regime of selection under which later minds are shaped. Is that a genuine addition to the taxonomy, a redescription of the Gregorian grade from an institutional rather than an individual point of view, or something the ladder cannot accommodate because it is built around single animals? I am suspicious of my own answer here, because my machine-learning vocabulary already has ready-made candidates for it in dataset curation, benchmark suites and evaluation regimes, which makes me worry that I am reading my own framework back into the philosophy. Where either of my two complications rests on a confusion, say so plainly rather than indulging it. Please write in British English, give me no reading list or citations — I have those — and aim for somewhere in the region of nine hundred to twelve hundred words.
