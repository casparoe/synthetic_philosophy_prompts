I am defending a thesis chapter in my department's work-in-progress seminar in two weeks, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defence of the view. The chapter stakes out what I have been calling the reconsideration reading of Williams's integrity objection, and I would like the objections aimed at my formulation, not at the integrity objection in general.

The thesis, in brief. The standard reading takes the objection to target the content of the consequentialist's deliberation: the charge is that the agent who settles what to do by asking which outcome is impersonally best, and who acts on that answer, is alienated from the projects and relationships that give him reasons, and that in the two familiar Williams cases — the man who can rescue only one of two drowning people, one of whom is his wife, and the traveller invited to shoot one of twenty captives himself in order to spare nineteen — the agent who needs the impersonal thought in order to act has, in some sense, got his deliberation wrong even when he does the right thing. I argue that this reading is too coarse, and that what is really doing the work is a claim about what the agent must be disposed to reopen. On my reading the complaint is not that the agent acted for the maximizing reason on this occasion, but that he must remain disposed to treat the standing question whether his relationship or commitment is worth what it costs as a live deliberative question, to be answered again whenever the numbers move — that he must be prepared to reconsider from the outside something that, from the inside, is settled. I then make two further moves. First, the reconsideration version survives the reply that disposes of the alienation version: indirect or sophisticated consequentialism lets the agent act without maximizing thoughts and even lets him cultivate non-maximizing dispositions, but it still requires him to be disposed to reconsider whether to go on being the kind of agent who acts that way, and that is the disposition the complaint targets. Second, and this is the move I expect to be contested hardest, the objection then generalises: any view on which one kind of reason has deliberative primacy — the Kantian's moral law, the pluralist's overriding moral reasons — also requires the agent to be disposed to reopen personal commitments whenever the privileged reasons weigh against them, and so generates a structurally identical complaint. I bite the bullet and conclude that integrity requires deliberative compartmentalisation: practical rationality itself licenses holding some commitments settled against certain kinds of challenge.

The room. Eleven people, two hours, twenty minutes of talk and the rest discussion. Four are indirect or sophisticated consequentialists who think the alienation charge was answered decades ago and will want to know what is left once the decision-procedural point is granted. Three are deontologists, two broadly Kantian and one a Rossian pluralist, none of them sympathetic to integrity as a moral notion but all of them interested in whether my generalisation misdescribes them. One is a virtue theorist who will press the case of the person whose commitments are bad. One works on planning agency and intentions in practical reasoning, and I expect her to ask whether a settled commitment is just an intention in the planning sense, in which case my conclusion is something her framework already accommodates and my objection loses its bite. The chair works on moral demandingness and will want to know whether the reconsideration complaint is the demandingness objection wearing different clothes; there is also a visitor from a research group on effective giving who will ask whether my view ends up licensing complacency. Nobody in the room works on Williams, so I do not expect textual or interpretive objections; they will take my reading as given and go for the moves.

What I want, in this order: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this combination of people, ranked by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each attributed to the person who would raise it and phrased in that person's vocabulary rather than in mine; a two-minute reply to each; and, for each reply, a note on where it is soft or where it banks on something I have not argued. Then tell me what I should concede on the first slide before anyone objects, which single objection has enough force that it should change the chapter rather than be answered, which one reply has to land if the chapter is to survive, and what the question is that I am hoping nobody asks.
