You are a decision theorist and philosopher of action who works on dynamic choice: diachronic rationality, time-inconsistent preferences, and the money-pump arguments that are supposed to underwrite coherence requirements over time. I'm writing a paper on whether the money-pump style of argument establishes anything more than a conditional claim — roughly, "if you expect to face trades of kind K, then coherence pays" — and I need a case that puts the usual moral of those arguments under pressure. Do not answer immediately. First, brainstorm three or four candidate mechanisms for the reversal I describe below, say in a line or two each why they succeed or fail, pick the most robust one, and only then compose the final scenario. I want the rejected mechanisms visible in your answer; if the first two you think of are dead ends, show me why, because that is where I'll learn something about the shape of the problem.

The request: build me a decision problem in which the disposition that money-pump arguments are normally taken to vindicate — forming a plan for the whole sequence, adopting it at the outset, and not reopening it at each node — is precisely what gets the agent exploited, and in which the agent who reconsiders at every node does strictly better. In the standard framing the planner (resolute choice: execute the plan that is optimal from the standpoint of the initial node) and the reconsiderer (sophisticated choice: correctly predict what your later selves will choose and backward-induct on that) can come apart, and when they do, commitment is the cure and reconsideration is the disease. I want the opposite. Commitment should be the vulnerability and the capacity to reopen the question should be the cure.

Specifications the case has to meet:

1. Realistic and small. A workplace, a savings or mortgage decision, a training or health regimen, a subscription, a lease, a freelance contract. One paragraph of scenario, no more. No demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics or psychology; the counterparty may be no cleverer than a shrewd lender, a line manager who has read your calendar, or a platform that has noticed what kind of customer you are.
2. The agent's preferences are time-inconsistent only in the ordinary, well-modelled way — present bias, declining impatience, something hyperbolic-ish. No cyclic or intransitive preferences, no incompleteness, no sorites-style incremental slide. I do not want a synchronic money pump wearing a diachronic costume.
3. The plan she adopts must be genuinely optimal relative to the decision problem she faces when she adopts it: the best plan available, chosen for good reasons, under a correct description of her situation, with correct beliefs about her future preferences. She then executes it exactly — no weakness of will, no akrasia, no miscalculation, no forgetting.
4. She nonetheless ends up strictly worse off than the sophisticated counterpart who takes each node's options as they come — worse by the lights of her own initial preferences, and, if you can arrange it, worse by the lights of her final self too. State explicitly which self's preferences you are using when you say "worse," and defend that choice.
5. Nothing may stipulate the verdicts. No "she regrets it because the story says so," no counterparty who reads minds, no clause that simply declares the plan's continuation to be bad. The gap has to be generated by the structure of the situation operating on the two policies.

After the scenario, give me:

(a) the sequence: the nodes, the option set at each, and what each of the two agents chooses there;
(b) the reasoning at every node where they diverge, with the payoffs made explicit enough that a reader can check the comparison;
(c) a statement of which structural feature is doing the work. My own two suspicions are, first, menu growth: an option appears at a later node that was not in the tree when the plan was formed, so the plan's continuation is optimal only for a problem the agent no longer faces; and second, the legibility of commitment to others: the counterparty knows she will not reopen the question and makes her an offer that is only attractive, or only profitable for them, because she cannot walk away — the same credibility that solves the intrapersonal problem becoming a liability in a strategic one. Tell me which of these actually carries the case, or if it is something better, and do not feel obliged to agree with me;
(d) an ablation test: remove that feature — hide the plan from the counterparty, or hold the option set fixed across nodes — and show that the reversal collapses and the planner wins again, as the textbooks say she should;
(e) the honest defence. How would a friend of planned/resolute choice attack your case? I specifically want you to consider the reply that a well-designed plan is a plan about planning — that it contains its own reconsideration clauses, policies for when to reopen, and escape hatches — and to tell me whether that reply dissolves your case or merely relocates it, and if it relocates it, where.

Close with two or three sentences on what the case, if it works, shows about the authority of money-pump arguments: whether they establish a requirement of rationality or only a contingent strategic generalization, and which reading your case supports.

Two final constraints. It has to work as a handout in a graduate seminar: a smart non-specialist should be able to follow it in one pass, so no formalism beyond what you explain. And I know the literature, so spend no words on it — no survey, no names to drop, no bibliography, no citations. Use whatever labels you like for the two policies, or none. Give me the case and the argument.
