I write fiction by trade and have no philosophical training, and I am trying to make one chapter of a novel in progress honest rather than merely plausible. The chapter turns on a dispute inside a small digital-preservation studio, and I have realized that the dispute only makes sense given a particular view about what a computation is — but I cannot tell which view, or how strong it has to be.

The practice I want to understand is, I am told, utterly standard outside fiction. In programming-language standards (C and its descendants are the ones that were explained to me), the meaning of a program is specified by describing an idealized "abstract machine" that executes it step by step, and the standard then adds that an implementation is conforming if the program's observable behavior is as if the abstract machine had run it — whatever the implementation actually does internally, and however many of the specified steps it skips, reorders, or never performs at all. "Observable" here means roughly what the program does at its interfaces with the outside world: what it writes to files, prints, or sends to devices. The same idea seems to govern preservation by emulation, which is my character's job: a game or an interactive artwork from the 1990s is said to survive on hardware that did not exist when it was written, provided it behaves as it did.

Two details from the novel, both of which I believe are real rather than invented. First, a rival firm sells a cheaper "preservation" product that does not emulate anything: it records a very large library of play sessions and, when a visitor does something, replays whichever recorded session is closest. No process of the original program's kind occurs; it is a lookup table with a friendly face. The studio's contracts require only that observable behavior match. Second, a different character writes cryptographic code that must take the same amount of time on every input, and her compiler keeps deleting the code she wrote to guarantee that, because its licence to rewrite anything that leaves observable behavior unchanged does not count elapsed time as observable.

So: what theory of computation does this as-if practice presuppose? I would like it reconstructed rather than named — what it takes a computation to be, what makes two executions the same computation, what makes a physical process an implementation of a given program, and which rival accounts it quietly excludes (I have in mind the view that computational descriptions are observer-relative and must be anchored in a mechanism's causal organization, and I would like to know whether the practice is compatible with that or secretly inconsistent with it). Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with.

Does it presuppose the Church–Turing thesis, and if so which one: the formal result that every reasonable formalism of effective computation picks out the same class of functions; the substantive claim about our informal notion of an effective procedure; or the physical claim that nothing in our world computes beyond a Turing machine? My sense is that an as-if rule would be perfectly content with an implementation that availed itself of a hypercomputer, and I would like to know whether that instinct is right. Relatedly: does the practice have any resources for saying that the replay archive is not a genuine implementation, or does excluding it require something beyond Church–Turing — a uniformity condition, or a constraint on resources — and if it does require something extra, is that a defect in the practice or a feature of it?

And is the practice internally coherent, given that it defines meaning via the steps of a machine while enforcing only the results, and then insists, in some quarters, that some things which are not results — timing, power draw — are part of what has to match? Is "observable behavior" a fixed notion, or a stipulation renegotiated case by case; and if the latter, in what sense does the standard fix a single thing that the program means? Finally, since no finite amount of testing can establish that two implementations agree on all inputs, the practice seems committed to facts about what a program means that outrun any evidence — and I would like to know where its entitlement to that comes from, proof or definition or something more like a corroborated conjecture, since that appears to be what the argument is actually about.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single most important claim about what this practice presupposes — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my questions. A numbered list of theses, each with a verdict on whether it is strictly presupposed, merely convenient, or optional, would serve me best: I intend to paraphrase parts of it in dialogue, so I need formulations a working programmer or archivist could say out loud without embarrassment.
