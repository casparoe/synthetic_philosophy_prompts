I do a column on AI and ideas for a science magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the whole "is a large language model an agent, a simulator, or something else again" fight. I've now sat through maybe forty version of this argument and they all go the same way: someone points at goal-directed behaviour, someone else says that's a character being played, a third person says it's autocomplete with good PR, the thread everyone ends up loosing is the one about consciousness, and nobody's position has actually moved. I don't want another round of that. What I want is the spec.

So: write me the list of requirements that any account of what a deployed LLM system is — agent, simulator, tool, or some new category nobody's named properly yet — would have to meet before it's worth printing. For each requirement give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say what it rules out.

Here's my rough list, so you can see the shape of what I mean and then fix it, reorder it, and add what I've missed (I assume about half of it is confused):

- It has to say where the property is supposed to live: in the weights, in weights-plus-context, or in weights-plus-context-plus-the loop the thing is sitting in (tools, memory, a scaffold that feeds its own output back in). Same weights, different scaffolding, apparently a different kind of object. An account that only works for one of those setups isn't an account of the model. Similarly: an RL-tuned model and the base model it was tuned from — same kind of thing, or different? Your account has to have an answer, and the answer has to follow from the account rather than be stapled on.
- It has to handle the fact that in-context learning changes what the system does without changing a single weight. There's persona formation on the fast timescale and on the slow one. Which one is the agent-having one, if either — and if the answer is "the fast one", what does that do to the idea that agency is a disposition?
- It has to say what happens to the agency that's supposedly being simulated. The people who wrote the training text had goals; the system is in some sense re-enacting them. Is borrowed agency agency, or does the borrowing use it up? And how much does it matter that the borrowing is statistical rather than intentional?
- It has to say what would settle it. If "agent" and "simulator" make identical predictions for every observation you could actually make inside any fixed budget, then either show me why they're still genuinely different claims, or say plainly that the choice is a frame and tell me what the frame is for.
- It has to respect the gap between what the system says about itself in the first person and what's true of it, without that gap just being the conclusion smuggled back in. (There's a reason we don't let the defendant's testimony be the whole case.)
- It has to handle the no-neutral-prompt problem: there's no unprompted baseline, so any claim about what the model itself does rests on a choice of framing that the theorist gets to make quietly. Require a privileged default framing, or an argument for why none exists.
- Unity/coherence: we read agency off each other partly because you're roughly the same person in different situations. These systems generalise weirdly — impressive on distribution, strange off it. Should cross-contextual robustness be a condition on calling something an agent at all, and if so does anything pass, including us?
- It shouldn't collapse into the consciousness question, or into "does it understand", unless you can first show me those really are the same question. I want the agency question held apart long enough to be answered.

Three more things I need from you.

First: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just prejudices of one camp. I have a feeling the unity/coherence one is a human-derived bias that's doing less work than people think, but you tell me.

Second — and this is the bit I most want — I suspect some subset of these can't all be satisfied together. Maybe "the account must make some observable difference" pulls against "the property lives in the weights alone". Maybe borrowed-agency and coherence come apart. Maybe the no-neutral-prompt requirement quietly kills the whole project. If the list is internally inconsistent, say so explicitly and show me where; don't smooth it over to make the piece tidier.

Third: one paragraph at the end on which single requirement knocks out the biggest share of the popular positions. My guess is that something in there sinks both the it's-just-a-tool line and the obviously-it's-an-agent line for the same underlying reason, but I can't put my finger on what the reason is.

Keep it under about 900 words, plain English, and no citations, reading lists, or named papers — I can't check them, my editor cuts them, and I'd rather have the argument. A short table at the end is fine if it genuinely helps.
