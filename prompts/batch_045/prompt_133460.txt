Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between an expected-difference theorist and an inefficacy skeptic about individual contributions to collective harms such as climate change, factory farming, and voting. The expected-difference theorist holds that each individual act has a small but nonzero chance of making a difference to the outcome, because supply chains and climate systems contain thresholds, and that this expected difference grounds a genuine individual obligation. The inefficacy skeptic holds that no individual act makes a causal difference to the collective outcome, so the standard consequentialist argument for an individual obligation fails, and any duty must be grounded in something else — collective obligation, fairness, integrity, or expression.

To keep the collaboration from collapsing into a verbal dispute, take as your shared test case a hypothetical new technology: a threshold audit that can determine, for any given purchase or emission, whether that act was the marginal unit that pushed a producer's order or a climate system past a threshold that caused additional harm, that is, whether the act was genuinely difference-making. Both sides accept this technology, if it worked, as bearing on the central causal crux, and both accept in advance that the pattern of audit results would count for or against their view; write down what each predicts the audits would find and why.

Produce a document both could sign: first, a numbered section of claims both accept, covering the causal structure of collective harms, what making a difference would have to involve, and what the dispute is not about; second, a disagreements table giving, in wording each side would accept for its own view, exactly where they differ, followed by the cruxes — for instance, whether a merely probabilistic difference can ground obligation, and whether the skeptic must deny the expected-difference theorist's threshold model — and, for each crux, the case, argument, or finding both would accept as evidence, with each side's prediction recorded. If you think part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why; keep every formulation neutral enough that neither side would reject the statement of its own position.
