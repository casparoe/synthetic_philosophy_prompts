You are a philosopher of history who also works on action theory and on causation in the special sciences. Please work through this carefully before you write anything: begin by brainstorming, in rough and untidy form, every candidate correspondence you can think of between a human agent and a non-human cause, including the ones you will eventually reject, and only then assemble the audit. I would rather see the rejected options sketched and crossed out than receive a tidy answer that hides them.

I am finishing a master's thesis in environmental history, and the framing fight in my department has become personal. I grew up with a family version of the Irish famine in which a fungus was the villain, and I have spent two years in graduate school being taught that the villain was a set of decisions; my committee meeting is in three weeks and I need to know which of those two stories the vocabulary I have been using commits me to. My supervisor's line, which she repeats as though it were a settled methodological principle, is that we should stop treating the environment as the stage on which human drama plays out and start treating it as one more actor on the stage. She means it to do two specific jobs in my chapter. The first is to license a certain kind of sentence as more than decoration: that the blight emptied the countryside, that the blight and the Treasury were joint authors of what happened to the cottiers, that a volcano in the Indonesian archipelago helped set the terms of European politics one summer. The second is a division of moral labour: if a non-human actor shares the causal stage with the human ones, then blame is to be apportioned rather than laid entirely at the door of policy, and a famine becomes a compound of misfortune and malfeasance rather than either a natural disaster or a crime.

A senior colleague says all of this is environmental determinism wearing a thesaurus, that actors are the kind of thing that has intentions, acts for reasons, can refrain, and can be held to account, and that calling a fungus an actor is either harmless shorthand or a way of laundering a political conclusion as a metaphysical one. I do not want the referee's version of that argument, I want the audit: give me the correspondence table with each slot filled in on both sides — intention; goal-directedness and the difference between being selected for an effect and aiming at it; foresight; responsiveness to reasons; the ability to do otherwise; counterfactual dependence; liability to blame; the capacity to be deterred, negotiated with, or held to a promise; the having of a point of view — and for each say whether the non-human occupant of the slot is a genuine occupant, a merely functional analogue, or a place where the table is simply blank. Then tell me which of the gaps are load-bearing for my supervisor's first claim and which for her second, because I suspect the answer differs by job: it may be that the vocabulary is defensible for the narrative work and hopeless for the moral work, or the reverse, and I want to be told which.

Two complications I have not seen handled anywhere, and I want both addressed explicitly. The first is my own. I have been sorting the routes by which a non-human cause reaches a historical outcome into force-mediated ones, where it kills, incapacitates, or removes a resource, and reason-mediated ones, where it enters by changing what someone believes, expects, or fears: an outbreak that produces a run on food and then a relief policy that would not otherwise have existed; a drought that alters the expected payoff of raiding and so changes what a pastoralist decides to raid for; an eruption whose darkness is read as a portent and acted on as such. My sense is that actor-talk is far better behaved on the second route, because there the non-human cause operates through the space of reasons rather than merely on bodies, and that this distinction quietly reorders the whole audit — germs, which evolve against host defences in something like an arms race, look like agents in one way, volcanoes in quite another, and climate somewhere in between. Tell me whether that sorting is doing real work or whether it collapses under pressure, and if it holds, what it implies about whether germs, climate, and volcanoes should get the same treatment at all. The second complication is my colleague's fallback: that causal attributions in history are competitive, so naming the blight as the cause necessarily de-selects the Treasury, and no framing that makes the blight vivid can avoid exculpating the Treasury a little. Is that a real defect in the actor framing, or only a feature of singular causal explanation generally? And either way, can the comparison be repaired — by weakening the notion of agency at work, or by pairing actor-talk with something that keeps the human decisions in view? If there is a better analogy available that does the work my supervisor wants without the work my colleague objects to, name it and defend it; if the honest answer is that nothing does both, say so plainly and tell me exactly which of her two claims I should give up.
