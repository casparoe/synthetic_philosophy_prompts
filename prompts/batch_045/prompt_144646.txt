I'm retired now — I spent about thirty years doing NMR on membrane proteins — and for the last couple years I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the university, mostly in the role of the token scientist who gets asked whether any of this is physical. We're currently working through Gödel, Escher, Bach, which I first read in grad school and remembered as being much more convincing than it looks to me now.

Here's what I want help with. One member of the group, a retired computer scientist, keeps leaning on the book's central analogy: the move from a self-referential formula — the one that in effect says of itself that it can't be derived within the system — to a self-aware mind, by way of the strange-loop / tangled-hierarchy idea. A system rich enough to encode its own structure gets tangled up in itself, and in the tangling there appears a core, an I. He is using it to license a strong conclusion: once you have a system with rich enough self-representation there is nothing extra left to add, self-awareness is not a further ingredient, and something like a large language model trained on enough text, including its own output, is on the same road we are on.

I feel the pull of it, and I can also see that it may be doing two jobs that come apart: the negative Lucas–Penrose-style one, where a mind outruns any formal system because it can see the truth of what the system can't prove, and the constructive one, where self-reference of a certain richness just is what a self is. As far as I can tell the book itself doesn't want the first argument — the author is at pains to say the I is a kind of mirage and that nothing in principle bars a machine from having one — so I'm mainly interested in the second. Correct me if I've got that backwards.

So: audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the formal system, of the arithmetization that lets the system talk about its own syntax, of the derivability predicate, of the self-referential sentence itself, of the consistency or soundness assumption, of the fact that the sentence's truth is established from outside the system, and of the levels that get tangled. Then the places the mapping breaks.

A few things bother me and I want them actually dealt with rather than gestured at:

- The sentence is a static string. It doesn't do anything: no dynamics, no causal role in the system, no temporal thickness. A mind is a process. Is that a real disanalogy for this use, or does the analogy only ever need the structural fact?
- Fixed points are cheap. Quines, the Y combinator, a camera pointed at its own monitor, a compiler compiling its own source. None of those look even faintly self-aware. So the existence of a self-referential loop can't be the whole story — but what would a defender say the extra ingredient is, and is that extra ingredient coming from the formal side at all, or is it smuggled in from somewhere else?
- The actual result is a limitative one about derivability in a consistent system. Taken literally, the mapping seems to make the degenerate case the best case: an inconsistent system derives everything, so its loops are everywhere. And it seems to predict that the more a system talks about itself, the more self-aware it is, which strikes me as exactly backwards for minds. Maybe I'm reading the mapping wrong; tell me if so.
- For my own notes I've started separating what I call loop-existence — the structure is there — from loop-phenomenology — there is thereby something it is like to be the loop. My suspicion is that the entire persuasive force of the analogy is a slide from the first to the second, and that nothing in the formal case bridges them. Is that fair, and if it is, does the defender have a reply?

What I actually want at the end: does my friend's conclusion survive? Not the observation that all analogies have limits — I know that. I mean: with the disanalogies on the table, is anything left of the claim that rich self-representation is all it takes, and is there a surviving version that's still worth saying?

If there's a better analogy, propose it. The one I keep toying with comes from the engineering side: a controller that maintains a running, lossy model of itself, where the model is causally coupled to what it models and being wrong about itself has consequences for the system. Does that do the work better? And does it lose what the Gödelian picture was there to explain — the paradoxical, self-undermining, this-very-sentence quality, the sense that a self is a trick a system plays on itself?
