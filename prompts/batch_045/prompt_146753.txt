Hi — I'm the standards editor at a national newspaper, and I need something practical here, not an essay about the concept.

Short version of the problem: roughly once a month someone on the foreign desk files copy that wants to call something a genocide, and I'm the person who has to say yes or no. I've got about forty minutes, the deputy news editor wants a one-line answer, our in-house lawyer usually glances at it, and whatever I decide has to be defensible in a corrections column six months later. Our house style at the moment is basically "don't use the word until a competent court or the UN does", which I think is useless: courts take the better part of a decade and we're writing today, and half the things we'd want to name will never be litigated at all anyway.

What I've been using instead is my own rough heuristic, which I half-invented and half-absorbed from somewhere: the surrender test. If the violence would have stopped had the victims stopped resisting — handed over the fighters, given up the land, converted, stopped voting the wrong way — then whatever it is, it's conquest or repression or counter-insurgency, and the g-word is the wrong word. If it goes on regardless of anything the victims do, then it's aimed at the group rather than at anything the group is doing, and that's when I let it through. I'm not attached to it. If it's a bad test, say so and replace it — but replace it with something, don't just tell me the question is hard.

So: write me a decision procedure my deputies can actually run on a real case. The person running it has a journalism degree and no law or history training; what they've got is our own reporting, maybe one NGO report, the government's statements, and Wikipedia-level background. I want the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a yes, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the thing over to a human because it's a values question dressed up as a factual one. I also want it to output more than yes or no: give me a short menu of labels it can land on (genocide, crimes against humanity, ethnic cleansing, war crimes, mass atrocity, plain "massacre"), and a rule for how we attribute the label in the sentence as we'd actually print it when the answer is contested — who we say is calling it what.

Three things it has to handle, because they're where I get stuck. One: Lemkin's own idea was broader than the legal definition that got adopted in 1948 — for him destroying a group meant dismantling the things that hold it together, its language, institutions, land, religion, not only killing its members — whereas the Convention turns on intent to destroy a protected group as such, and lists five kinds of act, some of which (deliberately inflicted conditions of life, forcibly transferring children) aren't killing at all. I need the procedure to say which of those two it's actually applying, and to be honest that they come apart. Two: "in part". How large a part of a group has to be in the frame before it counts — courts have read it as needing to be a substantial part, but "substantial" is doing a lot of work there, and I want a rule of thumb with a number or a structure in it, not a shrug. Three: the evidence is always thin and one-sided. What's the default when the intent evidence is missing but the pattern is striking, or when the only documents are the government's own and they deny everything? Presume, withhold, or split the difference in the wording? Pick one and own it.

It also has to be honest that the word isn't a neutral description. It's thought to trigger duties to prevent and punish, it enrages allies and gets us accused of taking sides, it can matter enormously to survivors to hear it said out loud, and it's the first word everyone reaches for, which is exactly how it gets worn out. Don't let the procedure pretend those aren't there, and don't let it decide the question on the basis of them either — I want to know where they belong in it.

Three composites to run it on. I've deliberately made them not-real so nobody sues us, but they're close to things we've covered:

1. Over about four years a government destroys the irrigation, grain stores and clinics of a lowland region whose population is an ethnic minority; there's a famine and tens of thousands die of starvation and disease. Leaked memoirs and a budget document describe the region's population as a "demographic problem" that needs reducing; the government says it's counter-insurgency, and a guerrilla movement does recruit there. Men from the minority who leave aren't stopped.

2. For thirty years a state runs a programme of removing children of a highland people from their families and placing them with majority-language families, bans the language in schools, dissolves the group's communal land tenure, and resettles the adults into townships. Nobody is killed. Births continue. As a distinct people, two generations in, it's essentially finished.

3. A government bombs a rebel-held province for two years to retake it. Sixty-odd thousand people die, mostly civilians, and most of them belong to one ethnic group because that's who lives there. Targets seem to have been chosen by who controls the ground rather than by who lives on it; loyalists of the same group were killed too; there's plenty of ugly rhetoric from officers but no document showing a plan about the group. The death toll is many times case 1.

I've a feeling the procedure is going to give me an answer on number 3 that I won't like and our readers will hate, so don't soften it — give it, and then tell me what to do with the fact that it's unwelcome.

Please commit. Don't hand me a menu of defensible options and leave me to choose: pick the threshold, pick the default under uncertainty, say whether the surrender test survives (and if you keep it, say what it's actually for), choose which of the two concepts we're applying and defend the choice, and tell me the cost of each call. Where you think I'll come to regret a choice, say so in as many words.
