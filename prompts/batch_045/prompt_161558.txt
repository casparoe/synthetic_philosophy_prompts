I would like you to write a speech for me, and I want to set the situation out fairly carefully first, because I think what makes it difficult is the awkwardness of the occasion rather than the writing of it.

My father-in-law is being naturalized as an American citizen in three weeks, at seventy-four, after fifty-two years in Ohio. He arrived at twenty-two to work as a tool-and-die maker and stayed forty-four years at the same plant. I have been asked to speak for about six minutes at the reception afterward, to roughly thirty people: his two brothers and a cousin who have flown in from Baden-Württemberg and speak almost no English; his three grown children and their partners; and a dozen or so people from the plant and from the street he has lived on since 1979. English throughout, with his brothers reading along from a translation I will make afterwards.

I have no academic background in this. I read in the evenings, badly, and entirely on my own. But for three or four years I have been chewing on a difference I keep bumping into between the way Germans and Americans seem to picture a decent person, or what I suppose you would call a difference in folk morality. On the one side: a person is decent to the extent that he does the thing that is the thing to do, keeps the rule, keeps the slot, keeps the path cleared, and this is expected of him on ordinary Tuesdays when nothing is at stake, and the smallness of the occasion is not thought to excuse him. On the other side: a person is decent to the extent that he answers to something in himself, and is ready to break a rule, or refuse an order, or stand up in a meeting, when the rule asks something his conscience will not give; and the American story is full of people honoured for exactly that. Each side has a stock of examples in which the other side's ideal produces something ugly, and I have never been able to settle which of the two has the better of it.

Here is why I am raising it with you. He is the same man on both sides of that line, and I want the speech to say how. Two things from his life, and these are the only biographical facts I want used:

First, for thirty-one years he kept a handwritten logbook of every machine setting he changed. After 2004 the plant logged everything digitally and nobody ever read his book again; he kept it up anyway, every day, until he retired. His answer when someone asked him why was that it is simply what you do. Second, around 1990 a foreman told him to sign off on a run of parts he had not measured himself, ahead of a Friday shipment. He refused and was moved off that line for a while. That is all I know about what it cost him, and I would rather you did not invent consequences. He has never mentioned it himself; I heard it from a former coworker.

The thought I have arrived at, and which I would like the speech to carry rather than decorate with, is this. I have started sorting rules into two kinds, and I have not found anyone else making the distinction, so it may be simply wrong. A load-bearing rule is doing a job at the moment you follow it, and its point can be explained to anyone in a sentence. A training-weight rule does nothing in the moment and everything over a lifetime: no one is watching, nothing goes wrong if you skip it, and its job is not to keep the machine running but to keep you running, so that on the day when following a rule will cost you something real, the habit of doing the thing because it is the thing you do is still warm and available to you. On that reading the two pictures are not rivals about what to do on a Tuesday. The rule-habit stocks the account and the conscience spends it; the German picture wins the small cases and the American picture wins the large ones, and they only look like opponents because each is blind to the other's half. My father-in-law's fifty-two years are evidence about that, and I want the speech to end by saying which side his life supports, and why, without implying that the people in the room who live the other way are fools.

What I need from you, then: an eight-hundred-and-fifty-word speech, in spoken English that a machine-shop foreman could follow at a reception, that explains the load-bearing and training-weight distinction in plain words inside its first third, tests it against both failure modes, applies it to those two facts from his life, and closes by taking a side. Constraints that I care about:

Do not resolve the tension with the formula that you follow rules when it does not matter and conscience when it does. I have tried that and it collapses, because the German claim is precisely that the small cases are where the mattering happens.

Do not use the twentieth century, or the phrase following orders, or any German history, as a debating point. His brothers are sitting ten feet away, and whatever I intend it will land on them as an accusation. If the speech needs to acknowledge the dark version of rule-following, it must do it without pointing at a country and without using it as a club.

Do not say he is finally becoming one of us, which is false and insulting to the men who flew here, and do not say he has stayed German at heart, which is wrong for the day.

No names of philosophers, no isms, no citations, and no borrowed anecdotes, studies, or historical examples of any kind. If a sentence in the speech depends on a fact, it should be a fact I have given you, because I do not want to stand up there and have to fact-check myself afterwards.

At most two everyday German words, and nothing in the speech may depend on the audience recognising them.

He hates being made a fuss of. No hero language about him, and nothing that calls anyone else in the room a coward.

After the speech, in a short note addressed to me and not part of it, tell me which single sentence is doing the heaviest work in the argument and where you think that sentence is weakest. I would like to know where I would get pushed if somebody there disagreed with me and said so.
