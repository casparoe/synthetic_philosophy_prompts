hey — I'm putting together a handout for the game theory module I TA for (third-years; they've done normal-form games, best responses, and the usual "cross out the dominated strategies" routine, but rationalizability isn't until next week). I want to open the session on domination by mixed strategies with a poem instead of another definition slide, and I'd like you to write it.

You're a professional philosopher who works on decision and game theory and who also writes real poetry, not verse-shaped greeting cards. Please think carefully: work the game out yourself first — actually check what beats what, and in what order — then brainstorm two or three forms, pick the one that does the most work, and only then start drafting. Answer in American English, please.

Here's the game I made up (row player: Up / Middle / Down; column player: Left / Center / Right; payoffs are (row, column)):

           Left      Center     Right
Up         4, 2      1, 3       0, 5
Middle     0, 5      1, 3       4, 2
Down       1, 0      0, 4       1, 0

Things the poem has to carry, in whatever order serves the verse:

- If you only let a strategy be killed by a single other strategy, nothing moves — all nine profiles survive, zero deletions. The whole thing only starts once you allow a strategy to be dominated by a *mixture*. That's the session's whole point, so it should be in the poem's bones.
- Row's Down is beaten by an even mix of Up and Middle, and by neither of them on its own. This is the bit they always find wierd, and I don't want it asserted — I want it seen. My idea was that the line that gets deleted gets replaced by a line that is literally half of one surviving line and half of the other, spliced or interleaved, with the poem registering at that moment that neither line alone could have done it.
- The second deletion only works because of the first. Center isn't dominated at all while Down is still in play (it does great against Down — 4 to 0); once Down is gone, an even mix of Left and Right beats it. So that same blend should appear earlier in the poem and fail, then come back and work. Iteration has to be structural, not just a word in the poem.
- Where it stops: five of the nine profiles are gone, four survive, and the procedure has nothing left to say — no prediction, no story about which of the four happens. And if you solve what's left, the mixtures that did the destroying turn out to be exactly what both players end up playing, though nothing in the elimination procedure told you that. I'd like the ending to sit with that rather than tidy it up.
- The worry underneath it all: the deletions lean on the thought that rationality can require you to randomize, which is contested — you don't obviously do better for having tossed a coin. No lecture about it; just let the last lines make the reader feel that something was borrowed.

Form: I was picturing a villanelle or a pantoum, something with real repetition, since the returning lines can be the strategies — but if a decaying sestina or something else carries the deletion better, definately use that. Two constraints: the poem should visibly thin out as it goes (eliminated lines stop coming back), and every deletion must be done by a blended line, never by a single surviving one. Somewhere in the 24–40 line range. It should read as a poem to someone who's never seen a payoff matrix and be accurate enough that I could put it on the board next to my own working without a game theorist wincing. Don't put "Nash" or "rationalizability" in the poem — the ideas belong in the structure, not the vocabulary. Skip the prose explanation afterward; a short note on which line is which strategy is enough.
