I'm on the consent-forms working group at my hospital (we report up to the clinical ethics committee, which meets every other week and wants our recommendation before the new consent system goes live in about ten weeks).

We're deciding whether to replace the risk section of our elective knee-replacement packet with picture-only Isotype-style sheets: no digits, no words in the data area, repeated same-size figures for the complications and blocks of area for the expected benefit. The vendor pitches it as straight Neurath — the Vienna Method / Isotype work from the 1920s and 30s (the social and economic museum in Vienna, Gerd Arntz cutting the signs, Marie Neurath doing the transforming), the rule that you show more by adding more identical figures rather than enlarging them, the slogan that words divide and pictures unite. It's tempting: a solid chunk of our surgical patients aren't comfortable reading English and a few aren't literate in any script at all, so the current paragraph of percentages is basically decorative for them.

Two things split us. Our pilot: people recalled the picture version much better a week later, but when we put the two options side by side something like half couldn't say which carried the higher complication rate — the sheets show proportions, not denominators, and we havent found a way to draw this probably won't happen to you, or if you do nothing it gets slowly worse over a year. And the Neurathian case cuts against the proposal too, which is what I keep going back and forth on: he seems to have thought the signs only work as part of sustained visual education, a helping language alongside words and never a replacement for them, not a one-off rebus handed to someone the morning of surgery, and that the transformation should be done by someone trained — whereas here our marketing team would be picking the message and drawing it.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate: adopt, adopt with conditions, or drop it, with a concrete timeline, what we'd need to see to change course, and a fair statement of the strongest argument on whichever side loses.
