I've somehow ended up as the person defending Reflection in my formal epstemology reading group — van Fraassen's principle: your credence now in A, conditional on your later credence in A being x, ought to be x. I keep swatting away the obvious counterexamples and I'm starting to worry that's just because I'm good at swatting, not because the principle is well insulated. So: what would actually count against it?

One thing I've noticed that I want checked, because it changes which objections even get off the ground: the standard "but your future self will be worse informed / will have forgotten stuff" objection looks to me like it doesn't bite at all as stated. If the forgetting is a clean coarsening of the partition, then your future credence just reports which coarse cell you ended up in, and conditioning on that is exactly right — Reflection is satisfied and deference is correct. So the naive information-loss objection is a dud. The cases that do bother me are the ones where the degradation isn't a coarsening:

(i) The leaky-archive case. You're an epidemiologist who knows that tomorrow the per-patient covariates will be gone and you'll be left with a rounded weekly aggregate; you also know that inclusion in the aggregate (follow-up attendance) correlates with the outcome you care about, and you know you won't remember the attendance rate. Your future self will treat the aggregate as if it came from the full sample. So the future credence isn't your current credence conditional on any cell of a partition of your total evidence — it's a systematic distortion of it, in a direction you can name. Learning that your future credence is 0.7 seems like it should move you to something like 0.6, not to 0.7.

(ii) The nudge case: you know you'll get pulled toward the room's consensus by some amount you can't calibrate precisely, so your future credence is post-evidence credence plus noise you can't subtract out.

(iii) Centered / self-locating stuff, where there's no partition of "evidence" in the ordinary sense — an amnesia setup where you'll later be unsure whether it's your first or second awakening, say. And the variant where you'll be duplicated and there are two successors with different credences, so it isn't even clear whose credence is the one you're supposed to reflect on.

What I want from you:

- Which of these have a real chance of being defeaters, sorted fatal → merely awkward, including the ones I haven't thought of (I'm sure I'm missing the good ones).
- The internal line: which of these would someone who has actually defended Reflection-flavoured principles concede as counting against the view, as opposed to dismissing as changing the subject? I want what the defenders would give up, not what the critics say.
- What Reflection predicts that plain conditionalization ("be a Bayesian over time") doesn't. If the honest answer is "nothing, except where conditionalization isn't even defined", then what work is the principle meant to be doing — grounding deference to your future self, making self-manipulation of your own beliefs incoherent (the lash-yourself-to-the-mast-so-your-future-self-can't-act-on-what-he'll-then-believe case), grounding deference to experts? And has it been qualified down to the point where it's either trivially satisfied by any conditionalizer or vacuous? If it has, say plainly whether that's a problem for a principle with those ambitions or whether it's fine.
- The thing that would move me most, personally, and I want you to tell me which of the two is the more serious threat and why: (a) a case where Reflection's verdict is clearly wrong and the Dutch-strategy / sure-loss argument still seems to run — because then the real casualty is the sure-loss style of argument rather than Reflection; or (b) an argument that Reflection is automatically satisfied by anyone who plans to conditionalize, so the literature has been selling a re-description of Bayes.
- And the accuracy angle: as I understand it, for a strictly proper score your future credence is expected-accuracy-improving, so the epistemic-utility framework seems to endorse deference even in (i) and (ii) where my gut says don't. Is that support for Reflection, or a sign that accuracy-based epistemology is the wrong tool for thinking about deference?

Be honest if the conclusion is that the principle is basically insulated and that this is fine — but tell me what it would cost to admit that.
