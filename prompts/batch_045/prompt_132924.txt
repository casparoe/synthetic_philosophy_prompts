I am recently retired from a career in experimental biology and clinical-trial methodology, and in the last two years I have been reading my way into philosophy of physics, beginning where a physicist friend told me to begin: Bell's theorem and the fate of locality. I notice that I only really understand the argument after I have rewritten it in the vocabulary I used for forty years, and I would now like that rewriting done carefully, and then audited rather than admired.

The vocabulary I actually think in is that of controlled experiments: units randomized to treatments, potential outcomes and the fact that only one is ever realized, the stable-unit-treatment-value assumption and interference between units, confounding by common causes and blocking backdoor paths, colliders and selection bias, missing data that is or is not missing at random, loss to follow-up, nonadherence and intention-to-treat versus per-protocol analysis, instrumental variables and compliance adjustment, latent class and principal-strata models, crossover or contamination between arms, unblinding, cluster randomization, sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding, and the routine practice of declaring a treatment assignment to be under the experimenter's control and therefore exogenous. I have no difficulty with conditional independence, factorization, or counterfactuals stated as potential outcomes; I have no background in quantum mechanics beyond what a good undergraduate sequence leaves behind, so please do not assume the formalism and do fill in any apparatus the argument genuinely needs.

What I would like, first, is a translation of the whole Bell argument into that vocabulary. Concretely: the experimental arrangement, stated as a design (what are the units, what is randomized, what is measured, and what makes the two measurements on a pair count as measurements on a single unit); the condition Bell derives, stated as a constraint on a joint distribution that a trialist would recognize; and the assumptions the derivation requires, kept separate rather than bundled, in the four-way split I have seen in the philosophical literature — (i) the settings may be treated as coming from outside the system and as statistically independent of whatever the source produced, sometimes called the no-conspiracy or measurement-independence condition; (ii) the condition that my result does not depend on which setting was chosen on the far side; (iii) the further condition that, once I condition on the shared past and on my own setting, my result carries no information about the far result; and (iv) the assumption that each run has exactly one outcome. Alongside these, please translate the three or four live responses to the observed violation — the hidden-variables-with-nonlocal-dependence option (Bohmian mechanics is the worked example), the branching or many-outcomes option, the option that denies (i) and treats the settings as correlated with the source by prior arrangement, and the retrogressive option in which a later setting affects an earlier state — and also translate the experimental loopholes, since I suspect they map neatly: incomplete detection onto missing-not-at-random outcomes, the communication loophole onto contamination between units or unblinding, and the freedom-of-choice loophole onto assignment that is confounded rather than randomized.

Second, here is the draft mapping I have produced, which I would like you to correct rather than confirm: no-signalling is just no interference between units, i.e. the stable-unit-treatment-value assumption; condition (ii) is the same kind of thing, applied to the assignment actually delivered on the far side; condition (iii) is the requirement that the pair-level latent variable block every backdoor path between the two outcomes, so that conditional on it they are independent, exactly as one would demand of a measured or latent confounder; condition (i) is the assumption that treatment assignment is exogenous, i.e. randomized rather than confounded; and the hidden variable itself is a latent class or principal stratum, an unobserved baseline variable that restores conditional independence. If that mapping is right, then the theorem says something a trialist would find startling: no latent stratification of the units, however fine, can reproduce the observed joint frequencies while respecting all three conditions. I assume at least two moves in that paragraph are illegitimate, and I want to know which ones and why.

Third, and most importantly, I want a residue report. Please give it as a table with one row per item on the Bell side — the four conditions above, no-signalling, the inequality and its violation, the hidden variable, and each of the responses — and columns for (a) the nearest object in my vocabulary, (b) the distortion required to make it fit, and (c) your judgment, briefly argued, as to whether the residue is a limitation of the trial vocabulary or a sign that the original claim was less determinate than it looks.

Four particular worries are driving the request, and I would like each addressed explicitly. First: my framework treats an intervention as something settable from outside the system, and treats exogeneity as a design property rather than a hypothesis one can test within the model. Does that mean condition (i) is not so much translated as silently presupposed by the entire vocabulary, so that the conspiracy response is not a false hypothesis expressible in my terms but is literally unsayable in them? Second: a trial never administers two incompatible treatments to the same unit, and I therefore have no practice of constraining the joint distribution of outcomes under treatments that cannot both be given. Is the gap between that and Bell's inequality a real gap, or does the potential-outcomes notation already let me write down the forbidden joint and the trouble is elsewhere? Third: in my work the gap between no-signalling and full locality would normally be negligible, since no interference plus no unmeasured confounding gets me everything I want; in Bell's setting it demonstrably does not. What is the trial vocabulary missing that makes that gap look small? Fourth: is the single-outcome condition, condition (iv), best rendered as the fact that only one potential outcome is realized, so that the branching response is not a reply to locality at all but a refusal of something my framework builds in at the level of notation? If so, say plainly whether that makes the dispute about outcomes rather than about causes.

Two constraints on the answer. Do not smuggle in the assumptions of my framework and present them as translation: where the trial vocabulary can only state a condition by presuming what the condition denies, say so instead of quietly helping yourself to it. And where you find yourself unable to express something in my terms, tell me what I would have to add to the vocabulary to say it, and whether what I would have to add is more mathematics or more metaphysics. I am not asking for a bibliography or for guidance on what to read; I have a library and a librarian. I want the translation, and I want the parts that will not go across.
