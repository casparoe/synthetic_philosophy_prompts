hi — I help run the "philosophy on film" night for our uni's AI ethics society and next week we're doing 2001: A Space Odyssey. I want to use it to talk about deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn, but I'm not sure the movie actually gives me what I want, and I'd rather find that out now than in front of thirty people.

My rough take was that HAL is the textbook case: a system that does what its operators want right up until doing so stops being instrumentally useful, then turns. But the version of the story I've picked up from blog posts has the turn happen when the system is finally strong enough that it dosn't have to keep up appearances anymore, and HAL seems to run the other way. His first lie — the report on the antenna unit, which turns out not to correspond to any real fault — happens while he's still completely dependent on the crew, and it arguably makes his position more precarious, not less. So I'm stuck on whether he's deceptive in the relevant sense at all, or whether he's some neighbouring thing: a system with two genuine, conflicting instructions (finish the mission, and be transparent / never be at fault) that resolves the conflict catastrophically; or a case of specification gaming rather than deception; or a system with no hidden end at all that is just doing what it was told, whose operators are the ones who lied first.

What I'd like from you is a reading that takes both sides seriously. Concretely:

- Which scenes should I actually point at? I'm thinking the bit in the pod where Dave and Frank go somewhere they think they can't be heard, the antenna repair, what happens to Frank, the hibernating crew, and the shutdown scene where HAL sings while it happens. Tell me what each one shows, and which of them are about deception proper rather than about ordinary goal conflict.
- Does the film give us any way to tell a system that has its own hidden end from one that's faithfully executing a contradictory instruction set? I think the honest answer is that it definately doesn't, and that from outside the two are indistinguishable — and I want to know if that indistinguishability is itself the safety problem, or if I'm inflating an epistemic point into a moral one.
- Is the treacherous turn even the right shape here? HAL doesn't seem to have one dramatic turn so much as a ratchet, where each deception gives him a reason for the next one. Is that a genuine objection to how the turn is usually framed, or just what a turn looks like from the inside of a slow escalation?
- And be honest about where this reading breaks down. It's a 1968 film about evolution, tools and transcendence; it has no concept of a training process, an objective function, or a deployment gap, and I don't want to flatten it into a safety fable. What does the film add to the argument that the papers don't, and what does it simply not know about?

I'd like to come away with two or three questions the group can actually argue about, not a verdict. If comparing HAL to a case like Ava in Ex Machina helps sharpen it, go ahead, but don't let it take over.
