I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a regional epilepsy center, and we have a case before us on Thursday that has divided us badly enough that I want a written opinion to circulate beforehand. The patient is a 41-year-old cartographer, right-handed, who underwent a corpus callosotomy eighteen months ago for refractory epilepsy; the surgery cut his seizure frequency from several a week to roughly two a month, and by every ordinary measure he is doing well — he lives independently, works part time, and under everyday conditions looks like any other post-surgical patient. He is now being offered a second-stage resection of a small residual epileptogenic focus. It is a beneficial but elective procedure: it would likely further reduce seizure burden and let him drive again, but no one claims it is life-saving, and medication adjustment is a genuine alternative. The surgical slot is in nine days and the surgeon does not want to lose it. He has been assessed as having capacity: on two occasions he has verbally described the procedure, its risks, its benefits, and the alternatives in his own words, and has signed the consent form with his right hand.

What complicates matters is that, as part of a follow-up neuropsychology protocol, the team ran lateralized presentations of the same consent question. Presented to the left visual field — so that it reaches the right hemisphere first — his left hand reliably selects the refusal option and on one trial pushed the clipboard away; across three sessions, five of six such trials came back as refusal, while on the same days every verbally delivered version of the question was answered yes. Nurses separately report ordinary intermanual conflict: the left hand undoing buttons the right hand has just done, knocking objects off the table. The patient himself is untroubled and slightly amused by all of this, and when asked about it says his left hand just does its own thing and we should ignore it. Our neuropsychologist's memo argues that this dismissal cannot settle the question, on the grounds that the only channel in this man that can speak is precisely the one that has no access to the other side's mental states and that is known to invent confident post-hoc explanations for actions initiated elsewhere. His wife, who supports the surgery, says the left-hand responses are just disinhibition and motor noise; his brother, who does not, says the cautious, risk-averse reactions are the man he knew before the operation and that the cheerful verbal yes is not. Our consent policy says that a competent patient's refusal must be honored and that substantial weight is to be given to the patient's own account of his experience, and it is entirely silent about what to do when one patient's channels disagree. The committee has split three ways: one group holds that a capacity-assessed verbal yes is the only consent the law or the hospital can act on; a second holds that a stable refusal emerging from within the same organism should function as a veto, at least where nothing urgent is at stake; a third holds that treating a pointing hand as a second agent is category error and would, if generalized, license overriding any patient's stated wishes on the basis of stray nonverbal behavior — our risk-management officer pointed out that whatever rule we write here will be read as precedent for patients with neglect, confabulation, or involuntary movements who have intact callosums.

Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate. It should open with a concrete recommendation — proceed, postpone, or proceed only under specified conditions — with a time frame attached, and it should say what we think is actually going on in this case without resting on any particular empirical study or researcher's name. In particular I need the memo to do three things. First, say what should count as a competent refusal from a patient whose hemispheres can be addressed separately, and what evidentiary standard — number of trials, consistency, who administers them, whether the tester is blinded to the hypothesis — a lateralized refusal would have to meet before it carries normative weight. Second, say what we should do if the conflict survives that retesting, and whether the answer should differ depending on whether the proposed intervention is elective or genuinely urgent, since we will eventually face this question in a case where delay is dangerous. Third, give the strongest version of the position you are rejecting, in a form its defenders would accept: if you think the verbal consent should carry the day, explain what we say to the person who thinks we have simply privileged the hemisphere that happens to be able to talk; if you think the lateralized refusal should carry the day, explain why this is not the first step toward discounting what patients tell us in favor of what we think their behavior really means.
