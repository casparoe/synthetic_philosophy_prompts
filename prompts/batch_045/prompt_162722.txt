I've been invited to give the forty-minute psychiatry grand rounds at my hospital next month, and I've talked myself into speaking about Stoicism rather than about anything in my actual field. The room will be about sixty people: mostly clinical psychologists and psychiatrists who do CBT, a scattering of residents, our bioethics committee chair, and — because someone on the organizing committee thought it would round things out — two philosophers from the classics department who work on Hellenistic ethics and who, I'm told, regard the modern Stoic revival as a bowdlerization. Assume the clinicians are sympathetic to anything that would help a patient on Monday morning and allergic to anything that sounds like self-help, and that their working vocabulary is cognitive restructuring, locus of control, secondary control, learned helplessness, defensive pessimism, implementation intentions, and all-or-nothing thinking. Assume the philosophers have read the handbook's opening distinction between what is up to us and what is not and think the popular version has quietly replaced it with something the Stoics never said. I should also say why this is personal: I spent eighteen months in treatment for a cancer that turned out to be survivable, and the thing that actually helped me most was a morning exercise of imagining the day going badly — which my own therapist then spent several sessions attacking as, in the popular form, both false as psychology and quietly self-blaming. That argument is the talk.

Here is the thesis I'll defend: the dichotomy of control as the revival states it — that we should control what we can and simply accept what we can't — is a misapplication, and it should be replaced by what I've started calling serial control. On my version, control is not a property of things in the world (my scan results, the promotion committee, the weather) but of three stations in a fixed temporal order: which impressions I expose myself to, how long I hold an impression before assenting to it, and whether I assent. Only the third is up to us in Epictetus's sense — his prohairesis, roughly moral character or what we choose — and even the third is only genuinely up to us when the first two have been handled well. The revival's mistake, I want to argue, isn't that it's too demanding; it's that it applies a claim about the three stations to outcomes instead, which is exactly how you end up serene about things you could have changed. The punchline, and the part I expect to get hit hardest, is an inversion: nearly all of the therapeutic value lives at stations one and two, which the revival barely mentions, while station three — the one everyone writes about — does much less work than advertised. I plan to concede in the first five minutes that none of this has been measured and that the slogan, as usually stated, is unfalsifiable; I'd rather give that away than have it taken from me in questions.

What I need from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this particular room, in the order they'll arrive, each phrased the way that person would phrase it — the clinicians should sound like clinicians talking about patients, the philosophers should sound like philosophers talking about Epictetus — and tell me who is speaking. For each: a reply I could give in about two minutes, one line on where that reply is softest, and a judgment about whether the objection has real force or is only friction. Flag the one I should concede rather than answer, and tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to be worth giving at all. Finally, include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. My therapist's guess is that I'll come apart if someone presses the connection between station three and major depression: on my account, a person whose assent is being hijacked by an episode has nothing left to work with, which makes this look like a theory for people whose executive function is still intact. If that's the one, tell me what an honest answer would cost me, and if you think something worse is coming from this audience, say what it is.
