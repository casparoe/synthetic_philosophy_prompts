So I've ended up defending a specific reading of anatta in my grad seminar, and I want it stress-tested before I commit to it in print. Roughly: I don't read the no-self doctrine as the metaphysical claim that there are no persons. I read it as a claim about ownership — no item in the causal stream that makes up a life is related to the rest of that stream by a primitive 'owned by' relation; ownership is always something the stream does to itself, a bit of bookkeeping layered on top of dependent arising. Persons survive as conventional posits, but only in the way a university or a nation survives: real causal structure underneath, no further unifying fact over and above it. And here's the bit I've added myself, which is where I think I'm most exposed: I claim that prudential concern across time — caring about your own future, being answerable for what you did last year, and, on the Buddhist side, karma — doesn't need identity at all. What it needs is what I've started calling delegation: an earlier stage of the stream adopts a standing policy toward later stages, and the normative force lives entirely in the policy and in the causal ties that make it effective, not in any fact about who the later stage is. That, I think, is how the tradition keeps ethics and liberation while giving up the self.

What I want from you is the defeater map, and I'd like it ranked, fatal at the top and merely embarrasing at the bottom. Skip the moves I can already write for myself and that I take to be question-begging: I'm not interested in being told the view is just Parfit again, or that it's eliminativism in cosmetic disguise, unless you can tell me which one it collapses into and why that's a cost rather than a feature. I've also quietly dropped rebirth from the picture, and I want to know whether my reading secretly leans on it.

The things I actually lose sleep over: (a) the phenomenal side — experience does, much of the time, present itself as mine, and there's a whole contemporary literature on the 'minimal' versus 'narrative' sense of self and on what happens in depersonalization; does any of that land a real hit, or does the doctrine just absorb it? (b) a dilemma: either my conventional person tracks enough real causal structure that the view collapses into garden-variety psychological continuity reductionism, in which case anatta hasn't said anything distinctive, or it doesn't, in which case delegation is trying to conjure normative force out of a fiction. (c) Whether delegation is a substantive claim at all or just a redescription of the very thing that was in dispute. (d) The old personalist line of attack — the schools that wanted a person neither identical to nor distinct from the aggregates, and pressed the who-questions (who remembers, who is liberated, who inherits the karma); I take those seriously and I want to know how much of my position they actually threaten. (e) Whether the whole thing has insulated itself: if the doctrine predicts in advance that the sense of self will feel compelling and inescapable no matter what, is there anything left that could count against it? And if there isn't, is that a problem for a view that's partly soteriological rather than purely theoretical, or is it fine?

For each of these, tell me whether someone defending my reading would concede it as counting against them, or dismiss it as resting on a mistake about what the doctrine is for. I'll tell you what I'd personally find most persuasive so you can weight things: I'm moved much more by cases where the view has to say something flatly wrong about first-personal experience — agency, anticipation, the sense of being the one who acts — than by intuition pumps about fission and teleportation, which seem to me to presuppose that there's a further fact to be had. If you think the position is well insulated and thats acceptable, argue for it; if you think it's insulated and that's fatal, say that instead.
