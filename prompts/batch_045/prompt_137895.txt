I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar on normative ethics — 16 students, mostly philosophy majors with a handful of economics and public-policy students mixed in, a room with movable chairs and one whiteboard. We have spent two weeks on what to do when you are unsure which moral theory is true, and the discussion keeps sliding into abstraction: everyone agrees that you should somehow "weight" theories by how confident you are in them, and then they argue about the metaphysics of the weights instead of about anything they can see. So I want a game. The setting is a funding committee that must allocate a budget across two rounds of decisions, and each student gets a private card, assigned rather than chosen, that states a confidence distribution over three moral views and each view's verdict on the options on the table. The card is theirs alone; they may reveal it, misreport it, or keep it hidden. What I most want them to feel is not that uncertainty is hard but that aggregation is a substantive moral act: the three views on the cards should not speak in the same idiom. One should give unbounded cardinal values, one should give only a ranking plus a satisficing threshold below which an option is simply unacceptable, and one should give nothing but binary permitted/forbidden verdicts. Because they have to reach a group decision by majority vote, and because the scoring has to be in a single currency of points, someone in the room will have to invent a rule for putting those three idioms on one scale, and that invented rule — not the confidence weights — will turn out to decide the outcome. I would rather they discover this by watching their own arithmetic than hear it from me.

The mechanics I have in mind, which you should feel free to improve or replace if you can do better: two decisions in sequence, where the options available in the second decision depend on what was chosen in the first; a single random draw at the very end that selects which of the three views was the true one for scoring purposes, with each student's individual score being the point value their committee's choices had according to that view, summed over both rounds. Please choose the numbers so that the option with the highest expected score in round one is not the one that opens the better path, so that a group that maximizes round by round visibly does worse than a group that plans. If it fits, add a beat between the rounds where students may make a public pledge to vote for someone else's preferred theory in round two in exchange for a reciprocal pledge, and build in some reason to break that pledge when the second vote actually comes. Stakes should be points plus one genuinely real thing: the committee's final choice determines which of two local causes gets a modest donation from me, and I would like you to say why that is worth doing and what I should do if a student objects to being experimented on. No lecture up front, and no one should hear phrases like expected choice-worthiness, intertheoretic comparison, myopic versus non-myopic, or moral hedging until after the points are counted. Tell me what to put on the cards, how to run the two rounds minute by minute, and how to score without a spreadsheet.

The debrief is where I need the most help. Map at least three specific moments in play onto the three problems I named above, and say which moment each student will actually remember. Then give me the honest failure modes: I expect students to read the closing draw as though moral uncertainty were a coin flip, to spend the whole time bargaining strategically over the vote rather than reasoning about the case, to let the unbounded view swallow the other two no matter how the scale is set, and to get lost in arithmetic. Tell me what to do in each case, including how to handle the student who says the whole setup presupposes that there is a fact of the matter about which theory is correct. Finally, be frank about what this kind of game cannot show: I assume it cannot reproduce the fact that confidence in a moral theory is not a well-defined probability, and that it cannot raise the question of whether one ought to aggregate across theories at all rather than simply acting on one's own best judgment. Say clearly which parts of the view only I can supply in words, and where the activity will actively mislead if I do not correct it.
