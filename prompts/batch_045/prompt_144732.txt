I'm on the technology comittee at the worker co-op where I've worked for eleven years. Food plant, about 340 people, partly worker-owned, and we have a no-layoff clause in our contract with the parent company. Management has to show us automation proposals before they buy anything. We meet monthly, we've had six proposals this year, and realistically we get two weeks and about half a day of a shop steward's time per proposal. We can only recommend — the worker assembly votes, and management can ignore us.

Here's what I keep chewing on. Reading Marx on machinery — the chapter on machinery in Capital, and the machines section of the Grundrisse — there's this stubborn distinction between the machine considered technically and the machine as it exists under capital, and the claim that what's wrong with it is the use rather than the thing itself. Then there seem to be three separate ways it goes wrong: it destroys jobs, it intensifies work (the machine sets the tempo and the worker becomes its appendage), and it breaks a skilled job into fragments you can staff with cheaper, less trained people. And something about the working day: the time a machine frees up doesn't stay free, it gets taken back as shorter hours, or as more output for the same hours, or as fewer of us.

Can you turn that into something my comittee can actually run? Questions in order, what counts as a real answer to each one — vendors won't tell us hours saved, only "productivity uplift" — where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to stop and say this is a values call dressed up as a fact. Also a rule for when we just can't get a number at all.

Then run it on three: (1) a palletizing robot that takes four of twelve warehouse jobs; (2) a dispatch and scheduling system that cuts nobody but tracks every stop and takes routing decisions away from the drivers; (3) a machine that would eliminate the worst job in the plant, the hot-end line that's wrecked people's backs, and the older members on it want it gone.

I have a feeling that on (3) your procedure is going to tell me somthing I don't want to hear — Marx apparently says somewhere that co-operative factories end up reproducing all the defects of the existing system, which is not encouraging — so say it anyway. And tell me what the procedure can't do: we can't veto, we can't see effects beyond our own plant, and sometimes our own members are the ones pushing for the thing.
