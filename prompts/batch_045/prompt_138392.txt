I run a seminar section attached to a course on philosophy of language, and we recently spent a session on default assumptions in ordinary vocabulary: Hofstadter's early-1980s satire on word reform, the one that hands the complaint about person-substitutions (chairperson, personhole cover and the rest) to a narrator who cannot hear why anyone is upset, and that trusts the reader to notice that the narrator's outrage is only intelligible from inside an unmarked position he takes to be simply how things are. My students liked it, but two of them made objections I can't dismiss: first, that satire of this kind only lands on readers who already agree, since the joke is just the reader's prior conviction wearing a costume; second, that the piece never actually says what it thinks language reform is *for* — whether it is about changing speech, changing thought, or merely about making the previously unmarked group pay a small cost in attention. So I want to run the experiment rather than argue about it: I'd like you to write a short story, roughly 900 to 1,400 words, that does the same kind of work on a default nobody has mobilised against yet.

Set it in the city of Meridian, where a municipal Commission on Chronological Equity has just circulated the third edition of a proposed Chrononeutral Usage Manual. In this world a large and organised minority — call them the Second Watch — are nocturnally active by disposition and by culture, and the Commission's proposed rule is that the generic day be replaced by person wherever the referent is not specifically solar, with night retained only where a contrast is genuinely required: so personlight, personbreak, persondream, personcare, and, at the far edge of the rule, someperson and holiperson. Your narrator is Marley Dane, the longtime copy chief of the Meridian Ledger, writing an aggrieved open letter to his own paper's letters page; his colleague Bram, who has run the night desk for twenty years and thinks the whole campaign is theatre, should be given the best available version of the sceptical case rather than a strawman — including the point that a shared default is a coordination device and that de-marking a term imposes a small cost on every speaker for a symbolic gain. I want a few specific things to happen rather than a string of jokes. The neutral substitute should visibly fail to be neutral: person carries its own meaning, so the reformed words keep pointing at the category they were supposed to dissolve, and at least one of them should be unpronounceable in good faith. Somewhere the mechanical substitution rule should break on words whose daylight etymology is dead — nobody hears someday or day one as being about the sun — and the story should let that be a real problem for the reformers rather than a punchline at their expense. And I want the turn at the end: Marley is himself of the Second Watch, has been passing as diurnal for most of his career, and is writing the letter at three in the morning in idioms that give him away; he is not a villain, he is someone who has made his peace with a default that costs him almost nothing personally, and whose letter is nonetheless the strongest evidence the Commission could want.

Two further constraints, both of which I care about more than the comedy. First, the analogy to gendered and racialised generics should be allowed to fail somewhere, and the story should register the failure: the diurnal default has a naturalistic rationale that the male generic arguably lacks — most people really are awake in daylight, and the sun is a genuine coordination point — so a reader can grant everything the satire shows and still think this default is justified in a way that one is not. Let that tension surface, ideally through Bram, and let it stay unresolved; I would rather the story indict its own premise than pretend the mapping is clean. Second, I do not want an appended explanation: no afterword, no footnotes, no characters stepping out to say what it all means. If the piece has a thesis about whether reforming words can do anything that changing conduct cannot, it should be dramatised — in what Marley loses by the last line, or in what the Commission's rule actually changes and what it leaves untouched. You may add at most two sentences after the story's end, in your own voice, saying what you were claiming; if the story does the work, say nothing.
