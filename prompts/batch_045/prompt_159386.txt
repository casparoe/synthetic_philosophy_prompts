I'm a high school senior doing an independent project on combinatorial games, and I want to test my grip on one particular step of the strategy-stealing argument rather than on the argument as a whole. As I currently understand it: suppose that in some game an extra move of your own can never make your position worse. Then the second player cannot have a winning strategy, because the first player could open with some throwaway move, then ignore that move for the rest of the game and follow whatever strategy was supposed to win for the second player, treating the spare move as a bonus that does no harm. In a game with no possible draw, this is supposed to hand the first player a win.

What I would like from you is an example that puts pressure on the opening premise — the claim that a spare move of your own is never a liability. One thing I read in passing suggested that in some games the throwaway move has to be chosen carefully rather than picked arbitrarily, which is what made me suspicious. So: please describe a game with rules simple enough that a friend and I could play it out on paper in a few minutes — a small board, or a handful of counters — in which making an extra move of your own can genuinely make your position worse, so that the argument above does not even get started. Please don't reach for the case everyone reaches for, where the game can simply end in a draw and the conclusion is only weakened to at least a draw. I understand that case, and it is a different failure: there the premise holds and the conclusion is merely weaker. I want the case where the spare move itself is the problem.

Then I'd like the example used to sharpen the diagnosis, in two ways.

First: exactly what goes wrong? Is it that the spare move removes options you would have wanted later; that it blocks your own pieces or your own future plans; that it changes what the opponent is able to do; that it hands the opponent information; that it forces you to commit to something you'd rather leave open? I'd like you to say which of these your example turns on, and ideally to give me the short line of play I could set up on the board to watch the failure happen, rather than just being told that it happens.

Second: suppose we changed the rules so that a player may pass — make no move at all, let the opponent move again, with the pass leaving nothing behind on the board. It seems to me that this repairs the argument, since a pass costs nothing and leaves no trace, so the first player could pass and then adopt the second player's supposed winning strategy. If that is right, then the real premise was never an extra move never hurts, but something closer to there is some available action that leaves the position otherwise unchanged — and the failure in your example is really about the rules forcing you to move. But if passing does not rescue the argument, or rescues it in some games and not others, I want to know why. Please also be explicit about what adding a pass does to the game itself: I realize it may turn the original game into a different game, and I want to know whether that matters for the conclusion, or whether it is just a harmless technicality.

If you can, I'd also like one example from outside board games — an election, a debate, a negotiation, two firms competing — where the same do what the other side would have done, plus one free extra action move is supposedly available to the first mover, and where it fails for one of the same reasons. I care less about whether the real-world case is realistic than about whether it isolates the same mechanism cleanly.

One constraint on the answer: please keep it self-contained. I don't want to look anything up, and I don't want references or suggested reading. If your example is a game that already has a name and a literature, that's fine, but state the rules in full and assume I know nothing about it beyond what you tell me.
