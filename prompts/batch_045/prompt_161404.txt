I'm part of a reading group attached to a public-law course, and six of us have spent a term arguing about prostitution policy without getting anywhere. Two of the group defend what is usually called the Nordic or end-demand model — criminalize the purchase of sex, decriminalize its sale, keep third-party profiteering illegal — on the grounds that buying sex is a form of gendered domination, that demand is what sustains both the trade and the trafficking that feeds it, and that the criminal law has an expressive job to do alongside any deterrent one. Two others defend New Zealand-style decriminalization — remove the prostitution-specific offences and let sellers fall back on ordinary contract, employment, and workplace health-and-safety law, with no penalty on either side of a transaction — on the grounds that much of the measurable harm to sellers comes from criminalization itself, from policing, and from the bargaining power that managers and clients hold when sellers cannot organize, screen, or call on the state. The rest of us keep pointing out that the German and Dutch regulatory regimes are a third thing again, and that both camps invoke them selectively. What our sessions actually consist of is talking past each other: one side cites shrinking street markets, the other cites the conditions of migrant and street-based sellers, and neither side's evidence ever touches the other side's criterion of success.

So I would like you to draft the document this group should have produced: an adversarial-collaboration summary between a Nordic-model advocate and a full-decriminalization advocate. Give me, first, a numbered section of claims both could sign — about what the law can and cannot do, about who is in the trade and why, about which parts of the current harm are caused by criminalization and which are not, and about what would count as a better or worse outcome for the people actually selling. Then give me a disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated twice, once in wording the Nordic-model advocate would accept for her own view and once in wording the decriminalization advocate would accept for his, with a column marking whether that disagreement is empirical, normative, or merely verbal. Then give me the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and, for each, the case or argument both would accept as bearing on it. If you conclude that some part of our dispute is about words rather than the world, say which part and why, and say plainly whether there is any evidence both sides would accept as settling a crux.

Here is the case I want used as the shared test. A mid-sized European city is considering a package that: repeals soliciting and third-party offences; licenses small worker-run collectives of up to six sellers, from which no outside manager may take more than a fixed share of earnings; retains a criminal penalty on buying sex, but only from sellers who are not in a licensed collective; and funds a well-resourced support service that a seller may use whether or not she intends to leave the trade. For each crux, tell me what each side would predict about this scheme — about street-based sellers and about undocumented migrant sellers, about prices, about whether violence gets reported to the police, about the size of the market — and what mechanism each prediction rests on. I am not asking for citations or study names; both camps have thrown numbers at each other all term and I trust none of them, so give me the mechanism-level reasoning instead. Finally, add a short metanote that separates what is settled among people who actually work on this from what is genuinely contested, flagging any agreed claim you wrote that is really an unsettled empirical matter rather than a consensus, and marking any clause you suspect one of the two camps would refuse to sign.
