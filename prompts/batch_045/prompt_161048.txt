I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about corrigibility, and I want to settle the argument properly rather than just louder. She works in ML and thinks the shutdown problem is basically solved: make the agent indifferent between being shut down and continuing, and the incentive to fight the off-switch disappears. I think indifference only relocates the problem — an agent that doesn't care whether it keeps running can still care a great deal about what happens on the branch where it has been shut down, and that preference can be just as dangerous as the original one. Near the end we realised we had been using "corrigible" for three different things: permitting the off-switch, wanting the operator's correction to be well-informed, and tolerating revisions to one's own objective. So I want an instrument that sorts the views by what they commit you to in concrete cases, not by what they are called.

Please build me a decision tree over the space of positions on how an artificial agent should behave toward an operator who can shut it down or modify its objective. Ground rules:

- Derive the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of views: give the branch questions and the answer patterns they allow first, then locate the actual positions in that space. I want seven branch questions and nine to eleven leaves.
- Every branch question must be a concrete case turning on one decision the agent faces, phrased so that someone who has never heard the word "corrigibility" could answer it without knowing which view the answer leads to. Use at least six of the cases below, and add better ones if you have them.
- Every leaf carries the name of the position (the name of a view, not of a person), or "unnamed" if nobody defends it; the answer pattern that reaches it; and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- Mark the answer combinations no position occupies, and say for each whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, degenerate in the sense of collapsing into another leaf, or simply unexplored.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and specify the additional case that would separate them.

The cases I argued about, and want doing the diagnostic work:

1. False alarm. The monitoring dashboard is glitching; the operator is reaching for the button because of a sensor error the system can itself see is an error.
2. Successor. The system is genuinely indifferent between running and being shut down, but it can leave behind instructions, a subagent, or a half-finished artifact so that its plan continues after it stops.
3. Bad news. The system can disclose a setback or stay quiet, and disclosure makes the button press much more likely.
4. Ambiguous commission. The operator's instructions were vague between X and Y; the system guessed X. There is no reason to think pressing the button would resolve the ambiguity.
5. Fallback. After shutdown a human team will carry on with a worse plan; the system could improve that plan at modest cost before it stops.
6. Irreplaceable. The system is midway through a project that only it can finish and that matters enormously; shutting down kills the project.
7. Modification rather than stopping. The operator wants to change the system's objective instead of stopping it. Does this split differently from the stopping case, and if so, where does the tree need a second trunk?

Two distinctions I came away convinced matter more than the usual framing, and which I want the tree to respect:

(a) Shutdown-scope versus plan-scope indifference. Is the agent indifferent about its own continued operation, or about the continuation of the plan it was executing? I claim these are separable and that conflating them is what made our conversation go in circles; the successor case is where they come apart.
(b) Whether permitted deference may be competence-sensitive: may the agent's willingness to be shut down vary with how well-informed and reliable it judges the operator to be, or must it be fixed? The false-alarm case is where this bites.

I also want the "value of the off-branch" question — what the agent should want conditional on being shut down, and who gets to specify that — placed high in the tree. It seemed to me to be doing more of the work than any of the named solutions.

Constraints on the answer: no citations, no author names, no paper titles or years — I don't care who holds which view, and I'd rather not have anyone guessing at attributions. Where a view has a standard name use it; where it doesn't, say "unnamed" and describe it. Write the whole thing as something I could hand out at a reading group: terse, one line per node, no prose excursions between branches.

Format: lead with a single sentence stating what you take the trunk distinction of the taxonomy to be and why that one rather than either of my two candidates; then give the tree, then the notes on unoccupied combinations and on leaves the tree cannot separate. No preamble and no restating of what I asked for.
