I'm an undergraduate student member of my university's Senate Committee on International Academic Partnerships. I'm also taking a political-theory seminar this term on democracy and its discontents, which is why I've been asked to draft our decision memo. I'd be grateful for a first-pass draft I can revise before circulation.

The committee administers the exchange agreement with the University of Maritsa, in the Republic of Maritsa. Clause 7(c) of the standard agreement lets us suspend for "sustained erosion of academic freedom or institutional autonomy" — a phrase the agreement never defines and that the committee has never applied. Please treat the following dossier as stipulated and argue from it, not from comparative cases or literature I can't verify.

1. In 2021, a parliamentary majority holding two-thirds of the seats, won with 48% of the vote under an electoral system that same majority had rewritten the year before, transferred the public universities into "public interest trusts" whose governing boards are appointed by a minister for nine-year terms and are removable only for cause certified by a newly created audit office.
2. In 2023, the national accreditation council — now staffed by ministerial appointees — withdrew accreditation from the country's only master's programme in gender studies, citing insufficient labour-market demand. The teaching staff were reassigned.
3. In 2024, a foreign-influence transparency statute required any department drawing more than 15% of its budget from abroad to register as "foreign-supported" and to file syllabi and reading lists sixty days before term, subject to ministerial objection.
4. In 2025, the university's independent faculty association was dissolved after failing a representation audit and replaced by an employee association whose officers are elected from a ministry-approved list.
5. Elections are still held, honestly counted, and contested; turnout is high; two opposition parties hold seats; newspapers remain critical of the government, though most are owned by foundations tied to the governing party. The government won the last election outright with 49% and says it is implementing its manifesto.

We have two submissions. The Maritsa Faculty Association in Exile — three dismissed professors now visiting fellows in our history department — asks us to suspend and to recognise them as the university's legitimate academic representative. The rector, appointed under the new boards, argues that suspension would punish students, that every measure was enacted by a democratic majority, that each has an analogue in a settled democracy (foundation-governed universities, demand-based accreditation closures, foreign-funding disclosure, union recognition rules), and that a foreign university committee has no standing to grade another state's constitutional order. She adds that our own democracy scores have been falling and that our state legislature has recently restricted what public universities here may teach on contested social questions, so that by our own standard we should suspend ourselves.

I've circulated a three-part test, and I'd like the memo to use it rather than ignore it. (a) Asymmetric entrenchment: does a change make reversal by a future majority harder than enactment by this one was? (b) The two-audience test: is the measure justified domestically in terms that would not survive being restated to an external or oppositional audience — measurable, I suggested, as the gap between the justification entered in the domestic official register and the one in the ministry's English-language release. (c) The burden-of-proof rule: where a change alters the rules of political competition itself — who votes, who counts, who may organise, who may teach — the burden falls on the incumbent, not on those claiming erosion.

Please draft the memo addressed to the committee, no more than 1,000 words. It should: recommend exactly one of four actions — unconditional suspension, suspension with conditions, no action with review next year, or referral to an external body — with a time frame and conditions our administrator could actually check; engage the losing side at its strongest, including the "each item has a democratic analogue" argument, the standing objection, and the tu quoque; and tell me where my three-part test does real work, where it distorts the case, and where it merely relabels the dispute.
