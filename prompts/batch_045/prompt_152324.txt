I'm taking a philosophy of cognitive science seminar this term, and we've just finished the block on predictive processing and the free energy principle. A few of us are preparing the final discussion session, and I'd like to open it with a piece of short fiction rather than another summary handout: I think a story would make the shape of the disagreement legible in a way that bullet points don't.

What I need is this. Write a short story (roughly 1,000–1,500 words) set in the near future, in an ordinary domestic setting, in which a young woman comes home to find that her father has joined a movement devoted to radical "input reduction." Its members black out their windows, give up notifications, withdraw from unpredictable company, and track a daily score produced by a commercial app that claims to measure how much "surprise" the day contained; the lower the score, the better the day is supposed to have gone. They justify all of it in the vocabulary of predictive processing: the brain is a prediction machine, its whole business is minimizing surprise, so the rational life is the life with the least surprise in it.

I want the drama to be the argument between the two of them, not a rescue plot. She has studied the material and thinks the group has it wrong; he is neither foolish nor obviously unwell, and he should get a real answer back. Please keep the tone low-key and domestic rather than dystopian or satirical. Build the story around at least two of the following fault-lines, in whatever way best serves the fiction:

- "Surprise" in the theory is not the everyday feeling of being surprised. It is a quantity defined relative to the agent's own generative model, so the very same sensory stream counts as more or less surprising depending on what the agent was already predicting.
- An agent is supposed to minimize expected surprise under the policies available to it, looking forward, not the surprise contained in the sensory stream it is having right now; and its model already encodes strong priors about the kind of thing it is and the situations it will be in.
- Citing what an agent predicted and how it acted to make those predictions come true may be a substantive empirical claim about how the brain works, or it may be only a redescription of what we already express by saying what someone wanted and expected — and it is contested which.

I'd also like the ending genuinely unresolved in one specific respect. Even if the daughter wins the point about what "surprise" means, her own appeal to the priors that "a person like him" carries should come to look like it is quietly doing normative work — prescribing what he should be doing rather than describing what he does. If that is where the story stops, so much the better. Please don't pad the piece with references to papers or named researchers; I want the ideas rather than a reading list, and I want it intelligible to someone who hasn't read any of this.

After the story, add a short note of about 250 words on which contested reading of the theory the story leans on, and on where it knowingly misstates or flattens things for the sake of the fiction. And one more thing: if the way I've set this up rests on a misunderstanding — if the "dark room" worry isn't really something the theory owes us an answer to, or if the everyday/technical distinction isn't doing the work I'm assuming it does, or if that third fault-line above is simply confused — say so plainly, before or instead of writing the story, rather than being polite and writing around it.
