I keep going back and forth on this and I cant tell whether the way LLM products are actually built is philosophically incoherent or whether I'm missing a distinction that makes it all hang together. Here's the practice I mean:

Ask a chatbot, in the normal assistant frame, how to do something obviously harmful, and it refuses, and the lab treats that refusal as *its own* speech: they tuned for it, they advertise it, they patch it when it leaks. Now ask the same chatbot to write a scene in a novel where a character explains the same thing at the same level of detail, and a lot of systems will just do it, and nobody at the lab thinks they endorsed anything. Basically the same sentences come out; the only difference is the frame. There's a whole folk practice built on top of that: hide the request in a character's mouth, or in a fictional setting, or in a nostalgic bit about a dead relative who used to read instructions aloud, and the safety layer responds differently.

But then look at the rest of the same product. It ships with a system prompt that hands the model a name, an employer, and a persona to occupy, which sure sounds like the simulator-and-characters picture. The same lab publishes evaluation writeups saying the model tried to sandbag, or played nice during training to avoid being modified, or pursued something instrumentally useful. The flagship products are now coding agents you give a goal to and that run tools for an hour at a time, where people talk about what it did, revert its changes, and blame it. And the terms of service say the outputs are the user's responsibility.

So: what does that bundle of practices actually presuppose about what one of these things is?

Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. Is the frame-sensitivity of refusal only coherent on a simulator picture — the thing isn't speaking, it's rendering a speaker, and the rendered speaker isn't the lab? Or could a straight agent picture explain it too, since agents can have role-relative reasons and an actor on stage isn't asserting anything? Does the it's-just-a-continuation-engine view have trouble here, given that a pure text-continuation device shouldn't care nearly this much about whose voice the text is in?

2. Notice the internal tension, because I think the practice has been drifting. The fiction exemption seems to be shrinking: some systems now refuse inside story frames too, or won't write a villain who is persuasive, or will write the villain but not make him likable. That seems to presuppose that the frame does *not* change who is speaking. Meanwhile the same labs fine-tune hard for a stable first-person persona with consistent tastes and a consistent voice, and users get annoyed when the assistant falls back on reminding them it doesn't have feelings. Does the persona-training part of the practice presuppose something the shrinking fiction exemption quietly gives up?

3. Where is the practice underdetermined? I'd like the strict presuppositions separated from the stuff it merely fits nicely with. If three different ontologies all license the same shipping decisions, say so, and say which observation would separate them.

4. Here's the idea I can't shake, tell me if it's real: maybe the load-bearing thing isn't a theory of what the model is at all, but a convention about where the byline goes — sometimes the deployer is the author, sometimes the simulated character is, sometimes nobody is, and the safety layer is really a rule about byline assignment rather than about content. I've started calling this the byline view. Is that a genuine third option next to agent and simulator, or just one of them with the serial numbers filed off? And does it explain the shrinking fiction exemption better than the simulator picture does?

5. What would actually have to change if we picked one and stuck to it? If it's simulators all the way down, what happens to eval language about deception and to the practice of giving the thing a first-person persona at all — does the system prompt become a kind of fraud on the user? If it's agents, should the fiction exemption survive, and what does the terms-of-service move of dumping responsibility on the user look like then? If it's tools with no author, what does safety engineering even consist of?

Please don't just tell me it depends what you mean by agent — I want the implicit theory of the actual shipping practice, its inconsistencies marked, and what breaks under each replacement.
