You are a professional philosopher with research-level command of causation, explanation in the historical sciences, and the philosophy of agency. Please think carefully before answering, and do not rush to a verdict: begin with a clearly headed short section in which you brainstorm, as bullet points, the different things a person might mean by calling a non-human cause a "historical actor", and state which of them you intend to work with and why. Answer throughout in British English. Please do not cite, quote, or recommend any literature or authors: I have no library access where I am writing and cannot check references, and the argument is what I want, not a reading list.

I am a retired atmospheric physicist who has lately been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and I spent a working life fitting models to data in which the experimental design was never orthogonal. Last week two members of the group — Hugh, a retired geography teacher, and Marion, a retired general practitioner — got thoroughly stuck over a case the group had agreed to stipulate rather than argue about: the Irish potato famine of the late 1840s. Grant that the blight destroyed the harvest repeatedly, that roughly the usual estimates of death and emigration are correct, and that food continued to leave the country while people starved. The dispute was about what to say about all this. I would be grateful for a move-by-move adjudication.

(1) Hugh opened by saying the blight is simply the difference-maker: no pathogen, no famine, and no reshuffling of Irish land tenure or relief policy produces one. Disease and weather, he said, have killed far more people than generals and parliaments have, and historians who insist on explaining everything by class and policy are doing ideology while claiming to do causal analysis.

(2) Marion conceded the blight was necessary but denied it was what explains the famine. She proposed what she called the fragility test: don't ask whether the outcome would have occurred without the candidate cause, ask how much the outcome moves when you vary that cause inside a reference class of nearby possible worlds, as against how much it moves when you vary the social arrangements. Vary the relief and export arrangements and mortality swings enormously; vary the biological details of the blight and you get a bad famine either way. Her analogy was to partitioning variance among factors in an experiment — with the awkward difference, which she volunteered, that history supplies you with a single run, so the partition is a thought experiment rather than a measurement.

(3) Hugh raised two objections. First, the reference class is doing all the work: wiggle institutions across their full range and institutions look colossal, wiggle biology across its full range and biology looks colossal, and nothing in Marion's test fixes a principled range or a principled way of counting nearby worlds. Second, he said her test would convict the pathogen in a different case: take smallpox out of the encounter between Europeans and the peoples of the Americas and you get a different hemisphere, whereas no plausible rearranging of indigenous institutions gets you anything like the catastrophic mortality actually recorded.

(4) Marion accepted the second point with enthusiasm and turned it. That, she said, is the whole payoff: it shows that "non-human cause" is not a kind with a single causal power, and that the credit due to a pathogen or a volcano is case-relative and range-relative — in the Americas case the biological variable screens off the social ones because the outcome is robust to them, in the Irish case the social ones screen off the biological one because the outcome is fragile to them. She denied that this makes germs agents: evolving, or acquiring drug resistance, is not responsiveness to reasons, and feedback loops of that shape are everywhere in physics and economics without anybody wanting to call a thermostat an agent.

(5) Hugh then made the point I thought was his strongest. The entire point of saying that institutions caused the famine, he said, is to hold somebody answerable; a verdict of the form "land tenure has the larger variance share" blames nobody, and an account that cannot tell the difference between a landlord and a fungus has mislaid something the argument was supposed to deliver. He added that the blight was not exogenous anyway: the dependence on a single, genetically narrow staple was itself a human arrangement, so the biological factor and the institutional one are entangled, and any partition of the outcome between them is arbitrary.

(6) Marion replied that Hugh's blame point was the best thing he had said, and proposed a division of labour: a sensitivity notion of explanation for the "what should we fix" question, and a separate reasons-and-alternatives notion of agency for the "who is answerable" question. People reach for talk of "historical actors", she suggested, precisely because they want one verdict to do both jobs. On entanglement she conceded that the numbers are then unavailable, but said the question survives: you can still ask which factor you would bet on.

(7) Hugh would not let that go. If the numbers are unavailable, he said, her test is a metaphor dressed up as a measurement, and he would rather say plainly that the blight caused the famine and the landlords caused the dying, with "caused" doing different work in each clause. Marion said that just is her two-questions view and he had conceded. He said he had not, because in his view the blight would still have been the decisive factor under most of the institutional arrangements available at the time — which, he insisted, is an empirical claim, not a conceptual one.

At this point I intervened with two remarks of my own, and both of them looked at me. First, that in my old field you never got a clean partition unless the factors were uncorrelated, and here they are thoroughly confounded, so I doubt Marion's decomposition is identified even in principle. Second, that what her fragility test really seems to deliver is the answer to a "which lever should we pull" question, which may be no bad thing but is not the question Hugh started with.

Please rate each of the seven moves, and my two remarks, on a five-point scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with one or two sentences of justification each; say whether either party changed the subject at any point; say who is more right overall and why; and say what both of them are missing.
