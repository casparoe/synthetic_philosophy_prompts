I convene a small philosophy of medicine reading group — four of us: three graduate students in philosophy and one internist who keeps dragging the discussion back to cases — and for two weeks we have been stuck on an argument that the internist sketched on a whiteboard after we got nowhere with the usual dialectic. Her target is not the modest claim that the disease/non-disease distinction has vague or contested edges, especially in psychiatry. It is the stronger claim that the category is gerrymandered all the way down: that nosology is an administrative convenience, that there is no fact of the matter about where the line falls, and therefore nothing for a revision of the diagnostic manual to have been right or wrong about. I find the conclusion unacceptable and I cannot locate the break. Here is the argument, cleaned up and given numbered steps so we can stop talking past each other.

(1) Necessary condition. A condition C is a disease only if C involves some part or mechanism of the organism failing to perform a function that it has. Call this the dysfunction condition. Both camps in our dispute accept it — the value-free camp as the whole analysis, the value-laden camp as a necessary condition that must be conjoined with a judgment that the failure harms the person.

(2) Functions are historical. What it is for a mechanism to have a function is for that mechanism to have been selected for producing some effect in the evolutionary past of the population. The function is fixed by the causal history, not by what the mechanism happens to be good for now.

(3) Functioning is forward-looking and statistical. What it is for a mechanism to be performing its function now is for it to be producing that effect at a level of efficiency that is statistically typical for the mechanism within the relevant reference class.

(4) Contribution is relational. Whether a mechanism produces its effect at typical efficiency, and whether that efficiency contributes to survival and reproduction, is a property of the mechanism-plus-its-environment, not of the mechanism alone. The mechanism can be intrinsically unchanged and contribute positively in one environment and negatively in another. Our stock example is a phenotype that elevates fasting blood glucose: protective across an environment of periodic famine, diabetogenic across one of continuous caloric abundance, with the mechanism itself identical in both.

(5) The Thermostat Problem. So every dysfunction claim is implicitly relativized to a reference environment, and nothing about the intrinsic structure of the mechanism settles which reference environment is the right one. The reference environment is a setting on a dial, not a datum in the world.

(6) From relativity to non-factivity. If the truth of a dysfunction claim depends on a setting that the descriptive facts about the organism do not fix, then fixing it is a choice, and a choice answers to our values and purposes rather than to the facts. So the dysfunction condition is not a factual condition at all; it encodes a projection of our chosen setting.

(7) The slide to ubiquity. And the choice of setting is unconstrained in the following sense: for any trait whatever, there is some environment relative to which that trait is fitness-reducing. So any trait can be made to count as a dysfunction by a suitable turn of the dial, and no trait can be made safe from it.

(8) Gerrymander. Therefore which conditions count as diseases is settled by whatever dial setting a committee adopts, and the extension of the term at any given time is a gerrymander. Nothing is left to be right or wrong about except internal consistency.

(9) The historical corollary. Therefore the mid-twentieth-century classification of homosexuality as a mental disorder was not an error later corrected; and the nineteenth-century diagnosis devised for enslaved people who repeatedly tried to escape was not a mistake either, given the dial settings of its time and place. In each case the category was exactly what the dial was set to.

(10) The upshot. Abolish the concept. Keep the complaints, the lesions, the mechanisms, and the prognoses, and stop pretending there is a natural joint at which the diseases stop and the merely unfortunate conditions begin.

There are three claims I want to keep, and I suspect the argument forces me to surrender at least one. First, that the removal of homosexuality from the psychiatric manual was at least partly a correction — that the earlier classification got something wrong and not merely changed its mind. I am willing to concede it was also a change of values; I am not willing to concede it was only that. Second, that there are asymptomatic diseases: a small, painless pancreatic lesion in someone who will die of it, about which nobody yet has any attitude. It is a disease whether or not anyone disvalues it and whatever the dial is set to. Third, that some conditions are disvalued and involve a mechanism working badly, and still are not diseases — ordinary grief, and the perfectly species-typical decline in lung capacity that begins around the fifth decade. My internist says she would rather drop (2) and keep the forward-looking account of function, but she then thinks (5) is doing all the real work and (2) is decoration, and I am not sure she is right about that either.

Please do not send me to the literature or give me a reading list — we have read the standard material and the problem is not ignorance of it. What I want is the step. Which numbered step do you reject, why does it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost, both in general and with respect to the three claims I want to keep? If the flaw is an equivocation, name the two readings it trades on; if the argument is valid and one premise simply has to go, say which one you would give up and what you have to pay for it. Finally, be explicit about the meta-question rather than just delivering a verdict: tell me what would change your mind about which step is the culprit, what a defender of the step you reject could say that would move you to reject a different one instead, and where your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong. I would rather have a diagnosis with its failure modes marked on it than a confident one.
