I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the late 2000s and belong to a small forecasting group that grew out of that scene — we meet monthly, keep calibration logs, and nearly all our arguments are about back-of-envelope decompositions rather than about anything anyone could look up. At the last session I presented a Fermi-style estimate of the probability that a frontier lab's alignment plan fails within five years: I split the event into three conjuncts (continued capability growth, the plan's central assumption holding, no exogenous pause), gave each a number, and multiplied under an explicit presumption of independence. A friend who is better at this than I am then re-ran my own estimate under a finer decomposition, splitting the second conjunct into three sub-conjuncts, taking the numbers for those sub-conjuncts from what I'd have said if asked about them cold (the only way to get marginals for conjuncts I'd never considered), and produced a figure nearly an order of magnitude below my original one. He claims this is completely general and calls it the fragmentation lemma. Here is the argument as he gives it.

(1) A heuristic estimate of P(E) reached by decomposing E into conjuncts and multiplying is warranted only to the extent that the conjuncts are probabilistically independent, or at least not positively correlated; that assumption is where the epistemic work is done. (2) The correct formalisation of the presumption of independence — the default you are entitled to when you have no evidence of correlation — is maximum entropy subject to what you know: given the marginals and nothing further, the maximum-entropy distribution is the product of the marginals, i.e. independence. (3) Maximum entropy earns that role because it is the unique choice that adds no information beyond the constraints, and, on the concentration-of-measure reading, because it is what almost any distribution satisfying the constraints will look like. (4) But maximum entropy is sensitive to the description of the space: re-coarse-grain the sample space, or apply a non-linear change of coordinates to a continuous variable, and the maximum-entropy distribution changes although nothing has been learnt. So "adds no information" is well-defined only relative to a chosen partition. (5) In a heuristic argument the decomposition is chosen by the arguer, and there is no canonical decomposition of an event: any conjunct can always be cut further. (6) Hence the fragmentation lemma: holding the evidence fixed, I may refine the decomposition of E into arbitrarily many conjuncts, apply the presumption of independence to each refinement — every refinement being an equally entitled instance of (2) and (3) — and thereby move the estimate for P(E) by large factors without any change in what I know. (7) So no heuristic estimate resting on a presumption of independence is constrained by the evidence at all; the number comes from an arbitrary modelling choice, and such arguments are worthless. (8) But that cannot be right: independence assumptions plainly work sometimes — repeated randomised trials, spatially separated systems, dice — and abandoning them would take most of applied probability, causal inference from passive data, and decision theory down with them.

I want to know which step you would reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. My own suspicion is that (6) is where something slips: under genuine independence the product of the finer marginals has to agree with what I already believed about the coarser event, so the lemma must be smuggling in new judgements while calling them the same evidence — but I cannot make that precise, and my friend says the suspicion simply begs the question against (4). Assume I know the standard machinery — Bayes, the Markov condition and d-separation, why differential entropy is not invariant under a change of coordinates — so don't explain those. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (2) and (8) than (3); and if the maximum-entropy formalisation really is the thing to drop, tell me what should replace it — a prior over causal structures that penalises extra edges, an exchangeability assumption, a hierarchical prior on a correlation parameter centred at zero, or something else — and what each option buys and costs me in the arguments I actually have.
