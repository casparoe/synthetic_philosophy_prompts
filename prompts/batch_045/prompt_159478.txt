The literature on the wrong kind of reasons agrees on the cases and disagrees about everything else. Take the demon who will torture you unless you admire him: nearly everyone grants that his threat has some normative bearing on your admiring him, and nearly everyone also grants that it is not the kind of consideration that could make him admirable. What they cannot agree on is how to say this. Fitting-attitude analysts of value need a distinction between two kinds of reasons for an attitude; buck-passers about value recast the threat as a reason to bring the attitude about rather than a reason for it; a third camp denies that the threat gives a reason for admiration at all, only a reason to want to have it. The output has been a pile of taxonomies — object-given versus state-given, right-kind versus wrong-kind, reasons for versus reasons to — with no visible movement.

My hypothesis is that the taxonomy dispute is a proxy war: each camp's taxonomy is reverse-engineered from a prior answer about whether value explains facts about reasons for attitudes or the other way round, so the classification fight cannot settle independently of that. My second hypothesis is that the shared assumption of a sharp binary is doing as much damage as the metaphysics. There appear to be intermediate cases — reasons that are neither purely object-given nor purely state-given but relational, such as that he is my father, that she once saved my life, that the joke is funnier in this room tonight — which transfer to some subjects and not to others and which resist a clean sorting. If right-kind-ness is graded rather than binary, both camps may be partly right and the live question becomes where a threshold falls.

Give me a diagnosis, not a verdict. Using what each position actually claims, say whether this is a verbal disagreement about "reason", a proxy dispute about the direction of explanation between value and reasons, two questions run together under one heading — what counts in favour of admiring versus what makes admiration fitting — a dispute about which cases count as data, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis, including whether my gradedness suggestion dissolves anything or merely relocates the problem, or argue that no progress should be expected and why. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
