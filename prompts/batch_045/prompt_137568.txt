I am putting together a short, deliberately lighthearted closing talk for a graduate reading group on predictive processing and the free energy principle, and I would like to end on a joke that actually rewards the people who have done the reading rather than on one of the many "your brain is just hallucinating all the time" gags that everyone has already seen a hundred times. The audience will know the standard machinery: variational free energy as a bound on surprise, prediction error minimisation, precision weighting as the mechanism that trades priors off against sensory evidence, the Markov blanket that divides internal from external states, the point that an agent can reduce free energy either by revising its generative model or by acting on the world so that its predictions come true (active inference), and the nagging "dark room" worry that a thoroughgoing surprise-minimiser ought to curl up somewhere dark and unchanging and stay there forever.

What I am after is a joke that turns on one of those specific commitments rather than on the vague slogan that the brain is a prediction machine — ideally something whose punchline would only land for someone who understands the difference between minimising free energy by changing your beliefs and minimising it by changing the world, or between treating a signal as surprising evidence and treating it as imprecise noise you are licensed to ignore. To make it harder to phone in, please build it around a small scenario I can put on a slide: a scrupulous free-energy minimiser who has taken the dark room objection completely literally and has taken a job as the night guard of an unlit warehouse, and who is being visited by a colleague who insists the remedy is not to switch on the lights but to re-weight the precision on certain priors, or to redraw the boundary of the guard's Markov blanket so that whatever is blinding him now counts as part of him rather than as part of the world.

Please give me two or three such jokes, each no more than a few sentences, followed by a single line apiece explaining which piece of the machinery the humour depends on. They should be self-contained: no references to particular papers, authors, or experimental results that would need to be looked up, and nothing that turns on jargon invented for the occasion — the comedy should come from the theory's own commitments, not from a pun that merely sounds technical. If a version of the joke would be just as funny to someone who has never heard of prediction error, it is the wrong joke.
