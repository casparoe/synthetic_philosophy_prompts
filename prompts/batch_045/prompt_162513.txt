I am organizing a small workshop on the philosophy of psychiatric classification, and I want its centerpiece to be a joint statement written as if it came out of an adversarial collaboration between two camps that, in the literature, mostly talk past each other. Please draft that document in American English, in a formal register, as something both parties could actually sign.

The two parties:

- The Realist: psychiatric categories are answerable to something independent of our classificatory practice. Nosology is in the business of improving fit with underlying — ideally mechanistic, ultimately neurobiological or computational — dysfunction, and a category earns its place by supporting induction, explanation, and intervention. The Realist grants that the current manual is a compromise full of committee decisions, and treats that as a defect to be engineered out.
- The Looping Constructivist: the categories of the official manual are partly made by the people they classify. People who receive a diagnosis become aware of the description, are responded to differently, organize themselves, and are offered treatments and accommodations in light of it; their experience and behavior thereby shift; and because nosologists revise criteria in light of the population they now actually see, the category moves too. The Constructivist grants that this does not make mental illness fictional and that some categories are comparatively stable, and treats looping as a feature of the objects psychiatry studies, not a reason for despair.

I want you to work with three distinctions I will stipulate, since they will structure the document: (i) *first-order looping* — individuals change in response to being classified; (ii) *second-order looping* — the criteria, prototype, or prevalence figures themselves shift in response to that changed population, which then feeds back into how new patients present (a ratchet, not a single bounce); and (iii) *loop-resistant versus loop-amplifying categories* — the claim, which both parties accept, that categories differ in how sensitive their symptom profiles are to this feedback, with categories tied to identifiable peripheral pathology at one end and categories defined largely by subjective report and social impairment at the other.

The dispute should be made concrete by one hypothetical case, which I would like you to use as a running test bed rather than as decoration. Imagine that a proposal is before the committee for the next revision of the diagnostic manual: a new category, provisionally called *persistent derealization syndrome associated with immersive virtual environments*, defined by distressing and disabling detachment that patients themselves attribute to prolonged immersive-headset use, with a required duration and an impairment criterion, and no known mechanism. Suppose further that, two years after adoption, a multicenter consortium announces a candidate biomarker — a distinctive pattern of reduced interoceptive accuracy together with attenuated pupillary modulation — and that some committee members propose inserting the biomarker as a supporting criterion.

Please produce:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, substantive enough that each side's critics would find them costly rather than vacuous — not, for instance, merely that suffering is real. Include what each side concedes about the weak point of its own view, and where their practical recommendations converge.
2. A disagreements table. Each row: the issue; the Realist's own preferred formulation, worded so the Realist would accept it; the Constructivist's own formulation, likewise; and a verdict on whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal. Where you judge a row to be verbal, say exactly which term is doing the work — "real," "natural kind," "mechanism," "validity," "the same disorder" — and what each party means by it.
3. A cruxes table: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side to change something. For each crux give a concrete test, case, or argument that both parties accept in advance as bearing on it — a natural experiment, a historical comparison, or an argument, not a slogan — and record each side's prediction in writing before the outcome is known, together with what each side would count as a defeat for its own view. Label each crux as empirical, conceptual, or normative (some will turn on whether truth or clinical utility is what a category is for), and note where the relevant evidence is not realistically obtainable. Cruxes I expect to matter, though you should not feel bound by them: whether a mechanism-based definition would halt the ratchet or add a new gear to it, given that biomarker announcements alter self-conception, treatment seeking, insurance categories, and commercial incentives; whether predictive and treatment-guiding success can be produced by looping itself, so that utility no longer certifies validity; what happens to prevalence, presentation, and chronicity in a health system that assigns the code for administrative purposes but does not disclose it to patients, compared with one that routinely discloses it; and what happens when the category is exported into a setting where immersive-headset use is rare and the local idioms of distress differ.
4. A short closing section on what each camp would now recommend to the committee about this specific proposal — adopt, reject, adopt provisionally — and on any procedural mechanism, such as mandatory sunset review for newly adopted categories, that both can sign for their own reasons.

Do not cite or name-drop the scholarly literature, and do not supply a bibliography or reading list; the document must stand on its own for a reader with no background in the debate, so define every technical term you use. Do not adjudicate between the parties, and do not write this as a dialogue or as a diagnosis of why the debate is stuck. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, dense rather than padded.
