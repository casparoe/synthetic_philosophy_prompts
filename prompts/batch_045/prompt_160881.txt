I want an audit of my own position, not a lecture on Arrow's theorem (I know Arrow's theorem; I know Gibbard–Satterthwaite too, so I'm not asking for strategy-proofness, I know I can't have it).

Context: I'm a 4th-year PhD student in political science, comps field in comparative political institutions with a formal theory minor. Our department is rewriting two things at once — how we aggregate ballots in faculty hiring (full rankings of a 5–7 person shortlist) and how we run business meetings (motions and amendments, decided one at a time by majority). I got asked to write the "principles" memo, and writing it made me realise I'm not sure my principles cohere. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Arrow kills the idea of a collective preference ordering. There's no "will of the department" as an ordering over candidates. I want to evaluate rules as functions from ballot profiles to outcomes, judged by how they behave on profiles — not by whether they satisfy axioms that look like rationality constraints on an agent. If the induced relation cycles, fine; there was never a relation.

2. But agenda-dependence in the actual meetings is a real pathology to me, not a curiosum: if which motion passes depends on the order they get called in, something has gone wrong. At the same time I'm basically relaxed about deciding meeting business issue-by-issue by majority, even when the issues are clearly related to each other.

3. When one candidate beats every other candidate head-to-head, that candidate should win. It's the one straightforwardly majoritarian thing I want to keep.

4. IIA is the axiom to throw out. I'm drawn to scoring/positional rules like Borda precisely because they use the whole ballot instead of throwing away everything below the top rank, and I've never thought the case for IIA was more than a case for not being sensitive to noise.

5. Spoilers are disqualifying. If x would have won and then loses because somebody else entered the race, the rule is broken. That's my whole objection to plurality and half the reason I'm on this commitee.

6. I thought I had a cheap way out of 5: a condition I made up, call it rank-robustness — if x wins in a profile, then x still wins in any profile you get by adding a new candidate whom every voter ranks below x. I think Borda satisfies it (everyone's score just shifts by one?), which would let me keep 4 and still claim to have solved the spoiler problem.

7. Participation matters: adding ballots that rank x first should never cause x to lose. In a department where getting people to show up is already a fight, a rule that rewards staying home is unusable.

8. Last year we had a vote where the scoring rule picked someone nobody had ranked first — she was everybody's genuine third choice and the head-to-head winner came second. I defended that outcome at the time and I'd defend it again. But I've also said out loud that a winner with zero first-place votes is a winner nobody wanted, and I meant that too. I can't tell whether those are the same claim.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? Rank the real tensions by severity, tell me for each what specifically has to give and what giving it up costs me, and flag the pairs that only look inconsistent. I'm genuinely unsure where the fault line is — maybe I've smuggled in a majoritarian intuition I disavow in 1, maybe some of these are just incompatible full stop, maybe one of my items is really two claims. If two of them are provably inconsistent, say which result shows it. And if there's a single revision that would clean up the most, tell me that too — but don't just hand me a revision without saying what I lose.
