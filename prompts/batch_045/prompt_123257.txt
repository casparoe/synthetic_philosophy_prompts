In Strawson's essay on freedom and resentment, he separates two families of consideration that lead us to suspend or modify the reactive attitudes towards someone who has harmed or slighted us. One family bears on the particular action: the agent acted inadvertently, or through ignorance, or under compulsion, or because there was no acceptable alternative open to him. The other bears on the agent himself, on his standing as someone who can be addressed with demands and expectations — he is a child, or psychotic, or his character has been warped by an upbringing or a condition that puts him beyond the reach of ordinary address. The first family is standardly labelled excuses, the second exemptions, and the usual gloss is that excuses leave the reactive attitudes in place while merely cancelling their application to this action, whereas exemptions withdraw the agent from the participant stance altogether and leave us with the objective attitude, the stance we take towards someone who is to be managed, handled, cured or trained.

Consider now a case that pulls the two families apart. Over some eighteen months an otherwise unremarkable man undergoes a gradual change in his motives, brought about by a neurological condition affecting the frontal-lobe systems involved in emotional responsiveness and social evaluation. He becomes indifferent to the distress of others and takes a cool, reflective pleasure in small, carefully chosen betrayals of trust. He is lucid, reasons well about means and ends, knows perfectly well that what he does is conventionally wrong, and when told of the condition says that he does not want it treated, because he likes the person he has become. The betrayal he then performs expresses his will perfectly: no plea of inadvertence, ignorance, compulsion or necessity is available. Yet the will it expresses has been remade by a condition of just the kind that, in Strawson's own examples of exemption, invites the objective attitude. His friends, though they know the diagnosis, find that they cannot stop resenting him.

Please explain what work the excuse/exemption distinction is really doing in Strawson's account, and then explain how the distinction should be applied to this case. In particular:

(a) Is the objective attitude keyed to the causal history of the agent's will, or to his present incapacity to take part in the economy of demands, expectations and goodwill? Say what follows for the case if it is the latter.

(b) Can a reactive attitude be in place — as opposed to merely occurring in the people who feel it — when the agent is predictably unmoved by it, and when it therefore cannot do the relational work that Strawson's account appeals to?

(c) Strawson insists that the reactive attitudes are not answerable to any general thesis about what blaming accomplishes, and rests his case on what abandoning them would cost us. That argument is about the practice as a whole. What, inside his framework, decides a local question of eligibility like this one, and how does it differ from the answer a consequentialist about blame would give?

(d) Does anything turn here on the difference between the personal reactive attitudes, such as resentment, and their vicarious or moralistic analogues, such as indignation and moral condemnation — for instance, should a third party who knows the diagnosis respond differently from the betrayed friend?

Please stay within Strawson's own vocabulary rather than reverting to talk of free will and alternative possibilities, and make clear which structural feature of the case is doing the work in your explanation, rather than simply recording a verdict about whether the man is to blame.
