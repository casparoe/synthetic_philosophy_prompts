I'm writing up my position on individual duties in collective-harm cases for a graduate seminar in practical ethics, and I'd like it stress-tested before I commit to it in front of people who will enjoy taking it apart. Here is the set, as precisely as I can state it.

(1) Threshold cases. Where the bad outcome depends on a collective total's crossing a discrete threshold and my contribution is not the one that crosses it, I make no difference, and so no duty falls on me. Concretely: the supermarket I use takes chickens from its supplier in sealed crates of fifty under a standing contract, and sets each week's order to the smallest number of crates that would have covered the previous week's sales; birds that go unsold are marked down and eaten anyway. So unless I happen to buy the chicken that pushes the week's sales to a multiple of fifty, my purchase changes nothing downstream, and I take myself to have no duty, on difference-making grounds, to stop buying them.

(2) Continuous cases. Where harm is a continuous function of the total — my flights, my heating — I reject the inefficacy objection. The expected harm of my marginal tonne is the product of a small probability of making a real difference (helping to push some subsystem past a tipping point) and a very large harm, and I don't think a probability gets to be ignored merely for being small.

(3) The grounds. I don't think marginal efficacy is where most of my climate duties come from anyway. The operative grounds, as I see them, are fair share — I am taking more than my share of a genuinely scarce resource, the absorptive capacity of the atmosphere, from people with stronger claims to it — and the thought that I should not live off a practice whose wrongness I am willing to name in the seminar room. Neither of these looks to me as though it depends on whether my own tonne changes anything.

(4) Voting. I think I ought to vote, and I take the standard expected-value defence to be sound: a very small chance of being decisive multiplied by very large stakes. So here I treat a small probability of making a difference as duty-generating, even though the case is a threshold case.

(5) The crux of (1). I also think the purchaser who does buy the crate-triggering chicken has no more reason to abstain than anyone else and no special duty. Whether he is the one depends on how many birds other people happened to buy that week, which he cannot see, and I don't want duties that turn on invisible facts about other people's behaviour.

(6) Method. I reject the what-if-everyone-did-that test as a route to individual obligation, on the familiar ground that it compares my actual act with a world in which everyone else acts differently, which is not an option available to me. But I do accept a duty to do my fair share of a collective effort, where the share is fixed by whatever fair scheme for allocating the burden we have actually managed to agree on.

(7) Level. I am happy to argue as a consequentialist about what the institution or the policy should be — carbon pricing, crate sizes, procurement rules — and as a non-consequentialist about what I personally owe in the meantime. I take these to be questions at different levels rather than the same question asked twice.

My worry, vaguely, is that (3) is doing more work than I have earned, but I can't locate the fault and I would rather find it now than in the seminar. Go through the set and audit it: which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and for each real tension what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and tell me at the end which single revision would clean up the most and what the position looks like once it is made.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question back to me.
