Six months ago I joined the board of a small foundation that funds arms-control work. I am the only member without a policy background, and the others reach for the same picture whenever a grant decision comes up: nuclear deterrence is a game of chicken. Two drivers, one lane, the one who swerves loses, so the way to not lose is to make it visibly impossible — or at least visibly unlikely — that you will swerve. They use it to license a concrete conclusion: that de-alerting, no-first-use declarations, and giving up launch-on-warning are all destabilizing, because each is an advance announcement that you will swerve when it counts, and that a state is better off with a reputation for volatility.

What I want is an audit rather than a game-theory tutorial. Assume a two-state dyad with secure second-strike capability, no alliances, no non-state actors.

Start with the correspondence as a table: what plays the driver, the lane, the swerve, the steering wheel, the spectators, the crash. Then separate the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion above from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Four things in particular I need settled:

1. The payoff structure. In chicken each driver's best outcome is going straight while the other swerves. Is there anything in the nuclear case that plays winning — a first strike that leaves the striker better off than restraint? If there is not, what happens to the advice the analogy generates?
2. Two readings of looking irrational is an asset: visibly removing your own option to swerve, as a commitment made before any crisis, versus having a reputation for being hot-headed or uncontrolled. As usually drawn, chicken models the second only if there is a type space, and contains no move corresponding to the first. Which one is my board actually relying on, and does the picture carry it?
3. The crash. The cars collide because both drivers chose to go straight. Nuclear disaster also has inadvertent routes — false warning, misidentified launch, an accident nobody chose — with no counterpart in a game of two drivers. Does adding them change what counts as a good commitment?
4. There is no last round: the standoff does not terminate in either a crash or a swerve, it continues indefinitely. Does removing the terminal move change the reasoning, or is it inert here?

Then say plainly whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. If it does not survive, give me a better analogy — or tell me why no single two-player game pictures this well and what the board should reason from instead.
