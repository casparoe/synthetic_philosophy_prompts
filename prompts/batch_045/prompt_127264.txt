I am a novelist, and I am revising the second draft of a book—tentatively titled *The Vorticity Notebooks*—built around a device I am not yet sure I can carry off. Its narrator, a retired observational cosmologist named Ruth Hallam, composes a long, unsent letter addressed to Kurt Gödel, in which she tries to brief him, as a respectful junior colleague would, on three things that happened after his death and that bear directly on the argument of his 1949 essay for the Einstein Festschrift: that in some perfectly respectable solutions of the field equations—his own rotating universe above all—the light cones tip far enough that an observer could in principle revisit their own past, and that from this he drew a philosophical conclusion about time rather than a merely cosmological one. The three developments are: the cluster of semiclassical results and conjectures, associated above all with Hawking in the early 1990s, suggesting that quantum vacuum effects destroy would-be time machines, so that closed timelike curves may be nomologically excluded even where general relativity flatly permits them; the awkward discovery, in attempts to quantize the universe as a whole, that the fundamental equation seems to contain no time variable at all, which has led some physicists to say that temporal passage is not part of the basic furniture of the world; and the fact that observational cosmology has found no sign whatever of universal rotation—the actual universe looks nothing like the one he described. I want the letter to be disciplined rather than oracular: she should be reconstructing what a real, historically situated thinker would have made of these, distinguishing what he could have followed immediately from what would have needed explaining to him, and never letting him prophesy.

The part I most need help with is an invention of my own, which I should flag as counterfactual: in my novel a reanalysis of the microwave-background data turns up a small but nonzero global vorticity, an order of magnitude below the threshold at which the light cones would tip far enough to close, and slowly decaying rather than constant. Ruth's colleague seizes on this in a seminar and closes by saying, roughly, that nature has come as close to Gödel's universe as it can without crossing the line, and that this near-miss is more eloquent than either a clean falsification or a clean confirmation would have been. I am unsure how Gödel should receive that. As I understand him, the force of his argument lay in the mere existence of the solutions: what is at stake is whether temporal succession is an objective feature of the world at all, and that question is settled by possibility, not by what our particular cosmos happens to instantiate—in which case chronology protection, even if true, would be beside the point, and my colleague's rhetoric about a near-miss would be exactly the sentimentality he would have despised. But there is a harder version of the same worry that I want in the letter: chronology-protection arguments are carried out within semiclassical gravity, which already presupposes a time parameter, so one might object that the argument quietly assumes the very thing it is meant to secure. Would he have seized on that, and if so with what degree of suspicion? Relatedly, I want to know whether he would treat the disappearance of time from the fundamental equations as a second, independent route to his conclusion or as an altogether different animal—a formal artifact of a quantization procedure rather than a verdict about the world—and how sharply he would separate the three claims that my characters keep sliding between: that time is ideal in something like the Kantian sense, that time is unreal, and that time is emergent.

What I would like from you is two things. First, a draft of Ruth's briefing to him, five or six hundred words, in her register—formal, unshowy, mid-century epistolary, with no jargon she would not have paused to define for a reader who died in 1978—which puts the strongest version of what I take to be his likely position, including at least one place where she records that she does not follow him. Second, a short list of notes for my own use: where in that draft I have smuggled in something he could not have thought, where I have made him more mystically inclined than the texts support, and where Ruth's own reasoning is weakest. If you think the near-miss discovery is dramatically inert unless I change it in some specific way—raising it above threshold, making it a local rather than global effect, or letting it be an artifact that survives scrutiny—say so and tell me what the change buys philosophically.
