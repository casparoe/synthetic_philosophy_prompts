I teach a first-year seminar on social epistemology and I'm planning to screen Sidney Lumet's *Twelve Angry Men* (1957) alongside the Condorcet jury theorem. I want a reading of the film that takes both the formalism and the drama seriously, and I'd rather you not settle for the observation that it is "the wisdom of crowds" with a heroic holdout.

Start by laying out the theorem's structure precisely: voters whose verdicts are independent conditional on the state of the world, each individually more reliable than chance, aggregated by majority rule; the group's competence then rises in n and tends to certainty. Include the limiting case I'll call the redundancy limit — arbitrarily many perfectly correlated jurors add no accuracy whatsoever — since I want the independence premise, not the competence premise, to carry the weight of the discussion.

Now note three ways the film fails to fit the setup, and let these organise the reading. (i) The institution is unanimity, not majority: on the independence model the probability that all twelve reach the correct verdict is p raised to the twelfth, so enlarging and de-biasing the jury makes the state's case strictly harder, and the only thing that can rescue unanimity is a mechanism that correlates votes for the right reason. (ii) The theorem is symmetric between the two error types, while the film is saturated with asymmetry — reasonable doubt, the burden on the state, the insistence that it is worse to convict one innocent boy than to release a guilty one. (iii) The film never reveals whether the boy killed his father; the state of the world is withheld, so a truth-tracking vindication may simply be unavailable to it.

Here is the distinction I want you to use. Call it signal-sharing versus verdict-sharing: deliberation can exchange the private, local things a juror noticed — the marks eyeglasses leave on a nose, how long it takes a lame man to reach a door, how a switchblade is actually held — or it can exchange conclusions. The first can improve a group even while correlating it; the second can destroy accuracy by making errors redundant. Read juror 8's tactics in these terms: his refusal to argue that the boy is innocent, his re-enactments and physical experiments, his demand for a secret ballot. Then tell me honestly where the film's conversions are epistemic and where they are not. Juror 3's collapse is not a piece of evidence arriving; juror 10's tirade is the film's own picture of a shared, non-truth-tracking correlation.

Three questions I care most about:

1. If the state of the world is never disclosed, what property does the film actually vindicate — the suppression of one kind of error, the restoration of independence, or something else — and is that enough to make it usable as a Condorcet case study?
2. Does the film defend or indict unanimity? Juror 8's holdout is a veto, not an additional vote. Consider what the group's epistemic profile becomes when one veto defeats eleven, and whether his version of the holdout — a man who says he isn't sure, not a man who knows — is the version the pessimistic formal results about unanimity assume.
3. Where does the film resist the reading? It is rhetorically individualist, one conscience against the crowd, where the theorem is about numbers; several conversions turn on heat, fatigue, shame, and stage-managed demonstration. Say which scenes cannot bear epistemic weight without distortion.

Close by telling me what would change your mind: which scene, read differently, would flip your verdict about whether the film dramatises the restoration of independence or its failure, and where you think your own reading is most likely to break down.
