Design a thought experiment, set somewhere ordinary — a will, an insurance clause, a workplace rule, a hospital protocol; no skeptical scenarios, no lotteries, no fake barns, no one in the story needs to be a philosopher — in which what is practically at stake for a subject S in holding a true belief that p depends on whether S knows that p. Build the case so that the two main interest-sensitive accounts come apart in the following way: pragmatic encroachment (interest-relative invariantism) has no stable verdict about whether S knows, because supposing that S knows raises the stakes and destroys the knowledge, while supposing that S does not know removes the stakes and restores it; epistemic contextualism, by contrast, returns a single unproblematic verdict, because the operative standard is fixed by the attributor's conversational context rather than by S's practical situation.

Deliverables: (i) the scenario itself, in one paragraph; (ii) a specification of S's evidential position that places it above the low-stakes standard and below the high-stakes standard, so that the oscillation is generated rather than stipulated; (iii) the derivation of each theory's verdict, including why encroachment cycles instead of settling and why contextualism escapes the cycle; (iv) the one feature of the case doing the work, and what happens to both verdicts if you remove that feature and make the stake flat; (v) the strongest reply available to a defender of encroachment who wants a stable verdict — denying that knowledge itself can figure among the practical facts that set the standard, selecting a fixed point, supervaluating, or something else — and what that reply costs the view. Close by saying whether you think the case reveals a genuine defect in encroachment, or only a residual difference in the form of the two theories: is the instability an objection, or an artifact of asking a question the view was never designed to answer?
