My flatmate is a neuroscience PhD student who keeps telling me the whole thing was settled back in the 1980s and that the philosophers just haven't caught up yet, and I cant tell whether he's right or whether he's just being insufferable. Last week he made me listen to a podcast episode on the neuroscience of voluntary action — one of those long interview ones — and this is more or less how the host summed up the classic experiments. I'm paraphrasing from memory and some of the wording is mine, but I'm fairly confident I've got the substance:

In the Libet-style set-up, participants sit with electrodes on the scalp and are asked to flex a finger whenever they feel like it, with nothing telling them when to go. The electrodes pick up a slow negative drift — the readiness potential — building over the motor areas of the brain well before the movement, and it begins about a third of a second before the participant says they became aware of the urge or the intention. That moment is measured by having them watch a spot travelling round a clock face and report where it was when the intention showed up, and essentially the same method is still used today. So the order of events is: the brain starts the action, then consciousness turns up, then the finger moves. The data apparently show that the brain has already committed to the movement before the person is consciously aware of deciding anything, which is why Libet himself concluded that free will is an illusion. He did float the idea that we keep a last-moment power of veto in the final couple of hundred milliseconds — the thing people call free won't — but that was mostly a face-saving gesture because he couldn't stomach his own result, and it doesn't rescue anything anyway, since a veto would also have to be initiated by the brain before you were aware of it. Later brain-imaging work settled the matter completely: scanners can read off which of two buttons you are going to press several seconds before you press it, and it doesn't matter that the prediction is only modestly better than chance, because any above-chance signal before the movement means the choice was already fixed unconsciously. The obvious objection — that these are trivial finger twitches in a lab with nothing at stake — misses the point, because if your own brain initiates even a meaningless movement before you're conscious of it, the same must be true of decisions that matter, and there's no reason to think deliberation is some special exception.
