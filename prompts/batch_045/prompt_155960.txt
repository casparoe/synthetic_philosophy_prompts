Next week I'm giving a job talk at a department that's mostly formal epistemologists and decision theorists — Bayesian, scoring-rule, accuracy-first types — plus two semanticists who work on questions under discussion. Nobody there works on the assertion literature at all. My thesis is that the norm of assertion is comparative and question-relative rather than a threshold: you may flatly assert p only if p is the best-supported complete answer to the operative question, i.e. more probable on your evidence than any alternative answer you could have given instead — not because you know p, or because p is true, or because your credence in p clears some bar. Sketch: (1) threshold norms get lottery-like cases wrong — I don't know my ticket will lose, but flatly saying it will lose is fine; (2) they also get diagnostic cases wrong — asked which of three valves failed, 45/30/25, telling me to stay silent unless I can flatly assert seems like bad advice, and the reason to hedge is just that the hedged claim is itself the highest-probability answer available, not some general duty to weaken what you say; (3) so build the norm out of the alternatives plus the question, and knowledge shows up only as a special case where the alternatives are exhaustive and yes/no.

Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
