I'm on a tiny working group at my hospital drafting a policy on compensating living kidney donors. The proposal on the table is a fixed, non-negotiable payment — same sum for everyone, set centrally, no bargaining, no price discovery, no market at all — on top of the travel and lost-wages reimbursement we already do. (Our lawyer reads the federal ban on 'valuable consideration' as letting us reimburse costs but not pay for the organ itself, so part of the argument is about where that line even is.) At our last meeting a senior surgeon I like and respect shut the discussion down with 'there are some things money just shouldn't be able to buy — full stop.' Everyone sort of nodded and we moved on. I want to come back with something sharper than a counter-slogan of my own, and I dont think I can do that until I know what his sentence was actually claiming.

Here's my own attempt at splitting it:

1. The no-price reading: the kidney, or the body, or the relationship, is beyond price — no finite sum is adequate to it, it's 'priceless' in the tourism-brochure sense.
2. The blocked-exchange reading: whatever its value, this may not be exchanged for money at any price; the transaction is off-limits rather than unpriceable.
3. The corruption reading: putting money on the thing changes or degrades the thing itself, or the practice around it.
4. The motive reading: the wrong is the donor acting for money rather than out of care — money as the wrong kind of reason.
5. The coercion / dirty-background reading: given how unequal we already are, an offer like this is really pressure on the badly-off, so the consent isn't clean.

Can you lay that out properly as a menu — split any item that's secretly two claims, add the readings I've missed — and for each one say roughly whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false? Then the part I really need: for each reading, say whether it would sink *our* proposal specifically, or whether it leaves us standing. Two features of the proposal seem to me to matter and I want you to press on whether they do: (a) nobody is buying anything — the donor never meets a buyer, can't direct the kidney to any particular person, and the money comes out of a pooled fund, so there's no exchange between two parties in the ordinary sense; (b) the sum is flat, not a price, so nothing is being discovered or bid up. One of my colleagues argues the flatness actually makes it worse, since the same check is worth vastly more to someone broke than to someone comfortable — is that a real point against us, or does it cut the other way?

Also, use this as a test case, because it's where I keep getting stuck: we pay people to take on risk and discomfort all the time in other contexts — hazardous work, early-phase drug trials, egg donation — and nobody reaches for 'some things money can't buy' there. So for each reading, say whether it generalizes the way he'd want it to, or whether it either condemns stuff he'd want to keep or quietly collapses into the coercion point. And finally: which reading was he most likely reaching for, given that he used the line to end a discussion rather than to start one?
