I am a retired field biologist who, somewhat late in life, has started sitting in on a moral philosophy reading group at the university where I used to work, and I find myself more puzzled by the shape of the disagreement than by its content. In my old discipline, a controversy that ran for decades without anybody moving was almost always a symptom of one of three things: an ill-posed question, an unstated parameter that the two sides were quietly setting differently, or two research communities using a single word for two different quantities. I would like the corresponding diagnosis here, and I would like it as a diagnosis rather than as a verdict on who is right.

Our group has now spent more than a year on Singer's drowning child, and nobody has shifted. We agree on the descriptive facts and on every easy case: we all grant that one should wade in after the child, that physical distance and national borders do not by themselves carry moral weight, and that almost all of us give far less than we comfortably could. The disagreement is entirely about what follows, and it has become ritualised. One camp holds that the argument is sound and that the rest is weakness of will; the other holds that any principle which ends up requiring you to transfer resources until you are near the level of the people you are helping cannot be stating a duty at all, but is rather a portrait of sainthood that one is permitted to decline. Meanwhile the standard moves on both sides have all been played and re-played: that the pond case features a sole rescuer, an identifiable victim, and one vivid intervention, whereas famine relief involves many agents, statistical beneficiaries, and an open-ended series of transfers; that the escape clause comparing what is sacrificed with what is prevented presupposes a common currency, and the two sides are silently reckoning in different ones — units of welfare, or occurrences of a rights-violation, or some primitive scale of "moral significance"; that this clause can be read marginally, as a test applied to the next increment of sacrifice, or holistically, as a test applied to a whole course of conduct; and that the argument appears compelling or absurd depending on whether its conclusion is taken as a judgement of wrongdoing that licenses blame or as an ideal one may admire without being required to meet. I have also floated, and nobody has answered, a question about aggregation: whether duties of rescue combine across occasions at all, or whether each child is a fresh and self-contained claim that never sums into a demand on an entire life.

My own hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that the two camps share every fact and every inference and are simply operating with two different registers of "ought," and that the argument's peculiar power comes from trading on the slide between them. But I can just as well imagine that the real work is being done by a hidden empirical question about what other agents would have done anyway, or by a bare intuition about the permissibility of pursuing one's own projects that neither side can argue for. Please act as a professional moral philosopher with a strong taste for conceptual hygiene: think the matter through carefully before writing, and begin with a private inventory of three or four candidate diagnoses, including at least one that you find initially attractive and then discard — say in a single sentence why you discard it. Then give me your best diagnosis, with reasons drawn from what each side in the dispute actually commits itself to, and finish by telling me what would count as progress given that diagnosis, or else why no progress of that kind should be expected. Do not summarise the literature and do not suggest anything for me to read. Keep the entire reply under 500 words.
