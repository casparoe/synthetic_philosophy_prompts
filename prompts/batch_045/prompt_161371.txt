I'm writing my final paper for a philosophy of mind seminar on teleosemantics — the family of views that tries to fix the content of a mental state by its proper function, i.e. by what states of that type were historically selected (or, in the artifact version, designed) to do, with the function spelled out by reference to Normal conditions and to the downstream consumer mechanisms that use the state. We spent two weeks on the disjunction problem, and my own sense is that the appeal to history is supposed to be what stops a frog's firing from being equally about flies, small dark moving things, and food. My tutor thinks bringing a film into a paper on this is a stunt, so I want to do it as criticism rather than as decoration, and I want to be talked out of it where it doesn't work. No citations needed — I have the seminar readings; what I need is the reading itself.

The film I want to use is Blade Runner (1982). Three things in it seem to me to press directly on the theory, and I'd like you to take each seriously and tell me which survive: (1) The replicants' states are engineered rather than selected, and Rachael's most intimate memories turn out to have been implanted by her designers as emotional ballast. Does the artifact or derived-function move actually deliver content for her states, or does it hand content over to a designer's intention in a way that makes what her states are about depend on facts she cannot possibly occupy a position to know? If it does, is that an externalist feature the theory should happily accept, or does it show that function-based content and the subject's own take on her states come apart in a damaging way? (2) Here is the reading I'm least sure of and most attached to: the Voigt-Kampff machine, which reads involuntary responses to emotionally loaded questions in order to sort replicants from humans, looks to me like a consumer system in the technical sense — the very mechanism whose proper function the replicants' affective responses are supposed to have. If that's right, then the content of what Rachael or Leon feels in the test is at least partly fixed by the machine built to detect it, and part of the film's cruelty is that the test may be manufacturing the thing it measures. Is that a legitimate use of "consumer," or am I stretching the term past what it was introduced for? And where does the Normal-conditions apparatus bite, given that the whole interview setup is adversarial and so arguably not Normal for any system at all?

Then the part I think I need most help with: where does the film resist the reading, and what does it add that the frog-and-fly literature doesn't? I have two thoughts and I want them tested rather than flattered. First, the film's governing question may not be about content at all but about whether the replicants' inner lives matter — Roy Batty's decision at the end to save the man who has been hunting him is offered as proof of something a theory of content can't obviously cash out, and a reading that treats the film as a seminar example of biosemantics may have to say, honestly, that its real subject is consciousness and moral status, not representation. Second, the ambiguity about Deckard himself (the unicorn dream, Gaff's origami figures, the suggestion that his memories too may be somebody's design) looks like a first-personal point: no subject can tell from the inside whether her states have the right kind of history. Is that a point teleosemantics can absorb — of course not, it will say, content isn't up to the subject — or is the film quietly running a reductio? If there's a genuine cost here, I'd like it stated as one: something like the charge that a theory that cannot distinguish having a past from having a cushion for one's emotions has left out the thing the film is about. I'm aiming for about 3,000 words, so tell me which of these threads can carry a section and which should be cut.
