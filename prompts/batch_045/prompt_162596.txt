Two people I work with got into a long argument at a conference dinner about whether a government should discount future people's welfare *just because it arrives later* — the "pure" rate of time preference, as opposed to the parts of a discount rate that come from the future being richer, or from the chance that nobody's around to recieve the benefit. Neither would give an inch and I genuinely couldnt tell who was winning. Names changed, and this is roughly what got said:

(1) Marta: A positive pure rate is just discrimination by date. When someone lives isn't a morally relevant property any more than where someone lives is, so weighting their welfare down purely because it lands later is arbitrary. There's a famous old line about the practice coming from weakness of imagination rather than from any judgement about the future, and I think it's still basically right.

(2) Devin: That's too fast, because there's a real property that tracks lateness here — psychological overlap. Whoever exists in 200 years shares less with me than whoever exists tomorrow: fewer memories, less continuity of character, weaker connectedness all round. It isn't irrational to care more about a future self who is *more* the same person as you are now, and if overlap decays at roughly a constant rate, you get a principled constant pure rate out of it. That's not date-discrimination, it's discrimination by degree of identity, which happens to correlate with date.

(3) Marta: Two problems. First, social discounting is mostly about future *strangers* — people with zero psychological overlap with anyone alive today. If overlap is what grounds the discount, their welfare gets weighted at basically nothing, and that's a conclusion even you don't want. Second, the decay rate of psychological connectedness is a contingent psychological fact we could change — better records, longer lives, whatever. So your "principled" rate would drift with technology, which is a sign it was never principled.

(4) Devin: Fine, I'll take the personal and the social cases separately, because my argument for the social case is a different one. With no pure rate, and with a future that might be enormous, the sum of future welfare doesn't converge and every decision gets swamped by vanishingly small effects on the far future. You end up obliged to give up everything we have now for a microscopic shot at an astronomical payoff later. A positive pure rate is what makes the total finite and stops the whole framework from going fanatical.

(5) Marta: You don't need a *pure* rate for convergence though. The chance that we, or civilization, don't survive to collect the benefit already does that work, and it's an empirical hazard, not a judgement that later welfare matters less — it's exactly the kind of thing that already lives in the risk term of the usual decomposition. And notice what that gets you: if extinction risk is your effective discount, then the discount isn't a constant exponential at all, it tracks the hazard rate at the moment the benefit lands. So you don't get the tidy rate you wanted.

(6) Devin: The non-constancy isn't a bug, it's the honest answer, and it's worse for you than for me. If the hazard rate is high now and falls later, effective discounting comes out steep for near-term benefits and shallow for far-future ones — the exact opposite of what a constant pure rate does, and, frankly, the opposite of what anyone's patience intuitions actually say.

Can you go through this move by move: for each of the six, say whether it's clearly mistaken, weak-but-not-crazy, basically right, or clearly right, and why — not just who wins overall. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. I have a vague feeling there's a slide somewhere between "how should I weigh my own future welfare" and "how should a planner weigh different people's welfare," but I might just be misremembering the argument.
