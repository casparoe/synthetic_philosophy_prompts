I'm in the first year of a philosophy PhD, and I came to it from a chemistry degree, which is why I keep getting drafted into arguments about inter-theoretic reduction in a reading group some of us run on levels of nature. I wrote a short paper defending a deliberately old-fashioned, deflationary line — reduction is connectability plus derivability, and the extra conditions people bolt on are really just embarrassment about how thin that is — and a seminar-mate has spent two sessions turning that line into an argument whose conclusion I can't accept. I'd be grateful for a diagnosis.

Here is the argument as we've hammered it out. Say that a base theory T1 is *expressively closed* for a target theory T2 when, for every non-logical predicate F of T2, T1 can express the disjunction of all the T1-states that realize F — possibly an infinite disjunction, possibly over wildly heterogeneous states. Call the bridge principles you get that way *stowaway* bridges, because everything the special science wanted to say has been stowed away inside the bridge rather than derived from below. Then: (1) The textbook Nagelian account says T2 reduces to T1 just in case (a) connectability: for every predicate F of T2 there is a predicate or definable condition of T1 coextensive with F, and (b) derivability: T2's laws, restated using those bridge principles, are derivable from T1's laws. (2) If physics is expressively closed for a special science, then stowaway bridges are available for all of its predicates. (3) Physics is expressively closed for every special science: whatever realizes being a liquid, being a gene, or being five dollars in change is some physical configuration, and the disjunction of all such configurations is itself a physical condition. (4) Physics is complete in the usual sense: every physical fact is entailed by the total microphysical facts, so once a special-science generalization has been restated over its stowaway bridge, the restatement comes out entailed by physics, and derivability is automatic. (5) Therefore every special science reduces to physics — including theories nobody wants to vindicate. A theory whose central predicate has an empty extension is the worst case: the empty disjunction is trivially a physical condition, and its generalizations are vacuously true and trivially derivable, so an account of reduction in this form cannot say what was wrong with phlogiston. (6) Worse, coextensiveness is a symmetric relation and restatement runs in whatever direction the expressive resources allow, so if connectability and derivability are all there is to reduction, reduction is symmetric, and thermodynamics-reduces-to-statistical-mechanics and statistical-mechanics-reduces-to-thermodynamics stand or fall together. (7) But reduction is neither trivial nor symmetric: that chemistry reduces to physics was a substantive empirical achievement rather than an armchair theorem, and reduction is supposed to track which level is more fundamental. (8) So at least one of (1) through (4) has to go. My seminar-mate thinks the escape is (5): failed theories don't reduce because their terms fail to refer. I don't think that rescues anything, since I can rerun (2)–(4) with a theory whose terms do refer and whose laws are still false, and in any case an account on which referential vacuity is the only disqualifier is not an account I want.

So: which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please work through the chain in order rather than starting from your favorite theory of reduction. For the step you give up, explain why it looked innocent — what neighboring, true claim it trades on — and then price at least two alternatives, including the one you'd take second. If you reject (2) or (3), say precisely what a bridge principle has to be over and above being a true coextension, and whether that further demand can be stated without smuggling in the irreducibility I was trying to argue against. If you reject (1), say what replaces connectability and derivability — nomic necessity of the bridges, ontological identification of the properties, mechanistic explanation, something else — and what each replacement gives up, especially for the paradigm cases like temperature and mean molecular kinetic energy. I am most attached to (1) as a description of what reduction has usually been taken to be and to (4) as a claim about the completeness of physics; if I cannot keep both, tell me which attachment is doing the damage. Aim for about 700 to 900 words, and end with a single machine-readable last line with nothing after it, in exactly this form: Verdict: step (N) — the flaw, in eight words or fewer.
