I spent my career in observational astrophysics and have been reading philosophy for a few years since retiring, mostly probability theory and cosmology. I keep running into a pair of argumentative moves that I cannot get straight, and I want to know whether what I have noticed is already recognized, and if it is not, what it should be called and how exactly it should be cut.

First instance. An author argues that among two cosmological hypotheses otherwise equally well supported — one on which the universe contains about 10^11 observers, one on which it contains about 10^24 — I should be overwhelmingly confident in the second, on the grounds that a crowded universe is enormously better at placing an observer like me in existence at all. A chapter later, the same author argues that I should regard myself as a random draw from the humans who ever live, that my birth rank is therefore data, and that this supports the conclusion that our species will end soon rather than spreading through the galaxy. I checked the interaction: applying the first move inside the second argument cancels its conclusion, and applying the second move inside the first argument produces no shift at all, since under both cosmological hypotheses every observer has the same evidence. So the two are not two ways of saying one thing. They pull in opposite directions, and which one is reached for determines the answer.

Second instance, scaled down so I could check my arithmetic. A researcher creates either ten or ten thousand subjects, all treated identically; you are told you are one of them and asked which. The existence move gives a thousand to one for the larger batch. The random-draw move gives nothing, because every subject would have the same evidence either way. But if the researcher instead tells you your serial number is 7, the random-draw move suddenly bites hard and in the opposite direction: 7 is a far more surprising draw from ten thousand than from ten. So the bite of each move seems to depend on whether the datum is that I am, or where in the series I am.

My reconstruction is that the first move multiplies the prior odds by the ratio of the observer numbers the hypotheses allow for, while the second divides them by roughly that same ratio, which is why they cancel. I do not trust that reconstruction, and I do not know what I am looking at. So:

1. Are these two moves established, and do they have names? Is the cancellation a known result or an artifact of my crudeness?

2. What exactly is the datum in each case? Are they genuinely rival principles, or one principle plus a choice about what to condition on, or one principle plus a choice about the pool from which I am to be treated as a random draw? I suspect the pool does all the real work, since drawing it wider or narrower appears to move the conclusion anywhere one likes; if so, is there any principled constraint on the pool, and does the absence of one discredit the whole family of arguments?

3. Is the switching I noticed — one author, two rules, opposite conclusions, selected to suit — a recognized pathology with a name of its own, or does it need one? If it needs one, I want a definition and boundary cases, not a label.

4. Where does each move break? I can think of hypotheses on which the number of observers is infinite; pools that include observers who never in fact exist; extra observers whose evidence differs from mine; and extra observers I could never have been, being of another epoch, another species, or outside my causal reach. Does the machinery say anything disciplined about these, or does it simply return whatever the pool choice was set to?

5. A subsidiary puzzle: the datum in the first move is something I have known since childhood, long before I held any view about cosmology, and I do not see how it can be doing confirmatory work now.

I am not after a bibliography or a term to look up. I want the joints: what this phenomenon is, what it is not, which nearby established distinctions it resembles and how it differs from each, and where its edges lie.
