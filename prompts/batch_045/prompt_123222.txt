Bit of an odd request but here goes. I finished a clerkship over the summer and in January I start as a staff attorney at a state supreme court, and the judge I'll be working for has asked each of us to write a one-page memo on our own interpretive methodology before the next argument sitting — she says she wants to know in advance where we'll predictably disagree with her. I've been trying to write mine and I keep finding that the pieces don't obviously fit together. A friend from law school who is a committed purposivist read my draft and said it's just purposivism with extra steps and that I'm kidding myself; I dont think she's right, but I cant tell which bit she's pointing at. I mostly absorbed this stuff from half-reading a well-known textualist treatise in law school and then copying the judge I clerked for, so it's entirely possible I've picked up things that don't cohere. Here's roughly where I land:

(1) The meaning of a statute is what a competent, legally informed reader at the time of enactment would have taken the words to convey. It's fixed at enactment and doesn't get updated, so for new technology I ask what the enacting-era reader would have understood the relevant kind to cover, and I don't buy dynamic updating.
(2) Relatedly, I never read legislative history: no committee reports, no floor statements, no sponsor's statements.
(3) But I am fine with dictionaries, corpus-linguistics searches, treatises, and how the same phrase was used in neighboring statutes around the same time.
(4) I think you're also allowed to ask what that reader would have expected the words to cover in a case like the one in front of you, not just their dictionary sense — so in the old no-vehicles-in-the-park hypothetical I'd want to know whether an enacting-era reader would have understood the ban to reach an ambulance driving in to reach someone having a heart attack.
(5) I like the absurdity doctrine and the scrivener's-error fix: where literal application would be genuinely absurd the court should read it sensibly. I'd include the old style of case where a statute bans importing foreign contract laborers under a contract for labor or service and a congregation hires a minister from abroad — I think it shouldn't apply there even though the words on their face cover it.
(6) I think constitutional avoidance and clear-statement rules are legitimate and important: the legislature has to speak clearly before we read a statute to preempt state law, to tread on state prerogatives, or to raise a serious constitutional doubt.
(7) I'm happy with the textual canons — whole text, noscitur a sociis, eiusdem generis — but I think the rule of lenity is mostly junk and should be a last-resort tiebreaker after you've found real ambiguity. It annoys me when a court narrows a criminal statute that looks clear to me.
(8) I think statutory precedent should bind fairly strongly: once a court has settled what a statute means, a later court shouldn't reopen it just because it now thinks that reading was wrong, and the fact that the legislature has left the reading alone for years counts in favor of keeping it.
(9) When the text really is ambiguous I pick the reading that best serves the statute's purpose — but only a purpose you can find in the enacted text itself, preamble, findings, title, not one reconstructed from anyone's testimony.
(10) And the reason I like the package is that it constrains judges, it's more democratically legitimate than the alternatives, and legislatures are so bad at anticipating cases that asking what they would have wanted is usually asking a question with no answer.

Can you audit this for me? What I want is the pairs that are actually in tension, ranked by how severe the tension is, with what would have to give in each case and what giving it would cost me — not a list where everything gets flagged as equally suspicious. I also want to know which pairs only look inconsistent and why, because my friend is not going to accept it's all fine without an argument, and I'd rather hand her the good version of that response. Tell me anywhere I'm using a label to smuggle back in something I said I rejected in (1) or (2), and whether (10)'s rationale is doing work my other commitments quietly undercut. If there's a single revision that cleans up the most, say which one. And please don't just tell me to become a purposivist: I want to know what breaks, not to switch teams. No case names or reading recommendations, I have plenty of those.
