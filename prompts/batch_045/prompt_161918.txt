The question they were fighting over: can "avoid side effects" be defined without a full specification of the user's values?

They kept returning to one example. A greenhouse AI is told: "keep the seedlings alive until the botanist gets back on Friday." At midday the greenhouse starts overheating badly. The AI can open the vents — but that lets in pollen and cross-pollinates an experimental strain the botanist has been keeping pure, ruining three years of work. Or it can rig up shade cloth, using the last of a supply bought with grant money. Or it can do nothing and lose roughly a third of the seedlings.

Here's how it went.

(1) Marta: The whole point of impact measures is that you don't need a theory of value. Penalise distance from the inaction baseline — how different the world ends up from what it would have been if the agent had just sat there. That's a descriptive relation between world states, not a value judgement. If the task is "carry the box to the door," the plan that carries it there while smashing the vase gets penalised and the plan that carries it there without smashing the vase doesn't. No utility function over vases required.

(2) Dev: Two problems. First, inaction isn't a neutral baseline in general. Here, doing nothing is a world with a third of the seedlings dead, so every plan scores as enormous impact and the penalty can't tell the careful plan from the careless one. Second, and worse: "distance between world states" needs units. Is moving a vase three metres a bigger change than spending forty dollars? Than closing a door? Whether you count atoms displaced, energy dissipated, or how many of the user's later options got closed off, you've silently decided that some kinds of change matter more than others. That weighting is a value judgement that's just been pushed one level down into the choice of metric. You haven't avoided specifying value, you've hidden it.

(3) Marta: Too fast. There's a version that gets the weights without hand-coding them — the attainable-utility style approach. Don't guess which changes matter; measure how much the action shrinks the set of outcomes that could still be brought about afterwards, for the user, averaged across a very broad family of possible goals. Loss of optionality is bad relative to almost any goal, so you don't need to know which goal is the real one. That's about as close to value-free as anything in this area gets, and the greenhouse case comes out right: cross-pollination is irreversible so it's a big option-loss, shade cloth is a small one.

(4) Dev: "Almost" is doing a lot of work. Averaging over a family of goals still needs a distribution over that family, and the distribution is where value climbs back in: throw in enough exotic goals and the average is nonsense, restrict it to human-ish goals and you've built in a theory of what matters. And optionality-preservation has a structural bias — it penalises exactly the actions we most want an agent to take. Signing a lease, taking a job, having a kid, locking a door: all of these destroy options, often on purpose. Penalise raw option-loss and you get a coward that does nothing; penalise it only past a threshold and you have to say which option-losses are acceptable, which is value again.

(5) Marta: OK, I concede all of that, so let me restate the claim more carefully, because I don't think the concession costs me much. Nobody serious ever promised a fully value-free impact measure. The defensible claim is weaker and still worth having: an impact penalty can act as a tie-breaker among plans that are roughly equally good by the task's own standard. Regularisation, not optimisation. You only need it to be right about the gross cases — don't wreck the experiment, don't spend all the money, don't make irreversible changes to things nobody mentioned — and for that a crude, slightly wrong metric is fine. Insisting on zero value assumptions is a strawman, since every design choice in the system, including the decision to build it, embeds some.

(6) Dev: I basically agree with that, which is why I think the original question was badly posed. But here's the bit I actually wanted to argue, and I don't think we got to it. I think impact is the wrong object. What we care about isn't change, it's unconsented-to change. So: measure the cost to the affected party of restoring their own options — call it the reopening cost — and penalise that instead of distance from a baseline. In the greenhouse, the number that matters isn't how different the world is; it's how much the botanist would have to spend, in money and in years, to get back to where she was. Cross-pollination scores enormously, moving a chair scores seconds. And this is the same shape as how we already handle externalities between people.

(7) Marta: It's elegant but I think it collapses. Who counts as an affected party — future people, the seedlings, the grant agency? And what about a change every affected party likes but nobody would ever pay to reverse: suppose the AI quietly fixes the irrigation while it's at it, so everyone's options are strictly larger. Reopening cost is zero or undefined, yet that's still exactly the kind of unilateral action we were worried about. Worse, cost-to-the-party is denominated in that party's preferences, so now you have to model every affected party's preferences — a bigger value-specification job than the one you started with, plus a theory of consent on top. You've swapped a hard problem for a harder one.

(8) Dev: Partly fair. But I'd rather have a measure whose value assumptions I can point at and argue about than one hiding inside a distance metric. And notice what your own objection shows: baseline-distance, option-loss and reopening cost all fail to be value-free, and they disagree with each other in cases. So the real question was never whether we can define impact without values — we can't — but which smuggled assumptions are cheapest to get approximately right, and what to do when the measures disagree. That's my conclusion: no, and the honest programme is making the smuggled stuff explicit and checkable rather than eliminating it.

(9) Marta: Maybe. Although there's a move on the table that dodges the whole argument and I don't think either of us named it at the time: don't measure impact, make the agent stop and ask. Give it a small budget of unapproved change, and when the budget is spent it halts and checks in. Then "avoid side effects" isn't a value-laden comparison of world states at all, it's a procedural rule, and the only number you have to specify is how big the budget is.

(10) Dev: That relocates the problem rather than solving it. The budget size just is the value threshold, dressed up as a hyperparameter. It also fails exactly where you'd want it most — when the process is fast enough that stopping isn't an option, or when stopping is itself the costly thing. And an agent that consults you about everything is useless, so the budget will in practice be set by how much interruption users tolerate, which is a fact about patience, not about value.
