Me and my friend got into a massive argument last night about utilitarianism — I was defending it — and somewhere around hour two he pasted this into the chat as his summary of why the whole thing supposedly collapses:

"Rawls's point is that utilitarianism adds up everyone's happiness into one grand total, treating society as if it were a single person making trade-offs within their own life. But persons are separate: there is nobody who experiences the total, and no one life that all the sacrifices and benefits belong to. So utilitarianism isn't really a moral theory for the interpersonal case at all — it's just the principle of rational prudence for one person, sneakily extended to a lot of different people, and it only feels convincing because we half-imagine that we are that combined super-person. Once you actually take the separateness of persons seriously there's nothing left: a utilitarian can't say that one distribution is worse than another with the same total, which is why the theory can't even explain why slavery is wrong."

I feel like this is too strong somewhere but I cant locate it, and I'm not going to win the rematch by vibes. Can you go through it piece by piece and tell me what a careful utilitarian would happily grant, what they'd say is a conflation or an overstatement, and whether his "nothing left" conclusion really follows from the bits that are right? I also want to know whether this objection is actually a different thing from the plain complaint that utilitarianism ignores how a total gets distributed, or just a dramatic way of saying the same thing.

And while we were arguing I made up a pair of cases, and I want to know whether they show anything or whether I was just being weird at 1am. Joined case: suppose two human bodies share a single unified stream of consciousness — not two minds taking turns, and not telepathy, but genuinely one subject of experience who sees through four eyes and steers both bodies, and whose pains and pleasures are had together rather than added up. Split case: one person whose consciousness permanently divides into two streams that can never communicate, so there are two subjects of experience where there used to be one, sharing one continuously living body.

My hunch is that whatever is doing the work in the separateness objection is not the number of bodies or the number of legal persons, but something like the unity of a single conscious life — in the joined case I want to say you may add things up after all, and in the split case I want to say you suddenly may not. If that's right, isn't my friend (and maybe the objection itself) resting the whole thing on a metaphysical claim about what a person is, rather than on a moral one — and does that make the objection weaker, stronger, or just a different objection than the one he thinks he's making?
