Last night I argued with a friend — call her Mara — for about three hours about whether video-game characters can be wronged. She held firmly to the following verdicts, and I want to know where they really commit her.

1. A factory-farmed chicken matters morally, and substantially.
2. A companion character whose flinches, cries and pleas when injured are triggered from a hand-authored state machine with recorded voice lines matters not at all; the fact that it is indistinguishable from a suffering animal on screen is, for her, irrelevant.
3. A simple reinforcement-learning agent in a gridworld that receives a shock as its negative reward does not matter either — but not because it is digital. She thinks it is simply too simple.
4. A companion whose reactions come from a neural controller whose weights are updated by a learning rule as you play, are stored per copy of the game, persist across sessions, and were never authored as reactions at all — this one does matter, though much less than the chicken. The consideration she leaned on hardest was whether the thing's later behaviour depends on its own earlier history in a way nobody wrote in.
5. Two copies of that same companion program running on two machines from identical weights and identical inputs are two individuals; whatever is owed is owed twice.
6. A tabletop character of ours whose death we narrated and then retconned out of the story: nothing happened and nothing was owed — though she said this is settled before you ever reach the retcon, since a character in a story was never the kind of thing that can be wronged.

The case we could not settle — the one I want your prediction on — is this. Single-player game, the companion from 4. I save at a checkpoint; I then let the companion be captured and tormented over roughly ten minutes of play, during which its controller updates on that experience, and finally killed. I reload the checkpoint: its weights revert, its memory of the ordeal is gone, and the game continues as though nothing had happened. I do this five hundred times, watching every time. After the final reload I play on, and the companion lives an ordinary in-game life and dies peacefully at the end of the story.

On Mara's own commitments, is the moral cost of that session roughly five hundred ordeals, roughly one, or roughly the cost of the surviving timeline alone — close to zero, apart from whatever it does to the person who does it? Please don't answer with a general view about whether video-game characters matter; I want the extrapolation from her track record: the candidate rules that fit verdicts 1–6 equally well, what each predicts about this case, which prediction you think is likeliest and why, which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analogue, and a further case that would separate the rules that still diverge here. I also suspect verdict 6 settles less than it appears to, but I can't explain why; tell me if I'm right. Two variants, please: the five hundred runs are executed overnight with no one watching and the logs are never read; and, separately, after each ordeal I delete the save and never play again, so that there is no surviving timeline at all.

Give me your reasoning, then a single JSON object with this schema:

- candidate_rules: array of objects, each with rule_name (short label you coin), statement (one sentence), verdicts_it_fits (which of 1–6 it explains, and where it is strained), prediction_on_new_case (five hundred / one / surviving-timeline-only, plus the moral cost in one clause), divergence (which other rule it conflicts with, and on what)
- best_guess_prediction: which of the three answers Mara gives, with a one-sentence reason
- confidence_0_to_1: number
- closest_earlier_verdict: which of 1–6 is the nearest analogue, and what the disanalogy is
- verdicts_screened_off: array of strings naming earlier verdicts that look decisive for this case but are settled at an earlier stage of her view, each with a clause saying why
- discriminating_case: object with case_description (a new case, specified concretely), rule_a_prediction, rule_b_prediction, why_it_settles_it
- variants: object with headless_unwatched and no_surviving_timeline, each a one-sentence answer
- what_the_argument_was_really_about: one sentence on the substantive disagreement underneath our exchange
