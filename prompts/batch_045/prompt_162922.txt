For thirty years I have kept, alongside each novel, a ledger of characters, dates, and promises made to the reader. I consult it constantly, trust it without checking, and could not finish a book without it: by every functional test I have seen proposed for counting an external store as part of a mind, it qualifies. Yet it does not feel like mine. Deprived of it I feel impeded rather than diminished; when it misleads me I feel thwarted by something other, not answerable for a failure of my own. The asymmetry runs the other way too: a continuity file compiled by a collaborator now feels more mine than the early drafts I wrote myself and no longer recognize.

I gather that philosophers hold, on roughly those conditions — reliable availability, effortless access, automatic endorsement, and some history of having been endorsed — that such a ledger is as much a constituent of thinking as biological memory is. My case appears to meet all of them and still seems to lack whatever makes a state mine. Does this configuration have an established name, and is it treated as an objection to that view or as simply beside the point?

A label alone will not satisfy me. I want to know which of those conditions, if any, is meant to carry ownership, and why my case shows that it does not; whether dependence and felt ownership can genuinely come apart as I have described them, or whether I have misdescribed one of the two; where the line falls between my ledger and a reference work I consult just as often and trust just as implicitly; and what my unrecognizable early drafts are a case of. If nothing established fits, say so plainly, and give the phenomenon a name, a definition, and its boundary cases.
