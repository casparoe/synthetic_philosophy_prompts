So I've been chewing on the Platonia setup and the Luring Lottery again (twenty people, one telegram, huge prize only if exactly one person sends one; and the lottery version where the pot gets split by however many postcards show up). I've drifted into a position I'm not sure I can actually defend, and I want it stress-tested rather than affirmed.

The position: superrationality isn't just a party trick for degenerate cases where you're playing an exact copy of yourself. Its real content shows up in the *diluted* case. Suppose only some fraction of the N players reason the superrational way and the rest are plain expected-value calculators, and nobody knows the fraction — each player only has their own rough guess at it, and their guesses disagree. My claim is that the superrational recommendation then degrades smoothly in your confidence: as your estimate of the fraction of fellow superrationalists slides toward zero, what you should do slides continuously back into what ordinary CDT tells you to do. So superrationality contains CDT as a limiting case instead of merely contradicting it on puzzles. What I like about this is that it makes the view stick its neck out: it predicts a particular *shape* — no cliff, no threshold fraction where the answer jumps — and it predicts something its rivals don't, namely that with mixed populations you should be able to see the gradation, at least in principle.

What would count against it? Be specific, and rank it. I want the fatal stuff separated from the merely awkward stuff. In particular:

- Does the diluted case even have a coherent symmetric solution? Once the superrational types have different posteriors about how many of them there are, they'll pick different mixed strategies, and they can't correlate their randomness because there's no shared signal — each one is stuck implementing a private coin flip. I keep going back and forth on whether the whole thing just collapses there, or whether there's still a fixed point. If it does collapse, is that a problem for the pure case too, retroactively?
- Is the smoothness claim actually true, or is there a threshold effect I'm papering over by how I chose to parameterize the dilution? And if the only reason it looks smooth is that I built the parameterization that way, say so — that's exactly the kind of insulation I'm worried about.
- What would a *defender* of this concede as counting against it, as opposed to what a CDT person would say? I can already predict the CDT objections and I don't need those. I also don't need "just use a better decision theory" — I want something concrete about this particular claim.
- Is it empirically exposed at all? The informal runs people have posted about over the years are tiny and wildly self-selected and I don't trust them. But tell me what an actual experimental result would have to look like to move me, and be honest about which results I'd just rationalize away. Personal note: I'd find real data more persuasive than another clever case, so push hardest there.
- Finally: say plainly whether I've qualified this into unfalsifiability, and if I have, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or whether it's fine given what the view is trying to do.

Talk me out of it, or tell me where it's stronger than I think. Either way, no bibliography — I've read the obvious posts and I'll fill in references myself.
