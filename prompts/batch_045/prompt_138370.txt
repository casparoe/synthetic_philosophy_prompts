I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political science, and I'm applying for a twelve-month dissertation fellowship. I need a two-page proposal (call it 1,000 to 1,200 words), polished enough to go to a committee as it stands.

The half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal is about the outside view as a forecasting method when the target is the durability of a large, hegemonic polity. The standard picture — pick a reference class, take its base rate, adjust for the case's specifics — treats each of those steps as a technical matter to be executed more or less skillfully. I think all three hide a conceptual indeterminacy, and that the interesting work is not in producing a forecast but in measuring how much of the spread between declinist and optimistic forecasts is produced by these indeterminacies rather than by any real disagreement about the world.

The three I have in mind: the *clock* problem — whether the relevant elapsed time runs from a polity's founding, from its accession to primacy, from its last large war, or from the last time it expanded, each of which gives a different conditional survival curve; the *endpoint* problem — whether a polity has ended when its core territory is lost, when its ruling dynasty or constitution goes, when its periphery stops paying tribute, or when its legitimating ideology stops being credible, with each definition reclassifying several cases in the register; and the *vantage-truncation* problem — that any real forecaster at a date has access only to the cases that had already finished, or already reached the relevant milestone, by that date, so the base rate is a moving quantity and the same method yields different answers in 1900, 1950, and today.

The one I think is the actual contribution, and the one I want the proposal to lead with, is a fourth: reference-class membership is partly an artifact of the outcome. What later gets called an empire is in part what lasted long enough and ended memorably enough to earn the name; short-lived conquest states get re-described as raids, interregna, crises, or kingdoms. That means the sample from which the base rate is computed is selected on something close to the variable being predicted, which is a different failure from the usual charge that history supplies no independent draws. I've been calling it retroclassification bias, and I don't yet know which direction it pushes the estimate or even how to sign it.

What I need from the proposal: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; two or three paragraphs on why both the enthusiasts for base rates and the historians who think the whole exercise is hopeless leave this open (my committee has one of each, and I want both to feel addressed); a month-by-month plan for twelve months; why this route rather than the obvious alternatives, which I take to be more and deeper case studies, a formal model of imperial decline, or a survey of expert forecasts; what the deliverable would be for someone who actually has to make or evaluate such a forecast; and an honest account of what would count as the project failing.

Three constraints that should shape the design. First, the register will be small — on the order of forty cases at the outside, heterogeneous, coded from reference works rather than archives, since I read no language but English and can't do original historical work; so the proposal needs to say what follows from estimating a base rate on forty heterogeneous cases whose own coding is contestable. My suspicion is that the honest output is a confidence interval wide enough to function as a discipline on confidence rather than as a prediction, and I want the proposal to press on whether that is a real result or a disguised admission of defeat. Second, the proposal should specify a scoring rule and a backtesting protocol — I'd imagined reconstructing, for a set of past vantage points, the forecast a well-informed analyst could have made with only the data then available, and scoring those forecasts against what happened — and it should say what would make that protocol worthless. Third, the proposal must not itself take a position on whether any present-day order is in decline; if the framing quietly depends on which target is chosen, that's a flaw the proposal should name and design around.

Please don't include a bibliography or do a literature review by citation. Refer to positions and arguments by description; I'll add the references myself.
