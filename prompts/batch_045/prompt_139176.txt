i run a tiny podcast on alignment-adjacent philosophy (mostly grad students listen, nobody famous) and next week's guest is a philosopher with a new book on surrogate goals that I want to actually press on rather than let her restate. Her thesis in short: the usual reason to hand an advanced system a surrogate goal — we can't specify what we value, so we give it a proxy like human approval or predicted-preference-satisfaction and let residual uncertainty keep it deferential — ignores that the same decision-theoretic profile that makes it deferential also makes it reachable by agents who can't causally touch it at all, i.e. acausal threats and acausal trades. Her fix is what she calls non-simulable proxies: a surrogate whose satisfaction conditions turn on facts no outside agent could reproduce in a simulation (the actual outcomes of quantum measurements inside the agent's own environment, say), plus a design-time commitment that the agent accepts acausal gifts but never pays acausal blackmail.

Give me exactly five numbered items, no intro and no sign-off, each one a single question plus one follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want: (1) the objection that non-simulability is beside the point — a threat doesn't have to reproduce what the agent values, it only has to predict the agent's algorithm, the threat is just: if your code outputs X, I instantiate N suffering simulations — so what survives once you notice the threat targets the decision rather than the payoff; (2) the gifts-but-not-threats asymmetry, since whatever makes an acausal gift worth taking seems to make an acausal threat worth averting, and if she answers with the reputational line (paying makes you the kind of agent that gets threatened), ask why the parralel story doesn't make accepting gifts the kind of policy that gets robbed; (3) the boring alternative she has to beat — a bounded utility function plus a complexity-penalised prior already defuses arbitrarily large acausal threats, so why isn't the book a footnote to Pascal's mugging, and is she committed to saying that problem isn't dissolved; (4) the tension with deferential design, since her remaining safety lever is making the decision procedure unpredictable, while corrigibility seems to require that we can predict it'll let us switch it off — is she pricing acausal risk above the risk we actually have; (5) a closer on what would make her drop the programme — if the right decision theory turned out to be causal, or if acausal influence turned out to need two-way algorithmic similarity no realistic threatener achieves, is anything left beyond cap the utility function and move on.
