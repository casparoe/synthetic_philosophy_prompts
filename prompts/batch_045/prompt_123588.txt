Design a 90-minute workshop activity for a university effective altruism reading group: about 18 undergraduates, mixed familiarity with the movement, one facilitator, nothing beyond a whiteboard, printed cards and poker chips. The aim is to make participants feel the pull of three internal critiques of effective altruism — the measurability bias, the institutional (systemic-change) critique, and the counterfactual fragility of earning to give — without naming any of them until the debrief and without any lecture up front.

Run it as a three-phase allocation game in small groups of four or five, scored in points with a trivial prize. Phase one: a portfolio of roughly a dozen fictional intervention cards, half of them carrying precise cost-per-unit-impact figures and half described only in qualitative terms, plus an optional audit move in which a group spends part of its grant budget to replace a card's point estimate with a wide interval that is harder to compare against the others. Phase two: add a systemic option — lobbying, norm change, institution building — whose payoff is capped, delayed, and unlocked only above a funding threshold that no single group can reach alone. Phase three: each student individually picks one of four fictional career paths, each card listing salary, plausible giving fraction, what the work itself does to the world, and whether a named replacement would take the job if they declined.

Give me the rules, the materials, a payoff scheme needing no real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the exact facilitator script for the reveals. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the three phases onto the three critiques, including the moves participants made that you predicted and the ones they invented. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and be honest about which part of each critique the mechanics cannot deliver and that I will have to state plainly myself.
