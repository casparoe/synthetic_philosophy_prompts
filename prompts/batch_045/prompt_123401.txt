I should say something about why I'm asking before I ask it. I'm a novelist, two books in, working on a third set in a fictional town in Hesse where the 1922 memorial to the war dead is being retrofitted with a steel panel listing the town's deported Jewish families, and where my protagonist Anke — the municipal archivist, who is the one who has to find the names — discovers her own late grandfather's initials on bills of lading for the freight cars. A second strand involves a Berlin-based curator of Turkish-German background who is brought in to advise on the design and who has done similar work in Istanbul. I've been reading my way into comparative memory culture — Germany's long, self-dramatizing working-through of the Nazi past, and the standard contrasts drawn with Japan, Turkey, and the American South — partly out of research and partly because I don't want the novel to reproduce a complacent German story about how thoroughly Germany has faced its history.

What I'd like from you is a reading of Toni Morrison's Beloved through that comparative frame. Beloved puts an enormous, legally sanctioned historical wrong at the centre of a domestic novel and then handles it almost entirely without institutions: no trial, no commission, no monument, no curriculum, no newspaper. The reckoning happens in a house, on the body, in the clearing where Baby Suggs preaches, through the community of women who come to drive the ghost out, and through what the novel calls rememory — a past that is not owned by the person who remembers it and that keeps happening whether or not anyone chooses to recall it. Set against the German case, and against the vocabulary that comes with it (Jaspers's fourfold distinction among criminal, political, moral and metaphysical guilt; the idea of a society working through rather than merely mastering or burying its past; Adorno's suspicion that the official working-through of the past can itself become a way of being done with it), Beloved looks like a picture of memory-work that has no state in it at all. And set against the South's monument culture, its memorial associations and its counter-monuments, it looks stranger still: the only carved thing in the book is the headstone Sethe buys, and the word she can afford on it. So I want to know what the novel is actually claiming. Is its institutional emptiness an indictment of institutions, a claim that some wrongs are only ever dealt with at the scale of a house and a body, or simply a consequence of where in time it is set? Would a Japanese or a Turkish Beloved — one in which the wrong at the centre is still officially unacknowledged, or is officially remembered as something suffered rather than something done — have to be a different novel with different machinery, or does the form survive the transplant? And where, honestly, does the comparison strain: is the American South the right fourth term next to Germany, Japan and Turkey, or is the unit of comparison wrong?

I should also give you the two ideas I've been carrying around, since I want them tested rather than flattered. The first is a distinction between what I've been calling archive-wrongs — wrongs that are documentable, attributable, countable, the kind for which German-style trials, textbooks and memorials are adequate instruments — and weather-wrongs, which are diffuse, ambient, breathed by everyone and authored by no one, and which perhaps turn bureaucratic or monumental memory-work into a way of not noticing them. The second, related thought is that Beloved may depend on a post-abolition condition: the wrong at its centre has been legally ended and publicly named, which is precisely why the state can be absent from the reckoning and why the haunting can be a private, domestic one. Please tell me whether either of these survives contact with the novel, where each breaks down, and whether the second one quietly makes Beloved a more optimistic, more American book than I've been taking it for. I'd also like you to separate, as you go, what is settled among people who actually work on these memory cultures from what is genuinely contested — I am aware, for instance, that whether German memory culture is a distinctive achievement or a flattering national myth is fought over, and I'd rather you flagged that than leaned on it — and to say plainly when you are extrapolating beyond what you can support, or when one of my framings is my invention rather than something with scholarly currency. Don't give me citations or a reading list; I have a library, and what I need is the reading.
