I convene a graduate seminar each spring in which Moore's 1925 essay A Defence of Common Sense is read alongside the remarks on certainty that Wittgenstein wrote at the very end of his life and that were published posthumously as On Certainty. Every year the seminar arrives at the same question and every year I give the same unsatisfying answer. The question is what distinguishes a proposition that functions as a hinge from a proposition that is merely very well confirmed, or merely untested, or merely something nobody in a given community has thought to question. Wittgenstein's two images — enquiry turning on propositions the way a door turns on hinges that are not themselves at issue, and our certainties forming the bed of a river, part hard rock and part gravel that the current shifts about — strike me as evocative rather than diagnostic, and I have never managed to extract a test from them. So I want to try the other route: I will set out my verdicts on a set of cases and ask what principle, if any, they add up to.

One. A first-year student announces that he cannot rule out that his apparent memories of four years at this university are artifacts of an elaborate deception carried out by his family, and asks the seminar to take the possibility seriously. My verdict: there is nothing here to take up. But I equally reject the Moorean reply — I do not think it is a piece of knowledge that he was not deceived, because I do not think anything is being claimed or denied. And I do not want to say his sentence is meaningless; I want to say that he has stepped outside the practice in which the words have a use, and that the right response is to bring him back rather than to answer him.

Two. A colleague in archaeology says, of the dig she directed, that she knows the 2019 season located the second cistern. Verdict: an ordinary empirical knowledge claim, extremely well supported, defeasible in principle and on occasion in fact. Certain, but nothing like case one.

Three. The Verrill Archive: a forty-year oral-history project built on the assumption that the field tapes recorded between 1961 and 1963 were destroyed in a flood. Nobody had evidence for it; a research assistant who once suggested checking was told the question was absurd; the project's whole apparatus of citation, dating and transcription was organized around it. In 2019 a crate of the tapes turned up in a church basement and the project reorganized within a year. Verdict: this behaved exactly like a hinge, was plainly empirical, and was false.

Four. My certainty that the other people in the seminar room are conscious strikes me as hinge-like — not based on evidence, not the sort of thing I could check. My certainty that the university's payroll system has recorded my salary correctly strikes me as empirical: I could audit it, and after an error two years ago I did. The contrast is firm for me even though, on any given Tuesday, I am equally unlikely to doubt either.

Five. The case I keep returning to. A close friend, raised as the daughter of the man who raised her, learned at fifty-two, from a genetic test taken for medical reasons, that he was not her biological father. For fifty years the proposition functioned for her exactly as a hinge functions: she had no evidence for it, doubts about it were not live, its denial was unintelligible to her. Then it was overturned by a test. I want to say both that it was a hinge and that it was an empirical proposition with a determinate truth value all along — and I cannot see how that is consistent with my verdict in case one, where I refuse to treat the deception hypothesis as even a candidate for truth.

Six. In a town where I once did fieldwork, a religious community treats the recent separate creation of the earth and its living kinds as beyond question: it organizes what they read, how they raise children, what counts as an explanation. I do not want to say their certainty is true-for-them; I do not want to say they merely lack a hinge where my department has one; and I do not want to say that their certainty and my department's contradict one another in the way two hypotheses do.

Seven. A set theorist of my acquaintance says that for all anyone knows the standard axiomatization is inconsistent, and means it as a remark that opens research. Verdict: a genuine hypothesis. My graduate assistant joking about whether twelve times twelve might be a hundred and forty-five: not a hypothesis at all, and not a hinge either, somehow. So the line I am after cuts inside mathematics.

Eight. That there could be no reason of any kind for torturing a child for amusement. I hold this with the same immunity to evidence as case one, but I resist calling it a hinge, because it seems to be something I believe and would argue others into believing, not merely something that stands fast for me.

The best I have been able to do by myself is this: a proposition p is a hinge for a practice P just in case (i) within P, p is not established by evidence; (ii) within P there is no recognized procedure for settling doubts about p by evidence; and (iii) abandoning p would not be a change of belief within P but a change of P itself. Clause (iii) was meant to save me from case three; I no longer think it does, since the Verrill project abandoned its assumption and went on being the same project, only corrected. Nor does it settle case eight, which satisfies all three clauses, or case six, which turns on how finely P is to be individuated.

What I would like from you: take the eight verdicts as data and tell me what principle or small set of rival principles best captures them. State the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up and why. Then tell me what it predicts about a case I have not listed: a genetics lab whose entire pipeline presupposes that a particular reference assembly of the genome is correct, where a new postdoc proposes to spend a year testing it. Is that case three or case two, on your account, and what should the lab say to her? If the resulting account collapses hinge commitments into very confident empirical belief, say so and tell me what is lost; if it does not, tell me where Moore's gesture of holding up his hands is left standing.
