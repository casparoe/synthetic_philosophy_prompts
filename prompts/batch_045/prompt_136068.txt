I'm a high school senior doing an independent research project over one semester (roughly four months, meeting my supervisor for an hour every other week, final paper around 20 pages), and here is my half-formed idea. The view I keep circling around is a lexical threshold version of negative utilitarianism: the claim that mild pleasures and mild pains sit on one scale and trade off against each other normally, but that once a pain passes some intensity threshold, no amount of pleasure — not even an enormous amount spread over many people — can outweigh it. My friend and I got into an argument about this: she said she would accept a single minute of kidney-stone-level pain in exchange for a year of the best mild pleasure you can imagine, repeated, and I said no amount of it would be enough, and then neither of us could say what the disagreement was even about. What I want to test is whether the disagreement is really about one thing or several. It strikes me that both the people who say pleasure and pain are fully commensurable and the people who posit a lexical threshold are arguing as if the question were a single question, when it might split at least three ways: whether a pleasure and a pain can trade off inside a single moment of consciousness; whether they can trade off across time within one life; and whether they can trade off across different people at the same time. My suspicion is that there is a real argument for lexical priority in the first case that does no work at all in the other two, and that the threshold view quietly borrows its plausibility from the first case and spends it in the third. The argument I have in mind is about what intense pain does to a mind: at a certain intensity pain seems to take over the whole of your experience, crowding out everything else you could be feeling, so that the pleasure and the pain could never be enjoyed together — there is no joint state whose value we could then weigh, and so no exchange rate to be had. That also seems to give a non-arbitrary place to put the threshold, at the point where pain starts to monopolize experience, which is what the view badly needs. But notice that this argument only shows that no compensation can happen at that moment; it doesn't yet show that the moment can't be compensated for by what comes before or after it, or by what happens to someone else. So my real question is: what principle about how moments of experience compose into lives, and lives into outcomes, would let the lexical threshold spread from the single moment to the whole life and then to the whole population — and is that principle believable on its own, or does it only look believable because people already want the threshold view to be true?
