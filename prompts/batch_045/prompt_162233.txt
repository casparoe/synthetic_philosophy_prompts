so ive been chewing on that question socrates puts to euthyphro — whether the gods love a thing because its holy, or its holy because they love it — and the bit I keep coming back to isn't really the god part. Take the gods out and put something else in their place and the same question comes straight back: put suffering in, or human flourishing, or "what I'd want if i was calm and knew everything", and you can still ask whether it has that feature because its wrong or its wrong because it has that feature. And then whatever you answer, I want to ask the same thing again about the answer. My brother says the two answers might not even be rivals — that maybe its one fact being described from each end, like the same stretch of road walked in two directions — and I half think he's right and half think he's dodging.

I want a poem that does that argument instead of describing it, because I'm going to read it out loud at the end of a text argument with him and it should sound spoken, not poetical. Heres the shape I have in mind: 13 short lines, plain words, no rhyme scheme you have to obey, and the whole thing is a mirror. Lines 1 to 6 make the case one way; line 7 is a hinge; lines 8 to 13 walk back out through the same moves in reverse order, each line flipping the direction of the because in its partner — so line 13 partners with line 1, 12 with 2, on down to 8 with 6 — and if you read the poem from the bottom up instead of the top down, you get the other answer, argued in the same order. The hinge line is the one line that's true in both directions and doesn't take a side: the bare fact that the two lists match up, that whatever the gods love and whatever is holy turn out to be the same items. Also: the outermost pair, lines 1 and 13, should be the plain version with actual gods in it, and the innermost pair, lines 6 and 8, should be the version with the gods swapped out for something else, so the reader notices near the middle that the question survives the swap and then just climbs a level. Don't resolve it. I want someone who knows the dilemma to be able to say which half is which horn, and I want both my brother and me to come away still unsure whether the two halves are even disagreeing. Afterwards give me two or three sentences: which line is the hinge, which line does which work, and whether you think the mirror shows a real disagreement or the same thing twice.
