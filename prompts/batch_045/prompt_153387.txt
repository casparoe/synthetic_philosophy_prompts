I'm a high school senior working through Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* more or less on my own over the break — my school lets you do an independent reading project in the spring, and the math teacher who's supervising mine indulged me when I asked to do it — and I've gotten hung up on something in the early chapters that I don't think is just a detail. I understand the MIU-system itself: the axiom MI, and the four rules (if a string ends in I you may add a U; you may double everything after the M; you may replace any occurrence of III with a U; you may delete any occurrence of UU). I can also follow the usual solution to the MU-puzzle: every theorem has a number of I's that isn't divisible by three, because doubling preserves non-divisibility by three while the other rules either don't change the I-count or change it by exactly three, and MU has zero I's. What I can't get straight is what sort of maneuver that solution is supposed to be, and whether it really deserves the name the book gives it, jumping out of the system (JOOTSing).

Here's a taxonomy I've been playing with on my own, and I'd like you to tell me where it distorts things. Call a type-1 jump any reasoning I do *about* a system from outside it — like the divisibility argument, which is a proof in my own informal arithmetic about the strings, not a derivation inside MIU. Call a type-2 jump changing the system: adding a rule or an axiom so that the string that wasn't reachable becomes reachable. Call a type-3 jump the Gödelian move, where a system manages to talk about itself and throws up a sentence that is true but undecidable inside it. Call a type-4 jump the Zen-flavored move the book gestures at with the word mu — refusing the question rather than answering yes or no. My friend, who's further along in the book than I am, says the divisibility argument already is a type-3 jump, so that the MU-puzzle is a miniature Gödel theorem and shows that truth and provability come apart. I doubt that, but I can't say exactly why: it seems to me that MIU-strings aren't the sort of thing that can be true or false in the first place, and that MU is not a theorem isn't expressible in the language of MIU the way Gödel's sentence is expressible in the language of arithmetic. But then I don't know what the right relation between type-1 and type-3 is, or why the book bothers with the puzzle at all if the analogy is that loose.

Three more things I'd like folded in. First, a small experiment: if I change the doubling rule so that Mx goes to Mxxx, then MI goes to MIII goes to MU, and the puzzle collapses in two steps; but if instead I keep the rules as they are and *add* a fifth rule letting me replace any U with III, my friend and I spent an afternoon trying and failing to reach MU, and I have a vague feeling that isn't an accident — that the divisibility invariant survives adding that rule even though the new rule looks like it undoes the III-to-U rule. Is that right, and does it tell me anything about what makes an invariant an invariant? Second: does it matter, for the type-2 case, that adding MU as a new axiom seems to just leave me with a different system and no further puzzle about that particular string, whereas in the Gödelian case adding the undecidable sentence as a new axiom is supposed to generate a fresh blind spot? And third, my teacher said offhandedly that the real moral is that you can always step outside any system, but that the system you land in has its own hole, so there is no final escape — and that this is why Hofstadter thinks minds aren't machines, or something like that. I'd like you to separate the parts of that remark that are just the ordinary mechanics of reasoning about a formal system from without, the parts that are the incompleteness theorems properly speaking, and the parts that are a philosophical leap about minds and machines that the formal results don't by themselves deliver. Please be concrete about which of my four types are genuinely distinct and which quietly collapse into one another.
