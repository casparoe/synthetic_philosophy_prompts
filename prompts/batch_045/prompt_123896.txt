You are a philosopher of history who works alongside historians — fluent in the analytic literature on counterfactuals, causation and path dependence, and equally fluent in the historians' arguments about contingency, determinism and the uses of the counterfactual in explanation. I need you to design something, not to survey a field. Think carefully before you write: work out first which distinctions from that literature a procedure usable by a non-specialist must preserve, and which it can afford to collapse. Do not cite anything and do not name books or authors; I cannot check them and they would not help me. Use no technical vocabulary you have not translated into terms a working novelist could act on.

I write historical fiction, and my last three novels have been what I would call low-divergence alternate history: one or two changes to the historical record, everything else left as it was, the interest lying in what follows. I am contracted for two more, and my editor now wants each premise signed off before I draft rather than after. I make premise-level divergence decisions roughly thirty times per book, each one in half a working day, and a bad one is expensive: it is discovered in draft, it unravels months of work, and it is the first thing reviewers attack. My resources are a historical consultant for ten hours across both books, no training in philosophy or economics, and an editor who will read the procedure but will not read theory.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposed divergence, ordered as questions with stop rules. It must, at minimum, handle the following, in my terms:

Say what I mean when I say an outcome was inevitable. I keep conflating two things: an outcome that had several independent sufficient causes, so that removing the one that actually operated would only have delayed it or routed it differently, and an outcome that merely feels unavoidable because it happened and the accounts are organized around it. Give me a test that separates these, and a rule for what a premise must do when the outcome I want to avert belongs to the first kind.

Make me keep a cancellation ledger: the actual facts my premise silently negates — later events, works, institutions that depended on the thing I removed — and a stop rule for when the ledger gets too long for a low-divergence book.

Handle latency: the gap between the divergence and the first difference any contemporary could in principle have noticed. Some of my premises have a latency of days, others of two generations, and I do not know what the second kind commits me to.

Handle reconvergence: the well-known fact that most divergences drain back into something like the actual course within a few decades. I need to know whether my book is required to dramatize that draining away, and on what grounds I may decline to.

Flag hindsight contraband: places where a character's reasoning quietly presupposes a trend — the rise of this or the decline of that — that only became visible long after they lived.

And give me a name and a use for the pattern I keep falling into myself: the more badly an audience wants a divergence to be true, the less I scrutinize whether it was cheap. I suspect this is a systematic failure of my own judgment and not just carelessness.

Mark every point where the procedure stops with no, every point where it hands a question to my consultant as a factual matter, and every point where it hands the question back to me and my editor as a matter of values or of what kind of book this is. Be explicit about which distinctions you simplified away.

Then run it on three of my premises. One is a congress of the great powers in 1814–15 that breaks up over the disposal of a Polish and a Saxon territory, leaving no settled concert and a nineteenth century thatopens with general war. One is a world in which the great influenza pandemic after the First World War does not occur. The third is the one I have already drafted sixty pages of, and it is the one I expect you to damage: a ruling in a Westminster court in the early 1770s, reasoned differently by the judge, ending the Atlantic slave trade a century early.

Format: lead with a single sentence stating what the procedure is for and what it will refuse to decide. Then at most eight numbered steps, each with its sufficient-answer condition and its stop or hand-off rule. Then the three worked applications. Then a closing section on what the procedure cannot tell me. No footnotes, no bibliography, no headings beyond those four parts. The whole document must be no more than 1,000 words, including the one sentence you spend telling me which distinctions you dropped and why.
