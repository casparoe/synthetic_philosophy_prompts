I am in the eleventh grade, and I have two contexts I know well and a third I know only from reading. The first is my school's FIRST Robotics Competition team, where I do controls and drive practice: I think in terms of cycle time, throughput, bottlenecks, uptime, mean time between failures, changeover, sensor redundancy, false positives, and exception handling and escalation. The second is my job: I work about fifteen hours a week at a supermarket that installed eight self-checkout lanes two years ago and is now piloting an automatic bagging machine at one of them, so I am also used to labor hours per unit, capacity utilization, human-in-the-loop requirements, capital expense versus operating expense, leasing versus buying, and uptime guarantees in vendor contracts. The third is Marx's account of machinery, which I have been reading about on my own and which I do not yet have a vocabulary for. I am not asking for citations, reading lists, or passage locations; I want only the argumentative work.

Here are the claims as I currently understand them. Please correct me where I have stated one wrongly before you translate it, since a faithful translation of a misreading is useless to me:

1. Labor-saving machinery, introduced into production organized as it currently is, tends to lengthen and intensify the working day rather than shorten it, even though shortening it is exactly what the machinery makes physically possible.
2. Skill migrates out of the worker and into the machine; the worker becomes a tender or appendage of the machine, and what remains of the work is simplified, repetitive, and degraded.
3. Machinery cheapens labor power in two ways: by lowering the skill required, so that employers can draw on a wider and cheaper pool of people, and by displacing workers, whose competition for jobs holds down the wages of those who remain.
4. Displacement is not a temporary dislocation that clears up afterward; the maintenance of a surplus population of would-be workers is a standing feature of the system, not an accident of a particular machine.
5. The same development also produces the material precondition for greatly reduced toil, and under different arrangements it would show up as freed time. Under the present ones the possibility is realized the other way around.
6. At a certain stage the main productive force stops being the labor performed inside the workplace and becomes society's accumulated knowledge embodied in machinery, which is why the individual worker's hours become a poor measure of what gets produced.

What I want is a translation of each of these, one by one, as precisely as you can, into the vocabulary I actually use: throughput, cycle time, uptime, exceptions, bottlenecks, capacity utilization, capital versus operating expense, and the rest. Do not import economic assumptions I have not given you and present them as translation; where a sentence only works because you have quietly added one, say so.

I also have three half-formed thoughts of my own, and I would rather you adjudicate them than flatter them:

- My managers did not buy the lanes to shorten our shifts. The number they report upward is units per labor hour, and the gain from the machines has shown up as throughput rather than as hours returned to anyone. That looks to me like claim 1, but it might really be a claim about which metric the people in charge are rewarded for, which is a different thing that Marx's vocabulary may not contain. Tell me which.
- A teammate says that value in Marx behaves like energy in physics: conserved, transferred from the worker into the product, and transferred out of a machine over its working life. I suspect this is a bad translation that smuggles in the whole thing under debate, but I cannot say why precisely.
- What the machines actually did at my store is not what claim 2 predicts. They took the frequent, easy, pleasant parts of the job and left me the rare, hard, unpleasant ones: the barcode that will not scan, the coupon that fails, the customer who is angry at a machine, and now four lanes to supervise at once instead of one. The remaining work is less repetitive and more demanding, not more degraded. Nobody was fired either; they simply stopped backfilling vacancies and spread the same total hours across fewer people, each tending more machines. And we do not own the lanes at all, we lease them under a contract that guarantees uptime and lets the vendor push software updates whenever it likes. So: does the deskilling claim cover what I am seeing, does the leasing arrangement break any part of the picture, and is the stop-hiring version of displacement the same phenomenon as the one Marx describes or a different one that happens to have similar effects on wages?

Then the part I care most about, and please spend the most words here: the residue report. Which claims carry over into my vocabulary cleanly, which survive only after you bend them, and which cannot be expressed in it at all — not because you lack a synonym, but because the vocabulary has no slot for the kind of thing being said. For each piece of residue, give me your view on whether it is a defect of the vocabulary I use, which would mean I am blind to something real, or a sign that the original claim was doing less work than it sounded like it was doing. And one last thing: is there any point at which my vocabulary does not merely fail to express one of these claims but says something systematically opposite, in a way that looks less like a gap and more like a symptom of the very thing Marx was describing?
