I'm writing up my position on truth and logical consequence for a reading group in philosophical logic, and I would like the set of things I hold stress-tested before I present it rather than during the question period. The others are mostly classical logicians plus one or two people sympathetic to non-classical treatments of vagueness, so they will be glad to tell me that my commitments clash; I would rather learn now which of those clashes are real. Here is where I have landed.

1. Some contradictions are true. The Liar and its strengthened variants are the central case, and I also treat borderline applications of vague predicates as both true and false. So the Law of Non-Contradiction is not a law of logic; it has counterexamples.

2. The true contradictions are all abstract in a broad sense: semantic, set-theoretic, or generated by vagueness. There are no true contradictions about concrete empirical matters. When an empirical theory turns out to be inconsistent, the response is always to revise the theory, never to accept the contradiction.

3. The correct consequence relation is the three-valued one with two designated values, true-only and both, in which excluded middle is valid but explosion and disjunctive syllogism are not. This is a normative claim rather than a modelling convenience: an inference that is invalid by that relation is one that one ought not to make.

4. The negation of that logic is negation. I do not want to add a Boolean negation or a primitive determinacy operator as extra ideology; I want to get by with what the three-valued semantics already provides.

5. My own practice, including my metatheory, remains classical. I prove soundness and completeness results for the paraconsistent logic using ordinary classical model theory, disjunctive syllogism included. I take this to be unproblematic: in domains I have reason to believe are consistent, the paraconsistent and the classical consequence relations coincide, so nothing is lost and the metatheory is not endangered by what it theorises about.

6. Belief aims at truth. If I have good reason to think that a given contradiction is true, I should believe it. There is no freestanding doxastic norm requiring my beliefs to be consistent; there is only the norm against believing what is false.

7. Contradiction is still a methodological red flag. I accept a contradiction only where the argument for it is compelling and I can identify no culprit to revise in its place. Dialetheism therefore does not collapse into trivialism, or into a methodology in which anything goes.

8. When I say that the Liar is a genuine dialetheia rather than a symptom that some principle in my theory of truth is mistaken, I take myself to be saying something true and not also false. I take myself to be able to draw, using only the negation in (4), the distinction between true and true-and-false, and to mean the former when I state the restricted version of my view in (2).

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are. Rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally serious. For each genuine tension, identify precisely what would have to give, name the least costly repair, and state what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set: I would much rather surrender something small than discover that I have to surrender (1). For each merely apparent tension, explain why the appearance arises, and what the strongest form of the objection is that makes it look worse than it is. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most at once.
