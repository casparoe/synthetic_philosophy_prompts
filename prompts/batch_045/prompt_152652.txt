I'm TAing for an upper-level seminar on practical rationality this term, and last week in the grad lounge — three coffees deep, my notes a mess — our study group got into an argument we where not able to settle, and I want a pair of cases that either settles it or at least forces the disagreement out into the open.

Here's where we got stuck. Everyone agreed that hyperbolic (or beta-delta / quasi-hyperbolic) discounting is the standard formal story people reach for when they want to model weakness of will. But then someone pointed out that what the model actually delivers is a *change* in what the agent prefers between t1 and t2: at t2 she prefers the smaller-sooner option, full stop, discounted at her own current rate. So on the model she never acts against her own contemporaneous all-things-considered judgement. The "weakness" only shows up if you identify the earlier self and the later self as one agent and treat the earlier ranking as the authoritative one. Half the room said that's precisely the point and strict akrasia is a myth; the other half said no, there are perfectly ordinary cases where you know right then, in the moment, that you shouldn't, and you do it anyway, and no discount curve has anything to say about those.

So: build me a matched pair of cases that separates the two phenomena. Same agent as nearly as possible, the same two options (a smaller-sooner and a larger-later reward a normal person could actually face — dissertation writing, a night shift, the gym, saving for a new boiler, whatever works), and the same observable sequence: at t1, with both options still ahead of her, she ranks the larger-later option above the smaller-sooner one and says so or plans accordingly; at t2, with the smaller-sooner option now on offer, she takes it.

- In Case 1, the reversal has to be *generated* by present-biased discounting and nothing else — I want to see the common-delay signature explicitly (both options delayed → larger-later wins; the common delay removed → smaller-sooner wins), i.e. the pattern a time-consistent exponential discounter cannot produce.
- In Case 2, the same observable sequence, but the agent is time-consistent: at t2 she still ranks the larger-later option above the smaller-sooner one, and takes the smaller-sooner one anyway. Hyperbolic discounting should contribute nothing to the explanation.
- Both cases: ordinary life, one paragraph each, no exotic devices, no forgetting, no new information that changes the payoffs, no drugs or brain lesions. Don't stipulate the verdicts — generate them from the set-up, and make it a real matched pair, not two unrelated stories with the same moral.

Then give me the payoff: for each case, what she prefers and does at t1 and at t2 and why; in which case (if either) charging her with akrasia is actually apt, keeping synchronic akrasia distinct from mere diachronic preference change; the single structural feature that differs between the two cases and what happens to the pair if you remove it; and an honest note on the counterattacks — how someone who thinks the discounting story *is* the theory of akrasia would try to explain Case 2 away, and how someone who insists on strict akrasia would argue that Case 1 isn't weakness of will at all. I'm planning to run this in discussion section, so keep it tight enough to put on one handout.
