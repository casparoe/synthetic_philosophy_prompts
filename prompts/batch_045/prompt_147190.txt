I'm helping put together a methodology workshop on the science of consciousness, and the two people I most want in the same room have spent about a decade circling each other: on one side a committed Dennett-style heterophenomenologist, who says that a subject's utterances are texts to be interpreted rather than oracles to be consulted, that the experimenter should suspend judgment on whether anything experiential is really going on behind them, and that the deliverable of the method is a heterophenomenological world — the world as the subject's reports, maximally and charitably interpreted, commit them to; on the other, someone who holds that disciplined first-person methods (micro-phenomenological interviewing, descriptive experience sampling, training in suspending the natural attitude) are the only route to data about experience at all, and that a method which declines to take reports as reports of experience has already, quietly and without argument, taken a side. I don't want a dialogue between them and I don't want a verdict from a third party; I want the joint document a good-faith week of adversarial collaboration should have produced.

So: draft that document. Start with a numbered section of claims both can genuinely sign — I'd expect they'd converge on things like: no report is self-certifying; naive subjects' reports are unreliable in specifiable ways (the gap between apparent perceptual richness and what can be reported, confabulation under pressure, the effects of questioning on what is reported); the science needs public, repeatable, third-person-checkable data; and a subject's authority over their own case is defeasible by neural and behavioural evidence. Then give the disagreements, and here is where I want the real care: for each crux, state the heterophenomenologist's position in wording a heterophenomenologist would accept, and the first-person methodologist's position in wording they would accept, without either formulation being vacuous or a straw version of the other. The cruxes I suspect matter most are: (i) whether heterophenomenology's suspension of judgment is genuinely neutral between realism and eliminativism about experience, or whether its treatment of the objects of the heterophenomenological world as posits has already smuggled in a metaphysics — and if part of this is merely verbal, say which part and why; (ii) whether reports are data about experience or only data about a subject's dispositions to speak; (iii) whether disciplined training of subjects improves the evidential base or manufactures artefacts, and whether refusing to train them is itself a substantive empirical bet rather than a neutral methodological stance; (iv) what the method says about subjects who cannot report, or who report in only one bit — infants, nonhuman animals, locked-in patients on a binary channel — and whether the answer it gives there reveals a commitment it disclaims elsewhere; (v) whether there could be an empirically discoverable difference in experience with no difference in the heterophenomenological record, i.e. whether something like absent qualia with intact reports is a scientific hypothesis, a metaphysical distraction, or a contradiction in the method's own terms; and (vi) the bootstrapping problem, since the experimenter must interpret a token of the report vocabulary partly by analogy with their own case, which looks like first-person data entering through the back door.

For each crux, name one concrete case or test both sides would agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts it will show. I'd like you to use three cases in particular, which I've been chewing over. The first is what I've been calling the simulator protocol: a decoder is trained on a single subject's neural recordings together with the corpus of that subject's past utterances, and it generates new reports in that subject's voice; on some trials the generated report is more internally consistent and better predicts independent neural markers than the subject's own utterance on that trial, and there is a further condition in which the subject is under general anaesthesia and the generator, running on spontaneous neural activity, goes on producing structured, coherent, apparently experiential reports. Is the generated text part of the heterophenomenological record, and is there a heterophenomenological world with no experiencer? The second is the trained-describer asymmetry: a multi-lab replication in which disciplined describers cleanly separate two stimulus conditions that naive reporters systematically conflate, with the distinction validated by neural decoding, but only for stimuli differing in duration; for stimuli differing in spatial layout, training makes reports less rather than more consistent, and inter-describer agreement collapses. The third is a single-bit reporter: a patient with a reliable yes/no channel whose answers track an experimenter-manipulated stimulus with high accuracy but who cannot volunteer anything at all, asked whether they have a heterophenomenological world and whether it is the same kind of thing as yours. Format the whole thing as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, each side's own formulation, the agreed test case, and each side's advance prediction; close with a short paragraph on which of these disagreements you take to be merely verbal, and whether there is any single result both parties would accept as actually moving the debate rather than as more material to interpret.
