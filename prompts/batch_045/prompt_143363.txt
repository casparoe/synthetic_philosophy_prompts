I'm putting together a short position paper for a graduate seminar on experimental philosophy, and before I commit to it in writing I'd like someone to audit the package of views I've ended up with — a friend in the psychology department keeps telling me the whole literature rests on undergraduates clicking through vignettes, and while I think that's too harsh, I can't quite locate where my own set of commitments is unstable. Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) The chairman-case asymmetry is a real finding and it is about the ordinary concept of intentional action: in the vignette where a board approves a profit-maximizing program whose foreseen side effect is environmental harm, people readily say the chairman brought about the side effect intentionally, while in the version where the side effect is a benefit they deny it; I don't think this is an artifact of politeness, of conversational pragmatics, or of how respondents understood the question. (2) I think the best explanation is that moral considerations partly constitute the folk concept of intentional action — the valence of the outcome isn't distorting an otherwise morally neutral concept from the outside, it is part of what the concept is. (3) I also think that what is really doing the explanatory work is the agent's attitude: the chairman's indifference to the harm reveals something about his quality of will, and that is what drives the verdict. (4) Separately, I'm persuaded by the debunking side of experimental philosophy — philosophers' armchair intuitions are poor data, sensitive to order of presentation, framing and demographics, and theories shouldn't be built on them. (5) But I then want to use (1) and (2) as evidence in the theory of action: a philosophical account of intentional action should answer to the folk concept rather than ignore it. (6) I'm also drawn to the claim that the effect isn't specifically moral at all, that it shows up for side effects which are merely conventional violations or statistically unusual. (7) And I hold that philosophers are entitled to stipulate their own technical terms, so that if the folk concept turns out to be contaminated, the theory of action can simply define "intentional action" in a cleaned-up way and get on with the substantive work.

What I need is an audit, not a survey of the literature: which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, and for each genuine tension what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me. I'm not attached in advance to any of them, so please tell me which one has to go rather than assuming I'd defend it. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, ordered by severity with the most damaging tension first, with no preamble and no concluding summary; for each item, state which commitments are involved, whether the tension is genuine or merely apparent, and the least costly fix. If some pair I've listed is only superficially in conflict, say that plainly instead of flagging everything as equally problematic.
