I sit on the Serious Safety Event Review Panel of a four-hospital academic health system, and I've spent a long evening arguing with my friend Priya about the vote we have to take next week, and I came away convinced she was half right in a way I can't convert into a ballot. Our registry gives each event exactly one primary mechanism code, and the code is not cosmetic: three or more events sharing a mechanism within a rolling twelve months obliges us to open a system-wide corrective action plan and notify our state regulator, while separately coded events close locally with a unit-level fix and no notification. We have two events now and a plausible third from March, so our coding decision effectively decides whether we declare a cluster.

Here they are. In May, on a medical-surgical floor at our downtown campus, a nurse programming a heparin infusion selected an entry from a concentration scroll list; a software update three weeks earlier had re-sorted that list, so the item sitting in the screen position that had previously been correct now named a different concentration, and the patient received roughly a third of the intended dose for several hours until it showed up in lab values. In September, at a suburban campus, on a different vendor's pumps, a pump was unplugged for under a minute during a bed transfer and came back up in a default low-rate mode; the confirmation alarm that should have fired was routed to a central monitoring station that was itself being relocated that week, so it went unnoticed, and the nurse reasonably assumed the pump was running the rate she had set; again, hours of silent under-infusion, again found by labs rather than by anyone at the bedside. The March case is a cousin: a unit-convention mismatch between what the clinician entered and what the pump's drug library expected, with the shortfall noticed only when the patient's pain escalated. At a high level all three are the same story: the dose the patient received silently diverged from the dose the clinician believed was being delivered, and the layer that should have caught it didn't. At a fine level they are three unrelated stories: a menu re-sort, a state reset after power loss, and a units mismatch, on two vendors and three floors.

Priya's position, which I couldn't shake, is that same mechanism is a gerrymandered notion: any two events can be described identically at some altitude and differently at another, and what actually settles the altitude is what we want to do about it, so we should retire the word and code events by the intervention that would prevent them. She used the example of a car that won't start, where a flat tire and a dead battery are the same problem if you're deciding whether to call roadside assistance and different problems if you're deciding what to repair. My own candidate, which I floated and she partly mocked, is a swap test: take the upstream segment of one event's causal chain and graft it onto the downstream segment of another's, and if an engineer would still recognize one continuous process with the same failure signature, call it the same mechanism. Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the panel ahead of the vote. It should open with a bottom line: code these as one mechanism, code them separately, or decline both codes and change what the field means, with a concrete recommendation about the March case and about what the corrective action should be if there is one. It should give the panel reasons that the two nurses, the biomedical engineering lead, and our legal liaison could each actually answer, and it should be honest about the strongest thing on the side it rejects, including the worry that a cluster declared partly by our own choice of description is a cluster we manufactured. Say what further evidence would move you to the opposite recommendation, and close by stating how confident you are in the recommendation overall and singling out the one claim in the memo you are least sure of.
