DRAFT: Four corners and no answer: the tetralemma as a type error

Nāgārjuna's habit is to take some candidate existent and run it through four corners: it exists; it doesn't exist; it both exists and doesn't exist; it neither exists nor doesn't exist. Then he rejects all four. And the bit everyone repeats is that emptiness (śūnyatā) doesn't mean nothing exists — it means nothing has svabhāva, intrinsic nature, own-being, whatever you want to call it. To be is to be dependently originated, and a thing's dependence is not a defect in it or a veil over some more real thing behind it. Emptiness just is the dependence, described from a different angle.

Here's the thing I keep chewing on, though. The four corners are normally presented as mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive — a partition of logical space. But if they jointly exhaust logical space, then their disjunction is a logical truth, and rejecting all four at once isn't a subtle middle way between extremes, it's denying a tautology. And it's worse than that: by ordinary classical lights corner three is a straight contradiction, and corner four is the denial of an excluded middle. So on the face of it Nāgārjuna is either commited to something completely trivializing, or the four corners aren't the sort of thing they look like, and the whole "four truth values!" framing — the thing that makes people reach for Belnap-style four-valued tables or for paraconsistent logic — is a red herring generated by translating him into classical notation.

My suggestion is that the corners aren't four rival answers to one well-formed question. They're four ways a question can fail to get off the ground. Think of a database query that returns NULL rather than a row, or of the classic loaded question: "have you stopped beating your wife?" Both yes and no go wrong there, and not because one is false and the other is also false, but because the question smuggles in a presupposition that doesn't hold. The tetralemma, on this reading, is a diagnostic machine. Its job is to show that the question "does X exist intrinsically / ultimately / from its own side?" is ill-typed, because existence-with-svabhāva is a concept with no instances and the question is built to presuppose that it has some. Rejecting all four corners is then not a fifth, deeper answer. It's declining the question. The "neither" corner in particular isn't a report of a third truth value floating alongside true and false; it's the trace left behind when the question doesn't fire.

There's a technical point I lean on here, and I'm not sure I've got it right. The Indian logical tradition apparently distinguished two kinds of negation, prasajya and paryudāsa — roughly, a bare denial that commits you to nothing versus a denial that implies some positive replacement. If every corner of the tetralemma uses the bare kind, then the "not" in corner two isn't the Boolean complement of corner one, and corners three and four aren't conjunctions of a proposition with its own negation. None of them is the sort of item that can be plugged into a partition, so the argument that Nāgārjuna is denying a logical truth rests on mistranslating him — a bit like scolding someone for contradicting themselves when they said "no comment" four times. But this is exactly where I start to suspect myself: isn't "his negation isn't classical negation" just a slogan until you give a semantics for it? And if I cash it out with four-valued truth tables where "both" and "neither" are genuine values, haven't I turned him into a dialetheist — which I take it is precisely the reading most people reject, given that he rejects "both" as well?

And then there's the Bit where I think I've just lost the plot. I found myself writing that since nothing has intrinsic nature, ultimately nothing is real, and conventional truth is a kind of useful fiction that a serious person should be ready to set aside once they've seen how things are. That sounds like nihilism with extra steps, and I have a vague memory that Nāgārjuna goes out of his way to block it — something about emptiness itself being empty, and about emptiness being a medicine that becomes a poison if you take it as a view. Also it seems to collide with the two truths not being two separate things: if the conventional is all we ever have, "set it aside" can't mean what I want it to mean. Worse, I also want to say that Nāgārjuna's own position is that all views are empty — while also wanting to quote the bit where he insists he has no position (no pratijñā) at all. I can't have both, can I? Or is the second claim the one that saves the first?

The payoff I'm groping toward, if any of this survives: if the tetralemma is a diagnostic on questions rather than a menu of answers, then the interesting modern moral is about inquiry rather than metaphysics. Lots of long-running disputes share a presupposition that neither side has earned — the free will debate arguably needs a certain picture of the agent as a cause apart from the causal order, and the "hard problem" needs a certain picture of the physical facts as complete in a way that would leave something out. If you're right about that, the middle way isn't a third position between two extremes; it's the refusal to take the question up in the terms offered. Which is hopefully more than just "they were asking the wrong question."
