One resource you should feel free to use if its fair: the guarantee that makes those reward functions equivalent is pinned to a fixed starting state and a fixed set of dynamics, and you think that's where the trouble is hiding.

My opening move:

Say two candidate reward functions R and R' are related like this: for some function F on states and some discount factor g between 0 and 1, R'(s, a, s') = R(s, a, s') + g·F(s') − F(s). Then for any trajectory starting at s0, the total discounted return under R' equals the total discounted return under R minus F(s0) — a constant that doesn't depend on which trajectory you take. So R and R' agree on the ranking of every trajectory starting from s0, and they agree on what the optimal policy is.

I claim this is exactly parallel to something nobody gets skeptical about: a utility function representing someone's preferences is only ever determined up to positive affine transformation, and we don't conclude from that that preferences are unknowable or fictional. It's just representational redundancy. So my argument is: quotient out by this shaping equivalence, and what's left over is the real thing the inference procedure is trying to recover. Your famous underdetermination is representational vertigo, not a problem about preferences.
