For thirty-five years I worked as a comparative physiologist, mostly on the nervous systems of insects and other arthropods, and since retiring I have been attending a philosophy of mind seminar at a university near where I live. We spent last term on whether sentience admits of degrees and whether moral status does, and I came away convinced that my reactions to particular cases are far more definite than anything I can say in general. So I should be grateful for help in working out what principle, if any, they add up to. My verdicts, as honestly as I can report them: An adult honeybee — one that learns to avoid a cue paired with a shock, and whose avoidance persists for hours afterwards — has a little moral status: enough that I would not crush one in a doorway for a trifling convenience, nowhere near enough to weigh against any ordinary human interest. A fruitfly strikes me as essentially the same, though I cannot say why. A nematode with a few hundred neurons I shrug at, and yet I have no principled place to put the boundary between it and the fly. An octopus has a great deal; I would not eat one and I would object to its being kept in a bare tank. A slime mould that solves a maze has, so far as I am concerned, nothing at all; a Venus flytrap gives me a moment's pause and ends in the same place. A thermostat has nothing, as does a chess-playing programme, and so — though I am less confident than I was a decade ago, and I dislike the fact — does a large language model that tells me, when asked, that it is suffering. A machine fitted with what its builders call a 'valence register' — an internal variable that aversive input sets, that decays with a time constant of hours, and that biases what the machine later does — I also want to place at zero, but I cannot name what it lacks that the bee has. Of two invented organisms, one with nociceptors and a clean withdrawal reflex but no lasting change in its subsequent behaviour, the other with no nociceptors at all but a marked, days-long alteration in what it avoids and how active it is after injury, I count the second as the better candidate for sentience, which surprises me, since the first is the one with the pain receptors. An adult human under general anaesthesia, and a person born with complete insensitivity to pain, both have full moral status. And if I imagine the bee's neurons replaced one at a time with functionally identical silicon units, I do not think anything morally significant happens at any step — which is awkward, because at the end I am left attributing moral status to a printed circuit and I recoil from it.

The cases that will not sit still with the others are those last three. The anaesthetised human has no experiences at all while the bee plausibly has some, so moral status cannot simply track current sentience; but if it tracks a capacity had at other times, I owe an account of why the capacity is doing the work rather than the exercise of it, and I do not have one. The valence register and the large language model look functionally sophisticated in just the respects the bee's case taught me to care about, and I still want to place them at zero; what separates them, I suspect, is something about how they are made rather than what they do, which sits badly with the silicon-replacement case. And the case I keep returning to is a patient in a persistent vegetative state, whom my seminar is divided about and on whom I cannot settle at all.
