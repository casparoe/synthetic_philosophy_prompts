I host a long-form interview series on political and legal philosophy; each episode is a single conversation of about an hour with one guest, and the listeners are a mix of graduate students and interested non-specialists who will not know the literature. My next guest is a fictional composite rather than a real person — please don't attribute these views to any actual writer — a legal theorist I'll call Professor Ada Reventlow, who has built a book around a distinction she calls the situational/developmental distinction.

Her claim, as I understand it, is this. A Ulysses contract is legitimate only when the change it guards against is situational: a passing state — pain, craving, panic, fury, the effect of the Sirens' song — that the agent would herself repudiate at every other time, and that she can foresee from the inside without having to imagine being a different person. It is not legitimate when the change is developmental: a change in her values, projects, attachments or identity that she might, once it has happened, endorse from the inside and have no reason to undo. She then carries this over to constitutional precommitment. Entrenched rights, supermajority amendment rules, judicial review and the rest are justified only to the extent that they protect a political community against situational corruption of its judgment — a war panic, a week of sectarian or ethnic fury, a run on the currency — and they lose their claim on us the moment what they suppress is a developmental change in the community's self-understanding. Two things she says a lot: that most of the literature has been asking about the mast and never about the island, by which she means that writers have argued about whether precommitment is rational or permissible for the agent while ignoring what is distinctive about the danger being guarded against; and, when a hard case comes up, that it is a drafting problem rather than a legitimacy problem, or that the distinction is not in the text of a constitution but in the rationale for having one.

I want twelve to fifteen questions, in the order I should ask them, each written so that a listener who has read nothing can follow it, and none of the generic kind. For each question, tell me in a line or two what you expect her to say and then give me the follow-up that bites on that particular answer. I need at least one question on each of the following, and I've noted the reply I think she'll give where I can guess it:

The asymmetry in the analogy itself. Ulysses is foreseeably and temporarily impaired; a constitution mostly binds people who are not impaired at all and simply disagree with the framers about justice. If the distinction is meant to track that difference, does it not condemn most actual constitutional precommitment as illegitimate? She will probably say she is distinguishing corruption of judgment from mere disagreement — press on what theory of political corruption she needs for that move, and whether any such theory can stay neutral between competing conceptions of justice.

How anyone is supposed to tell the two cases apart. Ask for a test that does not collapse into changes I disapprove of are situational. Her likely move is something like stable endorsement under reflection — so press her with a population that has stably, reflectively, and over two generations come to endorse a hierarchy it was raised under, or with a founding charter entrenching secularism in a society that has since become devout: which of these is developmental, and how would a court know?

Time. If the force of a precommitment tracks psychological connectedness between the earlier and later self, then a clause written two centuries ago should bind less than one written twenty years ago, even if nothing else has changed. Ask whether she accepts that decay and what institutional consequence follows — sunset clauses, periodic re-authorization. She will likely like renewable entrenchment, so ask whether a clause everyone knows lapses in year twenty can still discipline a panic in year eighteen, and whether she is comfortable with the implication that yesterday's amendment binds more tightly than the founding itself.

The executant. Ulysses has sailors with wax in their ears, whose judgment is not at stake in his decision. The institution that decides whether a given change is situational or developmental is the one whose power the precommitment limits. Ask who guards the guardians on her own account. If she appeals to courts being constrained by having to give public reasons, or to the possibility of override by supermajority, ask whether the supermajority amendment rule can itself pass her situational/developmental test — since it is precisely the device that binds a future majority to a past one.

Circularity. Precommitments shape the people who live under them: an entrenched settlement partly constitutes the identity and preferences of the later self whose autonomy she wants to respect. Does that make the justification self-validating, and what does it mean for her view if the distinction is endogenous to the arrangement it is used to defend?

Who the earlier self is. None of the current citizens ratified the founding, and a people is not a person. Ask what makes a founding generation the earlier temporal stage of a present citizen rather than a different agent altogether, and what her view says about a polity whose population has been substantially replaced by immigration.

The medical analogue. In the jurisdictions that recognize psychiatric advance directives, a person can authorize in advance the very treatment she will later refuse, and regimes differ on whether a later competent refusal overrides the document. Ask which treatment her distinction recommends, what a clinician would have to do differently if the law adopted her rationale instead of a threshold competence test, and why the criminal law is so much more willing to hold a person to a prior consent than to a prior waiver.

Scope. Ask whether the distinction does independent work outside constitutions — addiction-treatment contracts, statutory debt brakes, independent central banks, cooling-off periods — and whether it survives being applied to a case where the later self is not worse off by her own later lights at all.

The last two questions should be built so that both of her stock moves become unusable: give me a case where the classification is obviously interest-relative regardless of how it is drafted, and then a closing question about what evidence or what kind of case would show the situational/developmental distinction to be false or idle. Finally, flag the three questions you'd keep if we ran short on time.
