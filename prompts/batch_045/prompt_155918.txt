I'm working on a seminar paper for my module on moral epistemology, on the puzzle of moral testimony — the question of whether there is something defective about holding a moral belief purely on the basis of someone else's say-so — and I've got stuck trying to construct the case I need.

The pessimist's complaint seems to me to be standardly cashed out in one of four ways: the hearer doesn't really understand why the thing is wrong; the belief doesn't reach beyond the exact case described, so the hearer can't extend the verdict to nearby cases; the belief is motivationally inert; or the belief is fragile, in that the hearer would drop it the moment the speaker retracted or an equally credible person disagreed. My suspicion is that there is a residue the pessimist actually cares about that none of these four captures, but every example I try to build collapses back into one of them.

Could you construct an example of a case in which all four diagnoses fail, and in which it nonetheless seems true to say that the hearer does not hold the belief as they should? I'd like the case to meet the following conditions:

1. It is ordinary and low-stakes — a workplace, a shared flat, a committee, a family arrangement — rather than a dramatic case like giving up meat or joining a march, since those seem to smuggle in confounds.
2. The hearer already has every descriptive fact that matters, so what the speaker supplies is only the moral verdict plus a compressed schema of the considerations supporting it; the speaker is in fact reliable, and the verdict is true.
3. The hearer is not lazy, evasive or self-deceiving, and has no bad motive for believing — no wish to be liked, to avoid guilt, or to offload effort.
4. The resulting belief survives the speaker's retraction, guides the hearer's action, can be extended correctly to nearby cases — including cases that differ precisely in the descriptive respects the schema mentions — and the hearer can reproduce those reasons accurately when asked.

If you can, please also give the closest variant of the same case in which the belief is entirely unproblematic, ideally differing in only one respect, so that it is clear which feature is doing the work.

Finally, briefly: is the residue you have isolated a defect in the hearer's epistemic position, in the sense that it bears on justification or knowledge, or is it better understood as a failure of some other kind — a norm about whose judgment it is, or about how one ought to be related to other moral agents? It would help me most if you indicated where these two readings give different answers to the question of what the hearer should have done instead.
