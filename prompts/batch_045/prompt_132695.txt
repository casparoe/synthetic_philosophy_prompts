I'm finishing a dissertation chapter on the ethics of arthropod pest control in agriculture, and before I write the section where I state my own position explicitly, I want to check whether the commitments I've assembled actually form a package. The chapter's working claim is that the moral cost of conventional insecticide use has been badly underestimated, but I can't tell anymore whether that claim survives the rest of what I believe. Here is where I've landed, stated as flatly as I can:

1. Even a modest credence in insect sentience, given the numbers involved, is enough to make agricultural insecticide use a first-order moral priority rather than a rounding error in agricultural ethics.
2. I accept replaceability reasoning about wild animals generally: where an organism's life would contain more suffering than enjoyment, painlessly preventing that life from existing harms no one and is pro tanto good. My honest estimate is that the lives of most r-selected pest species are net negative.
3. I deny that there is anything intrinsically bad about there being fewer insects. Alarm about insect declines has to be cashed out entirely in effects on sentient individuals, or it is sentimentality.
4. I hold that the killing/letting-die distinction and the intended/foreseen distinction do essentially no independent moral work here: pests that starve after a field is planted are neither better off nor better treated than pests killed by an application.
5. I think the shift toward more selective and non-lethal modes of control — mating disruption, sterile-insect releases, growth regulators that arrest development rather than nerve agents — is a genuine moral improvement and not merely an instrumental one, holding crop losses fixed.
6. I am inclined to think insect experience, if there is any, is very finely time-sliced. Holometabolous development and very short adult lives make me doubt that there is a single subject persisting across the life of a caterpillar and the butterfly it becomes, and I don't think we are entitled to assume anything like a temporally unified stream of consciousness.
7. In other decision contexts I reject Pascalian reasoning: I don't think one should act on a very small probability of a vast payoff, and I have little patience for arguments that run on astronomical stakes plus speculative mechanisms.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature — I know the literature and I don't need citations or a reading list, I need to know what commits me to what. Please work through the pairs, rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what dropping it would cost the chapter. I'd also like a verdict on which single revision cleans up the most. Note which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and say why they do, since I suspect at least one item on the list is fine as it stands. The item I'm personally most uneasy about is 6, but please don't assume that's where the trouble is: tell me if the real problem sits elsewhere. Finally, indicate how confident you are in each diagnosis, and single out the one claim in your reply you're least sure of.
