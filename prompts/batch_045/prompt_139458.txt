Infinitism (Klein's version) says a belief is justified only if there's an endless, non-repeating chain of reasons behind it, and it answers the "but we're finite creatures" objection by saying those reasons only have to be *available* to you, not all occurrently in your head at once.

I've been playing with a variant and can't tell whether it's a genuinely different view or just infinitism in a costume. Call it meta-infinitism: at the first order justification is boring and finite — reasons bottom out in experience, or in a coherent web, whatever, no infinite chain needed. The endless non-repeating regress lives one level up: it's the vindication of our habit of treating those stopping points as stopping points. Any reason you offer for trusting experience can itself be asked for a further reason, forever, and no link in that chain repeats an earlier one.

What I'd like explained:

1. Does this stay distinct from plain infinitism? My worry is that if the higher-order chain has to contain reasons concerning particular first-order beliefs, the infinite chain has snuck back into first-order justification anyway, and I've just redescribed the same view.
2. Does it still dodge the finitude objection, given that the meta-level chain is every bit as untraversable as the first-order one?
3. Does it still solve what infinitism was invented to solve — that no starting point should be arbitrary — or does the arbitrariness just relocate into the decision to run a vindication regress at all?
4. If the vindication never completes, is that a real cost, or should an infinitist be completely fine with an endorsement that never arrives?
