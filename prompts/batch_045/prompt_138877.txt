I hold something like the hinge of history hypothesis: that we live in an unusually short and unusually consequential period, that the long-run fate of humanity is largely settled within it, and that this makes the expected stakes of what we do now far greater than they would be at most other times. Tell me what would count against it.

I want the descriptive claim (that we are at a hinge) separated from the practical claim (that this makes present choices astronomically high-stakes), because I suspect the second could survive the failure of the first. On the descriptive side, take seriously two competitors. Call the first the rotating hinge: each major technology resets the risk profile and opens a fresh decisive window, so decisiveness is spread across many periods rather than concentrated in ours, and the per-decade stakes are diluted accordingly. Call the second the lock-in model: what makes our period matter is not the risk of premature extinction at all, but the fact that an early, effectively irreversible settlement of which values get propagated is taking place now. I also want to know how much weight to give the coincidence worry — a hinge as narrow as a century is a strange place to find oneself, and I can't tell whether that observation counts against the hypothesis or is simply what being at a hinge would look like from inside.

Rank what you find from fatal to merely awkward, and say which defeaters a defender of the view would concede rather than explain away. I am most likely to be moved by two things: whether there is any tractable lever on long-run outcomes at all, and the suspicion that the hypothesis has been insulated against every possible finding, since any period can be declared the hinge after the fact and any weak evidence met by retreating to expected-value framing with small probabilities. Assess whether that insulation is real, and whether it is a defect in a claim of this kind.
