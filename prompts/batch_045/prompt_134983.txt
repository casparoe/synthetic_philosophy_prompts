I'm drafting a position paper on rectificatory justice for a study group — most of the group works on climate loss-and-damage, which is why the question of what happens to a duty when the institution that incurred it no longer exists keeps coming up — and before I circulate it I'd like an audit of the view I've ended up with. Stated flatly, I hold: (a) reparative duties are grounded in the wrong itself, not in present distributive injustice and not in the good that repairing would do; (b) the non-identity problem is no bar — that present-day claimants would not have existed had the injustice not occurred does not weaken their standing; (c) the duty is inherited by the persisting political institution that committed the wrong, and current members owe their share in virtue of membership and inherited benefit, never in virtue of ancestral guilt; (d) partly because of that and partly because who your ancestors were is brute luck, the cost should be met from general revenue and not apportioned by ancestry, so that people whose families arrived after the injustice owe their share too; (e) on the receiving side, though, I do want eligibility to track descent from the wronged group; (f) where the wrongdoing institution no longer exists, the claim lapses, or at any rate weakens past the point of being enforceable; (g) more generally I am not a general rectifier — present holdings, most of which descend from conquest or theft somewhere back, deserve real respect and are not all up for redistribution; and (h) methodologically, I want amounts anchored to a but-for baseline rather than to negotiated symbolic sums, and where that baseline cannot be estimated I'm inclined to say the magnitude is simply indeterminate.

Two things sharpen the question. First, the case I keep using: Caldera and Meridian are islands where an identical system of forced plantation labour was abolished in the same year, with the same numbers subjected to it and the same present-day gaps in land ownership, wealth, and health between the descended community and everyone else. On Meridian the administration that ran it passed by continuous succession into today's democratic state. On Caldera it was run by a chartered company, dissolved with compensation to its shareholders, and the state that licenced it was overthrown and replaced by a new constitutional order that expressly disclaims succession and whose citizens mostly descend from people who were never its subjects. I want to say Meridian owes a great deal and Caldera owes nothing, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that verdict follows from my principles or is just (f) doing all the work. Second, a reviewer has proposed replacing the lapse rule in (f) with a role-succession test: a claim survives whenever some institution now occupies the normative role of the wrongdoer — holds what it held, exercises the powers it exercised, draws the advantages it created. My worry is that this proves too much, since on a long enough view every state occupies some predecessor's role, in which case it does not rescue (f) at all but simply dissolves it into the sweeping rectification that (g) is meant to rule out.

What I'm asking for is an audit rather than an overview. Go through the set and tell me which commitments are in genuine tension and how badly, ordered by severity; flag the pairs that only look inconsistent and say why the appearance dissolves; for each real tension say exactly what would have to give — the thesis itself, or only the usual motivation for it — and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set; tell me whether my Caldera/Meridian verdict is one my principles can actually deliver; and name the single revision that would clean up the most. If one of (a) through (h) is doing no real work, or is redundant with another, say that as well. Please write in American English.
