I've just come home from a long argument with an old friend about what it takes for something to be alive, and I'd like your help settling a bet without settling it by cheating. By cheating I mean looking the answer up: I don't want a report on what the textbooks, the committees, or the origin-of-life people have decided, and I don't want citations. I want the shape of the disagreement, and a prediction. My friend and I agreed on far more than either of us expected, and the residue that we couldn't agree on is what I'm writing about.

Here is her track record, as I managed to get it out of her over about three hours. A mule: alive, despite being sterile. A bacterial endospore that has sat inactive in a museum drawer for two centuries, metabolizing nothing: alive. A viable seed in a sealed packet from a garden centre: alive. Cells from an immortalized human cell line dividing in a dish: alive. A mitochondrion inside one of those cells: not alive, and she was firm about this, even though it has its own genome, its own double membrane, its own metabolism of a sort, and divides. A virus particle outside a cell: not alive. The same virus once it has taken over a host cell's machinery and is churning out more virus particles: still not alive; what is alive, she said, is the infected cell. A prion: not alive. A self-replicating RNA molecule in a test tube with a supply of activated nucleotides, which copies itself with variation and therefore evolves under selection: not alive, and here she was immovable, which is where I first felt the ground shift, because this thing satisfies the working definition that astrobiologists reach for when they need one. A ribosome: not alive. A fire: not alive, we agreed on that without discussion. A self-replicating computer program that mutates as it copies: not alive, also without discussion.

I've been turning her verdicts over since, and it seems to me that at least two quite different rules fit all of them. One is organizational: a thing is alive just in case it is a bounded chemical system that builds and repairs its own organization out of its own metabolism, with the endospore and the seed counted in because they retain the disposition to resume that activity when conditions allow. That rule explains the mule, the seed, the endospore and the cell line; it excludes the mitochondrion on the grounds that the mitochondrion does not make the proteins and membranes that constitute it, and it excludes the virus, the prion and the naked RNA because none of them maintains a self-produced boundary. The other rule is historical rather than organizational: a thing is alive only if it was produced by things of its own kind, so that life is a property a thing inherits from a lineage and not a property it could have in virtue of how it is put together right now. That rule also gets every verdict above right, including the mitochondrion, on the grounds that mitochondria descend from mitochondria but are built by the cell that contains them, and including the RNA and the computer program, which we made rather than something of their own kind making them. I've noticed that each rule has one awkward case: her mitochondrion verdict sits badly with the historical rule, since mitochondria really do arise from mitochondria, and her endospore verdict sits badly with the organizational rule unless that rule is read dispositionally rather than as requiring activity here and now. I take that as evidence that I haven't yet found the rule she is actually running.

So here is the case we broke on, and the one I want you to predict. Suppose a laboratory assembles a cell entirely from chemically synthesized parts: a genome printed from a stored sequence, membrane lipids and the rest of the molecular machinery supplied from the shelf. Once assembled, this cell metabolizes, maintains and repairs itself, grows, and divides indefinitely, and its daughters do the same, and their daughters after them. It differs from an ordinary bacterium in no organizational respect whatever, but it has no ancestors: nothing of its kind produced it. I asked her whether the first such cell is alive and she refused to answer, which makes me think she had noticed the same fork in the road that I had.

What I'd like from you is this: predict her verdict on the first synthetic cell; say which rule you think she is running, and why that rule is a better fit to her verdicts than the alternatives; set out the other rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well and say what each of them predicts about this case, including the ones that agree with your prediction for the wrong reasons; and then name the further case you would put to her that would best separate the surviving rules from each other, whether that is a question about the descendants of the synthetic cell rather than about the cell itself, or about something else entirely, and say why that case discriminates where this one only half does.

One stylistic request: write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email to a friend or a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
