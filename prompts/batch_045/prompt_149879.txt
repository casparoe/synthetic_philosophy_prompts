I teach philosophy at a British university — mostly ancient philosophy with some epistemology — and I've just come back from a departmental awayday in a slightly damp hotel outside Coventry, where we spent most of the afternoon arguing about whether to let students use LLMs freely in assessed work. My colleague in philosophy of cognitive science, who is lovely and a hard-line optimist about all this, kept saying it's just the calculator argument again: nobody now worries that calculators destroyed our grip on arithmetic, the calculator took over a mechanical step nobody enjoyed and freed us to do real mathematics, and an LLM will do exactly the same for writing and reasoning. A second colleague reached for Clark and Chalmers's Otto case — the man with a memory impairment who keeps all his directions in a notebook he carries with him everywhere, consults without deliberating, and trusts without checking — and said that if a notebook can be part of your mind on those grounds, an LLM is only a fancier notebook, so there's nothing to mourn. I don't want to be the department's resident grump, but neither of these feels right to me and I can't say exactly why, and I have to put something coherent into a committee paper by Friday.

So here's what I'd like: an audit of both analogies, in the sense of what maps onto what, where the mapping gives, and whether the conclusion they're being used to carry — that unrestricted use is fine and understanding survives intact — actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table. For the calculator case I've been playing with three tests of my own and I'd like them stress-tested, because I suspect they may be gerrymandered to give the answer I already want. First, the "checksum" condition: can the user, unaided, tell a right output from a wrong one in the relevant range? With a calculator you can still see that seven eights aren't fifty-four, and that residual competence is part of what school arithmetic is training; with an LLM's reading of a passage, or its summary of an argument, I often can't tell, and my students definitely can't. Second, the "bottleneck" condition: does the tool take over the step that used to be my error-detection site, or merely a routine execution step I'd already mastered? Long division is execution; deciding what the crux of an objection is, is judgement. Third, something I'm calling the "regeneration" condition: is the unaided capacity still being reproduced somewhere in the practice, or does adoption at scale quietly remove the people who could restart it if the tool vanished? Nobody in my department can knap flint and no one thinks that's a tragedy, but the disanalogy there is that nothing else in our intellectual life depended on anyone being able to knap flint. I'd also want to know whether I'm even asking the right question: is the erosion worry about an individual's competence, or about the transmission chain across a population, and does it matter that the second could be fine while the first is wrecked? And on the Otto analogy specifically — it looks to me as though the conditions for counting an external resource as part of your mind are conditions about availability and trust, not about comprehension, and that Otto at some point wrote or reviewed his own entries whereas an LLM output is authored by something whose reasons I never see, so "endorsement" is doing completely different work in the two cases. If that's right, the extended-mind analogy doesn't answer the erosion question at all, it changes the subject — but tell me if I'm being unfair to it.

Give me the correspondence tables for both analogies (calculator and notebook, side by side if that's clearer), sort the disanalogies into the ones that bear on my colleague's conclusion and the ones that are just noise, and then tell me whether the relaxed conclusion survives or whether the analogy was doing all the work. If neither analogy really carries it, is there a better one people in this area lean on? Three candidates I keep circling: the spellchecker, since we did lose a bit of spelling ability to it and nobody mourns it, presumably because spelling was never where the judgement lived; satnav, where we outsourced wayfinding and arguably did lose something with a phenomenology to it; and the complaint Socrates makes near the end of the Phaedrus, that relying on written marks will breed forgetfulness and give people the reputation of wisdom without the reality — which is interesting precisely because he looks to have been straightforwardly wrong, so I'd want to know whether that's evidence for the optimists or whether the fact that writing still left the reader to do the thinking is exactly the point that makes it a bad friend to them. If you can, finish with two or three sentences I could actually read out to the committee.
