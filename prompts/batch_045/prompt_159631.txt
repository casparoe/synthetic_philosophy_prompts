Me and a friend keep going in circles about Bongard problems, so I want the document we should have produced instead of another argument: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between us.

His view: solving one means recovering an explicit predicate over visual primitives, the kind of thing you could write down as a short sentence or a program, and any system that gets them right without that isn't doing concept induction at all, it's matching descriptions or similarity and happening to land on the right answer. Mine: the "explicit rule" requirement is a leftover prejudice from symbolic AI, and the only thing that should count is whether a system's decision function survives the right perturbations, whatever it's made of internally. (We also disagree about whether getting it from six-plus-six panels is the impressive part or just a presentation detail.)

So draft it properly: a numbered section of claims we could both sign — about the format, about what the few-shotness actually buys, about what should count as success — then a disagreements table where each row is phrased in the wording its own side would accept for itself. Then the cruxes, and for each one a test or case we'd both agree bears on it, with both of our predictions written down in advance.

Some candidate crux-tests, use them if they're any good and replace them if not: (1) vocabulary swap — same rule re-instantiated in a totally different visual vocabulary (squiggles and blobs instead of triangles and circles), with accuracy on the transfer set as the readout; (2) relational-invisible panels — both classes matched on every holistic statistic you like (same shape counts, same areas, same ink), and the separating property is purely relational, e.g. whether the dot falls inside or outside the convex hull of the other objects, so nothing short of representing the relation can work; (3) no-rule control — a pseudo-Bongard set where no single property seperates the classes but each panel carries a per-item cue, to measure how much of any reported accuracy is rule-finding rather than cue-grabbing; (4) swapped-member probe — move one panel across the line and ask which panel the solver now flags as mislabelled, since a rule-holder should flag the moved one and a similarity-matcher should flag whichever panel is most atypical.
