I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a long piece about risk-scoring tools — the pretrial kind (the whole fight a few years back where one side looked at false-positive rates across races and the vendor answered that the tool was calibrated) and, in my current reporting, a bank's credit model. Every source I talk to reaches for the same move, and they use it to walk off in opposite directions. The vendor-side guy: these impossibility results are just Arrow's theorem again, and Arrow's theorem never stopped anyone from holding an election, so this is a paper tiger and you should stop writing about it. The academic: it's Arrow's theorem again, which means fairness is mathematically impossible, full stop. Same analogy, opposite conclusions, and I can't tell who's getting away with something.

Here's the setup as I understand it, so correct me if I have it wrong. There's a score, two groups, and the groups have different base rates for the thing your predicting. The criteria on the table: the score should mean the same thing inside each group (calibration within groups — of the people handed a 70, about 70% actually go on to reoffend or default, in both groups), and it should make its mistakes at the same rate in both groups (equal false positive rates, equal false negative rates). The result, supposedly, is that you can't have all of it at once except in two degenerate cases: the base rates are actually equal, or you predict perfectly. So: audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table — is the voter the group, or the criterion, or the individual being scored, and what plays the role of the preference ranking and of the alternatives? Then tell me where the mapping breaks. My hunches, which I'd rather you killed than confirmed if they're wrong: (1) in Arrow there's no fact of the matter about the right ranking, but here there is a real ground truth and one of the three criteria is basically an accuracy condition, so this isn't a clash between equally respectable democratic desiderata; (2) Arrow's loophole is a dictatorship, which is morally the worst thing in the list, whereas the loophole here is equal base rates, which is morally the thing you wanted anyway — so the impossibility is partly a fact about an unjust world rather than about the mathematics, and I want to know if that's a real difference or a coincidence of the formalism; (3) Arrow is stuck with ordinal rankings and no interpersonal comparisons, while all three criteria here are cardinal and are explicitly comparisons across groups. Also: is there an actual formal result connecting the two, or is everyone just reaching for the most famous impossibility theorem they can name? I don't need a paper title; I just need to know whether I can write "the analogy is loose" without a researcher emailing me.

Then the paragraph my editor actually wants: does either conclusion survive? Is "Arrow didn't kill voting, so this won't kill risk scores" a good argument, or does it lean on a disanalogy about how often the pathology shows up in practice? Is "fairness is mathematically impossible" a fair thing to print? What does the analogy quietly import that isn't in the target — I suspect it makes the three criteria look like competing constituencies being weighed when they're really constraints on one function — and what is it simply silent about, so I don't write a sentence that's technically defensible but useless to the city council that has to decide whether to buy the tool. And if Arrow is the wrong ancestor, name me a better one: Simpson's paradox, a sensitivity/specificity-style tradeoff, something else, and say what it gets right that Arrow doesn't.
