I'm a PhD student in mathematics, and over the last year of formalizing a chunk of real analysis in a proof assistant I have drifted into scruples I cannot shake: I no longer feel entitled to assert A or not-A for an arbitrary mathematical A, though I am equally unwilling to assert the negation of that disjunction. I am happy with the double negation of each individual instance, and I want to keep the witness requirements intact, so that a proof of an existential statement hands me a witness and a proof of a disjunction tells me which side holds.

The trouble is that my scruples are not uniform, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Here are my verdicts. (1) For a bounded question — whether there is an odd perfect number below 10^60, whether the billionth digit of the decimal expansion of pi is 7 — I assert the disjunction: the search terminates in principle, and I could in principle be told which disjunct holds. (2) For Goldbach's conjecture, which a single finite counterexample would refute, I withhold the disjunction but I do accept that if it is not not-true, then it is true. (3) For the twin prime conjecture, where no finite witness settles it in either direction, I withhold both, and it strikes me as a different kind of thing from Goldbach, not merely a harder one. (4) For there is an odd perfect number — unbounded, but verifiable by a single finite witness — I withhold the disjunction yet accept that if it is impossible that there is none, then there is one; I am told this is Markov's principle and that it is contested. (5) For the program I started on my machine three days ago eventually halts, which I realize has the same logical shape as (4), I withhold both; this is the pair I keep going back and forth on. The difference I keep reaching for is that the machine's halting is something happening in time that may not yet be settled, whereas the odd-perfect-number question is timeless — but I worry that this imports an anti-realism about the future that I would then have to apply to mathematics itself if I also thought mathematical truth gets created as we prove things. (6) For the real coded by this computable Cauchy sequence with a computable modulus of convergence is strictly positive, I withhold the disjunction and have no verdict at all on the double-negation step; that is where my confidence runs out. (7) I accept the Brouwerian claim that every total function from the reals to the reals comes out continuous in the intuitionistic setting, yet I go on happily using the step function that sends negative inputs to 0 and everything else to 1 in my applied work, and I do not feel that I am contradicting myself.

What I want is a principle — or two or three competing candidates — stated precisely enough that I could formalize it, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. My own attempt is this: call A limit-settled when there is a computable procedure emitting guesses in 0 and 1 whose convergence I can prove constructively and whose limit is 1 exactly if A holds; then I propose to assert A or not-A exactly for limit-settled A, and to assert the double-negation step for A exactly when not-A is finitely witnessable. Please test that against my cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I should give up. Two things I especially want addressed: first, whether asserting the disjunction for every limit-settled A is compatible with keeping my witness requirement, since if a disjunction is assertible only when one side is provable, then asserting A or not-A across a whole class of statements seems to force every statement in that class to be already decided, which would collapse the position unless assertible and provable come apart; and second, whether the more expensive principle that saves both (4) and (5) by distinguishing timeless subject matter from ongoing processes is genuinely defensible or merely ad hoc. Then apply whatever principle you land on to cases I have not listed: the Riemann hypothesis, granting for the sake of argument that it can be written with a single unbounded universal quantifier over a decidable matrix; the statement that some specific program never halts; a statement of higher-order set theory such as the continuum hypothesis, where my computable-approximation talk seems to have no purchase at all; an ordinary empirical claim about a measurement; and the claim that I will finish this formalization by next June. Finally, please mark how confident you are in each thing you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
