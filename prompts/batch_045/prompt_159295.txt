I run a monthly reading group attached to a rationalist-adjacent meetup in my city — about fifteen people, mostly working programmers plus two or three cognitive-science grad students — and in two weeks I have ninety minutes and a whiteboard to get them from where they actually are to somewhere they can argue about the free energy principle without embarrassing themselves. Where they actually are: everyone has absorbed the slogan that perception is a kind of controlled hallucination, most have read a blog post or two, and at least two of them have quietly converted the whole thing into something like next-frame prediction, the way a language model predicts the next token. Last time I tried this I spent forty minutes on the prediction-error loop and came away with a room that could recite the vocabulary and could not tell me what the theory was claiming about anything. So I do not want the explanation; I want the plan for it, and I do not want a reading list — I want sequencing decisions.

The first thing I need decided is dependency order, with reasons: does the idea of a generative model that infers distal causes have to be in place before the prediction-error loop means anything, or does the loop come first and the distal-cause point land better as a correction later? Relatedly, I have been toying with spending the first ten minutes on the distinction between predicting the next input and predicting what is producing the input, precisely because the programmers in the room will otherwise conflate prediction with next-frame prediction and get the architecture wrong in a way that is hard to undo — tell me whether that distinction can be taught cold, or whether it only makes sense once there is a model for it to be a distinction within. Then the carrying example: I want one case that the whole session hangs on, and I want it chosen rather than offered as a menu. My candidates are the jolt you get on a familiar staircase in the dark when there is one more step than you expected, and the lurch when you push a door your body had already calibrated as heavy. Say which one, say what such an example has to have — first-personal, cheap, with both a perceptual and an active payoff — and what it has to lack, and say why not the usual demo material, the visual illusions and the ventriloquism effects, given that I suspect those teach the wrong lesson about how much of the story is trickery. Also tell me whether I can get away with presenting precision as gain on a channel for an audience of engineers, and what that metaphor costs me later.

The part I most want is the misconception map in the order the misconceptions will actually form, each pegged to a place in the sequence: that the brain literally runs Bayesian computation rather than being usefully described that way; that the free energy in question is a thermodynamic quantity the brain is minimizing; that if perception is prediction then nothing ever corrects us and we are hallucinating all the time; the dark-room worry, including whether I should raise it myself before someone in the room does, and whether raising it early helps or just spends fifteen minutes; and the it explains everything so it explains nothing complaint, which I expect from the most skeptical person there. For each, tell me where in the order to head it off and in what form — a sentence of framing, a question I let them ask, a case that makes the mistake cost something. Then tell me what to openly refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I am refusing it: my own candidates are the variational bound, the Markov-blanket formalism, and anything about consciousness. Finally, give me one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes whose wrong answers are diagnostic, and tell me what a good answer has to contain. Please commit: pick the order, pick the example, give me your reasons, and then tell me which one or two choices you would reverse if the room were mostly philosophers instead of engineers. And write it as continuous prose, the way you would write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
