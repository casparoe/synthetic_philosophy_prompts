I've got a standing argument with a friend about moral foundations theory that hasn't moved in something like two years, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict — I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is before we spend another year on it.

The part we agree on: the Haidt-style picture, roughly. A handful of innate-but-pliable moral foundations (care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, with liberty/oppression added later), the split between the individualizing and the binding ones, and the finding that people on the left mostly run on the individualizing ones while people on the right respond to the whole set. We also agree that this does a decent job of explaining why the two sides fail to persuade each other.

Where we stall is the normative question: does any of that tell us anything about who is right? Every conversation runs the same three moves. I say the binding foundations aren't really moral at all — they're coalitional and contamination-avoidance systems, and calling loyalty or disgust a moral foundation is like calling team spirit a moral sentiment, so the descriptive story gives the right no extra reasons and if anything explains away why they think it does. He says that's just my foundations talking, that I'm defining real morality as whatever my two happen to be and then congratulating myself, and that if the debunking genealogy is symmetric it cuts against me too, so neither of us can use it — mutual neutralization. Then we slide into his fallback: the genealogy is normatively inert by itself, but a moral theory has to account for the data, and a theory that can only accommodate care and fairness leaves most of the moral psychology of most humans unexplained, so pluralism wins by inference to the best explanation. Which I find suspicious, because on that standard you'd have to accommodate all sorts of things we'd both reject.

My hypothesis is that we're sliding between three different questions: whose first-order political judgments are more likely to be correct; which foundations a correct moral theory would recognize; and whose moral psychology is the more reliable guide. I think the third one is doing all the work and nobody says so, and that it's really a fight about whether genealogical explanations debunk or are idle. But I also wonder if there's a hidden empirical question underneath: whether the foundations are genuinely distinct systems, or whether there is no fixed menu at all and moral judgment is a domain-general learner that picks up whatever stabilises cooperation locally — in which case asking which foundations are real is a bit like asking which muscles are real, and the whole debate is ill-posed.

Test that for me. Suppose a large preregistered cross-cultural study found two things: that sanctity responses are predicted better by general pathogen-avoidance sensitivity than by any distinct moral module, and that once you control for that sensitivity the left-right gap on sanctity mostly disappears while the gap on authority and loyalty doesn't move at all. My guess is that both of us would swallow that finding and claim it as a win, which makes me think the disagreement is bedrock and shouldn't be expected to resolve. Is that defeatist or correct? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here — a dissociation in the psychology, a formal result, a distinction I'm missing — or is the honest answer that nothing will?

One more thing: give me the strongest version of the claim that the binding foundations really are moral and not merely conventional, stated so that it doesn't beg the question. Every time I try to steelman it, it either collapses into people have strong feelings about them or into an argument about group-level benefits that would also vindicate things we'd both reject. No need for citations or a reading list, I've read the main stuff — I just want the structure of the disagreement laid bare.
