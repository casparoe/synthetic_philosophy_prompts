I need a favour and it's a slightly weird one. I'm the person who writes the weekly flood-risk numbers for our regional water agency — every Monday I put a probability on something like "the river tops the gauge at the Main Street bridge within seven days," and the emergency crew uses it to decide whether to pre-position pumps. By our own scoring we were having a genuinely good spring. Then a creek that isn't on our forecast list at all came down in April and gutted three houses on my cousin's street, and it turned out nobody had ever assigned a probability to anything about that creek, and nobody had actually gone and looked at it in eleven years. I've been chewing on this ever since, and I've roped in two friends from grad school who've now spent about a year an a half talking past each other about it.

One of them is an accuracy-first epistemic utility theorist: the only fundamental epistemic good is how close your credences are to the truth, measured by a strictly proper scoring rule, and you can get probabilism and conditionalization out of that by dominance arguments without smuggling in anything pragmatic. The other thinks inquiry is governed by norms that aren't accuracy norms at all — that there's something epistemically wrong with sitting on a perfectly accurate number you inherited and never checked, and with being accurate on the question you happened to pick rather than the question that mattered, and that you can't get either of those out of a scoring rule because scoring rules grade states, not the activity of finding things out.

What I want from you is the document they'd both sign if they were being honest and somebody made them be precise. The claims both can accept — including about what a strictly proper scoring rule does and doesn't measure, about whether inaccuracy is at least *an* epistemic bad, and about whether accuracy considerations really do vindicate probabilism and updating. Then where they actually differ, phrased so each one would recognise their own view in the wording. Then the cruxes, by which I mean the sub-questions where settling it would move one of them. Then, for each crux, a concrete case or piece of reasoning both would agree bears on it, and what each of them would predict about how it comes out.

A few I definately want in there. First: the inherited-model case — a forecast handed down from head office that turns out to be extremely accurate and that nobody at our end understands or has checked; is anything missing there, epistemically, or not? Second: question-shopping — two forecasters who are equally accurate on the questions they each chose to answer, where one deliberately chose the easy ones; does accuracy-first have any complaint, and should it? Third: whether "we genuinely have no idea" is just a middling credence or a third thing, and what a framework that only scores credences can say about suspending. Fourth: whether going out and getting more evidence can actually lower your expected accuracy, and if the answer depends on the scoring rule, whether that's an embarrassment or just a detail — I have a foggy memory that this is scoring-rule-sensitive and none of us can reconstruct why. Fifth: how much the dominance argument for probabilism depends on which scoring rule you start from, and whether whichever way that goes helps either side. And if some of the fight is just about the word "epistemic," say which part and why, because at this point I'd take that as progress.

Two practical things. It's going into an email to our review board, so no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose, paragraphs, in roughly that order. And no references or reading suggestions; nobody here is going to look anything up, and I'd rather have your reasoning than a list of names.
