I'm trying to find out whether a pattern I've noticed in my own working life has already been described and given a name, and — more importantly than the name — how it should be carved up: what belongs to it, what merely resembles it, and where it stops.

The background: I'm a biostatistician in a consulting role, and for about two years I've used a language model as part of almost everything I do. I don't think I'm doing what the people who worry about this sort of thing usually describe. I don't copy output and pass it along. I reject a fair amount of what the model gives me — my sense is somewhere between a quarter and a third — and I can usually say what is wrong with it. My difficulty is elsewhere.

Three instances, so the shape is clear.

First. The model proposes an analysis, or writes the code for one, and I read it and it seems right, but I'm uneasy about one specific step: whether a random-effects structure is identified here, whether a robust variance estimator is the right choice, whether the missingness is plausibly ignorable. What I do with that unease is ask the model. Sometimes in the same conversation, sometimes in a fresh one, sometimes by asking it to critique the answer it just gave me. I then act on the reply. When I have checked independently — a colleague, a book on my shelf, actually running the simulation — the things the model flagged have mostly been real, and the things it cleared have mostly been fine. So the vetting works. But the vetting is done by the thing being vetted.

Second. I ask it to draft passages for reports and protocols. I can tell good prose from bad, and I rewrite heavily. But I've noticed that my sense of what the finished passage should sound like has drifted toward what the model produces, so that what I am now checking drafts against is a standard the model taught me. When I ask a colleague, they say the writing is fine. That isn't evidence, since they're reading it with the same drifted standard.

Third. Increasingly I ask the model what a careful reviewer would want checked. The checklist I use to audit the work is generated by the system that produced the work. If you had told me two years ago that I would end up asking a tool what counts as a mistake in its own output, I'd have said that was an obviously bad idea. It doesn't feel like one in practice, because the items on the list are sensible and I recognize them as things I would have thought of.

Two things make me think this isn't just ordinary tool use, and neither is the worry I usually see raised. The first is that I am not a layperson here. I could check these things myself — derive the result, work the example, look it up — I have the training, and the cost is a day, not a career. I simply don't, and over two years the number of times I have has fallen to nearly zero. So this isn't a case of having no choice but to defer. The second is that I suspect my blind spots and the model's are correlated. The cases where I feel uneasy and ask are the cases where I already half-suspect an error; the cases where I feel no unease and don't ask are exactly the cases where the model and I share a default assumption. The check is least likely to happen where it is most needed. That is different, it seems to me, from using a spellchecker or a calculator, where the errors I can't catch myself are of a different kind from the errors I can.

I've seen discussions of several things nearby, described loosely: research on how people forget what they can look up; the idea that a tool can become part of your own thinking; work on deferring to experts one cannot in principle check; work on over-trusting automated systems; and, in philosophy, worries about using a source to establish that very source's reliability. None of these seems to be quite my case, but I can't tell whether that's because mine is genuinely distinct or because I don't know the vocabulary.

So: does this configuration have an established name — retained critical ability whose grounds are supplied by the system being criticized, with errors correlated between critic and system — and if not, what would you call it, and how would you define it so as to exclude the neighbours? In particular I'd want the boundary cases settled: is putting the same question to a differently built model an independent check or not; does it change anything that I audit a random one item in twenty rather than none; does it matter that the tool is right most of the time; does the phenomenon disappear if the person could, with a week of study, check for themselves, and does it come back in a different form when they can't; and is a spellchecker or a calculator genuinely the same thing in miniature, or is there a real break, and if so where exactly does it fall?
