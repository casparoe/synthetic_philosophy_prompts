I sit on a committee at my university that advises the president on commemoration — which names stay on buildings, which get a plaque or a contextualising sentence, which come down — and I also TA for an introductory ethics course that just did a week on moral luck and on excuses. In both settings the same sentence keeps terminating the discussion: "he was a man of his time." I have now heard it said about Jefferson (a residence hall), about Churchill (a seminar room), and about Columbus (a dining hall named for the college that took its name from him). Each time it was used to do something different: once to close the question whether the man was himself blameworthy, once to argue that the name should stay, once to license continued admiration of the achievements while conceding the vice. And each time it worked — people who had been arguing stopped — even though I do not think anyone in the room could have said what the sentence asserted. That combination is what I find interesting: a remark that is simultaneously decisive in conversation and unstateable as a premise.

I have tried writing it out as an argument several times and a different premise does the work each time, which is why I suspect the slogan is really a family of claims that get run together. Here is my tentative list, which I would like you to replace or extend rather than endorse. (i) A causal claim: his beliefs were produced by his environment — true of everyone, so presumably not the operative reading. (ii) An epistemic excuse: the considerations against what he did were not available to him. But "available" splits: nobody anywhere had articulated them; they had been articulated but only by people he had no contact with; they had been articulated by his own contemporaries in print he could have read, but not in a form he had reason to find credible, which makes it a claim about moral testimony and deference rather than about evidence. (iii) A counterfactual or package-deal claim: had he not held the objectionable view, he would not have done the admirable thing — the leisure that slaveholding bought the author of the Declaration, the imperial commitments entangled with the resolve in 1940 — so that blame and credit cannot be separated into two ledgers. (iv) A claim about the reference class: "his time" is gerrymandered, since plenty of his contemporaries dissented, and the sentence works by silently selecting the contemporaries who agreed. (v) A claim about what we are doing when we name a building: not assigning blame but deciding whether commemoration is endorsement, record, or aspiration — in which case the sentence answers a different question from the one being asked and the blame question is only a proxy for it. There is also something I cannot get straight about asymmetry: whether withholding blame for a past agent commits us to withholding praise, and whether our practice is consistent about that.

What I want is a menu, not a verdict. Set out the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, ordered roughly from trivial to substantive to false, and say for each whether it is defensible and what kind of thing would settle it — a historical question about what was in circulation, a semantic question about "blameworthy," a question about the point of commemoration. Then tell me which reading, if any, would actually support each of the three uses I described, and whether the conclusion survives on the reading strong enough to state. I do not need a verdict on Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus; I need to know what the committee should ask itself instead. And if you think the sentence is doing something I have not listed — deflecting, expressing a hedged attitude, marking a refusal to keep arguing — say so, because that would explain why it stops conversations so reliably.
