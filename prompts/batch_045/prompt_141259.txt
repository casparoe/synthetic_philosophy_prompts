I teach an upper-division seminar on truth and knowledge — fourteen students, a ninety-minute block, one whiteboard and no AV. Nearly all of them are philosophy majors with a single logic course behind them: fine with truth tables, visibly uneasy as soon as quantifiers start nesting, so I'd like the rules of anything you design to be stated in plain English, with notation appearing only on the board during the debrief, if at all. Three of the fourteen are auditing from a computer science master's programme and will begin trying to formalise within about ninety seconds; if the design can put that impulse to work rather than be derailed by it, so much the better.

What I want them to run into is the argument that starts from a principle almost everyone in the room will endorse on reflection — roughly, that if a proposition is true then it is possible for someone to come to know it — and squeezes it into the conclusion that every truth is in fact known by someone. I want this to arrive as a discovery about a game they are actually playing, not as a derivation I perform at the front, so I don't want any mini-lecture up front and I don't want the name Fitch or the phrase knowability paradox used anywhere before the debrief. My rough idea, which you should freely replace, is a set of sealed envelopes or face-down cards, each standing for a state of affairs nobody in the room can currently check (a coin in a box no one will open, a counter drawn from a bag, and — importantly — at least one card whose text is about its own unopened status), together with a one-sentence Charter of Inquiry the class adopts collectively at the outset and must be willing to apply to any card whatever, plus some mechanism such as spendable warrants or an assigned Inspector by which finding a thing out is something that really happens in the room, and a Cartographer at the board logging every proposition the class has conceded. If the Charter is genuinely unrestricted, the sealed status of a card ought to collapse under it, and I want them to notice that themselves; and I particularly want a version of the collapse that does not depend on the self-referential card, since the predictable first response is to file the whole thing as a Liar-paradox cousin and move on.

So: design me the session. I need the setup and materials, restricted to what I can make with index cards, envelopes, coins and the board; the roles, the exact wording of the Charter, and the exact wording of the rules of inference players are permitted, phrased so that they read as commonsense rather than as premises of an argument; a timeline for the ninety minutes; at least one second round in which the Charter is weakened, made conditional, or otherwise restricted, so that the class can see what each escape route costs them; a debrief that maps particular moments in the play onto the steps the argument takes, gives the strongest available version of the two replies I most expect them to reach for too quickly (restricting the principle, and conceding an omniscient knower), and only then attaches the standard name to what they have done; an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it in the moment; and a frank statement of which feature of the actual argument the game cannot reproduce and which I will simply have to put on the board myself. Please keep the entire design under nine hundred words.
