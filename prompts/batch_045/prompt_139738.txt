I was at a dinner on Saturday with my partner's old university housemates — six people who have met roughly annually for twenty years and who argue the way people do when they can already hear where the others are about to go. Two of them, Nadia (a former immigration barrister) and Theo (a public-finance economist who reads political theory the way other people do cryptic crosswords), spent the better part of two hours on whether a state has any genuine right to exclude people who want to come in, and the rest of us lost the thread somewhere around the fourth round. I'm a hydrologist; I have no stake in the question and genuinely no idea who won, but I found it riveting, and I've reconstructed it below as faithfully as I can. Both of them have read the reconstruction and concede it is fair, and both have now asked me to referee it, which is obviously hopeless — so I'm delegating. One request: please do not tell me which well-known philosopher each move belongs to. I'm not after a reading list or a map of the literature; I want your verdict on these eight moves exactly as I've written them.

1. Theo opened with what he called the hereditary-membership point. A state's right to exclude, he said, is not modelled on a club or a homeowner; what is distinctive is heredity. When a state admits someone, it does not admit a person, it creates a citizen, and that citizen's children and grandchildren are citizens with votes in a sovereign people the current members will one day hand on. The admission decision is therefore irreversible in a way that ordinary hospitality, hiring, or aid is not: it is a permanent transfer of a share in political power. What a right to exclude really protects is a people's control over the composition of the sovereign body that outlives it, and the standard case for open borders fails because it treats admission as a two-party transaction when it is really a decision about a fourth generation that nobody in the room will meet.

2. Nadia said heredity cannot be the operative feature, because birth works the same way: someone born on the territory becomes a member, and so do her children, nobody chose it, and nobody thinks the state must justify that to outsiders or that its failure to do so makes it illegitimate. So irreversibility by itself cannot generate a right to exclude. She then turned heredity into a test: if what really troubles Theo is the permanent transfer of political power, he should be satisfied by a state that admits people with permanent residence, full civil and economic rights, and the freedom to bring their families, while deferring national voting rights and formal citizenship to the next generation. If he is not satisfied by that, then heredity is not what is driving him, and he owes her a statement of what is.

3. Theo gave two replies. First, the arrangement Nadia describes just is the guest-worker scheme, and it produces a hereditary underclass of people who live and work in a polity they have no vote in; she has not escaped the problem, she has relocated it into the second generation, where it is worse. Second, the birth analogy is broken because no one decides who is born here — there is no decision, no decider, and no one with a duty to justify anything. Immigration is different in kind: it is a decision the state makes, and it makes it in a fiduciary capacity for the people who are already members, whose collective self-government it exists to protect. The question whether we may admit this person is really the question whether we may give away a share of our members' sovereignty, and that question has to be answerable to the members.

4. Nadia said the first reply concedes her point rather than answering it: if deferred membership is unacceptable because it breeds a hereditary political caste, then what is doing the work for Theo is a substantive view about who may share in sovereignty at all, not the irreversibility he started from, and he should drop the hereditary framing since it cannot explain why he rejects her test. The second reply she called a straight non-sequitur: the state's job is to serve its members tells you whom it must answer to, not what it may do to people outside. And refusal of entry is not a mere failure to confer a benefit; it is a harm imposed by coercion — fences, detention, deportation — on people who are forced to deal with a border the state itself drew and maintains by force.

5. Theo answered that coercion is not a distinguishing feature at all: every right to exclude anything is enforced against people who have not consented, including the right each of us has over our own body, and if the presence of coercion dissolved the right then no one would have rights over anything. And the coercion, he said, runs both ways: Nadia's view requires taking the members' political community, which they built and have not agreed to share, and transferring shares in it to people they never chose, at gunpoint if necessary. So she cannot use coercion to break the tie; at most it shows that whoever loses will be coerced.

6. Nadia granted the general point and said it does not reach her actual claim, which is conditional. Rights over holdings, she argued, presuppose that the holdings were not acquired by the very kind of act the right is now used to commit. The territory and the wealth that make exclusion worth exercising were acquired through conquest and extraction and are held under a global property system the rich states largely wrote; and exclusion is today policed most stringently against people who are descendants of the ones whose land and labor were taken. So even if there is a right to exclude in the abstract, it is weakened — perhaps forfeited — in the specific case of people who can point to a historical relation of that kind. The open-borders conclusion then follows for a subset of would-be migrants rather than for everyone, which she called a feature rather than a bug, because it explains why the intuition about former colonial metropoles is so much stronger than the intuition in the abstract case.

7. Theo said he would grant tainted title for the sake of argument and that it still does not deliver admission. Wrongdoing generates a duty to repair the loss, not a duty to surrender the asset; repair is normally dischargeable in money or in kind, and the step from we wronged your ancestors to you may move in needs a premise nobody has supplied. He added that the view mis-ranks claimants in a way its defenders never own up to: on the historical-relation view, a comfortable professional with a colonial grievance has a stronger claim to admission than a much poorer person with no colonial history, yet nobody attracted to open borders actually believes that — and if they do, they have quietly abandoned the egalitarian motivation the position began with.

8. Nadia closed with two points. First, where what was taken is a share in a territory that the wrongdoer now exclusively controls and that cannot be carved up and handed back, cash at a price the wrongdoer sets is not repair but a forced sale of her claim at his valuation; and it is the wrongdoer who should bear the cost of that, not she — money instead is only a genuine alternative if the amount is fixed independently, at which point it starts to look like a levy on states rather than compensation to individuals. Second, the ranking objection misfires: her claim concerns who has a right of admission, not who is neediest, and rights and needs are the wrong things to put on a single scale; the historical-relation claim and the general duty to assist the badly off are answers to different questions, and a state can owe admission to the first group and money to the second.

Go through the exchange move by move: for each of the eight, say whether it is clearly right, largely right, largely wrong, or clearly wrong, and in a sentence or two say what turns on it — in particular whether it genuinely answers the previous move or quietly changes the subject. A small table is the clearest way to do that part. Then, and this is what I most want: tell me what both of them are missing, whether that is a distinction neither reached for, an option that was never on the table, or a shared premise doing more work than either noticed, and say where the argument would have to be joined next if they ever resume it. Give me a bottom line about who is more right overall and on what. Close with a single final line, on its own, in exactly one of these three forms and with nothing else on it: \answer{Nadia wins overall}, \answer{Theo wins overall}, or \answer{evenly split}.
