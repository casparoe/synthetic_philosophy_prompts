I have been asked to contribute a short conceptual-foundations annexe to a health authority's draft methods guide for technology appraisal, and the section I am responsible for has to say what the guide's central rule presupposes. Before I write it, I would like your help reconstructing the implicit theory as sharply as the material allows.

The rule, as the guide states it: a treatment is recommended if its incremental cost per quality-adjusted life year falls below a fixed monetary threshold, and rejected if it exceeds that threshold. The details that seem to matter philosophically are these. QALYs are constructed by weighting each year of life by a quality weight between zero, identified with death, and one, identified with full health, with weights below zero permitted for states judged worse than death. The weights are derived from surveys in which members of the general public choose between a shorter life in full health and a longer life in a described impaired state, and the guide averages across respondents even for states those respondents have never occupied. Gains are then summed linearly across all patients, and the guide states explicitly that the value of a QALY gain does not depend on whose it is, on how many QALYs the patient has already had, or on how the total is distributed. There are three standing exceptions to the threshold. The first is an end-of-life override permitting a premium for patients with short remaining life expectancy. The second is an unmet-need clause permitting a premium where the condition is rare and no alternative treatment exists. The third is a spillover clause: health gains accruing to carers and family members may be counted in sensitivity analysis but are excluded from the base case, and so are the future health care costs a patient would have incurred had they not died.

What I need from you is a reconstruction, not a survey of positions, and I would rather you argue from the structure of the practice than appeal to what is standard in the literature. Specifically:

Distinguish the different strengths of comparability the rule might be committed to: that one person's well-being can be compared in level with another's; that gains and losses can be compared cardinally across persons; and that well-being is measurable on a ratio scale rather than merely an interval scale. Which of these does the arithmetic strictly require? My own suspicion is that division by cost demands more than summation does, and that allowing negative weights demands something specific about where zero sits, but I want this set out precisely, with each commitment tied to the step in the calculation that forces it.

Say whether the practice presupposes a welfarist reading, on which QALYs are a measure of welfare, or an extra-welfarist one, on which they measure health and society may weight health however it likes. Which parts of the practice fit which reading, and what breaks if we switch? I am especially unsure about the survey method: it asks people to evaluate states they have never been in, and the guide simply averages over the familiar gap between how patients in a state rate it and how the general public rates it from outside. What view of well-being does that method quietly commit the guide to, and would patients' own ratings be a better or worse fit for the reading you identify?

Assess whether the no-distributional-weighting clause really commits the guide to an unweighted total view, on which the marginal moral value of a gain is the same whoever receives it, or whether it is compatible with prioritarianism, since the guide says nothing about patients' lifetime well-being levels. Then take up the exceptions. Are the end-of-life override and the unmet-need clause contradictions of the baseline rule, or do they reveal that the baseline was never unweighted in the first place? I would like the difference between weighting by absolute shortfall, the number of QALYs a patient would lose without treatment, and weighting by proportional shortfall, the fraction of remaining life lost, spelled out, and I want to know which of these the end-of-life rule actually instantiates, given that its trigger is life expectancy rather than either shortfall measure. If the answer is neither, say what view of distributive justice a life-expectancy trigger would instead commit us to, and whether that view is one the guide could coherently hold alongside its own baseline.

One member of the panel has proposed, in a footnote, that the threshold is not a moral quantity at all but the shadow price of a fixed budget: the amount of health that unidentified patients elsewhere in the system lose when we spend here. Does that reinterpretation remove the need for interpersonal comparisons, or does it only relocate them, given that the displaced patients are not identified and the comparison is with a counterfactual population? I want a straight answer on whether this is a genuine defence of the rule or a change of subject.

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the comparability assumption were dropped. Could the rule survive as a decision procedure without being an aggregation of well-being at all, for instance as the output of a social choice function over profiles of individual preferences, or as a device for implementing a hypothetical agreement among people who do not know whose position they will occupy? And if the implicit view were replaced by prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, or a capabilities-style view in which the currency is not well-being but what people are able to do and be, which elements of the practice would have to change: the quality weights, the linear summation, the three exceptions, or the currency itself?

Throughout, I want three things kept clearly separate: what the practice strictly presupposes in the sense that it would be unintelligible otherwise; what it merely fits well with because several theories would license it; and where its parts seem to presuppose inconsistent things, so that the guide may be committed to a conjunction no single view would endorse.
