I spent most of my working life as a population ecologist — forty-odd years on life-history theory, on the trade-off between producing many cheap offspring and few expensive ones, and on what happens to that trade-off when a lineage finds itself with abundant, previously unused resource. Since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group on long-term risks from artificial intelligence, and the group has split into two camps that have now spent several sessions talking more or less past one another. I rashly volunteered to draft the joint statement we would produce if we ran our disagreement as a proper adversarial collaboration, and I would like your help with it.

The two camps, for the purposes of the document: one holds that the overriding priority is to prevent human extinction or civilizational collapse, on the grounds that extinction destroys the entire future and therefore dominates any outcome that merely makes the future go badly; the other holds that the risks of astronomically large amounts of suffering are comparable in moral urgency, that extinction is only one way of losing the future, and that a future which persists but goes very badly is not obviously a better outcome than no future at all. I have given them these labels myself and I would rather you did not use them if a better neutral phrasing suggests itself; what matters is that each camp's position be stated in words that camp would actually sign, rather than in a version its opponent finds easier to refute.

There is one argument of my own that I want placed on the record in the statement, because it bore on a crux and neither camp answered it. It is just my old trade-off in a new setting. Where minds become cheap to make — digital minds on abundant substrate, if that is where this goes — the constraint that kept welfare-per-mind high in biological evolution largely disappears: a lineage, or a firm, or a process under anything resembling selection, can fill the available space with the least expensive viable mind rather than with fewer better-off ones, and the quantity-favouring strategy wins locally even if everyone ends up worse off. I take the one large-scale mind-producing technology we have already built to be a weak but real data point: industrial agriculture produced enormous numbers of vertebrate lives at a welfare level far below anything the same species would have had otherwise. There is a second, separate point underneath it. The biological range of suffering intensities is narrow because brains and bodies are expensive; engineered minds need not inherit that ceiling, so the worst available states may lie outside the range our intuitions were calibrated on. Please treat both points as input to whatever crux they bear on, and record what each camp predicts about them.

What I would like from you:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. I want these to be substantive enough that someone could deny them — not merely that both sides want good outcomes and are uncertain about the future. Include, where you can, what both accept about the size and shape of the long run, about what we can currently predict, about which near-term interventions both camps already favour, and about the limits of our evidence.

2. A table of the residual disagreements, with columns for: the crux; the extinction-first camp's formulation of its own view; the suffering-first camp's formulation of its own view; whether the crux is empirical, axiological, or merely verbal; the concrete case, scenario, or piece of evidence both camps would accept as bearing on it; and, written down in advance, what each camp predicts that evidence will show. I expect some of the cruxes to be about how to weigh contested views about value under moral uncertainty — how much of each side's case survives if you do not grant its axiology — and some to be straightforwardly empirical, about the distribution of welfare in technologically mature futures. If a disagreement turns out to be about words, say so and say which words.

3. A short closing section, in both camps' names, recording what each would count as having been wrong about: not a statement of who won, but the observation or argument each side would treat as reason to change its own priority ordering.

Please keep the whole thing under about a thousand words if you can, and write it as something two people who disagree could put their names to. We are not able to chase references in the group, so no citations, bibliographies, or reading lists — I want the argument stated in our own terms, with the reasoning visible rather than deferred to authorities.
