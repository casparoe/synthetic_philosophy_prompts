I got into about a two-hour argument with my roommate last night about Nozick's entitlement theory and I've been turning it over ever since. You know the setup: start with whatever distribution of stuff you think is just, a million people each voluntarily hand over a quarter to watch Wilt Chamberlain play, he ends up with a quarter of a million dollars, and the pattern you liked is gone without anybody forcing anybody to do anything. My roommate thinks this flatly demolishes every patterned principle and that any tax-funded transfer is on a par with forced labour. I don't go that far, but I also can't say where exactly I get off the train, so this morning I sat down and wrote out my gut verdicts on a pile of cases, gut only, no theory. Taxing Chamberlain's gate reciepts to hand cash to the worst-off: not ok. Taxing everybody, including people who never set foot in it, to pay for a public park: fine. Taxing everybody to pay for the opera: not fine, and yes I can see those last two have exactly the same shape, and every time I try to defend the difference I end up mumbling about public goods, which my roommate says is just utilitarianism sneaking in the back door. Somebody buys a vacant lot in a cheap neighbourhood, does literally nothing to it, and sells it thirty years later for forty times what he paid because a train station went in two streets over: fine, no complaint. A homesteader fences off a field back when there were twelve people around and land was everywhere: fine. Somebody fences off the last water hole in a desert: not fine, and I think he owes something to the people who now have to walk further. A company dumps waste in a river nobody owns and slightly sickens a thousand people downstream: obviously not fine, but I want to say it without saying the company took anything from anyone's holdings, and I can't work out what my view is even about there. A guy lies to an old widow about who he is and what a painting is worth and buys it for a tenth of its value, no threats involved: she has a complaint and should get it back. Downloading a song: not theft, don't care. But a company that spent a billion dollars developing a drug getting to stop anyone else from manufacturing it and charging way above production cost for years: fine, and I know that's in tension with the song one and I can't say why. A fortune whose whole chain of transfers for a hundred years has been perfectly voluntary, except that the original pile came from a factory owner who broke a strike with private violence: I have a strong feeling something is owed here, and no idea to whom, since everyone involved is dead. And the one I genuinely go back and forth on: inheritance. I think people should get to leave their stuff to whoever they want, and I also think a society where a bunch of people never work because their ancestors made money in something rotten is worse off, and I can't tell which of those two thoughts is about justice in holdings and which is just me disliking inequality.

My roommate's candidate principle, which he calls the chain-plus-baseline view, is that a holding is legitimate iff it comes from a chain of transfers each of which was voluntary and adequately informed, and every original acquisition left everyone else's options no worse than they would have been if the resource had stayed unowned. I want you to do three things with all this. First, say whether that principle delivers my verdicts, and where it doesn't, repair it — I'd rather have one principle with a clause I can point to a case for than a list of exceptions. Second, tell me what it says about three cases I haven't listed: a town where a single company gradually buys up every piece of land, always by voluntary sale at a price the seller was happy to take, and ends up as the only employer and therefore the only real option the residents have; a tax only on the unimproved value of land, with the money handed out equally to everyone; and a case where the transaction is voluntary and informed but the background that makes it the best available option was itself produced by earlier transactions that were all individually fine. Third, be honest about which of my verdicts have to go: if the clean version of the principle costs me, say, the opera-versus-park distinction or the drug patent, tell me that plainly and tell me whether you think that's a reason to give up the verdict or a reason to give up the principle. No citations, no reading list — just the argument.
