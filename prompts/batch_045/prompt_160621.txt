Write a deadpan five-year Impact Report of about 700–800 words, issued by a fictional consultancy, Circle Metrics Ltd., that audits its clients' moral progress under the expanding-circle picture of moral progress: the view that moral progress consists in extending moral concern outward to beings previously excluded, and that the extent of the circle is therefore the natural measure of it. Format it as an executive summary, three short sections, and a footnoted methodology appendix, in the exact register of a corporate sustainability report.

The comedy has to come from taking the metric seriously. Circle Metrics' headline figure, the Circle Radius — the number of beings whose interests the client currently weights — has risen every year, and every section must treat this rise as unambiguous improvement, while the report's own tables quietly show that things have worsened on any view that aggregates welfare, because each being newly brought inside the circle contributes its suffering to the ledger as well as its standing. Give the firm the following apparatus: "inclusion debt," the retrospective wrongness a client acquires when a being is admitted after decades of being used; "provisional exoneration certificates," which cancel that debt for conduct that was unavoidable at the time; and "circle arbitrage," improving the score by extending concern to beings whose welfare the client cannot affect, or by weighting distant beings so thinly that including them costs nothing. The appendix should disclose that most of this year's gain came from a reclassification of insects.

The reductio a reader should feel without being told: if the only thing that improves is who is counted, the cheapest way to make moral progress is to widen the circle and change nothing, and the second cheapest is to stop widening it, since every widening now creates the inclusion debts that later reports will score against you. Throughout, the firm must never state the objection; each absurdity should appear as a technical problem with a technical fix. Do not name or caricature any real person, living or dead, and do not cite real works — the target is a view and a style of measuring it, not its authors.
