I am putting together a seminar unit on explanation in historiography, on the question whether narrative form in a historical explanation is a vehicle of hindsight bias or a remedy for it. Every example I have tried to build collapses into the same familiar shape: the account with the better story is also the account that makes the outcome look like what was always going to happen, and the account that is careful about what the agents could have known reads as a flat list of factors with the life drained out of it. So I want a case engineered to break that alignment, and I want it built to a specification rather than asserted.

Use this vocabulary, which I am introducing in order to keep three things apart that usually travel together:

- **Retrospective inevitability** — the outcome is presented as the thing toward which the episode was heading.
- **Ex-ante intelligibility** — the agents' choices are exhibited as what a competent person in their position, with their information, had reason to do.
- **Narrative closure** — the episode has the shape of a story: a situation, a complication, a resolution.

The target is a specific mechanism for the pessimistic view, which I will call **the Inverse-Transparency Thesis**: the more an account makes the agents' reasoning visible, the more hindsight-biased it becomes, because a reader who is shown agents deliberating reconstructs their reasons using knowledge of how things actually turned out, and so reads the outcome back into the deliberation. The alternative, which I will call the **Intelligibility View**, holds that exhibiting the agents' reasons is precisely what blocks hindsight bias, because it forces the account to stay with what was knowable at the time; closure and surprise are separable.

Build me a case in which the Inverse-Transparency Thesis fails.

The design constraints:

1. One invented episode set inside an organization — a hospital trust, a utility, a mid-sized manufacturer, a city agency. Not a battlefield, not a financial market, and nothing drawn from real history; the case has to be self-contained, since I want it usable without any lookup.
2. The outcome must be genuinely unresolved in advance: a well-informed insider at the time would have put it somewhere near even odds. State that figure.
3. Write two accounts of the same episode. Account N should be the one a good editor would call the better read; Account S should be the careful, structural one. Each under 150 words.
4. The two accounts must cite the same causal factors — the same five, in whatever order and weighting you like. They may differ in ordering, grain, weighting, and in how the agents are represented, but not in what they say the causes were.
5. Account S may not be made hindsight-biased by crude means: no "obviously", no "inevitably", no "as anyone should have foreseen", no knowing narrator. Whatever bias it contains has to be carried by structure.
6. Account N must be high in narrative closure and high in ex-ante intelligibility, and low in retrospective inevitability; Account S must be low in narrative closure and comparatively high in retrospective inevitability.

Then show that the design works:

(a) Run two checks. The **blind-reader check**: say what a reader given only the pre-outcome portion of each account would predict, and why. The **substitution check**: hold the setup fixed, flip the outcome, and report, for each account, roughly what proportion of the text would have to be rewritten and what would have to change about the causal story.
(b) State what the Inverse-Transparency Thesis predicts about your two accounts, state what the Intelligibility View predicts, and show from the case which prediction is borne out.
(c) Name the single feature doing the work, and say what happens to each account if you remove that feature — in particular, whether Account N collapses back into the usual alignment.
(d) Give me the strongest attack available to someone who holds the Inverse-Transparency Thesis: the best reason to think your case does not really refute it.
(e) Add a short note on where your own construction is most likely to be cheating — for instance by smuggling inevitability in through aspect, grain, or word choice — and say what a better-designed case would have to look like in order to reverse your verdict.

Finish with exactly one last line, in this form: \answer{separation holds: <the single feature doing the work, five words or fewer>}; or, if your honest verdict is that the two cannot be pulled apart, the same with "separation fails".
