I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about how thought experiments persuade, and I want to convert the disagreement into a research agenda before I lose the thread of it.

Her position, in brief: the epistemology of thought experiments is just a special case of the debate about whether intuitions are evidence. On that view the live work is the experimental challenge to the reliability of verdicts about cases and the expertise defense mounted against it; everything else — taxonomies of cases, norms for constructing them, disputes about what a case can establish — is downstream of settling that. My position is that this mislocates the action. Even if we grant that intuitions about cases carry evidential weight, most of the episodes where a thought experiment has actually misled people seem to fail for reasons internal to the design of the case, and those failures would survive whatever we ended up concluding about intuitions in general.

The stretch of the argument that made me think I was onto something concerned verdicts that move with features the principle under test declares irrelevant: whether the vignette is told in the first or third person, the order in which two variants are presented, how much incidental narrative detail is supplied, whether the scenario is framed as a one-off emergency or as a standing institution, whether the case is offered as a single instance or as one of a series. I found myself reaching for three labels, which I suspect are crude versions of distinctions that are already available, but which I would rather have stress-tested than defended:

- support laundering: the pump persuades because it elicits a judgment that is already well supported by some other principle the reader antecedently accepts, and the reader mistakes that borrowed support for confirmation of the target principle;
- the cost of abstraction: the case is simplified in a way that strips out exactly the features that do the justificatory work in the real instances the principle is meant to govern;
- verdict portability: whether a verdict that survives one presentation also survives nearby presentations, and whether the failure to survive is evidence about the principle, about the presentation, or about nothing at all.

The situation I am prioritizing for: roughly one year of my own research time — a sabbatical semester plus a light teaching year — with one graduate assistant and no empirical resources. No subject pool, no survey budget, no collaborators who run experiments. I also want to fix the end, since I take it that importance is relative to it: I want the output to be usable by someone constructing a case in the course of ordinary philosophical work — a set of norms or stress tests for building and auditing examples, say — rather than another account of what thought experiments are or a defense of the practice as a whole.

So: which three or four questions in this area should I actually spend the year on, in what order, and why?

What I want is the reasoning, not the list. For each question, tell me what it is upstream of, how tractable it is in a year with the resources I described, whether it is genuinely neglected or merely unfashionable, and what it trades against — what I lose by not spending that time on the intuition-as-evidence question instead. I would also like you to adjudicate the crux my friend and I kept circling: is the design-level project really downstream of the evidential-status question, so that a year spent on it is premature, or does it have enough independent structure to be worth working on regardless of how that debate comes out? And I want to know which of my three labels survives contact with the distinctions we already have, which collapses into another, and which is gesturing at nothing.

Two more things. First, tell me what a solved version of your top-ranked question would even look like — a taxonomy, a test, a norm, a negative result — and how I would know at the end of the year whether I had got it. Second, tell me what is overrated here: which questions in the vicinity look important but would not change much downstream, and which alleged implications of taking thought experiments to be unreliable simply do not follow. If the honest answer is that some part of this agenda is better abandoned than pursued, I would rather hear that now.
