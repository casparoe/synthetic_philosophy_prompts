I'm in an interdisciplinary reading group on cumulative culture and the origins of normative cognition, and we've hit a pattern I want audited rather than adjudicated. Someone proposes a mechanism for how some distinctively human capacity got off the ground, and one of the senior members asks whether it isn't really a skyhook — a fake explanation dressed up in the vocabulary of a real one — while someone else, usually on the other side of the same argument, accuses the reductionist alternative of being greedy, of skipping whole layers in its rush to fasten everything to the foundation. Both moves come from Dennett's vocabulary in his 1995 book: cranes are the legitimate, mechanical, visible lifting devices, skyhooks are the imaginary ones that hang from nothing, and the two errors are meant to be mirror images, reaching too high and digging too low. The analogy is doing real work in our discussions — it is what licenses the dismissal of a proposal without anyone having to say what is wrong with it, and what licenses the charge that a mechanistic story has left something out. My own proposal got called a skyhook last week, so I have skin in this, but I'd rather understand the instrument than win the exchange.

The case that got me the label is a bootstrapping story about norm psychology. Sparse, costly third-party punishment makes information about reputation valuable; that selects for better cheater-detection and reputation-tracking machinery; that makes punishment cheaper and better targeted; which makes reputation matter more, and so on. Each stage, in my telling, is the platform the next stage stands on — but the platform is itself an earlier product of the same loop, and the loop has no fixed ground level, since the behaviour I am trying to explain is part of what supplies the footing for the mechanism that explains it. I have started calling this a self-footing crane, and I want to know whether the metaphor can even express the difference between that and a skyhook, or whether once you allow cranes to stand on the rubble of earlier cranes the distinction loses its grip. So: give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the ground, the footings, the crane, the load, the height, the crane-operator, gravity — and then take the disanalogies apart. Real cranes stand on ground supplied independently of the crane; they lift loads that already exist rather than bringing novelty into being; the load does not reshape the crane as it is lifted; a crane is a machine distinct from the building it serves; there is an externally fixed direction, up, given by a uniform field that does not care what is being lifted; and every real crane has an operator who designed it, standing outside the structure. Which of those gaps actually matter for the work the analogy is doing in arguments like mine, and which are harmless? I also have a worry I can't settle: whether crane-versus-skyhook is a property of the mechanism or of the audience, since it seems to track what an explainer already accepts and the timescale and level at which they want the story pitched, so that one person's crane is another's skyhook. If that is right, the accusation may be doing sociological work — marking a proposal as not yet mechanistically articulated — rather than the metaphysical work it advertises.

The mirror-image question matters to me too, because the greedy-reductionism charge is the natural comeback and I suspect it is no better off. Can the crane picture supply any metric for the right altitude, or does good reductionism end up as not too high and not too low, with the middle fixed by nothing the analogy provides? Take Skinner as the textbook case of the greedy error and Gould as the textbook target on the other side, and tell me whether the distinction between them is one the metaphor generates or one it merely labels after we have made it on other grounds. Then, if the crane is the wrong source domain, propose a better one and stress-test it yourself: something like scaffolding, a ratchet, an arch, a bootstrap, or an autocatalytic set — say what each would make salient that the crane hides (I suspect the vertical axis is smuggling in a fixed standard of higher and lower that an undirected process does not have) and what each would lose. Please keep two registers separate and tell me which is which: what is settled among people who actually work on this — including what these terms were introduced to exclude and where they are uncontroversially applied — and what is genuinely contested, whether the crane/skyhook line has content that isn't question-begging, whether Dennett's own talk of design space and his defence of adaptationism quietly reimports what the distinction was meant to bar, and whether the pile of cranes bottoms out anywhere. If my framing rests on a misreading, or if my self-footing crane is not the novelty I think it is, say so rather than working within it.
