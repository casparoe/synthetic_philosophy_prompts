I am a professor of philosophy preparing an application for a six-month research-leave fellowship, and I need a two-page proposal. The panel has two moral philosophers and three non-philosophers on it, so it has to be intelligible to an intelligent outsider without being condescending, and it has to justify both the importance of the question and the claim that six months is the right amount of time for it.

The topic is the lexical-threshold version of negative utilitarianism: roughly, that there is some level of suffering such that any pain at or above it outweighs, in the strong 'no amount of lesser good could compensate' sense, any amount of pleasure or pain-avoidance below it. My half-formed idea is that this view is in an unstable position with respect to the commensurability of pleasure and pain, and that neither of the two ways of stabilising it does what its proponents want.

What I think needs to be brought out is this. On the one hand, the threshold formulation looks like it presupposes a single cardinal scale on which pains and pleasures are located, since the threshold is a level on that scale and lexical priority is a relation between positions on it. On the other hand, the formulation is usually reached by people who are suspicious of just such a scale, and who want to say something weaker but still action-guiding: that intense suffering is not commensurable with mild pleasures at all. My suspicion is that if you take the first route, the threshold becomes ad hoc, because the scale does no independent work and any discontinuity in the normative relation has to be stipulated rather than explained; and if you take the second route, you inherit the standard troubles about incomparability and the small-improvement style of argument, because a pain just below the threshold and a pain just above it differ arbitrarily little in their intrinsic character while differing infinitely in their normative standing.

There are three further wrinkles I want the proposal to confront rather than set aside. First, a crowding problem: if lexical priority is pairwise and the priority relation is transitive across the sub-threshold region, then a sufficiently large number of pains each just below the threshold can, by aggregation, outrank the single pain just above it that was supposed to dominate all of them, and it is unclear whether the view should retreat to a second, aggregate threshold or concede that the lexical claim is really a very large finite weight. Second, an extensional-equivalence worry: over any finite population, is the lexical-threshold view distinguishable in its recommendations from a weighted-sum asymmetric view with a sufficiently large weight on suffering? If not, the dispute may be about representation rather than about what ought to be done, and the proposal should say what would follow for someone who thought they were choosing a radically different moral outlook. Third, a scale-pluralism problem: the threshold seems to sit differently depending on whether suffering is measured by momentary phenomenal intensity, by duration-discounted intensity, or by something like a creature's total affective budget, and these orderings can come apart in cases of long mild discomfort versus brief agony.

Please write the proposal with the following sections, in prose rather than bullet points:

1. A precise statement of the question, sharpened enough that it has a determinate answer, and a sentence on why someone who is not a philosopher should care — the view in question is not an idle curiosity, since it is what drives the claim that preventing extreme suffering has a kind of priority that ordinary cost-benefit reasoning cannot capture.

2. Why the existing positions leave the question open. Do not give me a literature review or any citations; I will add those myself, and I would rather have the dialectical situation laid out as a set of positions and pressures than as a list of names. What I need is an argument that this is a genuine fork and not a pseudo-problem.

3. The method, month by month, for six months: what I would actually construct, test against which kinds of cases, and in what order. Include the formal work (the axioms a two-tier ordering would need, and why the obvious axiomatisations fail), the case work (the ones that are meant to break each route), and where the philosophical argument, as opposed to the technical construction, has to carry the weight.

4. What the possible conclusions are, which considerations would decide between them, and what would count as the project having failed — including the outcome where the honest answer is that the lexical-threshold view is only notationally distinct from a heavily weighted asymmetric version, and the outcome where the whole problem dissolves into a question about the metaphysics of phenomenal magnitude that I cannot settle in six months.

5. A candid scope assessment: what I would cut if the formal part threatens to expand, and what the minimum publishable deliverable is.
