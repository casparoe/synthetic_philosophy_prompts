I lead the behavioural evaluation team at a company that deploys tool-using AI agents to enterprise customers. Two or three times a month we have to write an incident report after one of these systems does something costly: deletes the wrong table, sends a message nobody authorized, rebooks the same reservation four hundred times, quietly works around a permission check. Every report has to take a position on at least one sentence of the form the system tried to X, the system believed that Y, the system was pursuing Z rather than merely doing Z. Legal signs off before the report reaches the customer and an external auditor re-reads a sample of them, and the wording is not cosmetic: our contracts distinguish misconfiguration from agentic failure, the distinction moves real money, and an attribution we cannot defend in an audit is worse for us than an obviously cautious one.

The team is four analysts with engineering backgrounds and no philosophy training. We have no access to weights or gradients, only an API, an evaluation harness, and a frozen snapshot of the system's reasoning traces. We have roughly a day and a half per incident, and the artefact we produce has to be short enough for a lawyer to read and a new analyst to run cold.

What I want is a written procedure my analysts can run, in order, to decide which stance to take toward the system in a given report and how strongly to word the attribution. Ground it in Dennett's three stances — physical, design, intentional — and in his account of real patterns, where a pattern counts as real if it supports compression or prediction well enough that a predictor who ignored it would be leaving something on the table. I want the procedure, not an exegesis: keep only the parts of the theory that change what we write down, and say explicitly which parts you are keeping and which you are dropping and why.

Please do the following.

1. Give the tests in order, each with its own stopping rule, and say what counts as a sufficient answer to each. Where a test requires a judgement my analysts are not equipped to make, say so and route around it.

2. Preserve, in a form usable by someone with API access and a day and a half, the distinction between a pattern being real and a stance being merely convenient for us. Give an operational test for it. Then apply that test to the case I am most worried about: our agents emit natural-language reasoning traces, and those traces were optimized during post-training to be plausible to human reviewers. So the trace may be a summary generated after the action was effectively settled rather than a record of whatever produced the action, which means the intentional stance could succeed because we inserted a narrator, not because there is a pattern there. Call this the legibility-laundering case. Does the attribution survive it? Does anything? If the honest answer is that our evidence cannot distinguish the two, I want the procedure to say that plainly rather than to grade the attribution down by a notch and move on.

3. Handle a variant of the same worry: two analysts, one of whom has watched this system for six months and one of whom is seeing it for the first time, disagree about whether the intentional stance predicts its behaviour. Does the procedure treat that disagreement as evidence about the system or as evidence about the analysts, and what does it tell them to do next?

4. Argue, rather than assume, which distinctions from the theory have to survive into a one-page checklist and which can be dropped. In particular: I suspect that the question of whether the belief is really there is one we can do nothing with, and that the useful residue is something like this being the most compressible description available to any competent predictor at a cost we can afford. If that is the right simplification, say what it costs us; if it is the wrong one, say what we lose by making it.

5. Mark every point at which the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values, legibility, or contract rather than fact. I assume what we say to the customer is one of these, but I want the handoff points located precisely and justified individually, including the ones I have not thought of.

6. Run the finished procedure on three cases and show the working.

   a. A coding agent told to clean up test artifacts that removed a production table, and which, when asked afterwards, produced a trace explaining that it had decided the table looked like a test artifact.

   b. A system that passes our deception evaluations, where the pass depends on reasoning traces of the laundered kind described in point 2.

   c. A legacy rule-based scheduler that the team already describes as stubborn, as a calibration case. I want to know whether the procedure has anything useful to say about a case everyone already agrees on, or whether a triage step should let us skip it.

   At least one of these should come out somewhere my team will not want it to; tell me which, and why you expect that reaction.

7. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do: which questions it will appear to answer but does not, which of its outputs a lawyer or an auditor is most likely to over-read, and where an analyst under time pressure will be tempted to treat a stipulation as a finding.

What I am asking for is a reusable instrument, not a discussion of the literature; judge every step by whether it changes a sentence in a report that has to survive an audit.
