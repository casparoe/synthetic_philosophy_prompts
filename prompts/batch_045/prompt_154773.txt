I host a tiny podcast on philosophy of computation — maybe 300 downloads an episode, mostly grad students — and next week's guest is a philosopher defending what she calls resource-relative Bayesianism. Short version: she thinks ought-implies-can does real work in epistemology, so probabilism and conditionalization are only norms in their feasible form. An agent ought to have credences that come as close to the Bayesian ideal as the best procedure it could actually have run, given its actual computational budget, would have gotten it. And she insists this definately isn't subjectivism, because what's feasible is a hard fact rather than an opinion; her stock line is that "the best you can do with a fixed number of flops is a fact about the world, not a matter of taste."

Draft me eight to ten questions for a 45 minute episode. I dont want anything generic and I don't want her just restating the view. I want questions that push on where I think it actually hurts, each with a follow-up keyed to what she'll probably say in reply. No need to name papers or people in the questions themselves, she'll fill those in on air.

Where I think it hurts, roughly:

1. The arithmetic disanalogy. The standard reply to her is that the probability axioms constrain states of an agent, and intractability is a fact about our access to those norms, not about what they require — someone who can't do long division is still wrong about the product. She'll say arithmetic has a determinate answer the agent is failing to track, whereas the credal case is different somehow. Give me the question that forces her to say exactly what the difference is, and the follow-up.

2. Approximation. If rationality is approximate coherence, what sets the tolerance, and why doesn't the usual vindication (Dutch book, accuracy dominance) survive the move to near-coherent agents? Assume she answers that the accuracy framework can be redone with bounds instead of exact dominance. Follow up on that. Also worth asking: is the resource-limited agent justified, or merely excused?

3. Which notion of "feasible" — worst-case asymptotics, average-case under a distribution over problems, or wall-clock on her actual hardware? If it's average-case, or worst-case relativized to "the problems an agent like this actually faces," the objectivity she wants seems to leak: the best procedure is only best relative to a problem distribution. Assume she bites that bullet, and give me a follow-up on whether the norm has quietly become prudence.

4. The regress. Deciding how much computation to spend is itself a computation, and presumably an intractable one, so "do the best you feasibly can" may have no feasible specification. I especially want to press on whether bounded optimality (roughly: the best program you could run on your actual hardware, given your goals) collapses into descriptivism — if the architecture includes the procedure you actually ran, then whatever you did was feasible, so it was permitted. She'll probably say the collapse doesn't follow because the counterfactual programs are well defined. Give me the follow-up to that.

5. Evidence about your own heuristics. If she knows her fast-and-frugal heuristic is reliable in this environment, is she required to treat "my procedure output p" as evidence and respond to it? Doesn't the full Bayesian machinery then come back in through the back door, as a norm on how to respond to that evidence?

6. Hardware discontinuity. Buy a slower machine and the standard you're held to gets looser; buy a faster one and you become irrational without your credences changing at all. Reductio, or does she bite it? If she bites it, follow up on disagreement: two agents with the same evidence and different budgets can both be rational while disagreeing forever. What does the view say they should do?

7. What would count against it. My idea: if the Bayesian-brain people are roughly right that perception is near-exact inference over a rich generative model, does her claim that the ideal is unachievable in the cases that matter lose its bite — and does the view get less interesting the better our cognitive machinery gets? Assume she replies that brains doing something Bayesian-ish isn't the same as satisfying the norms.

Order the questions so the episode builds instead of peaking at minute ten, tell me which one to drop if we run long, and flag any place where you think I've got the pressure point wrong.
