I have to give the address at the dedication of a second commemorative tablet in Founder's Park, in the town of Marlowe Falls, northern New England, on the afternoon of the 26th. I need you to write it: seven to nine minutes, roughly a thousand words, speakable aloud by a man of sixty-two standing in a park in a coat, and I need the philosophical problem at the centre of it to do structural work rather than to be gestured at in a graceful sentence.

The first tablet is granite, put up in 1931 by subscription from the mill hands and their families. Its last line reads: AND ON THE NIGHT OF THE ICE FLOOD NOBODY WAS TURNED AWAY. That sentence is the town's founding story, and it is also the charter of the Marlowe Falls Relief Association, which has ever since paid for doctoring and burying for any resident in need, without inquiry, out of the proceeds of an annual supper at which the lamps in the old Odd Fellows' hall are lit and left burning. I am the Association's chairman. My grandmother, who raised me, told me the story every year of my childhood and meant it as an instruction about what kind of place this was.

Two years ago a state records project digitised the lodge's minute book, the mills' ticket stubs, the ferry log and the township's relief vouchers, and I was the volunteer who found what they contain. On 14 March 1907 the hall committee had in fact voted, by a majority, to open the hall to all residents. The mill agent, who was the lodge treasurer and owned the ferry, required admission by mill ticket and stopped the ferry at nine that night; within a fortnight the township paid to move forty-one people from the Flats — the shantytown across the river: seasonal hands, quarry families, recent arrivals — out of the township altogether. A dissenting committeeman, the hall janitor, wrote the overruled motion into the margin of the minute book. My great-grandfather was that janitor, and the margin note is why any of this is known. So the record does not simply refute the memory. It contains a defeated intention that the memory appears to have preserved, and preserved as a description of the night rather than of the vote.

The park will hold about two hundred people: the old families who have told the story; four descendants of Flats families who have come up from Hartford and Providence at the town's invitation and who are already being looked at; a state archivist and a town historian who both believe the correction should be stated in one plain sentence and that I will flinch; a selectboard that paid for the second tablet and would like a quotable line about reckoning with the past; two reporters; and the Association's own members, whose authority to pay out is the sentence on the old stone, and who have to vote on the winter's disbursements in December.

What the speech has to survive: it must not call my grandmother a liar, or be audible as doing so; it must not let the room congratulate itself for facing its past, since the second tablet was bought by people who would like to have done something by buying it; it must not use the four visitors as evidence, or as an occasion for a gesture in their direction, since they are in the audience rather than in the argument; it must not let ninety years of payouts redeem the night, or treat a false founding story as a lucky accident; it must not leave the Association with nothing to charter itself on, because it has to pay out in December on the authority of whatever I say that afternoon; and it must not reconcile the two tablets into saying the same thing, because they do not.

Here is the knot I want the address to work out rather than name. A sentence like the one on the 1931 stone seems to be doing two things at once — reporting what the people here did, and registering what they have undertaken to owe one another — and the ordinary way of adjudicating a conflict between a community's memory and the record treats it as doing only the first, so that the record wins the moment the report fails. I want the speech to work out what it would be for a memory to survive the falsification of its report by being converted rather than cancelled: inherited as a commitment instead of as a history, on the condition that a community inheriting it that way must be able to say, without self-deception, which of the two things it now has. If that conversion is only a euphemism for keeping the flattering version while appearing to have given it up, say so plainly in a short paragraph after the speech and give me the alternative instead — but do not hand me a text that has quietly picked one of the two tablets and called it the truth.

Two further requirements. The archivist has asked me to say, in so many words, that the first tablet is false; I do not think I can say it that way, and I do not think that refusing makes me a relativist. Work out what should be said in its place and put it in the mouth of the speech, not in a note to me. And the last minute should give the Association something specific to do with its own charter before the supper, which is in three weeks — the lamps are lit either way, and the speech has to say on what understanding.
