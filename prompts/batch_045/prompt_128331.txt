I serve on a university committee that reviews proposals for research involving human tissue. Last month the committee was enlarged from seven to nineteen members and we moved from a consensus requirement to a simple majority, and the member who pushed both changes defended them with an analogy: majority voting, he said, is just repeated measurement of the same quantity — each voter is one noisy instrument reading of the true answer, the noise is independent, the errors cancel, so a committee of nineteen stands to a committee of seven as nineteen measurements stand to seven. His conclusion was that we should always want more members and need not trouble ourselves much over how they are chosen.

I am not persuaded, but I cannot yet say precisely where the picture fails, and I owe the committee a short written response before our next meeting. Please audit the analogy for me. Give the correspondence table — what plays the role of the instrument, the true value, the error, the repetitions, the calibration — and then the disanalogies, sorted by whether they actually matter for his conclusion. Two worry me most. First, a vote is a one-bit summary of a judgment, whereas a measurement reports a magnitude: does that quantization cost anything the analogy needs? Second, our members all read the same dossier and hear the same discussion, so their errors are not independent in the way coin flips are — yet I have heard it claimed that sharing evidence is harmless provided the residual errors are independent given the truth, and I want to know which of those is right and why. I would also like the distinction, if there is one, between a large committee's being very likely to be right and any particular addition's making it more likely to be right, and between adding a member who is barely better than chance and adding an expert. Nearly every decision we take is yes-or-no, so set aside the multi-option case unless the analogy quietly depends on it.

Close by telling me what survives: does his practical conclusion hold, does it hold only under conditions we could actually impose on ourselves, and what, if anything, does the analogy still tell us about how the vote ought to be run? Please answer in American English.
