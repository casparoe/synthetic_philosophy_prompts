I write literary fiction, and the manuscript I have been carrying around for three summers is set mostly on a rented terrace above a harbour town where the narrator, Eduarda, a retired philosopher whose whole career was spent on the theory of pleasure, spends the season administering a questionnaire to two former students — Marta, a conservator who takes sensuous delight in solvents and varnish, and Kilian, a composer who has mostly stopped enjoying anything and has theories about why. The questionnaire is the spine of the book: each chapter ends with one of its questions, and I want it to be a genuine instrument rather than decoration, the sort of thing a reader could actually take and be sorted by. What I need from you is the instrument plus the map of the logical space behind it. The subject is what is usually called the heterogeneity problem: the pleasures — of a proof closing, of cold water, of scratching an itch, of revenge — do not obviously feel alike in any respect, and the question is what, if anything, they share in virtue of which they are all pleasures, and what becomes of hedonism if the answer is nothing much.

Please build it as a decision tree or taxonomy of twelve-question size whose branch nodes are claims and concrete cases, not labels, so that a reader is sorted by what she says about cases and never asked whether she is a realist about hedonic tone or anything of that sort. I want the tree generated from the logical space first and only then populated with named positions. The axes I have in mind, which you should use or improve on, are: what the shared feature is supposed to be, if there is one (a single phenomenal quality or felt tone; something attitudinal, such as the experience being one the subject welcomes or wants to continue; something functional or dispositional, a matter of what the state does to motivation, choice and learning; a merely disjunctive, clustered or family-resemblance unity; or nothing at all); where the pleasantness is supposed to sit — a separable ingredient present alongside the phenomenology of the activity, an adverbial or intentional mode in which the activity is experienced, a relation between the experience and the subject's concerns, a functional role, or nothing over and above the experience itself; and what the hedonist is to do if the unity fails — shrink the base class to one species of pleasure, pluralise value across kinds, treat pleasantness as a simple unanalysable property known only by acquaintance, deflate the notion into something like subjective well-being, or go error-theoretic about it. Each leaf should carry a name where a real one exists and "unnamed" where it does not, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Every combination the axes allow but that nobody occupies should be listed separately with a diagnosis: is the cell empty because the view is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored?

Six cases must appear in the questions, and you may add six more of your own to reach twelve; please tell me frankly at the end if one of my six turns out to do no discriminating work, and which. They are: 'the proof and the plum' — Marta reports that finishing an argument and eating a cold plum feel nothing alike, yet she uses exactly the same words about both and would give up neither; 'Kilian's stroke' — after a small lesion Kilian still seeks out coffee and his own recordings and calls them good, but reports that the shine has gone out of them, and insists he is not merely misremembering; 'the cinnamon room' — a room in the novel, built by a minor character who designs atmospheres, in which the air is at once delicious and faintly sickening in a way that cannot be split into two experiences, and about which subjects cannot say whether the whole thing is pleasant or unpleasant; 'the borrowed afternoon' — Eduarda is made happy by her grandson's happiness across a courtyard with no change she can locate in her own sensory field, and is certain her afternoon went better for it; 'the erased swim' — a character swims in the harbour and enjoys it, then takes a fictional amnesic treatment that leaves no trace of the hour, and the question is what, if anything, the swim did for him; and 'the discredited wine' — Kilian takes exquisite pleasure in a cheap wine and, tasting a better one an hour later, retroactively withdraws his endorsement while insisting the original pleasure was real. Finally, the scoring key should place Marta, Kilian and Eduarda in cells for me — I need to know whether any of my three characters has drifted into an unoccupied cell, since if so I have to rewrite her or him — and it should flag any pair of leaves the questionnaire cannot separate, saying whether the failure is remediable with an extra question or whether the two views are genuinely indistinguishable across all cases. Please skip the scholarly apparatus entirely: no bibliography, no citations, no attributions beyond a name for a leaf where one is genuinely standard, and no history of the dispute.
