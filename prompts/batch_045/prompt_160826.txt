I'm on the technical steering committee of a small open-source foundation — I'm a backend dev by day, no philosophy background at all. A friend who did some philosophy said the thing we've run into apparently has a name, something like the discursive dilemma, and told me to look it up; I haven't, and I'd rather you didn't lean on any literature I can't follow. Plain terms, no citations, no reading lists. I have to circulate a memo before Friday's meeting and I want it to actually settle something.

The setup: our charter says a spec version can be recommended by the council "only if" (1) the reference implementation passes the conformance suite and (2) there are at least two independent, interoperable implementations. Everyone agrees that wording makes A and B necessary but not sufficient — there's still a discretionary step, so passing both doesn't automatically mean we publish.

We're nine people. Last week the chair ran a straw poll on all three questions separately and it came out weird. Call them:

A: the reference implementation passes the conformance suite
B: there are two independent, interoperable implementations
C: we should recommend this version now

Votes, all nine members answered all three:

1: A yes, B yes, C yes
2: A yes, B yes, C yes
3: A yes, B yes, C yes
4: A yes, B no, C no
5: A yes, B no, C no
6: A no, B yes, C no
7: A no, B yes, C no
8: A no, B no, C no
9: A no, B no, C no

So A passes 5–4, B passes 5–4, and C fails 3–6. Nobody contradicted themselves individually — each person's three answers are consistent with "C requires A and B".

The council has split into roughly three camps:

- Camp 1: the real questions are A and B. Both passed, so we publish. Voting on C directly just throws away what a majority of us actually believe about the inputs.
- Camp 2: C is the only thing we actually get to decide; A and B were supposed to be findings that inform it, not the vote itself. The majority said no, so we don't publish, and its irrelevant that it's a different majority.
- Camp 3: this is a procedural accident. The straw poll wasn't a vote, we never fixed an order, and we should re-run it properly next week and talk until people move.

Complications you should know about:

- A isn't really an opinion question: the suite either passes or it doesn't. The suite maintainer is member 6, who voted no on A, and she says the two failing tests are bugs in the harness rather than in the reference implementation. Several people think A shouldn't be voted on at all, just reported.
- B is genuinely contested: there are two implementations, but one comes from a vendor that funded the other's early development, so whether they're "independent" is basically the whole argument.
- Because of the "only if" wording, people can vote no on C for reasons with nothing to do with A or B. At least two members said they'd vote no regardless, because a big downstream implementer has threatened to fork if we publish before their release cycle ends. Our annual conference is in nine days and that's when the chair wants to announce, so "defer for two months" isn't really available. Amending the charter takes a two-thirds vote of the full membership and about six weeks, so that's off the table for this decision too.
- Member 8 pointed out that Camp 1's rule depends on how you split the decision up. If the chair had broken it into different sub-questions — say one about implementations and a seperate one about ecosystem readiness — you'd get a different result, and whoever writes the agenda basically picks the outcome. Relatedly, we voted A, B, C in that order; someone noted that if C had come first and failed we'd never have reached A and B at all.
- Member 3 is the delegate of the vendor mentioned above and is pushing Camp 1 hardest. Some people think that's a reason to distrust Camp 1 outright. I don't know what to think, but the memo should say something about it.

What I want: a memo I can send to the council that (1) recommends a concrete decision on whether to recommend this version, with a timeline that fits the nine-day window, (2) recommends a default rule for how we aggregate votes on linked questions from now on, and (3) gives the strongest version of the argument you're rejecting rather than dismissing it — a memo that just says the other camp is being irrational won't survive the meeting, since every individual member's votes were perfectly consistent. Also say what to do about A being a matter of fact rather than judgment, and whether member 3's conflict should count for anything. Bullet points are fine, aim for something like 700–1000 words, and write it so the non-philosophers on the council can follow it.
