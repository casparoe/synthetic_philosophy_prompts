Hi — I'm a novelist, not an academic, and I've somehow let myself be talked into running a forty-five minute session at a writers' festival in the spring: about thirty other novelists, no slides, me and a flipchart and a room that will have had lunch. It's being billed as Can Your Characters Agree to Disagree?, and the thing I actually have to get across in the middle of it is the common prior assumption in Bayesian epistemology — not the maths, god knows, I've no maths — but the intuition that two perfectly rational people who trust each other and who tell each other everything they know shouldn't be able to end up still disagreeing, the famous result that says they can't, and the assumption underneath doing all the work.

Don't explain it to me, I've read around a bit. Plan how I should teach it.

What I especially want from you is the dependency structure: what has to be in place before the assumption even means anything, and why. My sense is that people will hear prior and file it under starting belief or prejudice, and once it's filed that way the rest of the session is unrecoverable, so I need to know what to put in their heads first so that a prior lands as the thing you'd believe if the only difference between you were the evidence you'd each seen.

The carrying example has to be fiction-shaped, because these are novelists. Mine is from my own draft: two women who've been friends since they were eleven, one of them thinks the other's brother is lying about where he was that night, each of them has private evidence, and they've told each other most of it but not all. There is no moment in time when they shared a prior — they've known each other forty years — so I need the sequence arranged so that the prior feels like a counterfactual rather than a point in time, or the whole thing collapses the second someone in the third row asks when the prior got set. Tell me what that example must contain and what it must not contain, and if it's the wrong one, say so and give me the right one.

Misconceptions I'm betting on, and I want each defused at the right point in the sequence rather than all at the end: prior as prejudice; the assumption being that everyone begins with the same opinions; it's a claim about how people actually reason, therefore obviously false, therefore who cares; if it's true then every disagreement in fiction is irrational and there goes drama, which is the one that will make the room turn on me; rational people with the same evidence must agree; and the clever dodge, somebody will say people just have different values and that settles it, and I want that headed off wherever it does least damage rather than wherever it's most fun to argue with.

I also want to use the no-trade thing — two people sharing a prior shouldn't both be able to expect to win a bet — because writers understand a wager in a scene in a way they won't understand a theorem. Where does that go: is it the thing that carries the session, or the payoff at the end?

What should I openly refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing? And give me one question to put to them at the end that tests whether they actually picked up the assumption rather than the slogan — ideally something they can answer about whatever they're working on at the moment, since that's the only version of the idea they'll retain past the weekend.
