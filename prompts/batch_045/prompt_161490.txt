Me and a friend ended up having a really long argument last night about how AI assistants handle requests they won't carry out, and I told him I'd ask someone who actually does this stuff for a living.

Here's the practice, described concretely so we're arguing about the same thing. You ask a general-purpose assistant for something that could help someone get hurt, help them manipulate another person, or get round a rule that protects people. What most labs now train it to do is: decline the specific request; give a short reason that points at a rule or a policy rather than at a tally of consequences; offer a nearby thing it will do instead — a general explanation, the defensive version of the same topic, a fictionalised version — and do all of that without telling the user off or saying anything about their character. Separately, the behaviour gets scored: refusals are graded as right or wrong, and the two directions of mistake, saying no to something harmless and saying yes to something harmful, get placed on one trade-off curve and tuned against each other, with panels of annotators as the ground truth about which is which. A newer variant trains for partial compliance, where the model does the harmless part of the request and stops at the boundary rather than refusing wholesale.

My mate says the whole package is obviously just expected-harm minimisation with a polite interface bolted on. I said it can't be, because a harm minimiser would sometimes moralise if moralising reduced harm, would be perfectly happy giving consequentialist reasons, and wouldn't care whether its reason was rule-shaped. We went round in circles for about an hour.

So: what does this bundle of practices actually presuppose? I'm after the implicit theory of moral judgement and of reasons. Does declining to participate come apart from blaming the person, and does the practice strictly need that distinction or is the no-telling-off norm just a house style? Is the single trade-off curve coherant with the rule-shaped reasons and with offering a safe alternative to someone you've just declined to help, or is the practice quietly running two incompatible theories at once? Where does it really require a particular theory, and where are several rival theories all able to license the same behaviour? And what would concretely have to change in the design if you swapped in, first, plain expected-harm minimisation, second, a view on which the machine must not make itself an instrument of someone else's wrongful end, and third, a view on which a decent moral agent has to express fitting attitudes and not merely decline?

Please write the answer in British English.
