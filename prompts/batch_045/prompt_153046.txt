I'm a third-year PhD student in economic history, and a classmate and I have been asked to put together a departmental workshop for next spring on causal explanation in the historical social sciences. Our opening session is supposed to be on the Great Divergence, and specifically on whether the question why did sustained industrial growth first take hold in Britain and not in China is a well-posed contrastive question at all, or whether its canonical formulation imports assumptions that fall apart once you look at them closely. At last week's planning meeting I watched two of the other organizers — Owen, who works on early modern European state formation, and Lena, who works on Qing political economy — argue about exactly this for about forty minutes. They asked me afterwards who had the better of it, and I honestly couldn't tell: every time one of them landed a hit, the other had a reply that sounded equally good. Since my classmate and I have to frame the discussion, I'd be grateful for a careful adjudication rather than an opinion.

Here is the exchange as close to how it actually went as I can reconstruct it. (1) Owen opened by saying the question is structurally ill-posed, much closer to asking why this lottery ticket rather than that one won than to a genuine request for an explanation: with that many interacting and partly independent causes, there is no single contrastive answer to give, only a very large pile of local causal claims, and the rather-than-China clause contributes rhetorical weight rather than explanatory content. (2) Lena replied that the lottery analogy is a category mistake: unlike lottery tickets, the two cases differ systematically on measurable variables of the kind historians actually argue about — energy costs, real wages, fiscal capacity, access to long-distance trade — and if we dissolve the question we also give up any ability to say what makes industrialization happen, which is a claim we plainly do make and use elsewhere. (3) Owen said discriminating is exactly the crux, and proposed what he called the fork test: a candidate cause genuinely answers why X rather than Y only if it also forks X off from the other non-cases in the contrast class — that is, only if it is absent in the places that likewise failed to industrialize first. By that test, he said, the usual suspects fail: cheap coal, high wages, and extensive trade were all true of other regions that did not industrialize, so they characterize a milieu rather than explain a contrast. (4) Lena answered that applying the fork test one variable at a time begs the question against conjunctural explanation, since a factor can be individually non-discriminating and jointly discriminating — cheap energy only mattered given a particular configuration of transport costs, demand, and institutions — and that requiring each factor to fork on its own rules out interaction effects by stipulation; the test, she said, should be applied to the causal package as a whole. (5) Owen said that applied to the package it becomes unfalsifiable, because with enough variables one can always assemble a conjunction that picks out Britain uniquely, at which point the explanation has become a description of Britain — he compared it to overfitting a model to a single data point — and so he proposed a second criterion, the second-case requirement: a Britain-versus-China explanation earns its keep only if, using the same variables and the same weights, it also gets right the ordering of a case it was not constructed from, such as why the Netherlands or Japan industrializes when it does relative to some third region; if the model has to be re-specified case by case, it is not explaining anything. (6) Lena replied that the second-case requirement smuggles in a general-model picture of explanation that historians of singular, path-dependent outcomes reject: a singular causal explanation can be perfectly respectable without transferring, and demanding transferability would disqualify most of what historians defend. She added that the contrast class itself is not gerrymandered, because China is chosen as the most likely alternative — the leading candidate on several independent measures — so the question is really why the eventual winner beat the favourite, which is well posed even if its answer is conjunctural and non-transferable. (7) Owen's parting shot was that choosing the contrast case precisely for its similarity on the very variables that are also candidate causes rigs the test, in the way that selecting a control group by similarity on the treatment variable would; and that, more importantly, his two criteria are jointly unsatisfiable, since any causal package fine-tuned enough to pass the fork test as applied to whole packages will be too case-specific to pass the second-case requirement, and anything general enough to pass the second will fail to fork.

Please go through their seven moves one at a time and rate each on a five-point scale (clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, half right, mostly right, clearly right), justifying each rating in two or three sentences and, where a move is partly right, separating the part that works from the part that doesn't — the point of the session is to teach people to sort good from bad moves inside a single argument, not to declare a winner. I'd also like you to say explicitly which claims each of them conceded implicitly but never acknowledged, and what each is still owed by the other.
