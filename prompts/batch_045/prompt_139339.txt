the paragraph says, more or less:

1. a strategy is strictly dominated when you have another single strategy of your own that does better against everything the others could do; cross those out, look again at the smaller game, repeat.
2. letting mixtures do the deleting buys you nothing new: if a 50-50 mix of T and M strictly dominates B, then either T or M already strictly dominates B on its own.
3. equivalently, a strategy survives iff there is some belief about the other players' play — a mixed strategy for each of them, picked independently — under which that strategy is a best response.
4. since a strictly dominated strategy is never a best response, no Nash equilibrium puts positive probability on one, so the survivors contain every equilibrium; and if the procedure pares the game down to a single profile, that profile is the unique Nash equilibrium.
5. order doesn't matter — cross out strictly dominated strategies in whatever order you like and you land on the same survivors, and that stays true when the things doing the eliminating are mixed.
6. the mixtures aren't just bookkeeping: if a 50-50 mix of T and M dominates B, then what the theory tells the row player to actually do is randomize 50-50 between T and M.
7. and in the game above the notes say it stops after deleting B, with {T, M} x {L, C, R} as the final answer.
