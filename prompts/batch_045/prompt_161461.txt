I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on Hofstadter's Ant Fugue setup — the colony that gets a personal name, Aunt Hillary, with the individual ants playing the part of her nuerons — and instead of another essay I want the centerpiece to be a "which theory of collective mentality are you actually committed to?" quiz.

The angle I care about is triage, not labels: given some system that's organised as a collective, do you treat it as one subject with a mind of its own, as something usefully describable in belief-desire terms without it really believing anything, as merely a container for thousands of small subjects, or as nothing over and above its parts? Those get run together constantly and I want the instrument to pull them apart. So:

- 8-10 questions, each a concrete scenario with 2-3 answer options. No question may just ask "do you think the colony has a mind?" — readers should get sorted by what they say about cases, not by which label they fancy. Scenarios I want in there: a swarm picking a new nest site by a quorum rule where no single member ever compares two sites; a colony spread across two nests joined by a trail; two colonies that fuse into one working unit and later split again; a colony where ants are swapped out one at a time for tiny robots running the same follow-the-trail rules; a box that reads the chemical state of the nest and prints English sentences, which the colony then "answers" by shifting what it does; and a case where a single colony-level decision takes six hours.
- Build it from the logical space first: give me the raw branching questions (what individuates a candidate subject; whether integration or mere functional organisation is doing the work; whether the time-scale counts against mentality; whether mind, describability in intentional terms and moral-patienthood can come apart; whether any of it generalises one level up to the whole wood or the ecosystem), then place the actual positions in the cells, and for each empty cell say whether it's empty because the view would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.
- Six to eight leaves, named where a name exists and "unnamed" where it doesn't. Must include full colony-mind realism, superorganism-yes-mind-no, the pluralist option where the cognition is real but the subjects are the ants, the deflationary "Aunt Hillary is just a façon de parler" view, and something for the view that mentality has to be inherited from or constituted by the members. One sentence on each leaf's most awkward commitment.
- A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves, plus which patterns land nowhere clean.
- Flag any pair of leaves your questions can't really separate rather than pretending they split, and say which leaves need a principled stopping point before the "well why not the whole forest?" move eats them.

Skip citations and further reading entirely — nobody in the group follows them up anyway, and I'd rather have the cases. Keep the scenarios readable for a bright undergrad who hasn't read any of the literature.
