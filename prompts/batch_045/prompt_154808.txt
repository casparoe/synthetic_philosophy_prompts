I sit on the panel at our council that signs off adult social-care support plans — fortnightly, a dozen or so cases a sitting, and whatever we decide goes in a file someone can challenge, so it needs to be defensible on paper and gettable in about ten minutes a case by someone with a social work degree and no philosophy.

What we keep tying ourselves in knots over: someone says "I don't want a carer from that estate", "I'd rather not go to the day centre, that's for people worse off than me", "I don't want to be a burden on my daughter" — and we can't tell whether that's a preferance we should simply respect or a bias (internalised stigma, an expectation worn down by years of being told no) that we'd be colluding with by writing it into the plan. Overriding it feels patronising; honouring it feels like laundering a bias as a preference.

Three tests I've been kicking around and can't get to work: substitution — if swapping in a higher-status version of the same option flips the reaction ("no carer from a professional family, they'd look down on me"), we're tracking status, not the option; who bears the cost — it counts only if they still hold it when honouring it costs them something they independently want; take-back — if the option were free, easy and nobody would know, would they take it? What makes me doubt all three is that people endorse their own adaptations sincerely, so asking them doesn't settle it either.

Turn this into a procedure we can run: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with "respect it", where it stops with "that's bias, don't build it in", and where it has to hand the call to a person because it's a values question not a factual one. Say plainly which of my three tests to keep, fix or bin. Lead with the bottom line in one sentence, then the reasoning. Then run it on: a 78-year-old man refusing a male carer; a woman who'd rather have fewer hours than ask her son for help; and a resident who won't go to the day centre because "that's where they put the people with dementia" — I expect not to like what it says about at least one of those.
