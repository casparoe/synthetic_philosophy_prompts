I'm a statistician, about 15 years in, mostly applied Bayesian work: hierarchical models, meta-analysis, measurement-error models, and lately a lot of calibration stuff — trying to figure out whether my own posteriors are overconfident, and whether "overconfident" is even the right diagnosis. I've read basically zero epistemology, so please don't assume I know what any of the jargon means, but also don't give me a survey or point me at papers, I don't have journal access anyway. What I want is a translation.

Here's the situation I keep actually running into. Sometimes I get information not about the world but about my own reliability, and I don't know what to do with it. Three flavors:

1. A colleague I respect, with the same data and comparable skill, comes out somewhere quite different from me. Naively I'd say: partial pool, weight by precision, done. But people in this debate seem to think something more principled is at stake: one camp says I'm required to give their view real weight just because they're a peer, another says I'm allowed to hold my ground.
2. The variant where I can name the mechanism of their error — I can see exactly what made them go wrong (think: I can see the spinach in their teeth, so their confident "no there isn't" is fully explained by their not having a mirror). Everyone seems to agree this case is different from case 1, and I want to know *how* that difference is even represented. Is it just that I now have a bias term to estimate, or is it doing something no model of mine captures?
3. The one that actually keeps me up, because it's the one my job is full of: I get a signal saying my own processing was degraded *at the time I formed the belief* — but I can't re-run the original analysis, the data's gone or it was a judgment call, and the signal's own reliability is something I can only estimate using the very methods it's calling into question. So it's a model of my own error, built by a model that might be in error, and I can't step outside the stack.

And then there's the state I end up in, which I gather has a name: I still believe p, while also believing that believing p is exactly what an unreliable version of me would do. I believe p and I believe I shouldn't. Is that supposed to be incoherent, or just unpleasant?

So: restate these three (or four) things — the disagreement case, the "I can explain their error" case, the self-referential unreliability case, and the akratic state — in the vocabulary I actually use. Point estimates and intervals, likelihoods, precision weights, partial pooling and shrinkage, hyperparameters on my own reliability, exchangeability across analysts, bias terms, calibration curves, posterior predictive checks, a model of a model of a model. Be as faithful as you can and flag every place where you had to fudge: if the translation only works because you quietly assumed I should always pool, say so, because that's the thing under dispute, not a neutral translation of it.

Then the residue report, which is the part I care about: what doesn't survive? My working hypothesis is that this whole area dissolves once you write it as a hierarchical model with a hyperprior on my own reliability — you shrink, the regress is just another level and it converges, done — and that the philosophers are only stuck because they want a single "rational credence" produced by *reasoning* rather than by a model with a prior, and because they want a verdict on what I'm permitted to believe, which isn't a quantity. Where is that hypothesis right and where is it a cop-out? Specifically:

- Is "defeat" anything over and above re-weighting? If I model it as a likelihood on my own process, what am I leaving out?
- Does my vocabulary have any way to represent the akratic state at all, or does the fact that I can just carry two numbers (an estimate and a meta-estimate) mean my framework is missing a normative requirement that theirs has?
- Is the convergence of the regress a real solution or a change of subject?
- And does any of it tell me anything about what to *do* — re-run the analysis, collect more data, defer, redesign the pipeline — or only about what number to output?
