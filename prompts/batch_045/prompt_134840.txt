I'm an attending anesthesiologist at a large teaching hospital, and in six weeks I have to give roughly ten minutes of remarks at an evening the hospital is calling "Learning from Harm." I'd like help writing them.

The background, plainly. About three years ago I made a dosing decision in an operating room that turned out, through nothing I did next, to be harmless: I drew from the wrong vial of two that sit adjacent in the anaesthetic trolley, the patient tolerated it, and there were no sequelae. I did not report it. Eighteen months ago I drew from the wrong vial again. A child died. Every review since — the hospital's, the coroner's, our own departmental one — has concluded that the two acts were not distinguishable by anything available to me when I performed them. What distinguishes them is what happened afterwards, and therefore what I now am in the eyes of everyone who knows the story.

The hospital has since reorganized its review process around what the new patient-safety director calls the symmetry rule. Every case presented, whether it is a harm event or an award, is to be presented alongside its counterfactual twin — the same decision, the same information at the time, the other outcome — and the write-up is to state only what the clinician had reason to believe when they acted. In practice this means that the citation for this year's Lifesaving Intervention Award, which is being given that night to a resident who made a call of exactly my kind in an emergency, will be read in the hedged register of a harm report: a decision that was one of several defensible options and that in this instance was followed by recovery. The symmetry rule is not a rhetorical flourish. The director means it, and the audience knows she means it.

The room will be about two hundred people: clinicians who have read the outcome-bias literature and take the symmetry rule as obvious, department chairs and trustees who think it is moral cowardice dressed as methodology, residents, and — in the front row — the parents of the child who died. They are members of the Family Advisory Council, the letter they wrote after the inquest is the reason the review process was rewritten, and they will hear every word.

What I want from the speech, and what I want it to avoid:

It has to work with the real distinctions rather than around them. I'd like the middle of it to separate resultant luck from circumstantial luck — that I was the one on call that night, on a service that had been short-staffed all winter, in a hospital that had been told twice about the vials and had moved them six inches instead of apart — and from constitutive luck, whatever it is about me that made me the sort of person who does not file a report. Those three are not the same kind of thing, and the symmetry rule can only conceivably do something about the first. I'd like the speech to say which of them a review process can actually touch, and to be honest that the answer may be: only the one that matters least to the people in the front row.

It must not let outcome-independence become a shield. I have been in rooms where "it was luck" arrived disguised as humility and functioned as a defence, and I will not do that in front of them. At the same time I have been told, and my insurers' lawyers have told me, that there is a narrow set of sentences I cannot say in a room that size; so what I need is a way of being answerable — for the unreported near-miss, for what I knew about the vials and didn't escalate, for the fact that I am still here and practising — that does not depend on the word blame and does not depend on the death having been my fault in the way everyone assumes it was.

It has to congratulate the resident without making her a prop for my argument and without pretending I agree that she deserves nothing. If the symmetry rule says her save is as unearned as my harm, the speech should be able to say what is true about her night without either endorsing or quietly retracting that.

It must never imply that the death was random in a sense that makes the parents' grief or their complaint misdirected. Their child is dead because of something I did; whatever luck does to my standing, it does nothing to the loss. I'd rather the speech risk being heavy than land a point at their expense.

Finally: no named philosophers, no mention of Greek tragedians, and none of the gallows humour this place runs on. I'd like it to end somewhere I can actually stand while they are still in the room.
