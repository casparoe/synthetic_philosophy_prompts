I've got a job-market paper (really a thesis chapter) that I'm presenting in three weeks at a two-day workshop on distributional cost-benefit analysis at a public policy school, and I'd like to not get eaten in the Q&A. The room will be maybe fifteen people: mostly public finance and applied micro economists who spend their days with social welfare functions, the inequality-aversion parameter in an Atkinson-style index, the transfer principle, and distributional weights in CBA; one social choice theorist who will immediately start asking which axioms I'm giving up; and two philosophers who work on population ethics and already think prioritarianism is a mess. Nobody there is a card-carrying prioritarian. My old supervisor is coming, and he thinks the whole view is just utilitarianism with a concave transformation plus bad PR. I get 25 minutes and then 20 for questions, which in practice will be the social choice guy for ten of them.

The thesis is a fix to prioritarianism that I've been calling baseline-anchored prioritarianism (my coauthor hates the name and wants counterfactual prioritarianism; we haven't solved it). Sketch: standard prioritarianism weights a person's wellbeing change by how badly off they are in the outcome being evaluated, which is exactly why it gets hit by the leveling-down objection -- burn the surplus of the well-off and benefit no one, and the weighted sum can still go up, because the losses fall on people whose wellbeing is high and so gets a low weight. My proposal instead fixes the weight by the wellbeing level the person occupies at the baseline, i.e. in the counterfactual where we do nothing, and applies that one weight to whatever change the policy produces for them. Pure leveling down then buys nothing (there's no gain to weight), the ordinary cases where you choose between helping the worse-off and helping the better-off come out the way prioritarians want, and -- the bit I actually like -- the view says something determinate in cases where the classic version is silent. The costs I already know about: the ranking is now a function of baseline-outcome pairs rather than of outcomes, so it isn't a welfare function over wellbeing vectors and it gives up welfarism; it can't be dropped into a model and maximized in the usual way; and because losses to people who are well-off at baseline are cheap, it will okay enormous harms to the rich in exchange for very small gains to the poor.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet for this audience, not for philosophers: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, roughly in the order they'll come (I assume the social choice guy goes first, and the applied people get to measurement, counterfactual estimation and error propagation somewhere in the middle), each phrased the way those people would put it -- in their vocabulary of welfare functions, axioms, weights, elasticities and estimation error, not in mine. For each one: how much damage it does if I don't have an answer, a two-minute reply, what I should just concede up front instead of being dragged into conceding, and where my reply is weak. Tell me which two replies I should actually practice out loud, and give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- my bet is something about what the view says when the baseline is itself something we messed up (if the worse someone already is, the cheaper further losses to them are, hasn't my fix made things worse rather than better?), but tell me if the real nightmare question is a different one. Please also say, objection by objection, how confident you are that the reply would land with people like this, and flag the single reply you're least sure about -- I'd much rather find out now which part of my position I'm bluffing on. And if you think one of the objections is simply correct and I should change the view before the workshop, say that plainly.
