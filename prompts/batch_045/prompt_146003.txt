Jackson's Mary learns every physical fact while shut in a black-and-white room, then sees a red tomato for the first time.
(a) Set the knowledge argument out as numbered premises and conclusion, and say exactly what the conclusion is meant to be — that physicalism is false, that there are non-physical facts, or that there are non-physical properties — and why the difference matters.
(b) Explain two physicalist replies: the ability hypothesis (Lewis, Nemirow) and the phenomenal-concept strategy, i.e. the old fact / new concept reply. Name at least one thing that separates them.
(c) Here is a variant, call it lockdown: the instant Mary's experience of the tomato is over, a device erases every dispositional trace of it — no memory of it, no improved recognition of red, no ability to imagine or recall what it looked like, and she cannot afterwards say or think what it was like. While she is still looking at the tomato she is asked whether she has learned something, and she says yes. Does lockdown cause trouble for the ability hypothesis that the original case doesn't, and does the phenomenal-concept strategy do any better with it? Say why in each case, and say whether you think the variant is coherent to begin with.

The answer I have in front of me:

Jackson's argument has four steps. (1) Before release Mary knows every physical fact. (2) On seeing the tomato she learns something she didn't know before, namely what it is like to see red. (3) So there was a fact she didn't know before release. (4) So not all facts are physical facts, and physicalism in the sense of the completeness of the physical is false.

What the argument establishes is that there are facts Mary did not know until release; since she already knew every physical fact, some facts are not physical. That is stronger than the claim that there are non-physical properties, and the difference matters, because someone can grant that phenomenal properties are picked out in a way no physical description captures while still holding that every fact about them is fixed by the physical facts — which is roughly what the phenomenal-concept reply goes on to do. The property version of the conclusion is weaker and doesn't by itself overthrow the completeness thesis, which is why the argument is put in terms of facts and knowledge. Jackson himself took the conclusion to support epiphenomenalism, though he later gave the argument up.

The ability hypothesis (Lewis, and Nemirow earlier) says Mary gets no new propositional knowledge at all: what she gets is a set of abilities — to imagine red, to recognise it, to remember what it looked like — and knowing how to do something is not knowing that something is the case. The phenomenal-concept strategy is the same move in different words. It also says Mary gains no new propositional knowledge and no new belief; the only difference is that it calls the new item a phenomenal concept rather than a skill. Both replies therefore reject (2) taken as a claim about knowledge, and both say Mary has only acquired a new way of accessing a fact she already knew, so the physical facts were complete all along.

Lockdown is worse for the ability hypothesis than the original case is. If what Mary gains just is the abilities, then once the device has wiped out every trace of them she has gained nothing, and yet she plainly did learn what red looks like during the moment she was looking at it, so knowing what it is like can't just be a matter of having abilities. The phenomenal-concept strategy does better here, because a concept is not an ability: it is a representational item, and her acquiring it during the experience isn't undone by the later loss of her imaginative and recognitional capacities. That is what makes her yes true. The variant is coherent enough — the erasure removes the effects of the experience, not the experience itself.
