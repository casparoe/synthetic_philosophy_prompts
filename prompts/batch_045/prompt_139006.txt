I am writing a paper on the structure of prudential and social discounting, and I need a thought experiment built to order rather than an illustration of a point I already have.

The dialectical background, briefly, so you can see what the case has to do. In welfare economics the discount rate applied to future consumption is standardly decomposed into a pure rate of time preference, a term reflecting the diminishing marginal value of consumption under growth, and a risk or mortality term. The prevailing normative orthodoxy holds that the pure term should be zero: future welfare counts as much as present welfare, and any residual preference for the near is a bias, traceable to something like a failure of imagination or sympathy across time. Defenders of a positive pure rate reply by appealing to the indexicality of practical reason, to the analogy between future selves and other people, or to the modest claim that a standpoint in the present is simply where agency is exercised. My interest is not in settling that dispute. It is in the assumption both sides share: that prudential discounting is a function of a single scalar applied along the axis of calendar distance. I want a case that isolates and stresses that assumption.

So here is what I want you to build.

The scenario. One paragraph, realistic, set in ordinary personal life: retirement planning, health, a mortgage, a family business, a career break. No science fiction, no exotic technology, no imaginary psychophysics. The protagonist should be around forty-five and choosing between two options that deliver the same kind of good at two different periods of her life, with the earlier period falling inside a stretch of her future to which she now expects to be weakly psychologically connected and the later period falling after that stretch, when she expects to have come back to herself. The alienation must be of a kind a reader would recognize: a well-documented recurrent major depression with a decade-long course and an expected remission, or a foreseen immersion in an ideological or religious milieu she expects later to repudiate, or a period of addiction she expects to survive. It must not be produced by anything that would independently justify discounting.

The three views. First, temporal neutrality: discount future welfare only for the probability of not being there to receive it and for diminishing marginal value; set the pure rate to zero. Second, positive pure time preference: a constant, exponentially applied pure rate, taken as rationally permissible, on top of the same risk and marginal-value adjustments. Third, the view I want tested, which you should state precisely before using it: call it the connectedness-indexed view, according to which the rate at which an agent may discount a future good is a function of the degree of psychological connectedness between her present self and the self who would receive it, where connectedness is a matter of overlapping memories, values, character, projects and felt identification. The important thing about this function is that it need not be monotone in calendar distance.

The specification the case has to meet. The first two views must return the same verdict: both must recommend the same option, and they must do so for different reasons, which you should state separately. The third view must dissent, and it must dissent in the direction of the later good, so that the dissent cannot be waved away as one more form of impatience wearing a different hat. The verdicts must fall out of the views plus the stipulated facts; stipulating that a view recommends something, or building the recommendation into the description of the option, is the failure mode I care most about avoiding.

Confounds to hold inert. The divergence must not be generated by mortality or non-existence risk, by diminishing marginal value, by the real return on capital, by anticipated change in tastes or values, or by uncertainty about what she will then want. It must also survive the stipulation that she has full imaginative and epistemic access to both periods: she can picture the later decade as vividly as the earlier one, so no appeal to weakness of imagination is available to explain anything. To make this checkable, choose a good whose value to her is roughly stable across the two periods, such as relief from a chronic physical symptom rather than a luxury whose worth depends on her then-current projects.

Show the arithmetic. Give a small table with the per-period payoffs, the survival probabilities, the continuity or connectedness weights, and the resulting evaluations under each view, so that a reader can see the three verdicts computed rather than asserted. Keep the numbers plausible; do not inflate a gap to make a point.

Then the diagnostics. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work. Show what happens to the verdicts under at least three variants: connectedness declining monotonically with distance, connectedness roughly flat, and the alienated stretch removed altogether. I expect the case to collapse back into an ordinary dispute about the size of the pure rate under at least one of these, and I want you to say which and why that matters for what the case was supposed to show.

Steelman the losers. Give me at least three distinct lines of attack available to a defender of whichever of the first two views comes out worse off, and rank them by strength. I specifically want you to address two of them. One: is the connectedness-indexed view merely pure time preference with a time-varying rate, and if it is, does the case still demonstrate anything other than that discount functions can have shapes? Two: the time-inconsistency objection. Assume no commitment devices are available to the protagonist, so that whatever plan she makes at forty-five she can revisit at fifty-five, and say what the connectedness view should be taken to recommend from which standpoint, and what it costs the view to answer either way.

Finally, a note on the epistemic status of what you have given me. Separate clearly, in a short closing section, what is settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested claim as if it were the consensus. I have in mind distinctions like the following, though you should not treat my list as exhaustive: that the decomposition of the discount rate into its standard components is a piece of bookkeeping rather than a moral claim; that a zero pure rate is the default normative benchmark in policy economics while remaining philosophically disputed; that whether psychological connectedness has any prudential significance at all is an open and quite deep question; and that whether a time-varying discount rate can be rationally permissible is contested. Also flag, explicitly, where your scenario takes liberties: which of its stipulations are empirically shaky, which rest on a partisan reading of personal identity or of prudence, and which a hostile reader would refuse to grant.
