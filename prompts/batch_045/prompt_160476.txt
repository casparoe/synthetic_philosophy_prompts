I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works for a victims' support NGO about a case our commission is about to hear, and I still can't tell whether our own precedent set settles it or just looks like it does. Her position is that amnesty is, at bottom, a currency the political community spends to buy truth it cannot get any other way, and that once you accept that, the identity of the seller and the purity of the seller's motives are beside the point — what matters is what the disclosure buys for victims and whether immunity is the least the state can give in exchange. My position is that amnesty is constitutively personal: it is something a natural person does when they stand up in a public hearing, avow their own acts, submit to being questioned by the people they harmed, and are judged to have acted with a political objective that a successor society can recognise as political even while condemning it. On her reading a company with a better archive than the state is just a very efficient witness; on mine it is not the kind of thing that can apply at all, and offering it immunity would convert the process from an exchange of accountability for truth into a purchase of information from a party that feels no accountability. I need to write a draft recommendation for the committee by Friday, and I want to know which of us our own track record actually supports.

Here is the record I have to work with, in the order the decisions came down. One: amnesty granted to a conscript who shot two men at a roadblock, after a full account and a finding that the killings were associated with a political objective. Two: amnesty refused to the section commander above him, who also disclosed everything, on the ground that the raid he ordered was grossly disproportionate to any political objective — the committee called it punitive rather than political. Three: amnesty refused to an applicant who confessed his own role in detail but would not name his handler or point to where the bodies were, the committee holding that full disclosure includes the chain of command and the recovery of remains. Four: amnesty granted, conditionally, to two applicants for a cross-border raid in which all the dead were nationals of a neighbouring state; the conditions were a hearing held in the affected communities and a reparation order, and the reasoning was that the community the act was done to must have a real role in the process. Five: the committee declined to entertain an application from an editor who had incited violence in print but had committed no act himself and whose account told it nothing new, saying that amnesty relieves liability for specific acts and is not available as a declaratory finding of blamelessness for someone with nothing to be relieved of. Six: amnesty granted, over a dissent, to a clerical worker who never used violence but passed lists of names to the security branch, one of which led to a killing; the majority said his account revealed how the informer network actually functioned, which no one above him had explained. Seven: the committee refused to let an applicant's offer to fund a clinic in the affected village count toward his application, saying reparative contributions cannot be traded for amnesty, though it recorded the offer separately in its findings. And there is one internal tension I should flag honestly: in an early decision the committee granted amnesty to a man whose account largely duplicated a police docket, saying the value lay in public corroboration from the perpetrator's own mouth, while in a later one it refused an applicant who had disclosed only what was already in the docket, saying he had told the commission nothing it did not have.

The case in front of us now is a mining company, Kalkrand Consolidated, which has come in voluntarily and handed over a complete personnel-and-transport archive covering 1978 to 1991. On the company's own account it supplied the security branch with payroll and housing data used to identify union organisers, provided vehicles and a disused compound that it knew was being used for interrogation, and paid a bonus to the liaison officer who made the arrangements; it says expressly that its motive was to protect production and its share price, and that it would have done the same under any government, which is to say it disclaims a political objective rather than claiming one. The archive names two regional commanders who ordered a 1984 massacre at a hostel and gives the burial sites — disclosure no individual applicant has given us in nine years, and which the families say they want more than anything. It offers a large reparations fund and to pay for the exhumations. In return it asks for a finding that it has no further civil liability to the victims' families, for immunity from prosecution for its present board (none of whom were employed by the company before the transition, though two are children of the era's executives), and for its name to be kept out of the section of our final report on those who profited. The dead were mostly migrant workers from the neighbouring state, most are dead, and the survivors are its citizens; that state has just opened its own commission and has written to us asking that no amnesty be granted and that the archive be transferred to it instead. The families are split, some supporting the deal because they want the bodies, some opposing it because they want the executives in court.

So, three things. First: what are the two or three rules that fit all seven of our precedents equally well, and what does each predict here — I take it one rule makes the grant turn on what the disclosure buys, another on whether the applicant is a natural person who avows their own politically motivated acts, and a third on whether the affected community has had a real role, but I want them stated sharply enough to see where they come apart. Second: give me your best guess at the decision and, more usefully, which precedent is the closest analog and why, together with an honest statement of what principle we would be adopting whichever way we go — including what it means if we take the company's money for exhumations while refusing to count it toward the application. And third: name the single further case that would best tell my friend's rule and mine apart, with what each verdict on it would show us — I have in mind something like the company's former personnel director, now elderly and ill, offering the same archive with a full personal account and agreeing to a public hearing, and also something like a corporation whose records duplicate material we already hold, but I want you to say which of those does the discriminating work and why. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so and tell me which of them we would have to overrule or distinguish to go the other way.
