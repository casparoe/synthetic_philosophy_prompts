I work on a small team that builds memory aids for people with acquired memory impairment — wearable devices that capture what the wearer has just seen or been told and later re-present it on cue — and an argument about how to design the next version has quietly turned into an argument about the extended mind thesis, which I now find myself defending in a version that is not quite the canonical one. My position is that a coupled external resource is literally part of a subject's cognitive system just when it supports what I have started calling a revision loop: the subject must be able to feed the resource's own outputs back into it as inputs to a further operation, so that the state of the external vehicle can be re-described and revised by the subject through that same vehicle, without the loop first passing back through an inner, biological format. On this criterion the diary you annotate in the margin and then re-read while writing a new entry counts, and so does the sketchbook you draw over; the phone number you merely read off a screen does not, and neither does a calculator you query or a turn-by-turn navigation app, even though all three are reliably available, trusted, and easy to consult. That discrimination is the whole point of the criterion: it is meant to answer the cognitive-bloat objection — the complaint that coupling is everywhere and constitution is therefore nowhere — without appealing to anything as vague as integration or as contested as parity with an imagined internal process.

What I want from you is a map of my exposure, not a restatement of the case for the thesis. Start with the defect I already half-suspect: the canonical notebook case from the original statement of the extended mind — the man with the memory impairment who consults his notebook for the address of the museum — looks like pure one-way retrieval, and if it is, my criterion excludes the very example the thesis was built on. Is that fatal, does it show the example was never doing the work its defenders suppose, or can the criterion be read so that updating and re-indexing an entry is enough? Then give me everything else that would count against the view, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which defeaters a committed defender of the thesis would herself concede as genuine costs rather than dismiss as misunderstandings: the coupling-versus-constitution objection; the fact that external vehicles are typically not under the subject's exclusive control and can be silently revised by other people, so that the whose mind is it question has no clean answer; the heterogeneity of external vehicles versus the relative uniformity of neural ones; the worry that the criterion is simply a redescriptive gloss on what we antecedently call cognition; and any result about persistence, agency, or responsibility that the view stands or falls with. Tell me which of these are live rather than merely logically possible.

Two further things would help me most. First, does a thesis of this kind risk anything at all, or has it been qualified into safety? I have caught myself retreating to the claim that the boundaries of the mind are simply indeterminate and that there is no fact of the matter whether the notebook is part of him — and I want to know whether that retreat is a legitimate metaphysical conclusion or an unfalsifiability move, and if it is the latter, whether that is actually a problem for a view of this sort or merely how disputes about constitution always end. Relatedly, what does the thesis predict that a well-conducted cognitive science would not predict anyway; if it licenses no new experimental question and no new intervention, is that idleness a defeater? Second, I should say what I would personally find most persuasive, since this started as a design problem: I am moved by the phenomenology of a well-made aid, the way it becomes transparent and using it feels like remembering rather than like consulting a tool, and I am correspondingly unsettled by the implication that deliberately building a device to be read-only — so that users stay dependent and the vendor keeps the data — is a way of keeping it outside the user's mind, and thereby keeping interference with it outside the category of harming the person. I would like to know whether that implication is one I should bite the bullet on or one that should make me suspicious of the whole position.
