I am the staff lead (not a philosopher, by training a public administrator) of a municipal commemoration and naming panel in a post-industrial city of about ninety thousand people. The mills closed over a decade ago, the city now sells itself partly on heritage tourism, and the current mayor wants the city's public face to look forward to its green transition rather than back to textiles. The panel has seven lay members appointed by the council, meets monthly, and handles thirty to fifty proposals a year: plaques and markers, street and park namings, small monuments, proposals to add to or rotate the city museum's permanent display, requests to deaccession or weed the collection, and changes to the annual civic pageant. I have one assistant. Every decision must produce a one-page rationale in plain language that is published, because decisions are appealed to the full council and roughly one in four is. We have about thirty-five minutes of meeting time per proposal, we cannot commission research, and we are explicitly forbidden from adjudicating contested questions of historical fact. We also carry a maintenance backlog on the plaques and monuments we already own, and can fund roughly one new permanent work every two years.

I want a decision procedure I can actually run with these people, grounded in Nietzsche's 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life (the second of the Untimely Meditations): its distinction between the monumental, antiquarian and critical relations to the past, what each of the three is for and which sort of person or community each serves, the way each degenerates when it stops serving life and becomes an appetite of its own, and its claims about the bounded horizon, the capacity to forget, and the power to take up the past into oneself and transform it rather than be buried under it. I need the ordered questions, what counts as an adequate answer to each, the gates at which the procedure stops with a no or a default outcome, and the points at which it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, with those points marked as such rather than dressed up as findings.

Three things I think the procedure has to handle, and I would like to be told if I am wrong about them:

First, the mode a proposal claims is often not the mode it will operate as. Removing a statue is usually presented in critical terms but can function as monumental, founding a civic myth of rupture. A proposal to honour a recent disaster is presented as monumental but can function as antiquarian, an act of tending a wound. So I need a way of classifying by operative effect, not by the language of the proposal, and I need to know what evidence a lay member can use to tell the two apart in half an hour.

Second, the modes seem to require different distances. Monumental treatment appears to need the subject to have become something that can stand as a case, which may make recent and still-lived events poor candidates for it in a way that has nothing to do with whether the facts are settled. Antiquarian treatment seems to need continuity and proximity. I would like this turned into a test with a threshold, including what to do when a proposal fails it but refusing outright would be read as indifference.

Third, I suspect most of our real failures are structural rather than one-off: commitments that bind successors. A condition attached to a gift, a permanent installation with no agreed mechanism for later reinterpretation, a collection we may never weed, an annual ceremony nobody can end. I want a succession test that asks what later panels are still free to do, and a rule about what form of revisability has to be built in at the outset rather than hoped for. Relatedly, we have no practice of letting things go. If the essay is right that a community also needs the power not to remember, I want that built into the procedure as an explicit step and not left as an omission.

Then run it on three live proposals. One: a permanent granite monument in the rebuilt riverside park to the flood of three years ago, listing the eleven dead, with an annual ceremony, backed by the survivors' association and the mayor. Two: an offer from the family of a former mill-owning dynasty to endow a permanent gallery of textile machinery, ledgers and family portraits, the endowment covering roughly a third of the museum's operating costs, on condition the display stay as installed in perpetuity, nothing be deaccessioned, and the gallery carry the family name; parts of the firm's labour record are contested but partly documented. Three: a proposal to rename the main square for the women who worked the mills through the two world wars, with a bronze figure of a mill worker. I should disclose that I support the third one and that my grandmother was one of those women, and I want the procedure applied to it without being asked to be gentle about that. Tell me if it disqualifies me from the decision and from which part of it.

Finally: say plainly what the procedure cannot do. I have in mind that it cannot tell us which mode this city currently needs more of, since that strikes me as a judgment about what the city is for; that it cannot help when the panel would have to settle a contested fact; and that it offers no protection against one faction capturing a mode for its own purposes. And I want one reflexive answer: whether a standing procedure of this kind is itself liable to become the thing the essay warns against, a piety toward the past administered as routine, and what the cheapest guard against that would be.
