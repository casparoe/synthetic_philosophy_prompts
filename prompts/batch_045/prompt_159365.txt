hi so I'm a junior and I'm on the tech committee for student council, which in practice means I built and now maintain the dumb little poll app we use for school stuff — class president, prom theme, movie night, that kind of thing. Really low stakes but ive gotten weirdly into it and I've noticed a pattern I don't have a word for and it's driving me a little crazy.

The basic thing: I cannot come up with a voting method where writing down what you actually want is always the best move. Every method I try, there's some situation where you do better by not saying what you want. And I don't just mean the obvious one:

1. Pick one out of three: everyone already knows this one, if your favorite has no shot you mark your second choice instead. People actually do this without being told.
2. Approve as many as you like: in the sims I ran, if you like A best and B almost as much, and B is A's main rival, you do better marking only A even though you'd honestly mark both. But this only pays off if you're roughly right about where everyone else is — guess wrong and you can hand it to the option you like least.
3. Rank them and give points by position: the trick here is shoving your favorite's strongest rival into last place even though you think they're fine. And there's a weirder one I found where you help your favorite by pushing a hopeless option *up*, so that your favorite faces someone weak at the end instead of someone strong. That one only works if you know almost exactly how everybody else is going to vote, and if you're even a little off you can knock your own favorite out instead.

So two things I've noticed that I want separated out. First: with only two options on the ballot I've never once found a trick — marking what you want always seems safe. Three or more and there's always something, even if I have to sit and hunt for it. Second, and this is the part I actually care about: the tricks are not all equally real. Some of them you could figure out in your head with no special information and they can't really hurt you. Others need you to know nearly everyone else's choices in advance and blow up in your face if you're wrong. When I explained the risky kind to people and let them try it, almost nobody bothered, and the couple of times a group did try it, the result made most of them worse off than if they'd just voted normally.

So my questions:

- Is there a name for the general fact that you can't design one of these where honesty always wins? I assume somebody proved it at some point.
- More importantly: is there a name for the distinction I keep bumping into, between a trick that exists in principle and a trick an actual person could find and safely use? Like, is there an established way of measuring how much you'd need to know, or how bad the downside is, or how likely you are to make things worse for yourself — and is there such a thing as a method where *every* trick is the hard, risky kind? Or does something rule that out too?
- If my distinction is real but unnamed, name it and define it, and give me the borderline cases: what if you care about more than just who wins (say you also care about how convincing the win looks), what if you're voting as a bloc with friends, what if everyone is a little unsure about their own ranking.

Please don't just hand me a label, I want the carving: what the thing is, what it isn't, the nearest-miss concepts and exactly how mine differs from each, and which one fits best. And I don't want a reading list or paper titles or author names — I can't get at any of that anyway and I just want the ideas.

Also: tell me where you're least sure. Like, if you think I've mashed two seperate things together, say which part you'd drop, and what would change your mind — what finding, or what argument, would make you say "no, there's no such thing, you're confusing it with something else."

Oh and could you write the answer in British English? Our debate thing uses UK spelling and I might reuse bits of it.
