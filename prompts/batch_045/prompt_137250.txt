So I've been going down a rabbit hole about the 2003 Northeast blackout and I want someone to run it through the chaos / determinism / predictability stuff properly, not in a hand-wavey way.

Facts as I have them, please just take them as given rather than re-deriving them: it's Thursday August 14 2003, a hot afternoon, high load in the Cleveland-Akron area, FirstEnergy loses a couple of transmission lines that have sagged into untrimmed trees. Now the bit I actually care about: the alarm system in FirstEnergy's control room had a software bug and more or less went quiet, so the operators didn't realise their own system was degrading and never redistributed load. Over the next half hour it cascades — 508 generating units at 265 power plants trip offline, roughly 55 million people across Ontario and eight US states lose power, and New York and Toronto don't get full service back until the 16th. There's a smaller sequel too: September 2011 in the Southwest, where a technician at a substation near Yuma skipped a step while disconnecting a capacitor bank, and something like 23 separate events across five different grids inside eleven minutes left San Diego and a big chunk of Baja dark.

What I want is a real two-way case analysis, and here are the specific things I want you to get into:

1. Is "chaos" even the right word here, or is this a different animal? I want you to separate chaos proper — deterministic, bounded, nonlinear, genuine sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the whole low-dimensional-attractor package — from a cascade or criticality story: branching processes running near a critical point, heavy-tailed event-size distributions, self-organised criticality, whatever you think is the right contrast class. Say which one fits these two blackouts better, and be concrete about what you'd have to measure to tell them apart (I'm thinking things like event-size distributions vs. divergence rates of nearby trajectories, but you tell me). Then say what the difference buys: is it just a labelling dispute, or does picking the wrong one lead you to different engineering and policy?

2. Most of the unpredictability here looks to me like it came from a measurement failure — the operators literally could not see the state of their own system — rather than from sensitivity in the dynamics. Follow that thread. My worry is that chaos is doing no work in this story: fix the alarm software and trim the trees and you get a boring managed local outage, and determinism-versus-predictability has nothing to say about either of those. Tell me if that's right, and if not, tell me exactly what the specifically dynamical contribution was and where it sits in the causal chain.

3. We can't rerun August 14 2003, so "sensitive dependence" here is not an observation, it's a counterfactual claim that has to be paid for by a model. What evidence could distinguish (a) the grid was sitting near a critical point, so any trigger of roughly that size would have gone big, from (b) the fine details of this particular trigger mattered a lot, from (c) plain luck? And don't skip the asymmetry: plenty of similar tree contacts and local overloads happened before without cascading. Do the near-misses support the criticality reading or undermine it?

4. On predictability: treat it as a slot-filling exercise and fill the slots explicitly — predictable by whom, relative to what information and what model, over what lead time, for which variable, and for the sake of which decision. Because I suspect the honest punchline is: individual events unpredictable, the frequency and size-distribution of events reasonably stable and estimable, and the distribution is the only bit anyone can act on. Is that a real insight or a cop out? And does "determinism does not imply predictability" survive being cashed out that way, or does it turn out to be too weak to carry anything?

5. Normative bit. Given the picture you end up with, what should the operators, the utility, and the regulators have done differently? I'm interested in whether this framework recommends margin, robustness, automatic load shedding and graceful degradation rather than better forecasting, and whether in-principle predictability of the Laplace's-demon sort is doing any work at all in the argument, given that the demon would need the state of every tree and every operator's attention span.

6. And then run it the other direction: what does the episode do to the theory? Two things I want pressed. First, the control-loop point: the grid is a controlled system with human operators and automatic protection schemes inside the loop, it's non-autonomous, it's driven by weather and load and markets, and its "dynamics" partly consist of the decisions of the people trying to predict it. Does the chaos framework even apply to objects like that, or does it quietly assume a fixed, low-dimensional, autonomous system that this isn't? Second, the excuse worry: "no one could have predicted it, it's a chaotic system" is exactly the thing a utility would love to say after failing to trim trees and failing to fix a known alarm bug. Does the chaos framing launder an institutional failure into a metaphysical one, and if so, where should the theory shut up and hand over to the engineers and the regulators?

Please skip the Lorenz-and-the-butterfly backstory and don't pad with suggestions for further reading — I know all that. What I want is the mapping, the places the mapping breaks, and a verdict on whether determinism and chaos are load-bearing in this story or just atmospheric.
