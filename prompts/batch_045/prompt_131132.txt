I write long-form fiction and am midway through a trilogy set in an invented world. Six of its cultures still need a religious system, I have to draft each in one evening before the chapter it appears in, and each then has to survive my editor and one sensitivity reader. What I keep producing is either costume — a creation story, a taboo, some incense, none of which would change a single character's behaviour if I deleted it — or, worse, a thin disguise for a real-world tradition, which the sensitivity reader rightly kills. I do not want an essay or a reading list; I cannot check sources and my readers do not care. I want a procedure.

So please turn what is actually understood about religion as a natural phenomenon — how religious representations get transmitted from person to person and why some spread while others die, what makes a practice convincing to the people inside it rather than merely asserted, why arduous or costly or uncomfortable actions cluster around them, and why they so often concern agents who want something of you — into a checklist I can run unaided, in under an hour, with no training. Give me the questions in the order I should ask them; what counts as a good enough answer to each; the points at which the procedure simply returns a no; and the points at which it must stop and hand the decision back to me because what is at issue is a value and not a fact. State the mechanisms in plain language I can build fiction from. Tell me which distinctions have survived being made usable, which you have dropped and why, and where a procedure like this will misfire on invented material.

Two tests matter to me especially. First, a test for costume: something that tells me whether the religion is load-bearing in the story or only scenery, ideally by asking what would actually change in the plot if I took it away. Second, a rule of thumb for accidental resemblance to a real tradition — when to rewrite a passage myself and when to send it to the sensitivity reader — since I cannot research six cultures' worth of comparative religion.

Apply the finished procedure to three sketches: the guild of lamp-tenders whose creed is that the Long Lamp must never be allowed to go dark, and who are forbidden to count the hours a wick has burned; the river nomads whose single annual rite is a public naming of wrongs, after which the settlement may never speak of them again; and the auditors, who keep clay tallies of favours owed to the dead and burn them at funerals. I expect it to clear one, to tell me to cut or rebuild one I am attached to, and to deadlock on the third — and I want to know, at the end, what it cannot tell me at all.
