I host a long-form interview program on science and ideas, and next week's guest is a computational linguist whose new book argues that Quine's indeterminacy of translation has been settled empirically, and settled against Quine. Her evidence, as she presents it: monolingual word-embedding spaces for unrelated languages, trained separately and with no parallel text, turn out to be approximately isometric, and a linear map recovered from the geometry alone aligns them about as well as one learned from a dictionary. That, she says, is a translation manual extracted from the world rather than negotiated by a field linguist, and therefore the one thing Quine's argument said could not be uniquely determined.

Draft eight to ten questions for her, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give; assume she is quick and has defended this before. At least one question should press on whether an alignment that preserves distances between vectors fixes reference, or only preserves a pattern of similarities that is indifferent among competing reference assignments, and the follow-up should take on her predictable reply that the map is selected by how well it translates held-out words — is that success measured against the very translations whose determinacy is in dispute? At least one should take up the rabbit case directly: if the system cannot be made to discriminate between rabbits, undetached rabbit parts and rabbit-stages, does the inscrutability simply reappear inside the vector, and if so has she refuted Quine or restated him in a new vocabulary? One should ask what she takes the vectors themselves to be, given that Quine's rejection of meanings as entities is what gave the indeterminacy argument its force; her likely answer is that they are records of use and nothing more, and the follow-up should ask what, on that answer, the result is supposed to be evidence about. One should ask how much of the argument depends on both languages describing the same shared world, and what remains of it in the radical-translation situation Quine sets up, where the linguist and the informant have no agreed subject matter to start from. Close with a single question about what empirical finding would make her abandon the claim that this refutes the thesis. Keep the wording a journalist could say aloud to a general audience; I don't mind if you name the standard machinery, only that you gloss it.
