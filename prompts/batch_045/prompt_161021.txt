Trying to write down my actual position on population ethics ahead of a reading group, and I want to know whether the package hangs together before I present it. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Totalism: betterness tracks total welfare summed over everyone who ever exists. I reject average views, partly because they say adding a decent life makes things worse and partly because they get weird about comparing populations of different sizes.
2. Mere addition: if you add a person whose life is worth living and nobody else is affected, the result isn't worse.
3. Equality matters: same total welfare, more equal distribution is better.
4. Betterness is transitive. I'm not biting any intransitivity bullet.
5. I reject the repugnant conclusion: a trillion lives barely worth living is not better than ten billion ecstatic ones. But I do bite the bullet on enormous populations at decent, flourishing welfare — including for digital or simulated minds, which I take seriously — that future looks better to me and I don't find it repugnant at all.
6. The procreation asymmetry: no reason to bring a happy person into existence, strong reason not to bring a miserable one. I lean on this to defuse the objection that 1 commits me to everyone having more kids.
7. Human extinction soon would be a catastrophe, and I want to say that's because of all the welfare that would be lost — the people who'd then never exist are part of why it's bad, not just the people alive now.

I genuinely don't know which one is the load-bearing mistake. Which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and for each real one what has to give and what that costs me?

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no closing summary: the first four should be the tensions ranked most to least severe, flagging any that dissolve on inspection and naming what I'd have to give up in each case, and the last one should be what would change your mind or where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
