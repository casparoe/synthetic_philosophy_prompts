For years now a small group of us — mostly physicists and programmers who found each other through the usual rationalist-adjacent blogs and meet monthly to argue — has been circling the same question: has philosophy ever actually helped science, or is it retrospective commentary sold at a markup? The discussion never goes anywhere, because both sides end up throwing one-liners at each other. My most stubborn interlocutor, a condensed-matter postdoc, says that philosophy is dead, and when pressed falls back on the quip that philosophy of science is to scientists as ornithology is to birds; she once wrote a version of it on the whiteboard of our reading group as the reason we should spend the term on papers rather than on the conceptual material, and we have a standing bet about whether the group will ever actually read any philosophy. I've never been able to find a source for that quip, and I'd rather argue with the sentence than with whoever is supposed to have said it, so please treat it as a free-floating slogan. There is also a milder version in circulation, usually attached to Weinberg, to the effect that the main service philosophy has rendered to physics has been a negative one — inoculating physicists against the bad ideas of other philosophers — which is a different claim entirely and, I suspect, a much better one.

I've tried to taboo both philosophy and useful, and what I end up with is a tangle, so here is my current map. Along one axis I sort the candidate claims by strength: an independence claim (working scientists can answer the questions their field actually poses without any philosophical input — the ornithology reading, and, note, arguably true of ornithology itself); an obsolescence claim (philosophy's questions either get converted into empirical questions as a field matures or turn out to have been badly posed, so what's left over is empty); and an interference claim (philosophical commitments don't merely fail to help, they actively distort research programmes and waste careers). Along a second axis I sort the jobs philosophy is supposed to be doing: midwife (conceptual clarification in fields that don't yet have empirical traction), auditor (surfacing hidden assumptions and internal inconsistencies in fields that do), and taste-maker (deciding what counts as an explanation, which research programmes look promising, what gets funded). My suspicion is that the one-liners are typically plausible for one job and hopeless for another, and that the argument survives only because nobody says which job they mean. She has also proposed what she calls the subtraction test: delete from the last century or so of physics everyone we would now classify as a philosopher, and ask how much later the surviving community would have reached the same results; if the delay is small, philosophy wasn't doing any work. I have a standing objection — that successful conceptual work gets absorbed into a field, stops looking like philosophy, and then gets credited to the physicists, so the test can only ever return not much — but I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a real defect in her test or just a device for making my own position unfalsifiable. She says I am doing apologetics for a field with no findings.

What I'd like is a menu rather than a verdict. For each precise thesis these slogans could be expressing: state the claim in a form where I could tell what would settle it; say what kind of evidence would in principle adjudicate it, and whether the subtraction test is the right instrument for that particular reading or the wrong one; label it as a platitude nobody should dispute, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something straightforwardly false; and — the part I actually care about — say whether it would get my friend the practical conclusion she wants, which is much stronger than anything the slogan literally says, namely that a working physicist loses nothing by never reading philosophy and may be better off for not having. Please also make explicit which reading you think people in arguments like ours are usually expressing, since I suspect it is not the one they would defend if it were stated plainly, and tell me where my two-axis scheme is smuggling in a conclusion or otherwise doing me a disservice. Skip reading lists and citations: I want claims, distinctions and arguments, not things to look up.
