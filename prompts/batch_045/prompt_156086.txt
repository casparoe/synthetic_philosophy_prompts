Me and my wife have been going back and forth about paid leave and daycare for months, and I've definately ended up somewhere that neither side in that argument normally lands, so I want to see the case for it made properly — not a summary of the debate, an actual argument for my position. I should say I've got no academic background in any of this, I'm just a guy who got annoyed at a family dinner.

Here's where I've landed. Everyone treats the question as how best to help families where both parents work, since that's most families now. But I think the dual-earner family is partly something policy helped build and then locked in, and that changes what counts as helping. Call it the ratchet: once mortgages, rents, school schedules and job schedules are priced and organized around two full incomes, no individual family can drop to one income on its own, even if a lot of parents would rather have someone home more. So when the state spends on daycare and on long job-protected leave, it isn't only clearing an obstacle in front of a choice people already wanted to make; it's also rebuilding the world so that choice quietly disappears, and then pointing at the fact that everyone still works as proof that everyone wanted to. I've started calling this the difference between enabling and channeling, and I think any argument from revealed preference — parents work, therefore parents want to work — is worthless once the policy has changed the menu people are choosing from.

So the argument I want is this: a family-policy settlement that spends heavily on getting both parents into full-time work, and that leans on one-earner households to help pay for it, is shrinking parents' real options rather than expanding them, and the honest target of policy should be keeping the one-parent-home option genuinely livable. Please also do two more things. First, take the obvious objection seriously and answer it instead of ducking it — that this is just the male breadwinner model in nicer language, that talk of choice conveniently lands on women, and that anything making staying home easier will get used to steer women out of work. Say what a policy would have to look like to avoid that: what exactly would have to be true of leave rules, part-time work rights, or a per-child benefit for it to keep the option open without pushing anyone into it, and how you'd tell the difference between someone freely taking the option and someone being pushed. Second, no studies, no statistics I can't check, no reading lists, no links or citations — I have no way to look any of that up and I don't want it. Just give me the argument itself, in plain continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, the way you'd explain it in an email to a friend.
