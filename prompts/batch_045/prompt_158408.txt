I'm putting together the closing session of a graduate seminar I teach on evidence, underdetermination, and the so-called historical sciences, and I've asked a colleague from our anthropology department — an archaeologist who works on later prehistoric settlement — to come in as a respondent. The session is built around a distinction I've been developing in my own work and want to defend in front of her. Call the first class inert residues: the unintended by-products of behaviour, such as the sheen on a sickle blade, strontium and oxygen signatures in tooth enamel, the spacing of postholes, the species and age profile of faunal material in a ditch fill. Call the second class legible residues: things produced at least partly in order to be read by somebody else — sealings, standardised weights and measures, coinage, monumental iconography, written records, and deliberate deposits that seem to quote or re-stage earlier deposits. The claim I want to make is that only legible residues can carry an inference to institutions. The argument is short: an institution — a postmarital residence rule, a debt-tribute obligation, a levy on grain, a regime of heritable rights in land — is a set of rules, and rules have to be representable to the people who live under them; where nothing was made to be read, there was no rule there to recover, only behaviour. So, on my view, the current wave of confident claims about Neolithic institutions, resting on ancient DNA and isotope work at sites with no seals, no metrology and no iconography, is overreach, and the honest inference stops at patterns of movement, diet and biological relatedness. My stock hypothetical in the seminar is a ditched enclosure in which the cattle bones carry pasture signatures from seventy kilometres away while the ceramics are local, and the aDNA shows that the adult women in the adjacent burials are the non-locals: I say this licenses at most a claim about where women came from, and not the claim that the community had a matrilocal marriage institution.

What I need help with is how to hold that line without collapsing into positions I don't want to occupy. I'm not sceptical about meaning in material culture, and I don't want to sound like the old processual suspicion that belief and normativity lie permanently beyond the trowel; I also don't want to lean on the indiscriminate form of underdetermination, since I think bridging arguments really can be disciplined by formation theory, use-wear analysis and taphonomy. The step I'm least sure about is the one from rules must be representable to those who follow them to only artifacts made to be read can evidence rules. Is there a defensible formulation — perhaps restricted to codified institutions, or to institutions that are officially recognised as such by their participants — that lets me keep the scepticism about prehistoric kinship-and-debt claims while conceding that house plans, storage capacity and the treatment of the dead can tell us a good deal about what people were expected to do? And I'd welcome your view on a connected worry I have not been able to shake: on my account, ritual and symbolic seem to function as residual categories that we retreat to precisely when the residue is inert, which is very close to the methodological vice the contextual archaeologists complained about thirty-odd years ago.
