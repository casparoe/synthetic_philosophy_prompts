I drafted the following argument for a seminar in normative ethics and have come to distrust it, though I cannot locate the fault. Its conclusion is that supererogation is impossible, and I am not willing to accept that. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

Four premises first.

(1) *Standard characterization*: an available act S is supererogatory for an agent A if and only if it is morally praiseworthy for A to perform S and not morally blameworthy for A to omit S. Call this the datum I most want to keep: that there is a category of acts that are good to do, and in some strong sense better than the alternative, but which no one can be blamed for not doing.

(2) *Optimizing Ought*: if, among the acts available to A, some act B is morally better than every alternative, then A morally ought to do B.

(3) *Bridge*: if A morally ought to do x, then A is blameworthy if she omits x.

(4) *Better-than-the-minimum*: a supererogatory act is morally better than the merely permissible act the agent would otherwise have performed. It is this, and not merely its cost or risk to the agent, that makes it go beyond the call and that distinguishes it from an act that is merely unusual, risky, or personally expensive.

Now the argument.

(5) Consider an agent whose only two options are to perform S or to omit it, where S is supererogatory by (1). By (4), S is morally better than the sole alternative; so S is better than every alternative; so by (2) A ought to do S; so by (3) A is blameworthy if she omits it — contradicting (1). So on this option set, S is not supererogatory after all.

(6) The reply I keep getting is that (5) is an artifact: real agents always have further options — a more generous version of S, a more effective one, a different beneficiary — so S is never *the best* available act and (2) never fires on it.

(7) But then run the argument on whichever act *is* best. Let B be the morally best act available to A. By (2), A ought to do B; by (3), A is blameworthy if she does anything else, since every other act is an omission of B. Call this the Saint Result. It entails that there are no morally optional acts at all: no permission to pursue one's own projects, no space in which an agent may permissibly do less than the most good she could do, and no praiseworthiness that is not merely the discharge of a requirement. By (1) and (4), that just is the claim that supererogation is impossible. So (6) does not rescue supererogation; it relocates the problem and makes it total.

(8) One escape is ought-implies-can: the best act is frequently beyond us, in knowledge or in will, so (2) does not generate a real requirement. But if the relevant incapacity is motivational — the agent cannot bring herself to do the best — then either that is not the kind of "cannot" that limits obligation, in which case the escape fails, or it is, in which case ordinary duties dissolve for agents who cannot bring themselves to keep a promise or testify honestly. Worse, on that reading the category becomes dependent on a defect: for an agent who *can* do the best, doing it is required, and so nothing is ever supererogatory *for her*. A saint would have no supererogatory acts available, which is not what anyone meant by the category.

(9) So the argument seems to be a dilemma: reject (1) and (4), the concept itself, or accept the Saint Result.

What I want from you:

First, name the step where the reasoning actually fails, and say why it looked sound. I suspect the fault is in the neighborhood of the move from "better than the permissible alternative" in (4) to "better than every alternative" in (5) and (7) — a scope or quantifier shift that goes through only if the betterness ordering is complete and transitive across the whole option set — or else in an equivocation in (3) between an evaluative or deliberative "ought" and an obligation-implying one. But I cannot tell which, and I cannot tell whether they are the same fault described two ways.

Second, price the alternatives honestly. As I see them, the options are: (a) restrict (2) — deny that being best generates an "ought," perhaps by letting an agent give her own projects weight beyond their impersonal value, or by treating duty as satisficing rather than optimizing, or as scalar, admitting degrees; (b) restrict (3) — allow that one can ought, all things considered, to do what one is not blameworthy for omitting, either via a threshold below which blame attaches or via a distinction between two senses of "ought"; (c) restrict (4) — deny that supererogatory acts are morally *better*, and explain the praise some other way; (d) block the argument through incomparability, so that no act is best and (2) never fires. For each, say what it costs: whether the satisficing or scalar route needs a non-arbitrary threshold, whether it reintroduces supererogation one level down or merely relocates it, whether the two-"ought" route leaves "ought" with any action-guiding force or makes it equivocal in ordinary moral speech, whether the prerogative route can be justified to the person the agent declines to help, and whether the incomparability route is available for the concrete cases — a bystander who wades into a flooded channel after a stranger, say — without collapsing into scepticism about moral comparison generally.

Third: is the dilemma in (9) exhaustive, or is there a way of taking the friend's reply in (6) that yields supererogation without the Saint Result? And if the argument does fail, does it fail *instructively* — does it show something about how fragile the required/optional distinction is, or about why the concept of supererogation needs more than the standard characterization in (1) to do any work?

Please be explicit about which premise you would keep and which you would surrender. I would rather keep (1) and (4) than (2), and I would rather keep both than accept the Saint Result, but I want to know in detail what I am paying.
