I work in machine learning, mostly on latent-variable world models for model-based reinforcement learning: learned transition kernels over a latent state, amortized variational inference to get the latent from observations, and a lot of time spent worrying about whether the latent I learned is identifiable or whether it is just one of infinitely many reparameterizations that fit the data equally well. My working vocabulary is latent states, priors and posteriors, identifiability up to symmetry, sufficient statistics, lossy compression, encoder versus environment, weights versus activations, and the difference between something being in the model and something being in the world. I have no philosophy training, and my physics stops at an undergraduate quantum mechanics course. A few weeks ago at a dinner I got into an argument with a friend who does condensed-matter physics, and she said something I cannot stop chewing on: that it is still genuinely open whether the quantum state describes something real or is merely a bookkeeping device for what an agent is in a position to predict, and that this is not a pseudo-question. So I tried to reconstruct the debate from memory. I am deliberately not naming any theorem or author, because my recollection of who proved what is worthless and I do not want the answer to rest on attributions; please engage with the arguments, not the sources, and correct me where my reconstruction is wrong. As I have it: the real dividing line is supposed to be whether two different quantum states can correspond to probability distributions over the underlying real stuff that overlap — if they can, the difference between the two states is ignorance rather than matter, which is the reading my gut prefers — but I also remember that something like this gets ruled out, by an argument that assumes that two systems prepared separately and independently get independent underlying descriptions, which in my terms sounds like an assumption that the ontic state space factorizes the way an i.i.d. sampling assumption factorizes. On top of that there are two views I find harder to place: one that takes the state's home space — which, as far as I can tell, has three dimensions per particle rather than three dimensions full stop — to be the real arena and treats ordinary three-dimensional objects as appearances, and another that says the quantum state is not a thing at all but more like a law.

What I would like is a systematic translation, not an explanation pitched at a beginner. Three rules of thumb from my own work keep pulling me in different directions, and I want to know which of them is quietly smuggling in an assumption that does not belong. The gauge rule: if a representation is defined only up to a transformation that leaves every prediction unchanged, do not reify what changes under it — which would seem to tell me that the overall phase of a quantum state is not real, and I cannot tell whether that reasoning is allowed to generalize. The amortization rule: if a representation is the output of a trained encoder, carried by an agent in order to make predictions, it belongs to the agent rather than to the environment, and asking whether it is real is a category mistake. But I am told the quantum state is supposed to be complete or maximal information, not partial information, and in my experience a posterior that is a sufficient statistic for every future observation leaves nothing further to be ignorant about, so the amortization rule may be aimed at the wrong target. The compression rule: a latent whose dimension exceeds the data suggests you have reified your own parametrization, which is exactly what the many-dimensional-space view looks like to me, and I would like to see whether that is fair or whether the analogy to latent dimensionality is a trap. My own tentative guess, which I would like you to adjudicate rather than flatter, is that the law view is really the weights-versus-activations distinction wearing a costume: the quantum state as something slow, global, and governing, rather than as an occurrent state of a system.
