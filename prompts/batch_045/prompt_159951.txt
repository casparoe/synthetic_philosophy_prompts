I'm about two years into a PhD on aesthetic testimony (my supervisor calls the topic "the gossip problem" when she's being generous about it). One line keeps doing an absurd amount of work in our seminars and in her margin notes: "you have to see it for yourself." Sometimes it shows up as "you can always tell when someone is repeating a verdict they haven't earned," sometimes as just "you had to be there," but it's always doing the same job: ending something.

The case that made me want to pin it down: I've got a chapter arguing that a novice can come to know — not merely guess — that a destroyed 1970s performance piece was devastating, on the basis of critics' testimony, given that nobody can see it now. Her margin note was "you have to see it for yourself." I'm sure that's wrong as stated, but I'm also sure she's groping at something real, and I cant work out which thing.

So: give me a menu, as a list, of the distinct precise theses that sentence could be used to express, from the ones that are near-trivial to the ones that are strong and probably false. The distinctions I keep circling but can't organise are roughly these:

- the boring causal reading (testimony can't pipe someone else's experience into your head; you can't *have* an experience on the basis of a report) versus an evidential reading (testimony is just weaker evidence than perception) versus a flat pessimism (testimony can never give you a justified aesthetic belief, or aesthetic knowledge, at all);
- the difference between testimony transmitting knowledge *that* a work is beautiful, clotted, awkward, devastating, and transmitting knowledge of *what it's like* — acquaintance, understanding, appreciation, call it what you like. Is the pessimism really only about the second, and does that matter for what I'm claiming?
- the normative reading, where it isn't epistemic at all: you owe the work your own attention, there's something faintly fraudulent about holding a verdict you didn't earn, first-hand encounter is partly constitutive of what valuing art is;
- a conceptual or semantic reading: aesthetic concepts (or taste concepts) are the kind of thing you can only possess if you've had the relevant responses yourself, so the sentence is about concept possession rather than about justification or evidence;
- and something like a subjectivity reading: aesthetic judgments aren't the sort of thing that can be true or false, or they're answerable to the speaker's own response, so there's nothing there for testimony to transmit in the first place.

Then the bit I actually need. For each reading: is it trivial, a platitude, substantive and defensible, or just false? And — most importantly — which readings would actually support using the sentence to knock out my destroyed-performance-piece case? My suspicion is that the readings strong enough to do that work are the ones nobody would defend in print, and that the readings people would defend don't get you the dismissal. But I want that made explicit rather than just asserted, and I'd like you to say where two of the readings collapse into each other.

Two wrinkles I'd like folded in, because they're the cases I think break any simple version:

1. Testimony from someone whose sensory apparatus isn't mine: a tetrachromat tells me a colour field is garish and jarring in a way I couldn't see for myself even standing in front of it. Here "you have to see it for yourself" seems to fail for a reason that has nothing to do with testimony being weak evidence, and I don't know what that reason is.
2. Testimony about a work in a language I don't read: a translator I trust tells me the original's lines are clotted and hard to get through. Is this the tetrachromat case again, or an ordinary case of taking a critic's word, or a third thing?

One epistemic request, since I'm going to have to defend this in front of her: please seperate what's basically settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely live controversy. I've sat in rooms where everyone talks as if testimonial pessimism is obvious and rooms where everyone talks as if it's obviously false, and I'd much rather you flagged that as a standoff than quietly picked a side and reported it as the consensus. If some reading's plausibility depends on a further commitment — say, about whether aesthetic properties are response-dependent, or about whether knowledge-that and acquaintance really come apart — say so rather than pretending the reading stands on its own.
