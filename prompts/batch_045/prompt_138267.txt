You are a scholar of Hegel's Phenomenology who also works in contemporary social philosophy and the theory of recognition. Please think this through carefully: before committing to an answer, sketch three or four candidate scenarios, note which of them quietly cheat, and only then write up the one that survives. I care much more about the case being structurally sound than about it being clever.

The task. Design a pair of cases, identical except for one feature, that isolates whatever it is that makes the lordship-and-bondage relation self-undermining. Hegel's story, in the minimal form I want you to work from: one self-consciousness stakes everything and the other does not, so the first becomes the independent one whose standing is confirmed by a dependent, and the second becomes the dependent one who works. The relation is then supposed to invert — the dependent party, in shaping things, comes to encounter its own formative activity in what it has made and so acquires a self-certainty it did not have, while the independent party's confirmation turns out to be worthless because it comes from someone whose standing it has itself denied. I want a case where that inversion does not happen, and where nothing has been switched off to prevent it.

Specifications for Case A.

1. Setting: contemporary and mundane — a research lab, a hospital ward, an open-source project, a data-annotation contract, a care home, a university department. No violence, no threat of death or physical force, no chattel slavery, no drugs, hypnosis or brainwashing. Legal and economic dependence is allowed and indeed expected.

2. Both parties are persons, and for both of them what is at stake is recognition, not merely money, comfort or status. The asymmetry must be held in place by the structure of who recognizes whom, not by force and not by the dependent party failing to understand the situation. Third parties may appear, but if they do you must say whose recognition is doing what work.

3. Both of the engines in the story must be running. There must be an analogue of the fear of death: a real, standing precariousness that genuinely unsettles the dependent party's given sense of itself. And there must be an analogue of service and labour: the dependent party's activity really reshapes something, under constraint, and it knows that it does.

4. No reversal. The dependent party comes to no independent self-certainty; the independent party's confirmation is not hollowed out by the low standing of its source.

5. The stability has to be generated by the mechanics of the case, not stipulated. Ruling out, explicitly: the dependent party not caring, the independent party being a narcissist or a psychopath, the dependent party failing to notice its own achievement, and anything of the form "the relation is alienating" used as an explanation rather than as a label for what needs explaining.

6. One paragraph, 120 to 180 words.

Case B. The same case with exactly one feature changed, such that the inversion does occur. Walk through the inversion: what the dependent party now encounters in what it has made, what happens to the independent party's dependence, and where the relation ends up.

Verdict check. Score both cases against two readings of the story, stated here so you need no outside sources.

Reading 1, the mechanism reading: self-certainty is produced when an agent sees its own formative activity objectified in a durable product and finds that objectification taken up by others; the independent party's confirmation is defective because it is given by someone whose standing it has denied.

Reading 2, the normative reading: recognition is a condition of self-consciousness and of normative standing, so an asymmetry that denies standing to one party is unstable in principle — the recognizer's own status cannot be secured by the recognition of someone it has made into a non-person.

For each case, say what each reading predicts (inversion, stability, or no verdict), and why. If the two readings come apart anywhere, say where, and say which reading's prediction you think fails.

Analysis. Name the single feature that is doing the work. Show that removing it from Case A — and only it — collapses the case back into the standard inversion, and show that the same variable is what you altered to get Case B. Then answer honestly, picking one option and arguing for it: is Case A a counterexample to the story, a case outside the story's scope, or a case the story can accommodate after all?

Defence. Give the strongest reply a defender of the story would make against Case A — not a strawman — and then your answer to it.

Purpose, so you know what counts as cheating. This is for a seminar exercise on whether the instability of the relation is an empirical-psychological claim about what labour does to a person or a structural claim about what recognition requires. So the one variable must genuinely carry the difference, and a case that simply asserts that the dependent party gains nothing fails.

Format. (a) Case A, (b) Case B with the inversion traced, (c) a short table of verdicts for the two readings across the two cases, (d) the feature analysis, (e) your verdict on what the case shows, (f) the defence and your reply, (g) one sentence on which part of Case A you are least confident survives scrutiny. No citations, no bibliography, no appeal to commentators or to scholarly disputes: reason from the mechanism as I have stated it. Total response under 900 words.
