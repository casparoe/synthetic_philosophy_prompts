Hi — I've got a slightly odd job and I need something I can actually use day to day.

I'm the only trained historian on a small team that produces the interpretive material for heritage sites and walking trails across one English county: panels of about ninety words, audio stops, the occasional small exhibition. We turn out roughly fourty items a year. I get around three days of research per item, two weeks at most for anything bigger, and everything has to be signed off by a community liaison officer and then by our trustee commitee. Most of the drafting is done by two junior researchers with history BAs who've never done any theory and don't especially want to.

The argument we keep having is the old one between history-from-below / microhistory and the history of elites and states — except we have it in a form where somebody has to decide what goes on a panel by Friday. We've shipped both failure modes. One: the item that is basically the archive of the state talking — a mayor, a charter, an act of parliament, a regiment — with the assumption quietly baked in that the document is the event and that its own categories are the categories the people involved used. Two: the item that is one vivid person and a lot of atmosphere, where the vividness is doing all the argumentative work and a single life is being made to carry a claim about ordinary people that it can't carry.

So: write me a decision procedure my team can run on a proposal. The questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It needs to be runnable by the juniors in half a day, so rule out anything that depends on months of archival digging, and tell me honestly which steps are judgement calls wearing a lab coat.

The hard cases it has to handle, because these are the ones that actually come up:

- The only reason we know a non-elite person's name at all is that a bureaucracy wrote it down for its own purposes — an assize file, a poor-law removal order, a workhouse admission book, a militia list, a tithe map. When is that fatal to the item, when is it survivable, and what has to go on the panel in each case?
- We almost never know how many other people lived like the one person we happened to find, so "typical" is usually unavailable to us. Which claims does that block, and which claims (if any) survive it? I want this nailed down, because it's the objection I can never answer when a trustee pushes back.
- Proposals that arrive already framed as celebration: a civic anniversary, a benefactor, a regiment, a great man the town is proud of. Is there a version of the procedure that lets us say yes without lying, and what has to change on the panel if so?
- Genuinely silent subjects, where the honest panel would be about an absence rather than a person. I don't want the absence turned into a sentimental victim-story either.
- Scaling: when does a close-up of one household license a sentence about the county, and when does it only license a sentence about that household?
- A reverse check on me personally. I was trained on policy and state papers, I find elite sources much easier to work with, and I'm slightly suspicious that microhistory is anecdote with footnotes. I want the procedure to catch me defaulting to the sources that are convenient rather than the ones that are right.

Then run it on three live ones.

1. An 1840s assize file and a parish relief roll that name the same woman, convicted of stealing bread, plus a bit of local newspaper coverage. I want a panel about hunger and crime in the valley.
2. A near-complete run of diaries from a tenant farmer, 1867–1901, with rent books and a letter book. The obvious pitch is "the voice of the rural poor" and I don't trust it.
3. The town council and the local branch of a veterans' association want a mural commemorating the Victorian mayor who paid for the library and the reading room. He was also the magistrate who sentenced the woman in item 1, and we've just found that out.

At least one of those should come out with an answer I won't like — tell me plainly which one and why rather than softening it, since I'll have to defend it to the council either way.

Please write it in British English, and give me something I can hand to a twenty-two-year-old on a Tuesday morning, not an essay for me to admire. Finish by saying what the procedure can't do: the situations where it'll misfire, and where it risks giving us false confidence.
