I'm in an applied ethics seminar this term and got into an argument with a friend who works in demand forecasting for a grocery chain — he basically lives in the software that decides how much chicken each store gets — and he's convinced that consumer-level boycotts of factory farming are morally inert. I've tried the standard comebacks and he blocks each one, but the argument also lands somewhere I really can't accept, and I can't locate the move that got us there. Here's his version, cleaned up a bit:

(1) An act can give you a moral reason against it only if it makes a difference to something that matters: if, holding the rest of the world fixed, everything would have gone exactly the same way whether you did it or not, then there is nothing here for an other-regarding reason to be about. (2) In a modern supply chain a single household's shopping doesn't make that kind of difference. The forecast re-runs weekly, orders go in pallet and container quantities — he says their fresh chicken arrives in lots of 720 birds — contracts carry minimum volumes, and when a store undersells, the surplus gets marked down, diverted into processed lines, or moved to another region rather than shrinking next month's order. So no individual cart changes how many birds are bred, confined, or killed. (3) So I have no outcome-based reason not to buy the factory-farmed chicken. (He's happy to grant that there might be reasons about what sort of person you want to be, or about not wanting to be the kind of person who eats that; he says those are reasons about you, not about what happens to anyone.) (4) The usual reply — well, there's some chance you're the marginal shopper — doesn't rescue it. That chance is not merely small, it's no bigger than the chance that your abstention gets swamped by effects running the other way (weaker sales, deeper markdowns, higher total volume moved, a supplier redirecting birds to another buyer), so the expected difference isn't just tiny, you can't even tell what sign it has, and a reason whose direction you can't sign isn't something you can act on. Besides, if tiny probabilities of large effects generate reasons, you owe an account of why they don't generate reasons for all the nutty Pascalian things, and he doesn't think anyone has one. (5) The other usual reply — that we together make a difference even if I don't — he says is just confused: there is no "we" that acts, there are only individuals, and each individual act is inert, so a heap of inert acts can't acquire a causal or moral property that none of its members has. If each act is one without which things would have gone the same, then the collection of them doesn't help. (6) And the mechanism in (2) isn't doing the real work: all that matters is whether your act is one without which the outcome would have been the same. So the argument covers redundancy as well as thresholds. Suppose 105 people, independently and knowingly, each tip a gram of a slow-acting poison into the same man's drinks over a week; 100 grams is lethal, and the extra grams make no difference to how soon or how painfully he dies. Any one of them could have stayed home and he'd have died anyway; each can truly say "holding the rest fixed, my gram changed nothing." So none of the 105 has any outcome-based reason not to tip it in — and if a 106th person turns up and sees exactly what's going on, she has no reason not to add hers either. (7) Hence there are no outcome-based reasons anywhere individual contributions fall below the resolution of the system: voting, boycotts, divestment, one more car in the jam, one household's emissions, petition signatures, all of it. Which also means, he points out cheerfully, that I have no outcome-based reason to be arguing with him about any of this.

I don't accept (6) — I think plenty of the 105 are doing something seriously wrong and the 106th obviously shouldn't join — but I also don't want to give up (2), because the supply-chain point seems empirically right to me and it's the whole reason the inefficacy worry grips me in the first place. On different days I suspect (1), or (5)'s claim about heaps of inert acts, or the slide in (4) from "very small and sign-uncertain" to "no reason at all," and I can't tell which suspicion is the real one. Which step would you reject, why does that step look perfectly fine when you read it fast, and what does rejecting it cost you — what does your position look like afterwards, and does it still handle the mundane chicken case that made the argument attractive? If your diagnosis of (6) also settles what to say about the 106th person, say so; if it doesn't, say what has to be added.
