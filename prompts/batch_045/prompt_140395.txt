I got into a comment-section argument about whether a guy who donates a kidney to a stranger is being a good person or doing something nobody had any right to expect of him, and someone dropped the word supererogation. I looked it up and got the usual one-liner: acts that are good to do, praiseworthy if you do them, not blameworthy if you skip them. That definatly feels like it's hiding the interesting part, so I want you to rebuild the thing in vocabulary I actually use and then tell me where it breaks.

What I have to work with: MUST / SHOULD / MAY in the RFC sense; spec vs implementation; merge gates; blocking vs non-blocking review comments; lint severities; SLOs and error budgets; best-effort delivery semantics; on-call rotations; performance rubrics; the gap between this pages you at 3am and this would be nice.

My first pass, which I want you to correct rather than humour: MUST = duty, MAY = merely permissible, supererogatory = SHOULD. I already think that's wrong, because in my world a SHOULD violation still has to be justified and still counts as a defect, whereas the whole point here seems to be that skipping the thing needs no justification at all. So give me the better mapping, or say plainly that my vocabulary has no slot for it and say why not.

Then run these through whatever mapping you settle on, and tell me whether they land in the same place: the kidney donor; a stranger jumping onto subway tracks after someone; me staying up till 2am fixing a bug in a teammate's service that isn't mine; handing back a wallet with cash still in it (zero cost to me); not tipping; quietly cleaning up a module nobody owns. That last one is a thing on my team we've started calling a shadow requirement: not in the spec, never blocks a merge, gets you visible credit at review if you do it, and literally nobody says a word if you don't. I want to know whether heroism and ordinary small favours are the same shape here or whether the word is papering over two different things, and I especially want to know whether cost to the agent is doing the real work — does the category require the act to be costly, or is that just an accident of which examples people reach for?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What doesn't survive the translation? My guess is that praise and blame have no honest counterpart on my side: I have credit-in-a-rubric and gets-flagged-in-review, which are roughly symmetric, and I have nothing for positive credit with zero penalty on the other side. In any scoring system I know, rewarded-if-done and never-penalised-if-skipped just collapses into weakly preferred — a preference or a stretch goal, not a requirement — so tell me whether the philosophical notion evaporates under that translation or whether the evaporation is itself the interesting finding. And say whether that points to something my vocabulary is missing, or to the philosophers leaning on something that only looks substantive because nobody ever had to implement it.
