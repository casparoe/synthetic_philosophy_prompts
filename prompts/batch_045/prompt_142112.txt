I convene a small fortnightly reading group — roughly half machine-learning researchers, half people who work on regulation and institutional response — and we are closing a term on the speed of AI takeoff. I would like to open the final session with a poem rather than another summary slide, and I would like you to write it.

The vocabulary we have ended up with is our own, so let me give it to you before you start. We distinguish the *speed* of a takeoff — how much capability arrives per unit of calendar time, or, better, how short the doubling time on the capability curve is — from its *loudness*: whether that capability arrives as a handful of discrete, publicly legible threshold events that anyone could point to afterward, or as a diffuse improvement spread across many systems and many tasks, with no moment anybody could have named while it was happening. We call the first of these loud and the second quiet, and our working view is that the two come apart, and that nearly all of our governance instincts — alarms, moratoria, emergency powers, the whole apparatus of response — fire on loudness rather than on speed, because an alarm needs a threshold to ring on and a quiet takeoff is all gradient. We also talk about *overhang*: capability already present in a system but not yet elicited or deployed, so that a long quiet stretch can be followed by what looks from outside like a discontinuous jump, when in fact the jump is only the release of what accumulated silently. And the thing that has troubled us most is a witness problem. The people best placed to notice a quiet takeoff are the ones inside the labs, and their evidence is mostly not public; outside, the change surfaces first as prices and products and gets attributed to something else; and near the end, the last doublings are in principle the hardest ones to see, because they are precisely the doublings that outrun the ability of the people watching to grade what they are looking at.

What I want is a sestina: six six-line stanzas in the standard rotation, unrhymed, plus the three-line envoi in which all six end-words return, two to a line, one inside the line and one at its end. Take the end-words as DOUBLING, CALENDAR, OVERHANG, ALARM, WITNESS, HAND, in whatever initial order you prefer. The reason I want this particular form is that I want the form to carry the claim: in a sestina nothing new is ever introduced, the same six words simply reappear in six arrangements and then all at once in the envoi, which is more or less what a quiet takeoff is. Move through the material in order — the naive loud picture first, then the redescription in doublings rather than dates, then overhang, then the witnesses and the fact that the final doublings outrun the capacity to grade them, then the mismatch between the capability curve's clock and the calendar our institutions actually run on — and let the turn land where it belongs: the discontinuity people argue about is not really a feature of the world but a feature of the relation between two clocks, so that calling a takeoff fast or slow is a claim about which clock you were measuring in. I would like the envoi to be a compression rather than a resolution: nothing new in it, all six at once, and the question left standing of which failure is the worse one — the takeoff nobody could have named while it was happening, or the one that was perfectly obvious and still outran the hand that was supposed to stop it.

Two constraints. Please avoid the tic-words of the genre: no singularity, no foom, no company or lab names, no dates, no named researchers, nothing that would date the poem to a particular week's news. And please make it accurate enough that someone who works on takeoff dynamics could not find a false claim in it, while still working as a poem for someone who has never thought about any of this — at bottom it should read as a poem about two clocks running at different speeds, and about what can and cannot be noticed from inside the slower one.
