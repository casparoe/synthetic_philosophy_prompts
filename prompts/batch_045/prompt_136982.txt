I'm a PhD student working on a seminar paper about deflationary accounts of truth, and I've ended up with an argument I can't find a knockdown reply to — which, given my track record, makes me suspect it is either confused or already standard in a form I haven't recognized. I'd be very grateful for detailed, blunt comments on the notes below: where the argument goes wrong, which of its steps is doing illegitimate work, and whether there is a paper in it at all. Please don't spend effort on the literature — I'll do that myself — but if what I've landed on is just a familiar position wearing a new label, I'd rather be told plainly than spend a term on it.

The thought started when a friend who works on machine-learning pipelines said, half-jokingly, that in practice her field treats "true" as meaning "output of the validated pipeline." That struck me as obviously wrong but hard to rebut from a deflationary starting point, and the notes below are my attempt to say why.

§1. The picture I'm assuming. Deflationism holds that there is no substantive property of truth to be investigated; whatever work the predicate does is exhausted by (roughly) the equivalence schema, together with the expressive and practical benefits of generalization, blind endorsement of others' claims, and semantic ascent more generally. The normativity of truth is inherited from the norms governing assertion and belief rather than from any metaphysically loaded relation between a representation and the world, and the substantive-looking questions we wanted truth to answer — about success, or about what realism amounts to — get offloaded onto the sentences and beliefs themselves. Call this the Standard Package.

§2. The gap: prospective commitment. Consider two cases. First, a long-running formal prover: I set it running, it will print one sentence per day for two hundred years, and I now assert "every sentence this machine prints is true." Second, a multi-generational observational pipeline: a detector array logs readings in a notation my successors will still understand, and I now assert "every claim in that log is true." Call this the Prospective Commitment Problem. It seems to differ from the three uses the Standard Package already handles. It is not disquotation of a sentence I can now assess: the instances don't exist yet, and when they arrive I will not be in a position to check many of them. It is not blind endorsement of a contemporary speaker: in that case the sentences exist and are in principle accessible to me, since I could in principle learn what Anna knows. And it is not the ordinary case of infinite or generic generalization, where my acceptance of the generalization is supposed to be explained by, or to consist in, my standing acceptance of the instances or of the pattern they instantiate. Here the explanatory direction looks inverted: my acceptance of each future instance will be derived from my acceptance of the general claim, mediated by a mechanism — a program, a tradition, a future community — to whose outputs I have already committed myself. Truth here seems to function as an entry in a commitment ledger that outruns my current capacity to descend to instances.

§3. The dilemma. Either (i) the deflationist says the general claim is nothing over and above the instances, and then owes an account of what it is to be committed now to a totality of instances that do not yet exist and that I may never be able to assess; the only account I can see is in terms of a disposition to accept each instance when it arrives, which looks like a substantive normative fact that the schema itself does not capture. Or (ii) the deflationist adds a further constitutive principle governing commitment transfer over time and across persons, in which case the view is no longer the Standard Package but a hybrid, and the added principle is doing real work that needs its own justification. Either way, I want to know: what is it about "true" that makes it the right vehicle for this kind of commitment, rather than "provable in the procedure," "warranted by the lights of the pipeline," or "accepted by whoever is running the pipeline at the end"? That uniqueness question is where my friend's joke bites, as far as I can tell.

§4. My proposal: Finite-Agent Deflationism. The content of "true" is fixed by exactly two rules. (E) A restricted equivalence rule: for any sentence the agent is in a position to assess, acceptance of it and acceptance of its truth-ascription stand or fall together. (T) A transfer rule: if an agent is committed to "everything in S is true" and comes to learn that sentence σ belongs to S, then the agent is committed to accepting σ — defeasibly in the sense that a sufficiently bad instance can force revision of the general commitment itself, rather than being silently absorbed. There is no property of truth beyond the practice these two rules constitute: no correspondence, no super-assertibility, nothing that requires the world to have a particular shape. The appeal, as I see it, is that it explains why truth-talk is indispensable for finite agents embedded in ongoing practices — science, testimony, computation — without inflating truth into a property, and it predicts that agents with no temporal or inferential limits would have no explanatory need for a truth predicate at all.

§5. What I can see going wrong. (a) (T) may simply be a theorem of the Standard Package once you allow unbounded quantification over sentences, in which case the whole thing is a footnote. (b) FAD may smuggle back a substantive property — being what the practice would converge on — and collapse into a verificationist or Peircean-looking view, which would be an inflationary account in deflationary clothing. (c) The uniqueness worry in §3 may have no answer that stays deflationary. (d) It's unclear how the account handles semantic drift: a case where the procedure eventually outputs sentences in a notation or vocabulary nobody currently interprets, so that even (E)'s restriction cannot be applied at the time of commitment.

Specific things I'd like comments on:

1. Is the Prospective Commitment Problem genuinely distinct from blind endorsement and unbounded generalization, or am I redescribing one of them? Where, precisely, should a deflationist push back hardest?
2. Is rule (T) really extra content, or derivable? If derivable, is there anything left worth arguing for?
3. Take the uniqueness question — why truth and not provability-in-the-procedure — and give the strongest answer available to someone who wants to stay deflationary. Then tell me how convinced you are by it, and why.
4. Which is the most damaging objection to FAD: that it is inflationary in disguise, that it is trivial, or something else I haven't listed? Pick one and develop it as far as you can.
5. My speculative corollary is that ideally unlimited inquirers would have no use for a truth predicate. Is that a virtue of the account or a reductio of it? And more generally: is the vaguely interesting bit here the ledger metaphor, the two-rule formulation, the uniqueness question, or nothing at all — and what, if anything, should the headline claim of the paper be?
