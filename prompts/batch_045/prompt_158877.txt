The extended mind debate has been stuck for years: one side says a notebook, smartphone, or spreadsheet can literally be part of your mind if it's reliably accessible, automatically trusted, and does the job memory or belief would do; the other side says that's just skilled tool use, the external stuff is only causally coupled to you, and the real representing and inference still happens in the brain. Everyone agrees outside resources matter and that we lean on them constantly, so the fight seems to be about whether being causally hooked up in the right way is enough to make something a constituent of a cognitive system. I've been trying to break the deadlock with a newish case I call the calibration ledger: an external device that doesn't just store what the user beleives, but tracks how much that user's own judgments have been over- or under-confident and hands back a discount factor the user adopts straight away whenever they feel unsure. Defenders say it's obviously cognitive because it's trusted, accessible, and sets the final confidence; critics say no, because the user still has to notice they're unsure, decide to consult it, and decide to defer, and all of that is done inside. My guess is that the disagreement is definately at least partly verbal: defenders use 'cognitive' to mean whatever reliably guides successful behavior from the outside, critics use it to mean where the representing and inference actually happens. But maybe there's a hidden empirical question about how much work is inside vs outside, or a bedrock intuition about what a mind is for that neither side can argue for. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this substantive, verbal, two groups answering different questions under one label, a hidden empirical or formal question, or just clashing starting points?
