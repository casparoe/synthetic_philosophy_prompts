I've been volounteered to do the consciousness session for the U3A philosophy group I joined last year — about twenty retired people, no science background, 40 minutes and a flip chart, and they asked for the 'consciousness is a user illusion' thing specifically because I used to be a sensory neurophysiologist (30 years of single-unit recording, so I know the perception side but I'm pretty new to the philosophy).

Here's what I want you to build around. I think the usual way of presenting this collapses three seperate claims into one and then everything goes wrong. Call them: (1) the resolution claim — experience is far thinner and more sparsely sampled than it seems, and we're just wrong about how much we take in; (2) the unity claim — the single smooth stream, and the self it happens to, are constructions rather than givens; (3) the ownership claim — the 'I' that seems to be having the experience is part of the display, not the thing behind it. My hunch is that only (1) is something I can actually get this audience to accept on the strength of ordinary examples, and that (2) and (3) have to be earned by analogy from it instead of being asserted next to it — but I'm not sure that's the right dependency order, and I'm not sure (3) belongs in a 40-minute session at all.

So plan how I'd explain it, not the explanation itself. What has to be in place before any of it even sounds like a claim rather than nonsense — I'm guessing something like 'a readout can misdescribe the process while still being exactly what you need in order to act', but say if that's the wrong first move. Give me one example that carries the whole thing, and say what it has to have and what it has to avoid: I was going to use something from the lab, either a trace that looks continuous when the underlying signal is discrete spikes, or the way a percept stays stable while the firing is wildly variable — tell me if that's the wrong shape, since I worry it'll make them think 'your instruments are crude' rather than 'my experience isn't what I took it to be'.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually come at me, and where in the sequence to head each one off: I expect 'so you're saying nothing is really experienced', 'who's being fooled then — if I'm the illusion, who's the user?', 'I know my own mind better than you do', and 'my arthritis pain is real, so stuff this'. Say if any of them is worth letting run for a minute on purpose. Say what I should flatly refuse to explain at this level (I assume the hard problem, but tell me where exactly to draw the line so it doesn't feel like a dodge).
