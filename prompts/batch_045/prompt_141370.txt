Been reading this stuff since the Sequences days and lurking in the same small Discord the whole time, and two people there (I'll call them M and N) got into a long argument last week about quantum immortality that I can't referee. Neither of them was being dumb, and I'm not going to say which one I think won, because I genuinely don't know.

The part I'd like you to look at is that they weren't arguing about the usual setup (guy with a quantum gun, beam splitter, pull the trigger a hundred times). M introduced a variant he called the coma machine, and then a pair of cases built on it. Setup: you're put under general anesthesia, and while you're completely unconscious — no experience at all, not even the minimal dreamless-sleep kind of thing — a quantum event decides whether you're painlessly killed, and an hour later whoever is still alive gets woken up. Two variants:

Variant A (branch-then-sleep): the quantum event fires first, while you're awake and reading a book. Both resulting copies are then anesthetized; one is killed while under, the other is woken an hour later.

Variant B (sleep-then-branch): you're already fully under, then the quantum event fires, then one branch kills you while under and the other wakes you an hour later.

M's opening point is that A and B have exactly the same branch structure, the same Born weights, the same bodies, the same everything physical — they differ only in whether anyone was conscious at the moment of branching. So, he says, whatever QI predicts has to come out the same in both, or it isn't a physical thesis at all.

Then the argument:

M: In variant B there is no subject of experience at the moment of branching, so there's nothing for the you-always-find-yourself-in-the-surviving-branch reasoning to latch onto. QI is silent here, or at best it turns out to be a claim about when there is a next experience rather than a claim about surviving anything. Which means QI was never a prediction about dying; it's an artifact of how you index first-person facts across time.

N: That's too quick, and the sleep case shows why. Unconscious gaps aren't a problem for continuity — you go under every night and nobody thinks you die and a successor wakes up. What matters isn't consciousness-at-branch-time, it's that the surviving branch contains a physically and functionally continuous brain that later has experiences. That's the same in A and B, so QI says the same thing in both: you wake up. Your timing distinction is a red herring.

M: It isn't, because in the ordinary sleep case both branches contain a continuing brain, so there's nothing for selection to resolve. In the coma machine one branch's brain gets destroyed. So what makes the surviving branch the one you find yourself in? If you say there's no one in the dead branch to do the finding, that has content in A but it's empty in B, since there's no one in either branch at the relevant moment. You're helping yourself to first-person continuity exactly when I'm asking what grounds it.

N: OK, but push it further and QI gets more interesting, not less. Take a third variant: the quantum event decides between killing you painlessly and keeping your body alive indefinitely with zero chance of consciousness ever coming back. Branch-count-wise you survive in one; experience-wise there is never another experience in either branch. Any sane version of QI has to say the prediction is about streams of experience, not bodies. Fine — that's what a view about first-person facts should say, and it's not a bug.

M: If QI collapses into you-always-find-yourself-where-the-experiences-are, it's close to vacuous, and it doesn't get you survival in the gun case either. In the gun case one branch has a fraction of a second of experience and then nothing, the other has years of it. To say that those years are yours you need a story about which future stream is the continuation of you. Under vanilla MWI both continuations are equally yours, and then what you should expect is the Born-weighted mixture, which is death with probability approaching 1. QI only works if there's a single thread of experience that follows the living branch, and that's extra structure MWI doesn't hand you — basically extra metaphysics.

N: The extra-metaphysics charge is symmetric though. Getting the Born rule out of MWI takes extra work too — decision-theoretic derivations, self-locating uncertainty, whatever your favorite is — so needing some extra posit can't be what sinks QI. And on the both-copies-are-equally-you horn: if what you care about is the total measure of your future copies, that's exactly why you should expect death in the limit, and QI dies there. But if what you care about is whether there is any continuation of your stream at all, the measure argument has no grip, because shrinking measure isn't something anyone experiences. Those are two different things to care about and I don't think either of us has said which one is the right one to have.

Then they went in circles for a while about whether expecting is even the right verb when there's no fact of the matter about which copy you'll be.

Can you go through that exchange turn by turn and tell me who's right about what at each step — not just who wins overall — and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'm especially interested in whether the A/B timing thing actually does any work, or whether it definately collapses into some argument that never needed the anesthesia in the first place.
