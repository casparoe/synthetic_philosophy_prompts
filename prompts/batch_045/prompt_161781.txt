So I've been going through eval papers that test concept learning with Bongard problems — you know the format, six little line drawings on the left that all satisfy some rule, six on the right that break it, and the system has to say which side a new drawing belongs to. And the practice seems weirdly two-faced about what counts as success.

On the one hand the headline number is held-out accuracy: get the unseen items right and you've "got the concept". On the other hand everyone treats it as a failure if the model is using a cheap feature — number of shapes, total ink, symmetry, convexity — even in the cases where that feature gets exactly the same accuracy on the test items. The people who build these sets even design against it: they balance the two sides so no single superficial statistic separates them, and they throw out any item where a bank of "cheap" features would do the job.

Now suppose someone built a suite (call it Bongard-Mirror) that scored every submission twice: once on held-out accuracy, and once on "concept match", where you only get credit if the rule you state is the curator's intended rule. And suppose the two scores come apart constantly — rules at 100% accuracy that get zero on concept-match, because the curator's rule was relational ("the small shape touches the large one at exactly two points") and the model's was perceptual ("the bounding box is wider than it is tall"), and both hold for every single item in the set.

What does this whole way of testing presuppose about what a concept is? It grades as if having a concept is just a disposition to classify new cases correctly, but then it leans on an intended rule, and on a legitimate/illegitimate distinction among features, that the accuracy number by itself cant see. Does it presuppose concepts are rules with necessary-and-sufficient conditions? Or prototype/similarity structures? Or something about which properties are natural or projectible, grue-style? Which theories of concepts does it actually rule out, which ones could happily live with it, and where is the practice just philosophically underdetermined so that several theories would license it? And if we dropped the intended-rule stuff and really meant it that a concept is whatever generalizes — what would the benchmark have to turn into, and would it stop testing anything distinctive?

One more thing: please mark which parts of your answer are basically settled (I assume the underdetermination of rules by finitely many examples isnt in dispute) versus genuinely contested among people who work on concepts, and dont sell me your favorite theory as the consensus view.
