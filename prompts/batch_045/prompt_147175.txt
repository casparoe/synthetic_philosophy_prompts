You are a philosopher of mind who works on the metaphysics and epistemology of dreaming — the Malcolm-versus-Dennett dispute about whether dreams are experiences, and the more recent literature on dreaming as imagination, on the reliability of dream reports, and on what sleep-side evidence can establish. Think carefully before you answer, and open with a short private brainstorming phase: enumerate the candidate principles that a set of verdicts like the ones below could be tracking, test each principle against every case in the list, and mark explicitly where the survivors come apart.

I am a research assistant to a philosopher who is finishing a monograph on dreaming, and I need to anticipate where she will land on a case we have not yet discussed. Her verdicts so far, paraphrased and in the order they arose:

1. A subject woken from REM who gives a coherent dream report: she says he dreamed, and that the dream was roughly as reported.
2. A subject woken from non-REM who reports dreamlike mentation: she says he dreamed, and she rejects any account of dreaming that builds in a requirement of REM.
3. A subject woken from REM who reports nothing: she says he did not dream, and she declines to assert that he dreamed and forgot it.
4. A subject permanently incapable of any report — a severely aphasic adult, an infant: she refuses both yes and no, on the grounds that nothing could settle the question.
5. A report given only under leading questioning, and that shifts across retellings: she says he dreamed, but that part of the report is composed in the telling, and she will not adjudicate between the versions.
6. A report whose content matches imagery decoded in real time from the subject's visual cortex during the preceding REM period: she says this confirms that the dream occurred during sleep rather than being composed at waking, while insisting that the decoding is evidence, not what dreaming consists in.
7. A report of a dream that lasted for hours, after only five minutes of REM: she says the duration claim is not to be taken at face value, and that there is no determinate fact about how long the dream lasted.

These seem to me jointly consistent with at least two quite different principles: a report-constitutive one, roughly Malcolm's, on which the waking report is the criterion and any question that outruns what could be reported has no answer; and an occurrence-based one, roughly Dennett's, on which dreams are experiences during sleep and reports are good but fallible, partly reconstructed evidence, with unremembered dreams possible in principle. Verdicts 3, 4 and 7 pull the first way, 5 and 6 pull the second, and 1 and 2 are neutral — so I cannot tell which she is running, and I suspect she could not either.

The new case: a subject has a REM period in which the lab's decoder reads out a structured, imagery-like sequence — walking down a long red corridor, then her brother stepping out of a doorway. Before waking her, the experimenters drive a replay of that sequence through her medial temporal lobe; the replay is lossy, and what gets consolidated is compressed and altered, so that what she has at waking is an apparent memory of a short corridor with her brother standing at the far end. On waking she says, unprompted and with conviction, that she dreamed of a short red corridor with her brother at the end of it. Stipulate, as the case does, that the original sequence was subpersonal: it was generated during sleep but not experienced at the time, and the only conscious episode in the story is the waking apparent memory. Subjects in this lab who are woken from REM without the replay report nothing at all.

Predict her verdict — did the subject dream, and if so was the dream the long corridor with her brother stepping out of a doorway, or the short one with him at the end? Name the principle she is implicitly running, name the rival principle that fits verdicts 1 through 7 just as well and say what it predicts here, identify which of 1 through 7 is the closest analog and why it fails to settle the case, and describe the further case that would best separate the two. I am less interested in the verdict itself than in the principle. Throughout, state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
