Please act as a professional philosopher of mind who has spent years with the literature on consciousness and causation, and who is also skilled at building clear classificatory tools. Before you write anything, think the problem through: sketch out for yourself the full logical space of combinations of answers to the questions below, and only then locate the actual positions inside it. I would rather have a tree built from the space of possible answers than a list of named views with branches bolted on.

Some context. I studied engineering and left university fifteen years ago; I read philosophy in the evenings and have no one to check my ideas against. The thing I keep getting stuck on is what I have seen called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If the smell of rain on hot asphalt does none of the causal work — if everything I do and say, including saying that I smell rain, is fully accounted for by the neural machinery — then my remark was not produced by the smell itself, and it starts to look like a coincidence that it is true, or worse, that I do not really know what I am talking about when I talk about what it is like.

I want a decision tree for the four other people in my reading group: seven or eight branch questions, no more than ten leaves, phrased as concrete cases each of us could answer without knowing any labels (no node may ask whether someone is an epiphenomenalist, or use any word ending in -ism). Cases along these lines: a person whose utterance is produced by a circuit that bypasses everything conscious; a scanner that reliably announces what you are experiencing while never being affected by it; a case where the same experience recurs and you have no way to compare the two; a case where the report and the experience come apart entirely. The leaves should include, at minimum: the view that accepts the skeptical sting; the view that knowledge and content do not require being pushed around by their subject matter; the view that experience is directly present to the subject in a way that is not causal; the view that there is no such thing as what it is like in the first place; and the views on which consciousness is not causally inert after all.

Please also place one idea of my own: that the remark is not caused by the feeling but is partly *constituted* by it, so there is no causal gap to bridge. I cannot tell whether that is a genuine option or whether it quietly collapses into one of the others; if the cell is unoccupied, tell me why. Likewise, flag any two leaves your tree cannot actually separate, and give a one-line diagnosis for each empty region of the space. No footnotes or reading suggestions — I have no library access, and I want your reasoning rather than someone else's.
