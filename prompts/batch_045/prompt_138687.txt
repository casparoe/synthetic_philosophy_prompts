I'm retired now — 35 years in an experimental physics group, mostly precision measurement — and in the last couple of years I've been sitting in on a decision-theory reading group at the local philosophy department, which is how I ended up with the following conviction. Take the original Allais pair. First you choose between a sure million and a gamble that pays a million on an 89% branch, five million on 10%, nothing on 1%. Then, separately, you choose between 11% at a million (nothing otherwise) and 10% at five million (nothing otherwise). Most people take the sure thing in the first problem and the five-million gamble in the second, and that combination contradicts the independence axiom. My view is that the combination is a mistake, full stop — not a different but equally respectable taste about risk. The analogy I keep coming back to from my old field is the Müller-Lyer illusion: the lines look unequal, the visual system insists on it no matter how many times you measure them, and you still don't conclude that for this perceiver the lines really are different lengths. Independence looks to me like bookkeeping of the same kind — like transitivity, or like requiring that the answer not depend on the units you measured in — and the fact that a stable majority of subjects violate it strikes me as evidence about the apparatus, not about rationality. I'm aware that whole families of theory (prospect theory and its descendants) drop independence and use weighted probabilities instead, and I'm not convinced they are thereby describing a malfunction.

Two things make me less sure than I sound. First, a variant I invented when I couldn't sleep: suppose both problems are implemented with literally the same physical randomiser — one decay, one number-generating event — so that the 89% branch of the first problem and the 89% branch of the second are the very same branch of the very same event. Then "you get the million on that branch either way" isn't a manner of speaking, it's a fact about the apparatus. My intuition gets noticeably stronger in that version, and its hard for me to see why, if independence is pure bookkeeping. Second, in the lab we never accepted a result from a single trial: if you ran someone through the pair five hundred times with real payoffs and the pattern persisted after feedback, I think I'd have to take that more seriously than one-shot questionnaires — though I haven't seen such a study and can't look one up, so treat it as a hypothetical.

So: what would count against me? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the ones a committed defender of independence would concede as counting against the view from the ones they'd be right to dismiss. Specifically: (i) is independence genuinely in the transitivity-and-unit-invariance category, a consistency condition a theory of rationality may insist on, or is it a substantive claim about tastes smuggled in under bookkeeping clothing, in which case I'm begging the question? (ii) Does my shared-randomiser version change anything, or is it the same choice described twice — and if it is the same choice, does the fact that the intuition intensifies tell us about the intuition rather than the choice? (iii) Is there really an exploitability argument here, or can a canny Allais-chooser simply decline the sequential game? (iv) Branch-wise: if you let the chooser commit separately in each branch, she ends up with the gamble she rejected — is that a reductio or a change of subject? (v) And am I insulated — if every departure from the axioms is by stipulation irrational, have I made my claim unfalsifiable, and is that a problem here the way it (arguably) isn't for transitivity?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — does my conviction survive, and how much of it? — then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question. Somewhere in there, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration would move you toward the "legitimate alternative taste" reading, and where your own argument is most likely to give. And flag anywhere my framing is confused; I'm new at this and I'd rather find out now.
