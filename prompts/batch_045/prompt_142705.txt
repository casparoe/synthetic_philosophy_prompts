I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I've got a weekend side project that's been bugging me for months. It's a little gridworld thing: an agent, a world simulator, and — this is the part that breaks my brain — the agent's own policy/predictor lives in the world state. So the world the agent is trying to model contains a readable copy of the thing doing the modelling. When the agent wants to do the normal "what happens if I take action A instead of B" move that basically all of RL quietly leans on, it has to predict what its own predictor will say it will do, inside a model of itself, inside a model of the world. Loop.

My hack so far: I replace the agent inside its own world model with a *stub* — a thing hardcoded to emit action A — and I treat the stub's output like an intervention coming from outside the system rather than something the model gets to predict. Then I only trust the resulting prediction if the stub's action ends up matching what the agent actually ends up doing (a fixed-point condition, sort of). Three failure modes, and I can't tell which one is the deep one: sometimes there's no fixed point at all; sometimes there seem to be several and I have no principled way to choose; sometimes there's exactly one and it's obviously the wrong answer.

I'd like a number, not "it depends on your decision theory" :)

The claim I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can: for an agent embedded in the environment it reasons about, there is no general-purpose, implementable decision rule that (1) always returns some answer without hanging or looping forever, (2) collapses to ordinary counterfactual reasoning on the easy cases where the environment doesn't depend on the agent's action at all, and (3) isn't arbitraty — i.e. doesn't have to just pick among several equally defensible answers by fiat. Call that E. My credence in E is around 0.5. A friend who reads way more of this stuff than me puts it at 0.85.

Give me your own number on E, but more importantly the decomposition. Here's the one I'd start from — fix it if you think it's wrong or if a term is missing:

- Is there even a determinate fact of the matter about "what would have happened if I'd done A" in the embedded case, or is the counterfactual just underdetermined — many mutually inconsistent-but-coherent edits of the world, no non-arbitrary way to pick one?
- If it is underdetermined: is there some principled extra ingredient that picks one out (treating the edit as an outside intervention vs. as a logical implication of the agent's own code, fixed-point selection, something else entirely) *and* still gives the right answer on the easy cases?
- If there is such an ingredient: can a bounded, computable agent actually find it, or does it hit a self-reference wall — needs a model of the world strictly bigger than itself, regress never bottoms out, that kind of thing?
- Separately: how much of this is in-principle vs. irrelevant in practice, because real environments model the agent only coarsely and approximations turn out to be fine?

Number plus one line of justification for each, then how you combine them (say so if you don't think they just multiply), which single term you're least confident about, and what argument or experiment would move that term the most. Also: between me at 0.5 and my friend at 0.85, which of those sub-questions is the disagreement most likely actually living in?

And please skip reading recommendations, paper names, who-first-said-it — I can't look anything up right now and I don't care about the bibliography. I care whether the stub idea is salvagable and exactly where it breaks.
