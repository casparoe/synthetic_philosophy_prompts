Something about the way the law treats our own future selves has been bugging me for a while, and I can't work out whether there's a coherent picture underneath it or whether it's just accumulated accident. Three things seem to be true at the same time in most rich countries:

1. Cooling-off. If a salesperson signs you up at your front door, or you buy something online, you get a short window — a couple of weeks in Europe as far as I remember, a few days for doorstep sales in the US — in which you can cancel for no reason at all, no explanation needed. And the bit that gets me: you can't waive it in advance. You can't sign something at the door saying "I've been told about the cooling-off period and I don't want it." That clause is just void.

2. Self-exclusion. If you tell a casino or a betting app that you want to be banned, they ban you, and as far as I can tell the ban sticks — you can't turn up the next evening, say you've definately changed your mind, and expect to be let in. In a bunch of places the operator gets in trouble if it lets you play, and if you do sneak back in and lose money you mostly can't get it back.

3. Ordinary contracts are just binding. No waiting room, no change of heart. And if you write yourself a real Ulysses deal about your own future behaviour — I'll go to the gym four times a week and if I don't I owe my brother 500 — a court won't enforce it against you. Not because they disapprove, they just won't do it.

So 1 and 2 both refuse to treat a moment of choice as final, but they push in opposite temporal directions: 1 protects the later self against the earlier one, 2 protects the earlier self against the later one, and 3 says the moment of choice is final after all.

What I want is the implicit theory of agency, or of a person over time, that this only makes sense under — and I don't want "it's paternalism" as the answer, that's just a label. Concretely:

- My guess is that it's a hot/cold hierarchy: the choice made in the calm, at-home, unpressured moment is the authoritative one, the choice made under pressure or in an aroused state gets discounted, and the law identifies which is which procedurally (where you were, how long you had, how much formality) rather than by looking at what you actually chose. Is that what the patchwork strictly presupposes, or is it just one story that fits nicely alongside others?

- Could you derive the whole thing without believing in akrasia or hyperbolic discounting at all? Cooling-off is easy to explain by what doorstep sellers *do* to people, and self-exclusion by the fact that a casino makes its money from your reversal. If that's enough, the theory of the self is idle here. Is there any piece of the patchwork that only makes sense if genuine diachronic weakness of will is real?

- Notice that in 1 and 2 the state regulates a counterparty — the seller has to refund you, the operator has to refuse you — and never punishes you. In 3 there's no counterparty and nothing happens at all. So does the practice really presuppose a view about the self, or only a view about relationships with people who profit from your hot moments?

- The selectivity is the part I keep circling. Self-binding is enforced for a short, morally loaded list, basically gambling. If the cold self were really sovereign, you'd expect a general register where any adult can bind their future self about anything — no new debt, no alcohol, no quitting the band — enforced against anyone. No jurisdiction has one, as far as I know. What does that selectivity presuppose? Would a consistent version of the implicit theory have to start judging the *content* of what people bind themselves to, and what would such a scheme have to look like?

- And the self-undermining bit: if people are present-biased, they're present-biased about signing up for protection too. Self-exclusion is opt-in; cooling-off isn't. Does the opt-in half of the patchwork defeat itself, and does that tell us the implicit theory is really about defaults and friction rather than about honouring anyone's authority?

If it helps to make it concrete, run your reconstruction on this: someone takes out a payday loan on their phone at 11pm after a genuinely bad day, and wants out at 9am the next morning. There's no cooling-off right for that. If the same person had clicked self-exclude on a betting app, they'd be protected. What does that difference in treatment presuppose, and is there a version of the theory that makes it come out right?

Tell me which features of the practice are doing the work, where it's philosophically underdetermined so that several theories would license it, and what would actually have to change in real institutions if a jurisdiction took the "cold self is sovereign" view seriously and applied it consistently — or took the opposite view, that every time-slice's choice counts equally.
