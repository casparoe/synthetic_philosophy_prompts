I have to run a ninety-minute evening session on the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism with a group of software engineers who read moral philosophy for pleasure but have no formal logic and no metaethics. Don't explain the argument to me; plan how I should get them to see it.

The frame I want to try, which you should tell me to drop if it's wrong: I want to present the problem as a failure of compositionality rather than as a worry about whether moral sentences can be true. On the expressivist picture a moral clause seems to have something like an effect where a semantic value is needed, and the trouble appears when the clause is embedded in a context that consumes values — the antecedent of a conditional, the scope of a negation, a question — so that what the clause does when asserted is not what is being done with it there. I'm hoping this audience grasps the shape of the problem faster that way than via the usual route.

Give me the order the pieces have to come in, and what depends on what. In particular: should the requirement that a solution assign the clause the same attitude in embedded and in unasserted occurrence be stated up front, as the standard any solution must meet, or does it only become intelligible once they have already felt the problem? And where does negation go relative to embedding, and must one precede the other?

One example should carry the whole session. My constraint on it: the embedded moral clause must not be paraphrasable as a report of the speaker's own present attitude, or the expressivist gets an easy out that stops the argument biting. What else must the example have, what must it lack, and where do I first use it and then reuse it?

Then the misconceptions, in the order I should expect them, each with the point in the sequence where I head it off. Mine are: that expressivism is the view that moral sentences are meaningless or merely emotive; that the difficulty is just that moral claims cannot be true; that the fix is simply to let moral clauses express attitudes and also be true; and that handling negation settles embedding. Correct whichever of those are wrong, and tell me which ones this audience will form because of the code analogy rather than despite it.

Finally: one question for the last ten minutes whose answer would show me the problem has landed rather than that they have memorised a slogan, and one thing I should openly refuse to go into at this level.

Be blunt where you are guessing. If the compositionality framing is confused, or the effect/value analogy will mislead this audience more than it helps, say so plainly instead of planning me a session around a bad idea. And mark the step you would most expect to fail in the room, with what you would fall back on.
