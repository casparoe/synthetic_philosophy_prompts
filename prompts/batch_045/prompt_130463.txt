I'm in a small reading group on truth and I've been the one defending the minimalist/deflationary line — the view that there's no substantive property of truth to investigate, that all there is to truth is the equivalence schema (<p> is true iff p, one instance per sentence), and that the point of having the word at all is expressive: it lets you generalize over infinitely many claims you couldn't list, endorse things you can't state, and so on. I felt fine about it until my friend Priya hit me with what she calls the cheap concepts argument, which I think proves way too much, and I've been going in circles for a week trying to find the exit. Her version, cleaned up a bit by me (so blame me for any sloppiness in it):

(1) Say a predicate is schema-trivial iff there's a biconditional schema, one instance per admissible substitution, with every instance knowable a priori, which lets you eliminate the predicate from that instance, and whose instances taken together exhaust everything there is to know about the predicate.
(2) Truth is schema-trivial: <p> is true iff p, for every p. That's exactly why truth isn't a substantive property with an interesting hidden nature.
(3) But so are a bunch of other things. Existence: x exists iff something is identical with x. Reference: for any singular term t, t refers to t (used, not mentioned). Property possession: x has the property of being F iff x is F. Meaning: the sentence s means that p, where p is what you get by disquoting s. None of these look any less trivial or any less a priori than the truth schema.
(4) If being schema-trivial settles that a concept isn't substantive, then existence, reference, property-possession and meaning aren't substantive either.
(5) That's false. Whether there are numbers, whether a name hooks onto its bearer by some causal-historical route or by a description, whether properties are universals or something else — these are real, contested, theory-laden questions, and nobody thinks they get answered by disquoting.
(6) So schema-triviality doesn't establish that truth is non-substantive; the main argument for deflationism is doing no work, or at least not the work I thought it was doing.
(7) So I should give up deflationism, or find it some completely different ground.
