Please take this seriously and think it through carefully before answering: I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who works on free will and moral responsibility, and I'd much rather have a slow, worked-out answer than a quick one. Start by brainstorming: before you commit to any prediction, set out in rough form the two or three different rules that someone could be running and still have given every one of the verdicts below, note what each of them would say about the new case, and only then tell me which one you think she is actually running and why. Show your working, and give the strongest version of whichever reading you end up rejecting. Please write in plain English, in British spelling, and please don't spend the answer telling me what these cases are called in the literature or who invented them, or give me a reading list — I can look that up, and the labels don't settle anything. What I want is reasoning.

A word about who is asking, so you can pitch it right. I left school at eighteen and have worked in insurance claims ever since. For three years I've been going to a small Sunday-morning reading group — six or seven of us, no one with any philosophy training — convened by a retired actuary called Marianne. She reads almost nothing on these topics on purpose (she says it would spoil it) and thinks everything out from scratch, but she is very careful and has twice changed her mind publicly when someone showed her that a verdict didn't sit with an earlier one. Over three years she has written down her verdicts on a series of cases. I've kept the list.

The old principle — that a person can't be blamed for what they did unless they could have done otherwise — she rejected early and has never gone back on. Her verdicts, in the order she gave them:

1. Ines is driving the getaway car. Her accomplice has a box wired into the steering, keyed to a signal in her brain that fires a moment before she is aware of deciding anything and before she makes any movement. If the signal had shown she was going to do anything other than turn left at the junction, the box would have taken the car over and turned left anyway. Ines turns left because she wants to. As it was set, there was nothing — not a twitch, not a hesitation, not even an attempt — that she could have done differently. Verdict: to blame. She wrote: "the box was only ever a shadow of her will — it was going to do whatever she decided, so what happened was hers."

2. The same case with one change: the box had a hair of slack in it, so the one thing Ines could have done differently was twitch the little finger of her right hand a fraction of a second early, which would have set it off. Nothing else. Verdict: to blame.

3. The same case again, except that Ines does hesitate, the box fires, and steers the car left while she sits there with her hands in her lap. Verdict: not to blame. "Then it isn't her act at all."

4. Over two years a clinical hypnotherapist quietly conditions his brother-in-law Dan to want to ruin his sister's restaurant — suggestions under hypnosis, small rewards, a grievance built up a grain at a time. Dan then reports her to the council for something he knows isn't true. He doesn't hesitate, he's pleased with himself afterwards, and he would have done exactly the same if he'd never met the hypnotherapist. Verdict: not to blame. "His will was made up for him. It doesn't matter that he's happy with it now."

5. Suppose (it's only a supposition) that everything anyone does follows from the state of the world a million years ago together with the laws of nature. A hospital administrator falsifies waiting-list figures after weighing up the reasons for and against, and does it for those reasons. Verdict: to blame. "I've never seen why that should make any difference. The question is the same in every case: were these her own reasons at work, or was she pushed?"

6. A man with a gun tells a cashier to open the safe or he'll shoot her. No one can help her, there's no time to do anything clever, and she opens it. Verdict: not to blame. "A shadow leaves your will alone. A gun at your head replaces it."

7. Moira decides to burn her neighbour's shed, strikes match after match in the wind, gets nothing to catch, gives up and goes home. Nothing burns. Verdict: "to blame for trying. There was no fire, so there's nothing else to blame her for."

The new case, which we're discussing next Sunday:

Priya has a grudge against the owner of a disused mill on the edge of town. At five to eleven at night she drives over and sets fire to it herself, on her own, for her own reasons, having thought about it for a week. There is nothing wrong with her: no device, no threat, no conditioning, no illness, no desperation. She had as much control over her arms and over her decision as anyone reading this. What Priya didn't know is that the owner had already hired a firm to burn the mill at a quarter past eleven that same night, for the insurance. The firm were going to do it whatever Priya did or decided; if she had changed her mind in the car and driven home, the mill would still have burned that night, a quarter of an hour later. Priya had no way of knowing about the firm and no way of reaching them. Her fire simply got there first, and the firm's foreman, seeing the blaze from the road, turned round and went home.

What I want from you:

(a) What do you expect Marianne to say — is Priya to blame for the mill burning down, and separately, is she to blame for what she set out to do? Give the verdict you think most likely on each, in her terms.

(b) Set out the candidate rules that fit her seven verdicts, say exactly what each predicts on the new case (including on the two questions separately, if they come apart), and explain where each one gets its support from her list — particularly from the things she actually wrote in 1, 5 and 6.

(c) Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and be honest about how much of the record is doing real work and how much is decoration. If you think two of her existing verdicts are already in tension with each other, say so.

(d) Name the one further case — described concretely, in the same style — that would best tell the surviving rules apart, and say what each rule would say about it.

(e) Finally: if you think her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say which way you'd advise her to go in order to stay consistent with herself, which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analogue, and what principle she would be committing herself to with each choice.
