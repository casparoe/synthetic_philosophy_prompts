I'm a high school senior in a philosophy elective, and we recently read part of Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality — the essay where he argues that our concept of guilt grew out of the much older and more material relation between a creditor and a debtor, and that punishment began not as a response to blameworthiness but as a kind of payment: the creditor was entitled to compensation, and if he could not be paid in money, he could be paid in the suffering of the person who owed him. I found the story hard to assess in the abstract, so I want to test it against something concrete.

Here is what I keep going back and forth about. In American law, if you cannot pay what you owe, you can usually file for bankruptcy and have most of those debts discharged; the whole institution is framed as giving you a fresh start, and the black marks on your credit report stop counting after a set number of years. But if you break the criminal law, you cannot discharge your sentence by paying for it, you cannot have it erased just because you have reformed, and the record of it can follow you into job applications indefinitely. And yet the two systems are not separated as cleanly as that contrast suggests: minor offenses are routinely punished with nothing but a fine, so at the bottom end money really does buy you out; people who cannot pay their fines or fees can be hauled back in front of a judge; and courts order offenders to pay restitution to victims on top of any prison time, which looks very much like an invoice for the harm.

What I want is the implicit theory behind this bundle. Does the split between the two tracks presuppose that owing money and owing punishment are different in kind — one a price that can be settled and released, the other a moral stain that has to be endured — or only that they differ in how much is at stake? And is the mixture in the middle a sign that the practice is incoherent, or just a sign that it is doing more than one job at once?

My own thought is that the difference may not be about kinds of owing at all, but about who holds the claim. When you owe money, you owe it to some particular bank or person who could in principle release you; when you break the criminal law, you owe something to the state or to society, and there is nobody in particular who is authorized to forgive it. If that is right, then the interesting question is what the practice presupposes about the state as a creditor — what sort of thing the state has to be, such that it can be owed and yet never release. But I am not sure that is even the right level at which to describe what is going on, or whether it just relocates the puzzle one step back.

So where does Nietzsche's story actually fit? I can see a reading on which the modern split is just what his genealogy predicts: the community has taken over the creditor's role, and because the community can never be fully repaid and never consents to settle, punishment stops looking like a price and starts looking like something of its own kind. I can also see a reading on which the practice is evidence against him, since we still treat a great deal of wrongdoing as payable in money. And there is a further claim of his, roughly, that punishment grows milder as a community becomes richer and more secure, because the offender starts to look too small to be worth the trouble — which sits oddly beside the fact that the United States is both very wealthy and, by any measure I know of, unusually willing to lock people up.

Concretely, then: reconstruct the theory of guilt, debt, and punishment that this pattern of practices only really makes sense under; tell me which parts of it the practice strictly requires and which parts it merely happens to fit; say whether a Nietzschean genealogy explains the pattern better than the alternatives or only redescribes it in new vocabulary; and tell me what would actually have to change in law and in ordinary attitudes if we took the genealogical picture seriously — would we end up settling crimes the way we settle debts? Please write your answer in plain American English, and please do not pad it with citations, bibliographies, or reading suggestions; I want the argument itself.
