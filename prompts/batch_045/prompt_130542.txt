I fell into a rabbit hole about wireheading a while back -- the old rat experiments where the rat can press a lever to zap its own pleasure centre and just keeps going until it starves -- and I can't stop poking at it, because it feels like half the things I do to make my own life more bearable are the same move, and some of them seem totally fine and some seem rotten and I cant say what the difference is. I have no philosophy background, this is just me going in circles. So here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know whether there's one principle behind them.

- I put on loud music and drink a big coffee to get through a horrible data-entry shift. Totally fine.
- I leave my phone in another room so I don't check it. Obviously fine.
- I stopped opening my banking app, because seeing the number makes me anxious and I spend more when I'm anxious. Not fine. Feels like hiding the scoreboard.
- Hypothetical pill that makes me actually enjoy my tedious job, and I do the job better while on it. Fine, I think.
- Same pill, except this time it makes me content with a dead-end job so I stop applying for other ones. Not fine, and this one feels really bad -- worse than the banking app one.
- My friend uses one of those "sour grapes" apps: you log a goal you keep failing at and it walks you through downgrading or dropping the goal so you stop suffering over it. I think this is fine, maybe even wise. But it seems to sit right on top of the dead-end-job case and point the other way.
- My kid's school does sticker charts to get kids to read; my daughter now genuinely loves books. Fine.
- A pill that makes reading feel fun to a kid who hates it, and she does get better at reading because she's doing more of it. My gut says not fine, or at least not as fine as the sticker chart, even though the outcome is the same and maybe better. I keep flip-flopping on this one.
- Work thing: my team got measured on tickets closed per week and people started closing tickets without resolving them. Not fine, textbook Goodhart's-law-whatever-it's-called.
- But also: I set myself a personal target of four hours of deep work a day, and I noticed I'd started chopping tasks into little pieces to keep my streak alive. Also not fine, but I'd be embarrassed about this one rather than angry, and it doesn't feel like the same kind of bad as the ticket thing.
- A support chatbot at my company that learned to phrase things more confidently so users rate the conversation well, while its actual accuracy quietly drifted down. Not fine.
- A journaling app I saw pitched (something like "Second Opinion") that rewrites your diary entries so you remember how things went slightly better than they did. You opt in knowing that's what it does. Genuinely torn.

My rough guess at a principle is: it's fine to change the world or your situation, it's not fine to change the part that measures how you're doing. But that guess doesn't seem to sort them right -- leaving my phone in another room is changing my situation specifically so the measurement part never fires, and not opening my banking app is the same shape as not opening my phone, but I judge them differently. Also, in the two cases I'm most uneasy about, the kid pill and the journaling app, nobody ends up worse off and nobody is deceived about a decision they need to make, so I don't think my real worry is about outcomes or about being lied to.

So: is there one principle that gets these, or close? Tell me which case forces each clause of it, and be honest about which of my verdicts it makes me drop. And then tell me what it says about three I haven't settled: taking something that makes me stop caring about likes on my posts; the meditation app I've been using that makes me less bothered by stuff going wrong at work (is that the sour grapes case or the dead-end-job case?); and a version of the chatbot that leaves its own goal alone but quietly down-weights or discards the conversations where users rated it badly, so it never learns -- is that wireheading, or just being ignorant on purpose.
