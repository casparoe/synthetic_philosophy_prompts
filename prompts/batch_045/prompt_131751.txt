A friend of mine falls back on one analogy whenever the direction of time comes up, and he gets a great deal of mileage out of it. The thermodynamic arrow, he says, is just a deck of cards being shuffled. Riffle a fresh deck and it drifts from ordered toward scrambled; keep going and it stays scrambled. No rule of moving cards around prefers scrambling to ordering — there are simply astronomically more scrambled arrangements than ordered ones — so what looks like a one-way street is only the deck wandering away from the unusual place it started. From this he draws three conclusions: first, that the arrow is not a dynamical asymmetry in the laws at all but a fact about where the universe started, a boundary condition rather than a tendency; second, that the direction we call the past is perspectival — stand in the middle of a long shuffle and you would say "it used to be neater" in both temporal directions, so our own arrow is an artifact of which slope we happen to be standing on; third, that entropy is only ever disorder relative to what someone bothers to track, so any arrow built on it says at least as much about us as about the world.

I have been turning the analogy over and it now seems to me to do less work than he supposes, but I cannot say precisely where it gives. Three things I noticed:

If you replace the riffle with a perfect faro shuffle — the kind where the two halves interleave exactly — eight out-shuffles return a 52-card deck to the order it began in; with the other convention it takes 52. The deck spends those steps looking progressively more mixed and then snaps back. So the scrambling the analogy needs is not a property of moving cards around in general; it has to come from a particular kind of repeated operation. Whether the physical case supplies one looks like the whole question rather than something the analogy gets to assume for free.

Second: I handed the same deck to two people at once. One likes it sorted by suit and rank. The other has a codebook assigning a digit to each card, and for her the prized arrangements are the handful that spell out the first fifty-two digits of pi. The fresh-deck order is, for her, entirely undistinguished, while a state my first friend would call maximally scrambled may be exactly her target. Watching a single shuffle, they disagree about whether the deck is becoming more or less arranged: one sees order decaying, the other sees it accumulating. It is not obvious to me which of them, if either, is tracking the quantity that is supposed to line up with time's direction.

Third: in his telling the deck is shuffled by hands, in a room, by someone who remembers opening a sealed deck earlier — an arrangement, a factory, an agent and an arrow, all supplied from outside the deck itself. I do not know what any of those correspond to, or whether the analogy can do without them.

Please audit the analogy. Give me the table of correspondences explicitly — cards, arrangement, order, scrambled, shuffle, shuffler, sealed deck, factory, perhaps the number fifty-two — noting for each whether the mapping does real work or is decoration. Then separate the disanalogies that actually bite from those harmless for his purposes, and say which of his three conclusions survives once the biting ones are on the table. In particular: is the faro case a genuine counterexample or a sideshow; does the codebook disagreement show the arrow is observer-relative, or only that my description of the deck was incomplete; and does the smuggled-in shuffler make the explanation circular? If a better analogy would carry the same conclusions, say what it is and what it repairs; if the analogy was carrying a conclusion it cannot support, say which one.
