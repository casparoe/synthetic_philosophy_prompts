I've been chewing on Sen's old impossibility result — no Paretian liberal: minimal rights over your own personal sphere, plus Pareto, plus some weak rationality condition on the social ranking, can't all hold at once. The illustration is the two readers and the dirty book: option A is the prude reads it, B is the other guy reads it, C is nobody reads it. The prude ranks C > A > B (nobody reads is best; but if someone must, better him than have the other guy corrupted). The other guy ranks A > B > C (he most wants the prude to read it and loosen up, then wants to read it himself, and likes nobody-reading least). Pareto then gives A > B socially, the prude's own-sphere right gives C > A, the other guy's own-sphere right gives B > C, and you've got a cycle.

Here's roughly where I've landed after thinking about it, and im not sure it hangs together:

1. Welfarism about social betterness: whether one outcome is better for a group than another is a function of how well off the individuals are. Nothing else.
2. Rights really can trump: it can be wrong to violate someone's protected sphere even when the rights-violating outcome is better for absolutely everyone and everyone also prefers it.
3. The result isn't an artifact of modelling rights as a social preference ordering, and the "rights are side constraints on choice, not entries in a ranking" reply doesnt dissolve it for me — because any real institution, law, or ML system trained on human feedback still has to take a profile of individual rankings and produce one output, and the same clash should reappear at that level.
4. But I also half think the real action in the book case is that the preferences are meddlesome — each guy cares what the other one reads — and that if preferences ranged only over people's own private experiences the cycle would never get started. Which would make the theorem really a result about external preferences in a rights costume.
5. My working patch, which I've been calling sphere sequencing: aggregate first over the non-personal dimensions of the options while holding each person's protected sphere fixed; then let each person's own-sphere ranking break whatever is left; and only if it still cycles, fall back on comparing welfare magnitudes. On the book case this hands each guy the decision about his own reading and gives zero weight to either one's distaste for what the other does.
6. I'd rather restrict the domain of admissible preference profiles than give up either Pareto or minimal rights — but I also think most domain restrictions in social choice are unprincipled gerrymanders built to deliver a result someone wanted anyway.
7. I dont buy the "accept cycles / accept an incomplete social relation" response either. If you're designing anything actual, you need a rule that always delivers something.

So: which pairs of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? My own guesses are that 1 and 5 are in trouble together (throwing away meddlesome preferences looks straightforwardly anti-welfarist, but maybe I'm wrong about that) and that 3 and 4 can't both be right — but I genuinely don't know which way to jump.
