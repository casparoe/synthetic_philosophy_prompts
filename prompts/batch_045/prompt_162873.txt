I'm in the final year of my PhD and I'm the teaching assistant for our department's upper-level undergraduate metaphysics seminar — 24 students, most of whom have taken one earlier metaphysics course plus a single survey chapter on grounding. So they know the slogans (that grounding is metaphysical determination rather than causation, that the fundamental is the ungrounded, that grounding is irreflexive and transitive and plausibly asymmetric) but essentially nothing beyond them. The professor has handed me one essay question for the final exam: 45 minutes, written in a blue book, worth 25 marks. I want a question that cannot be passed by reproducing those slogans neatly, and that separates the two or three students who have actually thought about the structure of the view from the many who have memorised its vocabulary.

The crux I keep circling back to comes out of my own notes rather than the readings. I've been arguing that "ungrounded" and "fundamental" can come apart, and I use two bits of homemade terminology that I'd like the question to put to work. First, I distinguish *core* ungrounded items — the ones on which the rest of reality depends, the places where chains of ground terminate — from *residue*: items that are ungrounded but which nothing depends on, which ground nothing and are grounded in nothing, and which sit outside the direction of metaphysical explanation altogether. Second, I call the claim that reality contains no residue *No Idlers*, and I've been toying with the idea that fundamentality had better be defined as core-membership rather than as mere ungroundedness. What makes this more than a terminological quibble is that nothing my students have read actually secures the equation "fundamental = ungrounded": they have been told that ground bottoms out, but nothing rules out structures in which chains descend forever, or in which some items fall outside the grounding relation's domain entirely (necessary truths, or abstracta, if grounding relates only contingent concrete matters), and they have also met the suggestion that facts about what grounds what might themselves be ungrounded. In each of those cases the equation seems to return a verdict that is either trivial or wrong, and I want students to have to say which it is, and why.

So I'd like you to draft the question and what a genuinely excellent answer to it looks like. Please work in the voice of a professional metaphysician who has taught grounding for years and who treats the biconditional "x is fundamental iff x is ungrounded" as a substantive assumption in need of argument rather than a definition. Take a moment to think through where exactly the difficulty lies before you start writing — I'd rather have one sharp question than three serviceable ones. Use the following sections, in this order:

1. **Brainstorm** — about 80 words: the two or three candidate cruxes you considered and why you settled on the one you did.
2. **The question** — 120–160 words, phrased exactly as it would appear on the exam paper, including any case or stipulation the students would need to be given. You may use my core/residue vocabulary, but the question should also be answerable by a student who finds that vocabulary unhelpful and wants to state the point in their own terms.
3. **What it is testing** — about 100 words: the single thing a student must see that no amount of slogan-recitation will supply.
4. **Model answer** — 650–800 words: what an excellent student would plausibly write in 45 minutes.
5. **Marking scheme** — 25 marks total, divided into five named points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on the specific error or omission that should cost a student those marks. The scheme should reward students who notice that the biconditional is doing real work and penalise those who merely restate it.
6. **A borderline B answer** — about 120 words: what a competent student who has mastered the vocabulary but missed the crux would write, and why it stops short of an A.

One constraint: please don't build the question or the model answer around citations, or around recalling who said what. Nothing in either should depend on the literature beyond that one survey chapter my students have read.
