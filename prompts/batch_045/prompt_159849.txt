My film club is doing It's a Wonderful Life next month and I'm the one stuck leading the discussion, and I want to use the bank run scene to talk about socially constructed kinds, money in particular. The idea I keep coming back to is what I'd call a run-able kind: one whose entire basis is ongoing reciprocal acceptance, so it can come apart all at once when that acceptance wobbles, as opposed to something like race or gender where the relevant uptake is way more diffuse, habitual, slow-moving, and isn't going to dissolve because a crowd panics in a lobby. Is that a real distinction or am I forcing it, and where exactly does money sit on it? The other thing I want to get at is the flip side of the scene: George doesn't win by arguing people back into accepting the institution, he hands over actual cash and then the pledges and telegrams start arriving — which makes me think the film is pointing at what I'd call acceptance infrastructure, the physical tokens, ledgers and public gestures that prop a shaky kind up, as opposed to acceptance being just a matter of what everybody believes. Can you read the scene that way without flattening it into a cartoon of status functions and constitutive rules, and is George's speech secretly a credit-theory-of-money argument? Mostly I want to know whether the movie shows something about social kinds that the theory gets wrong or misses, and which two or three moments I should point the group to.
