hey — I need help settling an argument I keep having with my roommate, who is very into whole brain emulation twitter.

His go-to move is the software/hardware analogy. The same program runs on a Mac and on a PC, he says, and I can emulate an old games console on my laptop, so the program clearly isn't the machine; therefore the mind isn't the brain either, and a detailed enough simulation of your neural functional organization running on a laptop is you, same mental life, same consciousness. He then pushes a corollary I find harder to shake: speed doesn't matter. A computation is a sequence of formal steps, not seconds, so if you run the emulation a billion times slower, over centuries, it's still you in there, living the same life from the inside — it just looks frozen to us. He uses the whole package to argue that anyone holding out for "non-computational" facts about the brain is doing mysticism.

Can you audit the analogy properly? I want:

1. A correspondence table (8-10 rows, don't pad it) — source code, compiler, machine code, instruction set, OS, CPU, emulator, input/output, the user: what does each of these map onto in the mind/brain case, and flag any cell where the mapping is forced or where the source side has something the target has no candidate for at all.
2. The disanalogies, separated into the ones that actually threaten his conclusion and the ones that are just noise. The ones I'm suspicious of: computers are designed to be indifferent to what they compute (a CPU is a universal machine on purpose) whereas neurons weren't designed for anything; "the software" only exists relative to a chosen level of abstraction, so multiple realizability looks either trivial or question-begging depending on where you cut; brains are analog-ish and noisy, so there may be no discrete state to port; and the identity/copying thing — two runs of the same program on two machines are the same program, so if the mind is the software, the analogy makes making a thousand of you sound like the most boring operation in the world.
3. Whether the slow-emulation corollary survives. My own stab at a counter-analogy: a conversation is substrate-independent — phone, letters, smoke signals, same conversation — but a conversation with a thousand-year gap between each line isn't a conversation at all. Does that go anywhere, or is it a different kind of case?
4. Whether there's a better analogy for what the functionalist is actually claiming, and whether that one does the work. Also: does the analogy hold up in the "whole country of people with walkie-talkies wired up to instantiate one functional organization" case, and whichever way it goes there, does that help or hurt him?

Be committed about it, please — don't just lay out the options. Tell me where you think the argument actually breaks, which premise you'd reject, whether you buy the slow-emulation thing, and which parts of your answer are doing real work vs. which parts are the analogy carrying the weight. And mark the places where you're going out on a limb or where reasonable people definately disagree with you, so I know what I'm quoting at him.
