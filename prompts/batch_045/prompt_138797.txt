I'm a PhD student running a 75-minute session for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on infinity and time: about 16 students around a seminar table, a projector, and whatever I can carry in myself. I can get hold of a laptop, a small microcontroller board with an LED, and a metronome app on my phone, but I would rather have a version that needs none of that.

The session is on Thomson's lamp — a lamp switched on at half a minute, off at three quarters, on again, and so on, with the switches crowding up against the one-minute mark. I want students to feel the standard moral rather than hear it: that the story as told does not determine what is true at one minute, and that every argument for a determinate answer is quietly importing an extra principle the story never supplied — a limit or continuity principle, or a claim about the parity of an infinite sequence of toggles, or a principle saying that what holds throughout every interval before the limit carries over to the limit itself.

The twist I want to build in, which I have not seen done: the participants actually run a truncated version of the supertask, halving the switching interval until it drops below the response time of whatever is doing the switching, and discover that the lamp does something the story has no vocabulary for — it stops resolving into on or off and settles into a third condition the description never mentions (a dim steady glow, a jammed switch, whatever the apparatus really does). The point I am after is that incompleteness can surface as an outcome the case's own terms cannot express, not merely as our ignorance about which of two permitted states obtains. I want that to land before anyone says indeterminate or underdetermined.

Please give me: the setup and rules; materials with a low-tech fallback that keeps the same punch; a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes; a short scored wager on the final state, no real money, whose payoffs expose that the group's confidence is not tracking anything in the case; and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific argumentative moves, including the rescues students will attempt for a determinate answer and what each one costs. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — hardware failure, the strongest student naming the moral in the first five minutes, the truncated run being taken for the real thing — and how to handle each. Be explicit about which part of the philosophical point the activity cannot stage and which I will have to supply in discussion.

On confidence: mark which design choices you are guessing at and which you are confident in, and be blunt if you think the hardware moment is a gimmick that will bury the point, or if the third-state framing misrepresents what is at issue. If the request rests on a confusion — if you think the indeterminacy reading is wrong, or that this setup trades on a bad picture of what a limit is — say so plainly instead of designing something polite around it.
