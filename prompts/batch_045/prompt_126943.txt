I am writing my third novel — a realist novel set in and around a family firm of chartered surveyors in a cathedral city in the West Midlands (Lichfield, though I am calling it something else) — and I have come to a standstill over a scene which I want to be, quietly, an argument about hypocrisy. At a reception after a funeral, two characters blame a third, Marcus, for the same act: he has used the firm's client book and the trust that comes with the name to set himself up with a competitor, letting his family believe almost to the last moment that he meant to stay. One of the two blamers is to be a hypocrite in the fullest sense — someone who has done precisely this himself, has never acknowledged it, and is at this very moment quietly doing a version of it again; no repentance, no reformation, no dawning self-knowledge and no prior punishment, since those are the easy ways out and I do not want any of them. The other blamer is to be a woman who is personally immaculate with respect to the norm in question — she has never done anything of the kind and is not remotely tempted by it — and who is yet to be someone the reader feels has no right to say a word.

What I need from you is a design for that scene, together with a demonstration that the design works. Please build it so that the hypocrite has standing to blame and the scrupulous woman does not, and so that both verdicts fall out of a relational account of standing — the "you of all people", fair-play sort of view, on which standing is a matter of the blamer's normative position: what she is owed, what role she occupies, what she has waived, licensed, or made herself party to — rather than of her record of compliance with the norm she invokes. Say which version of that view you are using and why it suits my novel. The second blamer's failure of standing must also be structural rather than merely personal: not that she is a stranger to the wrong, not that she secretly does the same thing, not that she is cold or envious or insincere, but something about her position with regard to this very token of wrongdoing — that she authorised it, induced it, is profiting from it in a way she will not disgorge, or disclaimed in advance any stake in it. And I should like everything else held fixed: one act, one target, one scene, both blames delivered to Marcus's face or within his hearing, and no character permitted to use the words "standing", "hypocrisy" or "tu quoque".

Then do the checks. First, run the scene under the account you have chosen and show me the verdict for each blamer and the reason for it. Second, isolate the feature doing the work in each case and ablate it: strip the hypocrite of whatever gives him his position and show that his standing collapses; strip the woman of her authorisation, inducement, profit or disclaimer and show that hers is restored; and tell me whether these turn out to be one feature seen from two sides or two quite different ones. Third, tell me honestly what a theorist who holds that blame is simply an appraisal — that hypocrisy is a separate vice and standing is never lost — would say about my scene, and whether the scene is any embarrassment to that theorist or merely irrelevant to them. Finally, a novelist's constraint: the reader must be able to feel the asymmetry without being told it, so please write the scene itself as well as the analysis — dialogue and a little stage business, no longer than a page — and add one closing sentence on which of the two blamers the reader should end up disliking more. I should be grateful if you would set the whole of your answer in British English.
