I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy or theoretical CS beyond the usual undergraduate algorithms sequence, and I recently read a long blog post — by a machine-learning engineer whose writing I otherwise trust — on what it called "the perfect theory of induction." I've been repeating some of its claims to colleagues and would like an accuracy audit before I keep doing that. Here is the passage as I remember it; I'm paraphrasing slightly but I believe I've kept every substantive claim intact:

"Solomonoff induction is the mathematically ideal form of Occam's razor. You enumerate every possible hypothesis — every computer program that could have generated your observations — and assign each one a prior probability of 2 to the minus its length in bits, so that shorter programs are exponentially more likely a priori. The length of the shortest program that reproduces everything you have seen is that data's Kolmogorov complexity, which is more or less what gzip is estimating when it compresses a file. Since the shortest such program is the simplest explanation of the data, Solomonoff induction amounts to the rule: always prefer the simplest hypothesis consistent with the data. Solomonoff proved that this predictor is optimal — for any computable stream of observations, its total expected error is smaller than that of any other prediction method whatever, so in the long run it beats every alternative. The catch is that Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable: no algorithm can find the shortest description of arbitrary data. So Solomonoff induction cannot actually be run, and remains a limit case. In practice we settle for approximations like minimum description length, and really every machine-learning technique is just a rough approximation to it — which is why the models that compress their training data best are the ones that generalize best."

Two things made me suspicious, though I can't tell whether they are real problems or just gaps in my own background. First, if someone really had proved that one predictor has strictly lower expected error than every possible alternative, that seems like the kind of result that would have ended decades of argument about whether simplicity is truth-conducive, and my impression is that it didn't. Second, the slide from "Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable" to "so every learning algorithm is an approximation to Solomonoff induction" feels like it might be trading on two different senses of "approximation."

Could you work through the passage claim by claim and tell me:

1. What someone who actually works in algorithmic information theory or inductive inference would accept as written.
2. What is a fair popular-level simplification — something a specialist would phrase differently or qualify, but would not object to in context.
3. What is straightforwardly wrong, and in particular whether "always prefer the simplest hypothesis consistent with the data" is really what Solomonoff induction does, or whether it describes a different, related method that the passage is conflating with it.
4. Whether the optimality claim, once stated in its correct form, is strong enough to carry the blog's closing move about compression and generalization — or whether that conclusion would need premises the passage never supplies.

If it's useful, close with a short rewrite of the passage that would still be readable by an engineer but wouldn't make a specialist wince, and note what you had to change to get there.
