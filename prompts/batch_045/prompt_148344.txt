I'm finishing a dissertation chapter on emergence and inter-theoretic reduction, and I'm due to present it at a small workshop in about three weeks, so before I commit to the structure I'd like an audit of the package of views I've ended up holding. I have a feeling it doesn't quite cohere, but I can't locate where. Roughly, I accept: (1) the multiple-realizability argument against type-type identity for special-science kinds, while also thinking that the derivational model of reduction — bridge laws plus deduction of the higher-level generalizations from the lower-level theory — is the right account of what reduction is, so that a successful reduction need not be an identity; (2) the causal exclusion argument, in the form that says that once you grant the causal completeness of the microphysical domain and you don't want systematic overdetermination, a distinct higher-level property has no causal work of its own left to do, and I take this to be a real argument rather than a verbal trick; (3) non-reductive physicalism about the special sciences, defended via causal inheritance, or what some people call program explanation: a higher-level property is causally relevant in virtue of its instances being realized by configurations that have the relevant causal powers, which is why I'm happy to say both that a recession causes bankruptcies and that the rigidity of a wheel explains why the axle does not sink; (4) an account of emergence on which a property is emergent just in case it supervenes on the base facts, is not derivable from them even in principle, and has novel causal powers, where the novelty is supposed to be captured by downward causation understood as constraint rather than as energy injection — the shape of a ratchet-and-pawl device, say, selects which molecular trajectories are available without adding anything to the micro-dynamics; (5) the further claim that reduction, even where it succeeds completely, does not make higher-level explanation dispensable, because explanation is interest-relative and a derivation of the macro generalization from the base laws need not answer the question we were actually asking; (6) a refinement of the non-derivability condition in (4) that I've been leaning on and that I suspect is doing more work in the chapter than I've admitted to myself, namely that when the only route from the base rules to the macro generalization is to run the system — the generalization is entailed by the rules and in that sense determined by them, but cannot be extracted from them by any shorter route, so that the macro-level law is accessible only simulation-wise — that is already enough for a robust, non-merely-epistemic kind of emergence; and (7) the quietist thought that being nothing over and above the base goings-on does not entail being unreal, which I use to answer the charge that non-reductive physicalism is an unstable halfway house. The case I keep returning to for (6) is a little grid economy I built myself: a few thousand agents, each trading only with its immediate neighbours, with a price pattern that stabilizes and then persists even when I rewire the neighbourhoods. The stabilization is, I think, simulation-bound in exactly the sense I described, and I want to say both that the price pattern is emergent and that it genuinely constrains what any individual agent will accept in a trade.
