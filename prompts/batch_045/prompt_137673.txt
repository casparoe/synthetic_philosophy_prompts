I spent my career in condensed-matter physics, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a reading group in the philosophy department — mostly as the person who keeps asking what the words mean. We have just finished a popular book on self-reference and consciousness (I would rather not name it; the author is a capable writer and I may simply be misreading her), and her chapter on "tangled hierarchies" left me unsure how much of it to believe. In my own field one moves between levels of description constantly — microscopic dynamics, coarse-grained hydrodynamics, effective field theories — and nobody treats it as paradoxical when the behaviour at one level constrains the description at another; the renormalization group is, one might say, a machinery for travelling up and down levels and coming back to where one started. So when the book makes a great deal of the claim that certain hierarchies are "tangled" and that some level in them must remain "inviolate," I cannot tell whether I am being offered a substantive thesis about self-referential systems or something that is either banal or false.

Paraphrasing rather than quoting, and trying to be fair to her, the chapter runs roughly as follows. A tangled hierarchy is what you get when the levels of a system stop being neatly stacked and begin looping back into one another, so that climbing steadily "upward" through the levels returns you to where you began. Escher's lithograph of two hands drawing each other is the emblem of this: neither hand is the real one, the picture is a visual contradiction — a flat image of an arrangement that could not exist in three dimensions — and it is really just the liar paradox done in ink. Hofstadter's claim, on her account, is that every hierarchical system, whether a formal system of logic, a computer, or a brain, needs one level that nothing else in the system can get at: the inviolate level. A system can never turn its own rules on that level, because doing so would require stepping outside into a meta-level, and then outside that, and so on without end; systems that try to dispense with an inviolate level by letting a level talk about itself fall into tangled hierarchies and breed paradox. Gödel, she says, showed that any formal system rich enough for arithmetic must therefore either fall into inconsistency or acknowledge a level it cannot itself formalize. She then applies all this to the mind: the self is a loop of symbols referring to themselves, Gödel showed that such loops cannot be captured from the inside, and so no machine running on fixed rules could ever have a self — which is why consciousness, she concludes, cannot be a computational phenomenon.

Something in me resists that last step, and I also cannot shake the feeling that the "inviolate level" is being described in a way that makes it a tautology about meta-languages rather than a claim about which level in a concrete system happens to be exempt from the system's own rules. One of our group, a retired electronics engineer, said the whole business is trivial — a compiler that compiles its own source is a loop with nothing paradoxical about it — and the person leading the discussion replied that this just shows the engineer has not understood Gödel; I would rather not take sides on the basis of my ignorance. So I would like an accuracy audit: take the chapter's claims one at a time and tell me which a sympathetic but careful reader of Hofstadter would accept, which are tolerable popularizations, and which are genuine misreadings — in particular whether the drawing of the hands is well described as a contradiction rather than as something weaker, whether "inviolate level" is being used for the thing it names or has quietly been turned into a remark about meta-levels, whether two different results about formal systems are being run together, and whether the conclusion about machines and selves follows from whatever survives. Please argue from what you know; I have no interest in a reading list or in chasing down page references, and I would rather have your reasons than citations.
