I'm taking an aesthetics seminar this term and two people in my study group, Lena and Owen, got into a proper argument after class the other day. They asked me to referee and I honestly cant tell who's winning.

We'd been talking about whether you can come to know that something is beautiful just from being told. They ended up arguing about a concrete case: Owen's friend Nate, whose taste lines up with his on basically everything, texts him "the new sound installation in the old warehouse space is incredible, drop everything and go." Owen hasn't been. Can Nate's message give Owen knowledge (or justified belief) that the piece is good?

(1) Lena: no. Aesthetic judgements are essentially tied to first-hand experience of the thing; a belief you picked up from a text message floats free of the object. At most the message gives Owen a prediction about his own future experience, and a prediction isn't knowledge of the work's value. Knowing you'll like it and knowing it's good are different achievements, and only the first is available second-hand.

(2) Owen: that distinction does less work than you think. Everything that matters here hangs on the prediction — that's what decides whether I spend my Saturday on it. And if testimony really can't transmit any aesthetic warrant, then critics, teachers and friends are all in the business of handing over nothing, yet we plainly treat a good critic as telling us about the work, not merely about herself.

(3) Lena: the critic case actually supports me. A useful critic doesn't just issue a verdict; she points at features — the way the bass never resolves, the clumsy shift in register — and then you go look for them yourself. Testimony earns its keep by redirecting attention, and once you attend, your judgement is first-hand again. The problem is specifically with bare verdict-testimony: "it's amazing, trust me." That's the bit that can't be handed over.

(4) Owen: ok, but run it in reverse. Say I go and I love it, and then everyone whose eye I trust tells me it's derivative garbage. I'd downgrade my view. But if my own first-hand verdict can be rationally overturned by other people's say-so, then testimony does real epistemic work here — my experience isn't self-certifying in the way your view needs it to be.

(5) Lena: downgrading just shows you treat them as better detectors, not that you borrowed their judgement. You revise, and the revised verdict is still yours and still rests on your own look at the thing. Nothing gets transmitted.

(6) Owen: new case. There's an app that's been trained on thousands of my ratings and reliably predicts how I'll react to new work — better than I predict myself, since my own first reactions are noisy (I listened to an album exhausted once, hated it, and now its one of my favourites). The app tells me this installation is a masterpiece. Two wrinkles: it isn't a person, so there's no attention-redirecting story available, and its better calibrated to my sensibility than my own off-day experience is. If the sensible thing is to trust the app over how the piece initially strikes me, hasn't first-hand acquaintance lost its privilege? And isn't that just verdict-testimony doing genuine work?

(7) Lena: it's a very good predictor of your reactions, and fine-grained reliability about your own responses is exactly the mark of prediction rather than appraisal. Wanting first-hand experience isn't a bet on reliability; it's that responding to the work is partly what the judgement is made of. You can be more reliable than yourself about what you'll feel and still not have made up your mind about the work.

(8) Owen: last thing — if bare verdicts can't transmit anything, then two people who've both seen the piece and disagree about it have nothing to trade either, and most of what an arts education consists in is exactly those verdicts. That's a big cost. (Lena shrugged and said she'd pay it, but that it's smaller than it sounds because attention is trainable.)

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about which specific point, rating each move somewhere from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly saying why? And then say who's more right overall, and what — if anything — both of them are missing. Please don't just tell me what the official literature says or who to read; I want the arguments themselves adjudicated. No biblio needed, we've got a reading list already.
