I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm applying for a nine-month fellowship to develop a magazine piece into a short book on cliodynamics: the project of modelling long-run history quantitatively and forecasting from it. The centrepiece of that literature is the secular cycle — a two- or three-century oscillation in population, state fiscal health, and elite competition, with elite overproduction (roughly: too many aspirants chasing too few elite positions) offered as the mechanism that drives the disintegrative phase.

My half-formed idea, which I would like sharpened rather than flattered: the public argument is stuck on whether the forecasts work. The modellers point to a call, made roughly a decade ahead, that the United States would see escalating political instability in the 2020s; historians reply that a model with an adjustable cycle length, phase, trend and threshold can be fitted to almost any stretch of the past, and that a discipline whose episodes are centuries long has almost no independent cases to test on. My suspicion is that both sides are largely right, because they want the model's output to do different jobs, and I want to press that by splitting predictive success into three grades: a date-bearing point prediction; an ordinal or phase claim (we are in the disintegrative phase, and things get worse before they get better, with no date attached); and a conditional structural claim (given this much elite overproduction and fiscal strain, instability follows within a generation or two, the time constant left open). My guess is that nearly everything actually asserted in this literature is of the second or third grade, that the standards of evidence appropriate to each differ sharply, and that the genuinely interesting residue of disagreement sits with the third.

Two further worries should be built into the proposal rather than set aside. First, the evidence base: the sample is a handful of long episodes in historically connected societies rather than independent trials, and the modern stretch of data most often used to check these models comes from a world of industrial growth, mass literacy and welfare states, so any out-of-sample test is also out-of-regime. Second, the audience problem: forecasts of instability are read by investors, risk analysts, think tanks and governments, and can be self-fulfilling or self-defeating in a way weather forecasts are not, with no possibility of calibration across repeated trials. What standard of evidence is the right one for an object whose target system reads the forecast, and does the existence of that audience raise or lower the bar?

Please write me the one-page proposal for the fellowship committee. It should state the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions — celebratory forecasting on one side, humanist scepticism about quantifying history on the other — leave it open; give a method month by month across nine months, saying what I would actually do rather than what I would read (I would want something like a small independent replication of one secular-cycle fit, a pre-registered scorecard of the field's live forecasts with indicators a reader could check in a few years, and interviews on both sides); say what the deliverable is and who it is for, given that it is a book for general readers and not a journal article; and end with what would count as the project failing, plus the most likely way its scope blows past nine months. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I will fill those in myself — and do not rest anything on titles or dates; work from the arguments, positions and cases as you know them.

Give me the proposal as exactly five numbered items, in that order — question, why it is open, method, deliverable, failure conditions and scope — one or two sentences each, with no introductory line and no closing line.
