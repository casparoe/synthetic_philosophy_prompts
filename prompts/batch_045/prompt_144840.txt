I am designing a seventy-five-minute session on escalation of commitment for about thirty mid-career managers in an executive-education program. They will have encountered the sunk cost fallacy in popular books, they will be quick to name it, and most of them will have a failing project of their own in mind that they would like the session to vindicate or to excuse. My aim is narrower than giving them a label: I want them to leave able to look at a live situation and tell whether it has the structure that makes each next step forward-looking and sensible while the sequence of such steps is ruinous.

The case I intend to build the session around is the classic dollar auction: a one-dollar note is auctioned in fixed small increments, the highest bidder receives the note, and both the highest and the second-highest bidder forfeit their final bids.

Do not write the session for me. Give me the plan for building it.

First, the order in which the pieces have to arrive and what depends on what. In particular: must the arithmetic of a single escalation decision — what a bidder gains and loses by making one more bid rather than stopping — come before the phrase sunk cost is allowed into the room, or is it better to let the misdiagnosis surface first and then dismantle it? Justify the ordering against the alternative.

Second, the carrying case. Should the auction itself carry the whole session, or should it be reached through something smaller and more familiar first? Say what the carrying example must have and must lack. I am especially unsure whether to actually run the auction live, with real money, in front of the group: tell me whether to, at what point, with what stake, and how the debrief would have to be built so that the person who escalates is not simply embarrassed.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order this audience will form them, and the exact point at which each should be headed off:

- that no rational person would pay more than a dollar for a dollar — and the harder point that a bidder who is high at a dollar and has just been outbid by ten cents does better to bid a dollar twenty than to stop;
- that this is therefore an instance of sunk cost reasoning, when the escalation decision as such is prospective, and the further swing of the pendulum into "so it was rational all along";
- that the fix is simply to fix a limit in advance, without asking what such a limit does to the behaviour of the other side;
- that every real escalation has the auction's structure, when many involve a prize whose value is unknown and re-estimable as you go.

I have taken to calling the structural feature — you forfeit everything already committed if you stop — the quit penalty. Tell me whether that is the right thing to name, and where in the sequence to name it.

Fourth, the check. My candidate is a variant in which the runner-up has forty per cent of their final bid returned, which lowers the cost of stopping without touching anything already spent; I also have a coarse-increment variant, where the minimum raise is half a dollar. Say which of these should be the closing question, what the sunk cost account and the quit-penalty account each predict for it, what an answer that shows real understanding looks like, and what an answer that merely sounds fluent looks like.

Finally, what I should openly decline to explain at this level and say I am declining — the full equilibrium analysis of the game, the experimental literature, or anything else you think belongs in that category.
