I have been living with Du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur for about two years and have been slowly turning it into a first-personal doctrine of my own, partly as a private exercise and partly because a small reading group has asked me to present it. Before I do, I want an audit of the commitments as a set. I am not asking whether they are faithful to her text, and I do not need citations or a reading list; the question is whether the package I have assembled is internally stable, and what the repairs would cost if it is not.

Here is where I have landed.

(a) Moment-totalism. Happiness is not a condition a person is in. A life goes well to the extent that it contains pleasant moments, and there is no further fact about the unity, shape, or narrative of a life that bears on how happy it is, over and above the number, intensity, and duration of the moments themselves.

(b) Idiosyncrasy. What pleases is settled by individual temperament rather than by anything universal, so self-knowledge is the primary instrument and no one is well placed to tell another person what will make them happy. I also reject any ranking of pleasures as higher or lower in kind. Temperament sets a rough ceiling on what is available to a person, which is part of why I distrust comparisons of happiness across people.

(c) The dominant passion. The passions are not to be extirpated, nor merely moderated one at a time; the soul needs one governing passion, and the promising candidates are those whose exercise is within our own power — study above all, and the love of the glory that attaches to it.

(d) Exercise-pleasures and outcome-pleasures. I distinguish pleasures whose object is the exercise of a capacity (working something out, making something, understanding something) from pleasures whose object is a state of the world (being chosen, being loved in return, having the book succeed). I hold that a happy life is one weighted toward the former, and I want to insist that this is not the qualitative ranking that (b) forbids: it is a claim about reliability and about what lies within one's own control, not a claim about worth.

(e) Anticipation and memory. Expecting a pleasure and recalling one are themselves pleasant moments, so a single pleasure can be had more than once, and one should cultivate savouring forward and re-reading one's past. I lean on one further stipulation here: the pleasure of savouring is confirmation-dependent, in that it decays unless it is periodically refreshed by evidence that the thing is genuinely coming.

(f) Virtue. Being virtuous is a necessary condition of happiness, in large part because we are dependent on the good opinion of others and cannot be at ease in our own eyes without deserving to be.

(g) Undeceivedness. Happiness presupposes freedom from prejudice and self-deception. Concretely: I would refuse a harmless pill that produced stable, unshakable, and never-unmasked false beliefs about how my life was going, even if I were persuaded that it would raise the total of pleasant moments and that nothing would ever undo it.

(h) Two verdicts on cases. First, the friend who gives six evenings a week for six years to training for a career with perhaps a one-in-twenty chance of succeeding is making a bad bet, and not merely because the odds are long. Second, the friend who takes a modest, honestly advertised job with a predictable routine and builds her evenings around a handful of durable private pursuits is doing the right thing, even granted that nothing much will ever come of those pursuits.

What I want is the audit rather than a tutorial. Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is, ordered by severity rather than presented as a flat list. For each real tension, say exactly what would have to be revised and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set — in particular whether (a) survives, since I take it to be load-bearing. Then tell me which pairs merely look tense and why the appearance dissolves, because I suspect at least one of the things that bothers me is a false alarm and I would rather not amputate a commitment unnecessarily. Finally: identify the single revision that buys the most consistency at the least cost, tell me if any item in the list is doing no work at all, and tell me what I should call the position I end up in — whether after the cheapest repairs I am still recognizably a hedonist of the sort I started as, or whether the repairs have quietly turned me into something that needs a different name.
