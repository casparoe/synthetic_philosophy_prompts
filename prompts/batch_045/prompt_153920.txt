Two friends of mine spent most of a dinner arguing about Newcomb's problem and got nowhere. I was there but stayed out of it, and neither would concede anything, so I'd like a careful verdict on the arguments as they actually made them.

The case they had in front of them: an opaque box containing either $1,000,000 or nothing, a transparent box containing $1,000, you may take the opaque box alone or both boxes, a predictor that has been right about 90% of previous subjects has already made its prediction and filled the opaque box accordingly, and there is no backward causation.

Their exchange, as close to verbatim as I can manage:

1. Marcus: The box is already filled. If it is full, taking both gets me $1,001,000 rather than $1,000,000; if it is empty, taking both gets me $1,000 rather than nothing. Either way I come out $1,000 ahead, and nothing I do now can change what is inside. So: two boxes.

2. Priya: That is a dominance argument, and dominance requires the states to be independent of the acts. Here they are not: the box is full with probability about 0.9 given that I one-box and about 0.1 given that I two-box. Expected value comes to roughly $900,000 for one-boxing against roughly $101,000 for two-boxing. The dominance move quietly assumes the very point at issue.

3. Marcus: All that shows is that you are treating your own action as evidence about a fixed fact and then paying for good news. That is exactly what is wrong with evidential decision theory in the Smoking Lesion case: it tells you to pass up a pleasant smoke, not because smoking harms you but because reaching for a cigarette would be bad news about your genes. Newcomb has the same shape — a common cause, your disposition, produces both the prediction and the act. If paying for good news is irrational there, it is irrational here.

4. Priya: The shapes are not the same. In the Lesion case the correlation is a mere symptom and it can be screened off — a genetic test, or simply knowing your own tastes, makes the act uninformative. In Newcomb nothing screens the correlation off, because the predictor is accurate in virtue of modelling the very procedure I am running now. So the dependence that matters is subjunctive rather than causal: if my procedure were to output one-box, the model would too. That is why one-boxing is not merely buying good news.

5. Marcus: If the model and I are running the same procedure, there is no possible case in which the outputs differ, so that is not a difference-making counterfactual at all — it is the correlation in a subjunctive costume. And my view has no trouble recommending one-boxing where it counts: at the point where you choose which decision procedure to run, or whether to install a commitment device before the prediction is made, one-boxing is causally better. So this case does not settle anything between the theories at the level that matters; it only shows that a causalist should want to be a one-boxer.

6. Priya: That retreat is unstable. A theory that tells you to acquire a disposition whose exercise it then calls irrational is self-undermining, and anyway the manoeuvre is unavailable to the person standing at the boxes, who cannot step outside their own deliberating and adopt a procedure — particularly if the prediction covered this very reasoning. You have also now conceded the practical question: the causalist's real advice turns out to be: be the sort of person who one-boxes.

At two separate points, both of them said that how the predictor achieves its 90% — simulation, brain scan, actuarial model — makes no difference, and that only the accuracy rate matters.

Please adjudicate the six moves one at a time: for each, say whether it is right, partly right, or wrong, and why, rather than just announcing an overall winner. Then say what you take both of them to be missing — including whether their shared assumption about the prediction mechanism is doing work that neither of them notices, and whether there is something about the accuracy figure itself that both have overlooked.
