I'm writing a magazine piece on the idea that some contradictions might be true, and I keep tripping over the same thing in my notes: roughly half my sources talk about paraconsistant logic and dialetheism as if they were one and the same commitment, and the other half insist they're completely independant. I need this straight before I file.

So: compare the claim that some contradictions are true with the claim that the inference from a contradiction to an arbitrary conclusion isn't generally valid. Which is the deeper commitment, if either is? Can you hold the second without the first — contradictions never actually obtain, but logic shouldn't detonate if one ever turned up in your data — and if you can, what do you and the dialetheist actually disagree about? Please also flag where the popular line that paraconsistent logic simply rejects the law of non-contradiction goes wrong, because I strongly suspect that's a howler and I don't want to print it.

One more thing, and it's the bit I really care about. A philosopher I interviewed drew a distinction I haven't come across in print, and I want to know whether it holds up. Call it damage-control paraconsistency: you weaken the logic so that inconsistent information — legal codes, databases, a scientific theory halfway through a crisis — doesn't trash everything, whilst still insisting that no contradiction is true. Call it endorsement paraconsistency: you weaken the logic because you think some contradictions really do obtain, and classical logic would smear that fact. Her claim was that only the second is a genuinely revisionary metaphysical view, and that it's much harder to defend, because you owe actual examples — and the usual candidates (a sentence saying of itself that it isn't true, the borderline cases in a sorites argument, the self-referential set-theoretic paradoxes) don't all behave the same way. Is she right that they pull in different directions, or is that a distinction without a difference? And does the damage-control version quietly collapse into the other one once you ask what the models of a weaker logic are even meant to represent — ways the world could be, or just ways a body of information can be messy?
