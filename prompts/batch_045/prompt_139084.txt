I am writing a chapter on paradoxes of legal self-reference, and I have committed myself in print to the view that a self-entrenched amendment rule cannot be repealed in breach of its own terms. Before I argue that, I want the strongest available case for the opposite conclusion, worked out on a concrete charter rather than in the abstract. Please write it. Argue as though you held the position: no hedging, no final paragraph conceding that the other side is also respectable, no concessions you are not forced to make. Give me the argument I would least like to have to answer.

The charter is this:

§1 (Amendment). The Assembly may amend this Charter by a two-thirds vote of its members.

§2 (Entrenchment). Section 1 may be amended only by a unanimous vote of the Assembly. An amendment of Section 1 enacted by a lesser majority is void. This section may not be repealed by implication.

§3 (Review). The Court may declare an amendment void only on a petition filed within sixty days after the Clerk issues a certificate for it.

§4 (Certificate). The Clerk shall certify the text of every amendment and the vote by which it was carried; the certificate is conclusive evidence that the amendment was enacted in the manner this Charter requires.

§5 (Severability). If part of an amendment is void, the remainder stands, provided the Assembly would have enacted the remainder without the void part.

The facts are these. The Assembly has one hundred seats. In 2011 it carried Amendment A by 68 votes to 31, with one abstention. A reads: Section 2 is repealed. The Charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of its members. The Clerk issued a certificate recording the vote as 68 to 31 and stating that A was duly enacted. No petition was filed within sixty days. In 2013 the Assembly carried Amendment B by 71 votes to 27: Section 1 is amended to read: the Assembly may amend this Charter by a simple majority of its members. Again a certificate issued, and again no petition within sixty days. In 2015 the Assembly carried Amendment C by 53 votes to 46, imposing a tax on land values. A taxpayer has challenged the tax, and the only question is whether the simple-majority route was open in 2015, which turns entirely on whether A and B are law.

Argue that the tax is valid.

Three constraints on how you argue it. First, be explicit about which ground you occupy. There is a substantive ground: A was valid when enacted, so B was validly enacted, so C was. There are also procedural grounds: the certificates are conclusive under §4, and the sixty days have run under §3. These are independent, and resting the case on the procedural grounds alone concedes that A and B were void when enacted, which is precisely the thesis I hold; if that is all you have, you have not given me what I asked for. Make the substantive argument first and in full, and then say plainly whether you are also relying on the procedural grounds and what they add.

Second, take a settled position on the distinction I think the case turns on. Call a change to a rule textual when it alters the words in which the rule is stated, and modal when it leaves the words as they are but alters what can subsequently be done under the rule, that is, which future amendments are available and by what route. Amendment A left the words of §1 untouched and re-enacted them verbatim; what it changed was §1's modal profile. Amendment B changed its words. So the question is what §2 protects when it requires unanimity for an amendment of Section 1: the words of §1, or the powers §1 confers. Choose, and explain why the reading you reject is not the one the charter should bear; or, if you think the distinction collapses once it is pressed, say so and supply whatever does the work instead. What you may not do is shift readings between A and B. And tell me what you make of §5: if the repealing sentence in A were void, could the rest of A stand on its own, and would that help or hurt you?

Third, answer the two objections I find strongest. The first: A was in substance an amendment of §1, because it changed the conditions under which §1 can be amended, and a unanimity requirement that any two-thirds majority may delete at will is no requirement at all, so §2 must be read to reach it. The second: an amendment that is void is not voidable but a nullity; there is nothing there for a certificate to be conclusive about and nothing there for a limitation period to run against, so the Court in 2015 is free, and indeed obliged, to disregard A and B however the Clerk described them.

Close by naming the single sentence of the charter on which your argument most depends, and by saying what becomes of your conclusion if that sentence is read the other way. Argue from the text of the charter and from general considerations about what it is for a rule to exist, to be amended, and to be void. I do not want citations to decided cases or to the secondary literature; assume there are none on point, and treat the charter and the facts above as the entire record.
