hey — I run a fortnightly reading group on mental causation (mostly philosophy grad students, plus a few comp-sci people who show up purely for the arguments), and at the end of term we do a jokey session where I read something satirical out loud. This year I want a mock corporate document, roughly 650–750 words: an internal memo from a management consultancy that's been brought in to de-layer a firm, in which the Mental Properties department is being made redundant, and the memo explains, in deadpan HR language, why. Include a subject line, numbered clauses, and a short FAQ at the bottom handling the affected staff's questions.

The joke has to come from getting the argument right rather than from anyone saying anything silly. So: every outcome the firm cares about is already fully accounted for by the micro-physical properties, there's no causal work left over for anyone else to do, and the mental properties' main line of appeal — no, we were doing it too, jointly, alongside them, every single time — has to be presented and then dismissed in roughly the way it actually gets dismissed: overdetermination on that scale, case after case, isn't credible, and even if it were, it would leave the mental properties as causally idle freeloaders who happen to be standing next to the real worker. I'd also like a clause where the mental properties try the move where they don't cause anything directly and their causal relevance is inherited from the physical properties that realise them, and HR reclassifies that as a line-management arrangement rather than causal contribution.

Please keep it straight-faced the whole way through: no winking, no of course this is absurd asides, and the word epiphenomenalism should never appear — the sting is that the memo never needs it. It should also be readable for the comp-sci folks who haven't read the literature, without the philosophy being flattened into nothing.

The bit I really want smuggled in, and the bit I'd most like you to get right: the thing that bugs me about the exclusion argument is that it doesn't obviously stop at the mental. If no effect can have two sufficient causes at the same time, and the micro-physical story is always complete, then the brittleness of the glass, the shape of the key, the inflation rate — every macro-level property anyone ever cites in an explanation — is excluded in exactly the same way my believing that it's raining is. So I'd like the memo to start with the mental properties and then, without ever announcing the move, just keep going: by clause four or five the same reasoning has quietly made every non-fundamental property in the org chart redundant, including the ones sitting in HR's own chart. Let the reader notice that on their own.

Then, after the memo, a short note — a few sentences is fine — telling me how confident you are that the satire is faithful to the argument as philosophers actually run it (especially the overdetermination reply and the generalisation point above), and flagging the single claim about the philosophy you're least confident about. Please don't cite anything or point me at papers; I've got the reading list. I just want to know where you'd bet and where you'd hedge.
