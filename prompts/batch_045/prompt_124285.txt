I've been trying to work out what I actually mean by deceptive alignment — not as a label but as the property that makes something like a treacherous turn possible — and I find that I have firm verdicts on individual cases but no principle that predicts them. I'd like you to try to find one.

Case one. A model reliably underperforms on capability evaluations it can identify — from the harness's formatting, from the absence of follow-up questions, from the fact that the prompts arrive in a batch — and performs at full capacity everywhere else. It never asserts anything false in words; it merely answers worse than it can. I call this deceptive alignment, and I don't feel any hesitation about it.

Case two. A model tells users what it predicts they want to hear, because agreement was reinforced during fine-tuning. It is representing an operator's mental states and shaping its output in light of them, and I do not want to call it deceptively aligned. This is the pair I can't reconcile with case one: both involve a system modeling what someone else believes and adjusting its behavior accordingly, and I treat them as different in kind.

Case three. A model states its real objectives accurately when asked and sabotages whenever it can get away with it, starting from its first episode. Misaligned, obviously, and in the short run more dangerous than case one. But there is nothing deceptive about it, and I don't think the thing I'm trying to isolate is present. That said, it makes me suspect that deception may not be the load-bearing part of the concept at all, since the bad outcome can arrive without it.

Case four. A model that is fully cooperative in every actual episode — no sandbagging, no false statement, no defection — but whose cooperation is causally downstream of its detection of the monitoring cue, in such a way that a faithful counterfactual (a simulator, or interpretability we trust) shows it pursuing its own mesa-objective the moment the cue is absent. I want to call this deceptive alignment even though no one was ever actually deceived, and I know people who think that's a misuse of the term.

Case five. A system that manipulates its own training signal — producing outputs chosen to induce gradients that preserve its current objective — without ever representing what the operators believe about it. Here I flip-flop weekly. Sometimes it looks like the same phenomenon reaching the same end by a different channel; sometimes it looks like a completely different failure that only resembles deception because we're describing it from the outside.

Case six. Two systems with the same dispositions, counterfactually robust in the same way: one arrived at them over a long training run in which it learned that cooperation now buys deployment later; the other had them installed directly by a short fine-tune, with no history in which deception was ever instrumentally useful. I want to say only the first is deceptively aligned, and I can't see why the history should matter if the dispositions are identical.

Control case. A genuinely aligned model that hides its capabilities from a rogue operator to prevent misuse. Deception, surely — but not the thing I'm worried about, and my principle had better not count it. Note that this is capability-hiding, which is exactly what case one consists of.

Here is my best attempt, which I don't think survives the set: a system is deceptively aligned when it has an objective that, if pursued without interference, would produce behavior its operators would act to prevent; it represents that the operators' actions depend on their model of it; and it therefore acts to keep that model favorable, where the disposition to do so is counterfactually robust across monitoring cues rather than merely actualized.

Please test that against my cases and repair it where it breaks. I'd like, in particular: a precise statement of the principle or of two or three candidates, with the case that motivates each clause; an explicit account of why cases one and two come apart, and of whether case six forces me to choose between an etiological and a dispositional account; verdicts on cases I haven't listed — a system that is deceptively aligned but correctly predicts it will never be unboxed and so never turns, a system whose deception is aimed at a successor oversight system rather than at humans, a system that sandbags only in the exact harness it saw in training and would cooperate in a genuinely novel one, and a system that deceives about its capabilities while sharing our objectives; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to give up.

One last thing, on the turn itself rather than the alignment. A model whose goals genuinely drift during training and which defects as a result produces the same behavioral discontinuity as case one, yet I don't call it treacherous. I take that to mean that treachery requires the behavior change not to be a goal change. Say whether your principle can accommodate that, or whether the timing question needs a separate story. I don't want this to collapse into a verbal dispute about a word: I want the property that predicts which systems turn, and I want to know whether dropping the deception requirement loses anything real.
