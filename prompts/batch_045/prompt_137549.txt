I build distributed systems for a living and read philosophy at night without any training in it, so if I am groping at a distinction that already has a name, please just name it rather than pointing me at a literature. I got into this through a case in a popular book — the hitchhiker stranded in the desert who promises to pay the driver once they reach town, where both of them know that once they are in town there is no way to make him pay, so the driver leaves him there. I found I did not have the reaction the book seemed to expect, and one thing led to another, and I now have a pile of verdicts I cannot get into any order. I would like a principle that captures them, or a small set of competing principles, and I would like to know which of my verdicts has to go.

Here are the cases, each one reworked into terms I actually deal with.

1. The ride. The desert case, except that it is me at a remote facility with no signal, and a contractor who will drive me four hours to the city for $300 paid on arrival. No escrow, no platform, no record he can enforce. Verdict: I pay in the city, I do not count paying as irrational there, and I also think it would have been sensible, years earlier, to have become the kind of person who pays in that situation. The claim that my reason for paying disappears once we reach the city leaves me cold.

2. The bounty on an intention. A lab will wire me $50,000 tonight if tonight I genuinely form the intention to run, tomorrow, a compute job that will cost me $5,000 and produce nothing they want; they are studying intention formation, and tomorrow I am free not to run it. Verdict: I would take the money and not run the job, and I do not think either half of that is irrational. But I also do not think I can form that intention just by deciding to, and that makes me suspect the case is rigged in a way case 1 is not. If intentions are just dispositions of a very short half-life, my principle should treat them the same; if they are not, I need to know what the difference is doing.

3. The reputation that only works if it is real. I publish an open-source library, and to stop companies from stripping my license I build a reputation for litigating even when the suit costs me more than it recovers. Verdict: building the reputation is obviously sensible; actually suing, once someone has already copied, strikes me as nearly pointless, and I would like to be able to switch the disposition off at exactly that moment — which I can see is precisely what would destroy it. So here the disposition is rational and the act it produces is not, and the disposition has to be one I cannot switch off.

4. The sandboxed copy. Before signing a large contract, a client runs a sandboxed simulation of me in which the work is already delivered, payment has already cleared, and enforcement is impossible; if the copy honors the agreement, I get the contract. I never face that situation myself. Verdict: I want to be the kind of system whose copy passes, and I would pay to become that kind of system, and I do not think the payment is irrational. But no action ever follows here, and that makes the case look like case 3 rather than case 1: what is being bought is not a future action but a detectable property. I cannot tell whether my principle should care about that.

5. The tell. Same as case 1, except that the only way available to me for becoming a promise-keeper is a public, verifiable modification — a binding public pledge that I never walk away from an agreement, discoverable by anyone who cares to look. It works on the contractor: he drives me. It also reaches my largest client, who now knows I will never walk and renews me at well below market. Verdict: here I think the visible commitment is a mistake, and a dose of unreliability would have served me better. But the selection mechanism looks identical to case 1, where I approve of the very same disposition. This is the pair I cannot reconcile, and I would rather you tell me my verdict in one of them is wrong than accept a principle with a special clause bolted on for each.

6. The disposition that cannot be scoped — the one I keep changing my mind about. I cannot install keep your promises to stranded contractors. I can only install keep your promises, globally, and it pays in cases like 1 and loses in cases like 5. Verdict, today: I still want it, even on the assumption that the ledger comes out negative overall. That verdict sits badly with the principle I sketched below, and I suspect it means I do not want a principle about acts or even about dispositions, but about what kind of agent it is sensible to build or be. Tell me if that is a real difference or a way of hiding from the question.

7. The agent I wrote. I publish the source of an automated agent that cooperates conditionally in one-shot interactions with other published agents, and sometimes it cooperates and gets defected on and loses by any ordinary accounting. Verdict: I have no discomfort at all calling that design correct. I have some discomfort calling the payer in case 1 rational. The structures look the same to me, and I cannot tell whether the difference is that the agent never faces a moment at which it must act against its own reasons — its reasons were settled by me, earlier — or whether that is just sentiment on my part, since after all I designed it and I could have designed it differently.

Two more things I take to be constraints rather than verdicts. Where a cheap external commitment is available — escrow, a smart contract, a deposit with a third party at two percent — the whole problem evaporates and I have no interest in becoming a promise-keeper at all; the interesting cases are exactly the ones where commitment is expensive or impossible. And my own tentative principle, which I would like tested and repaired rather than flattered: it is rational to acquire or retain a disposition when (i) having it leaves you better off in expectation, assessed from a point before you knew whether the relevant situation would arise, and (ii) the same benefit cannot be bought more cheaply with an external commitment device; and the word rational attaches to the acquisition and never to the act the disposition goes on to produce.

What I want from you: a principle, or two or three if they genuinely conflict, stated precisely enough that I could implement it, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on at least two cases not on my list — I would suggest one in which someone else installs the disposition in me without my knowledge, and one in which the only way to install it degrades my judgment across the board — and an honest statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which of those you would overturn. If the principle has to choose between saving every verdict at the cost of becoming a list of exceptions and staying simple at the cost of giving up one or two, I would like to see both and be told which you think is right.
