I hold the following view about the epistemology of markets, and I want it stress-tested rather than defended.

The view: market prices are epistemically privileged aggregators of dispersed private information. When many agents trade on information I lack, the resulting price carries evidence about the world that I would not otherwise have, and my confidence about a proposition should move toward what the price implies unless I have a specific reason to think I am on the inside of that information. On this picture, the familiar limitative results about markets under asymmetric information describe boundary conditions rather than refutations. The no-trade result — that risk-neutral traders with a common prior, commonly known information partitions, no transaction costs, and no hedging or liquidity motive will not trade at all — shows only that trade requires some heterogeneity of priors, of risk exposure, of liquidity need, or of reasoning. And the companion paradox — that a price which fully revealed everything traders had paid to learn would destroy anyone's incentive to learn it, so that a fully revealing price cannot be an equilibrium — shows only that revelation must be partial. Neither, I claim, blocks the inference from price to fact.

Tell me what would in fact count against this.

1. Which observations or arguments would be decisive rather than merely awkward? I mean decisive in the strong sense: something whose truth would make the aggregation reading false, vacuous, or collapsed into a rival, rather than something that merely shows it needs a caveat. Separate the two classes explicitly, and say what each member of the second class actually costs the view.

2. What would a committed defender of the view concede as genuinely counting against it? I am not interested in what hostile critics allege the view is committed to; I want the concessions an advocate should make, including any that are usually suppressed.

3. Does the view predict anything its rivals do not? For instance, something about when volume should occur, how prices should respond to news arrivals, or how price informativeness should vary with the number and diversity of traders. If a rival reading — prices as the outcome of risk-sharing, liquidity provision, inventory control, or simply of noise — predicts something different, state the discriminating test and how clean it is.

4. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, with a sentence on why each lands where it does.

Three lines of objection I would personally find most persuasive, which I would like you to assess rather than simply ratify:

(a) The decision-market objection. Suppose a market is run precisely in order to settle a decision, and the participants have stakes in which decision gets taken, not merely in being right about the question traded on. Then each participant's payoff depends on the price through the decision it triggers, and an order is no longer a pure expression of belief. Does this undermine the reading of price as a belief aggregate in a way that generalizes — that is, does every market whose price feeds into outcomes that participants care about inherit the difficulty, and how much of the epistemic privilege survives once it does?

(b) The boundedness objection. The standard results assume agents who share a prior and can in principle compute what is common knowledge among them. Suppose instead that a large and growing share of trading is done by systems trained on overlapping data, with overlapping inductive biases and hard computational limits. Is the no-trade conclusion robust to arbitrarily small relaxations of common knowledge and of logical omniscience, or does it break discontinuously, the way coordination breaks down in examples where two parties exchange confirmations and common knowledge fails by an arbitrarily small amount? If it breaks discontinuously, what follows about how much the theorem ever actually constrained real markets?

(c) The scale objection. Purely informational motives should, on the theorem, produce no trade, and observed volume is enormous. If the residual after crediting risk-sharing and liquidity motives is best explained by noise, overconfidence, or differences of prior, is there a quantitative tension rather than merely a conceptual one — a question of whether the volume we observe can be reconciled with the amount of information prices appear to impound? Or is that residual itself the measurement that would settle the matter, and is it in fact measurable?

Finally, give an honest verdict on exposure. Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — for example by retreating to the claim that prices aggregate whatever information some agents happened to acquire, so that no possible observation could contradict it? If it has, say whether that insulation is a defect here in the way it would be in an empirical science, or whether a claim about how much weight one should give prices can legitimately be insulated from refutation, and at what price.
