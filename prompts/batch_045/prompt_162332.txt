I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am drafting a long-form essay on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism about well-being: the worry that the many things we call pleasures (sensory enjoyments, intellectual satisfactions, aesthetic raptures, even malicious delight) share no single felt quality, and so cannot all be the one intrinsic good that hedonism requires. I want to press a specific, somewhat technical solution. Please argue for the following position: the common feature of pleasures is not a distinctive felt quality, hedonic tone, or quale, but a structural property I will call recursive motivational luminosity. A state has this property when (a) it is broadly accessible to the motivational and decision-making systems of the subject, and those systems treat it as a reason to sustain the current activity or policy; (b) it is phenomenally transparent, in the sense that the subject is aware of the state itself as seeming worth continuing rather than merely inferring this from outward behavior; and (c) it is counterfactually stable, in the sense that the subject would still judge the state worth prolonging across a wide range of changes in its non-affective accompaniments, such as changes in sensory content, object, or surrounding belief. In your argument, explain why this property can be multiply realized across very different neural and phenomenal profiles, and why treating it as the essence of pleasure avoids collapsing hedonism into a desire-satisfaction theory or a purely cognitive judgment theory. Then address the objection that a state could have this property without feeling good, as in addiction or obsessive compulsion, and argue why such cases are not counterexamples but rather cases in which the luminosity is fragmented, defeated, or fails to be suitably integrated with the broader motivational economy of the subject. Please write in a rigorous but accessible register, and do not refer to specific authors, papers, or scholarly citations.
