I spent last night arguing with a friend who does macroeconomic forecasting about whether the outside view has anything to say about how long a political order will last. She says it is base-rate fetishism: the cases are too few, too differently caused, and too transformed by changes in technology and administration for any historical frequency to be evidence, and that a forecast which is not a story about a mechanism is not a forecast at all. I agreed with more of that than I wanted to, but I now have to settle the question in a practical form. I run a small team that produces about a dozen forecasts a year for a foundation, almost all of them about the durability of states, unions, occupation arrangements, and sanctions coalitions, and nearly all of them currently written as stories about why this case is different. I want a procedure, not a position paper.

The procedure has to be run in about half a working day per forecast by people with graduate training in history and political science and no formal statistics beyond a spreadsheet, and it has to survive review by a senior colleague who thinks the whole approach is numerology. Give me the steps in the order we would actually run them, what counts as an adequate answer at each step, where the procedure stops and returns no usable number, and where it hands the question back to a person because the remaining issue is one of values or of definition rather than fact.

Three things I want built into it, because they were the cruxes of last night's argument. First, a sealed reference class rule: a case may enter the class only if we could have named it at the forecast date from information available then, and only if we did not learn about it in the first place because of how it turned out; alongside it, a backtest protocol in which we re-run the whole procedure on past forecasts whose outcomes we mask from ourselves and record whether the base rate would have beaten our in-house story-driven judgments. Second, a stationarity check: split the class into sub-periods and refuse to quote a single base rate across a split where the frequency is unstable, unless we state explicitly which direction we think it drifts and why. Third, a rule for handling the claim that this case is unique, by requiring that it be restated as a predicate we could apply to historical cases without knowing their outcomes, and then actually applying it to the class and reporting what it would have selected.

The procedure also has to say how far the inside view is allowed to move the anchor once the base rate is fixed, what to do when the class has fewer than eight cases, what to do when the outcome itself is contested (what counts as the same polity persisting: continuity of territory, of the capacity to tax, of the ruling coalition, of the name), and how to handle forecasts that may affect the outcome they are about.

Please run it end to end on three of our live questions: whether a large continental federal union still pools fiscal authority in twenty-five years; how long a multilateral sanctions coalition holds together once the initial shock has passed; and the one where I expect to dislike the answer, which is our house view that a present great power's alliance network is qualitatively new and has no historical analogue. For that last one, tell me plainly if the procedure gives us nothing.

Finally: at the end of each major step, note how confident you are that following it would improve on what we do now, and single out the one step or judgment you are least sure about.
