I work at a mid-sized software company that reorganized last year into self-managing pods, and since a product launch went badly this spring our new Chief People Officer has fallen in with a consulting firm selling something called the We-Mode Operating System. The training deck is full of claims I half-remember from my philosophy degree, delivered without any of the care: that collective intentionality is a primitive feature of minds and cannot be reconstructed out of individual intentions plus mutual beliefs; that a genuine joint action requires the participants to be mutually responsive to one another's intentions, to be committed to the joint activity rather than merely to their own parts in it, and to stand ready to help one another out when things go wrong; that a joint commitment is not an aggregate of personal commitments, that it generates obligations no single party can dissolve alone, and that parties to it thereby constitute one plural subject that believes and acts as a unit; and that acting as a group member differs categorically from acting as a private individual, in a way you can slip out of by something like a mental toggle. None of this is stated carefully, but it is all clearly doing the work, and the operational conclusions being drawn from it — that no one can be credited or blamed individually for what a pod does, that exiting a joint activity is a matter for the group rather than the individual, and that subplans need only mesh rather than be complete — are exactly what you would get if someone took those views with total literalness and then wrote HR policy on top of them.

What I would like from you is a piece of satire I can circulate on our internal newsletter under a pseudonym: an internal memo from People Operations announcing the rollout of these policies, in deadpan corporate-compliance prose, roughly 700 to 800 words. The joke has to be in the content rather than in the vocabulary: I want colleagues who sat through the training to recognise that the memo is drawing something genuinely out of the view instead of caricaturing it, and I want anyone who has actually read the philosophical literature on joint action to wince in recognition. So whatever you do with the irreducibility claim should make trouble for the practice of writing individual performance reviews; whatever you do with joint commitment and the plural subject should turn unilateral resignation, or unilateral withdrawal from a project, into a discipline problem; and the requirements about mutual support and meshing subplans should generate a paperwork obligation that cannot in principle be satisfied, since subplans are meant to be filled in later rather than specified in advance. The reductio I am after is that a management doctrine built to take collective agency seriously ends up, with perfect seriousness, making individual accountability unstatable — and I would rather that land as something the reader notices than as something the memo argues. Please do not name any real philosopher or any real theory: attribute the framework only to the fictional consultancy, keep the technical vocabulary of the literature out of the memo's own voice, and invent the machinery it runs on — a certification, a scoring rubric, a preclearance form, a toggle in the HR system — rather than leaning on the jargon alone.

Two further requests. The piece should stay legible: a reader who has never opened a philosophy book should be able to tell that the memo is being ironised without the memo ever winking, so avoid any line that is too absurd to have come from a real People Ops department, and keep the target the doctrine and its operationalisation rather than any person, fictional or otherwise. And because this is going into a plain-text newsletter editor, I need continuous prose throughout: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic text, no tables, and no numbered or lettered clauses, with paragraph breaks only — if the memo wants a title or a To/From line, fold it into the first sentence instead. Invent whatever names, dates, and corporate voice you need.
