I'm a fourth-year PhD student defending a job-market chapter in three weeks, and what I actually need is Q&A prep, not help with the slides. The chapter defends a view I've been calling partitioned realism (I'm open to a better name — someone at a conference said it sounds like a theory about land ownership): cross-cultural variation in folk moral judgment is real, but it's structured, and the structure is metaethically load-bearing. Three claims. (1) The asymmetry: variation across samples is much larger for what I call communal-order items — purity, respect, loyalty, in-group obligation — than for welfare items, i.e. harm and reciprocity-flavored fairness, and the gap doesn't go away when you control for how the vignettes were translated and who ran them. (2) Most of the variation that's left over is weight divergence rather than content divergence: groups disagree about how much a given feature counts and where the threshold sits, not about which features count at all. (3) So objectivity is domain-restricted — something like robust realism is defensible for the welfare core and basically indefensible for the communal domain, where a constructivist story does better. I'm definitly most exposed on (2), because the coding of "same features, different weights" versus "different features" was done by two RAs and the inter-rater agreement was okay, not great.

The room is the hard part. It's a philosophy search committee, but the composition is: an experimental philosopher who runs online vignette studies and will want effect sizes, preregistration, and whether I ran any multiverse analysis; a visiting cultural psychologist who thinks the harm/purity split is Moral Foundations Theory arrived at fifteen years late and will ask what's new and why she should care about the philosophy; a metaethicist who's a fairly hard-line robust realist and thinks no survey result could possibly bear on whether moral facts exist, since that's a constitutive question about the metaphysics of normativity rather than an empirical one; and a political philosopher who works on global justice and is suspicious that "objectivity" talk in the welfare domain is a way of laundering parochial liberal intuitions into metaphysics. There's also usually an anthropologist in the audience who says that vignettes about imaginary strangers can't reach thick moral concepts in any culture, including mine. I don't want the objections I'd get from an online rationalist crowd — I want the ones this room will actually raise, in the vocabulary each of them would use.

Can you draft me a prep sheet in these sections, with rough lengths:

- The objections (~700 words) — the seven or eight I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased as a sentence a real person would say out loud in a job talk (not as a topic heading), tagged with who it's from and what they care about, with a ~90-second answer for each and one line on where that answer is weakest.
