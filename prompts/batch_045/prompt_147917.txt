I am putting together the centrepiece handout for the third session of a graduate seminar on panpsychism and the combination problem, and I would like it to be a decision tree: a branching questionnaire that starts from the commitment the whole room already shares — that there is something it is like to be an ordinary awake human being, and that this fact has some ground in the intrinsic or categorical nature of the physical — and deposits each student at a named position on how a composite subject is possible.

Requirements of form

- No more than eight branch questions and no more than ten leaves.
- Every branch node must be phrased as a question about a concrete case or a concrete modal claim, answerable by someone who has not read the literature and cannot tell which leaf the question leads to. A node of the form "do you think the macro-subject is constituted by the micro-subjects?" is useless to me; the same content rendered as a case is exactly what I want.
- Present the tree as nested, indented nodes (a markdown or ASCII flowchart is fine), followed by a table of leaves with four columns: label | the answer pattern that lands there | a one-sentence statement of the view | its most awkward commitment. By awkward commitment I mean the thing a defender would least like to say out loud, not the strongest objection available in the literature.

Built from the logical space, not from a list of names

Derive the tree from the questions: state which combinations of answers are genuinely available, then locate the actual positions in that space. Where a combination is unoccupied, keep the empty cell in the tree and give it a one-line diagnosis of why nobody is there — incoherent, merely unattractive, or (you suspect) simply unexplored. I want at least three such cells. Equally, flag at least two pairs of leaves that your tree cannot separate, and say either what further case would separate them or why no case could.

Cases that must be used

At least four of the following must appear as branch nodes. Rephrase them freely, but do not swap them for easier ones.

1. The census question. In a room containing three awake humans, one human in dreamless sleep and a cat, how many subjects are there on your view? This is meant to force the question whether the view permits nested or overlapping subjects: whether the micro-subjects are additional subjects alongside the human one, or are somehow absorbed into it, or were never subjects in the first place.
2. The split-brain patient. After callosotomy, is there one subject, two, or is the question ill-posed?
3. Fission and recombination. A subject's two hemispheres are separated and each continues; later they are reconnected. Is the post-reconnection subject identical with the pre-split subject, identical with one of the two interim subjects, or a new subject? This is meant to test whether the view allows diachronic combination or fusion, or only synchronic constitution.
4. The inverted-bonding world. A world physically identical to ours and identical at the level of every micro-experience, but with a different macro-experiential outcome — no unified human experience at all, say. Is that genuinely conceivable on your view, or excluded as a matter of necessity? This is meant to separate views on which composition is a necessary phenomenal law from those on which it is contingent, nomic or brute.
5. The grain shift. Suppose we discovered that the categorical bases of the physical are instantiated at the level of whole neurons rather than at the level of fundamental particles — or, in a second variant, at every level at once. Does the view's answer to the combination problem change, and how? This is meant to test whether a view has any settled answer to the question of which grain the base facts sit at.
6. The organoid and the colony. A cortical organoid of a million neurons; a mature ant colony; a large firm. For each: one subject, many, none, or a matter of degree? I want this node to force every view to choose between a sharp threshold, a continuum of degrees of subjecthood, and outright denial that the question has an answer.

Leaves that must appear

Give the standard label where the position has one, and a descriptive name followed by "no standard name" where it does not.

- The view on which micro-subjects stand in a further, irreducible phenomenal relation with its own laws and the macro-subject is constituted by them.
- The same view, but with the relation contingent or merely nomic rather than necessary — split these into two leaves only if one of your questions genuinely separates them, and say which one does.
- A view on which there is no further relation: the facts about the macro-subject are nothing over and above the micro-subjects plus the physical or functional relations among them. Treat the grounding-style and the identity-of-composition-style variants as separate leaves if your questions separate them, and as one leaf if they do not.
- The view on which the macro-subject is a genuinely additional ingredient in nature, related to the micro-level by a brute emergence with no mediating principle.
- Panprotopsychism: the base facts are not experiential but proto-phenomenal, and macro-experience is constituted by them rather than composed out of experiences.
- Cosmopsychism, with an account of how the one cosmic subject relates to the many apparent individuals — the mirror-image problem that replaces the combination problem on this view — including the variant on which apparent individuals are aspects or modes of the single subject.
- Macro-subject eliminativism: there are no composite subjects at all, only the micro ones, and the unity of our experience is in some sense an appearance.
- The grain solution, on which no combination is needed because the base facts already sit at a coarse enough grain — give it its own leaf only if your tree has a home for it distinct from the leaves above.
- At least one leaf outside the family: a position that grants the starting commitment only for the sake of argument and denies that there was ever anything to combine — a functionalist or illusionist leaf, for instance. Mark it as the leaf where a student who only pretended to grant the premise ends up.

Constraints

- British English throughout: behaviour, organise, analyse, favour, and so on.
- Assume only the standard menu of positions. Do not build a leaf around a variant defended in a single recent paper; if you want to mention one, place it in an unoccupied cell and say that you have done so.
- No citations, footnotes, bibliographies or reading lists. The handout is used in a room without internet and the tree must stand on its own. Label positions by their usual names where such names are standard, and do not attribute any position to an individual philosopher.
- Define every technical term at first use, in a single clause — including whatever term of art you use for the problem a cosmopsychist faces in place of the combination problem, and whatever you call the question of which physical level the base facts sit at.
- Keep the tree and the table together under about 1,300 words.
