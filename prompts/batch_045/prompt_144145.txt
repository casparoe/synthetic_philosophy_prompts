You are a professional philosopher specialising in social ontology and in the game-theoretic and evolutionary theory of social norms — someone who has David Lewis's *Convention* (1969) and its later descendants (the expectation-based accounts, the evolutionary/dynamic models, and the critics of both) at your fingertips, and who is equally comfortable saying "that has a name" and "that has no name, and here is why not."

Please work carefully and slowly. Before you write anything, spend some effort on private brainstorming: generate a list of candidate concepts from the neighbourhood of this phenomenon, check each against the details I give below, and discard the ones that fit only loosely. Do not settle for producing a label. A bare name would be useless to me; what I need is the carving — what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, where its edges lie, and which of the nearby notions it collapses into (if any).

I am looking for a name for something I keep running into while writing a paper, and I cannot find a term for it. Here are three instances.

1. In a university department, seminar questions used to be opened by one particular senior professor; the pause before she spoke was a way of deferring to her. She retired years ago, the department has more than doubled, most current members never met her, and the talks are now mostly on subjects she never worked on. There is still a marked pause after every talk. Nobody benefits from it; no one is waiting for anyone in particular. Yet when a junior visitor once asked a question straight away, several people afterwards described it — to me, privately, and with a slightly apologetic shrug — as "a bit abrupt." Nobody could state a rule that had been broken.

2. At a company I know, everyone still copies a particular administrator on client correspondence. Her role was absorbed by an automated system four years ago and the address now goes to an unmonitored mailbox. Everyone knows the mailbox is unmonitored; everyone knows that everyone knows; several people have mentioned it in meetings. The practice continues. It costs about two seconds. But new hires who omit the copy are quietly corrected, and the correction is not phrased as "you'll get in trouble," it is phrased as "we just do it."

3. A faculty committee begins every meeting by reading aloud the minutes of the previous one. The minutes are circulated in advance and are, by admission of everyone I asked, never actually listened to. Abolishing the reading has been proposed twice and defeated both times, without anyone offering a consequence that would follow from abolishing it. The proposal itself was described to me as "disrespectful to the process."

What strikes me is not the persistence. Persistence is easy to explain. What strikes me is the residue: the faint sense that deviating calls for an excuse, the mildly disapproving reaction, the way "that's how it's done" is produced as if it were a reason. It is not quite disapproval of a person; it is more like a small normative charge attached to a regularity whose point has expired.

Let me say what I think it is not, so that you don't send me down familiar roads.

- It is not a case of lock-in or switching costs. Unilateral deviation costs nothing and risks nothing; there is no installed base, no network effect, no coordination to be lost.
- It is not a case where people go along because they falsely believe others endorse the practice. On the contrary: everyone correctly believes that everyone else is also just going along, and correctly believes that nobody has a reason.
- It is not sustained by sanctions in the ordinary sense. Nobody punishes anyone; there is no reputational mechanism I can identify; the mild reaction does not seem aimed at changing behaviour.
- It is not individual habit. These are reflective people who would stop instantly if they thought stopping were fine, and the regularity is social — it survives complete turnover of the individuals.
- And it is, as far as I can tell, no longer doing any coordinating work. That is the part that matters for the framework I am working in, where a convention is supposed to be a self-sustaining solution to a recurring problem of getting everyone to do the same thing, held in place by expectations, and arbitrary among alternatives.

So my questions, in order of importance:

First, does this have a name in the literature on convention and the evolution of norms? If several nearby notions compete, tell me which fits best and why, and for each of the others tell me precisely which feature of my cases it fails to capture. I would rather be told "your cases are not really a single kind" than be given one tidy term.

Second, how should the Lewis-style apparatus classify these regularities? On a strict reading they have stopped being conventions at all, since the coordination problem that made them equilibria is gone; they look like mere behavioural regularities, or parallel individual customs. But that classification seems to throw away the residue, which is the interesting part. Is that a genuine difficulty for the account, or only for a naive version of it? Does the apparatus have resources — a notion of a convention's *point*, or of expectations outliving what they are expectations about — that handle it?

Third, and this is the boundary I care most about: consider a variant where the regularity outlives its original function but quietly acquires a new one. Imagine that in case 1 the pause has come to serve as a signal that the audience is being respectful, so that abolishing it really would now cost something. Is that the same phenomenon continuing, or a different one — the same shell repurposed? Say what would have to be true, in a real case, for us to be able to tell the two apart, since from the inside they feel identical and informants give the same shrug in both.

Finally: if there is no established term, coin one. Give me a definition tight enough to be argued with — conditions, and two genuine hard cases at the margin, one that falls just inside and one just outside — and say what would count as evidence that the residue in my cases is really normative (a faint "ought") rather than mere startlement at the unexpected. Where it helps, be explicit about what your definition predicts for a case where the practice is unanimously disliked and yet the residue persists anyway.
