Marco and I spent about two hours in the kitchen last night arguing about Death in Damascus and we were definately talking past each other by the end, so I want to build something better for the reading group I'm running next week. It's a small second-year group: they've all met Newcomb's problem, they haven't seen any of the formal machinery, and half of them use 'causal' and 'evidential' as insults rather than as labels. What I want from you is a decision tree (or a quiz with a scoring key, if that fits the material better) that takes someone from the plain case — you're in Damascus, Aleppo is the alternative, Death is an infallible predictor of where you'll be at dawn, same city = dead — and lands them on a named position. The rule I care most about: every branch node has to be a concrete question about a concrete case, phrased so a student can answer it without already knowing which theory it leads to. Nothing like 'do you think causation matters?'.

The nodes have to be built out of variations on the story, and these are the ones I want used: (1) the plain version; (2) 'coin-flip Death', where Death committed to a city by a fair coin toss years ago and does no predicting at all — I want this in there because I suspect half of any room is pattern-matching 'Damascus means weird answer' and I want them caught; (3) 'the rented randomizer', where you can pay a small fee to have a fair coin pick your city, and Death predicted the coin's output too, plus a sub-variant where Death simulated you but never simulated the coin ('the unsimulated coin'); (4) 'the leaky predictor', where Death isn't reading your deliberation at all but a proxy record — a ticket purchase or a scan taken yesterday — so the correlation runs through something that may or may not already be fixed; (5) 'the 90% predictor', and I want two versions of it, one where the 10% of errors are scattered at random and one where they cluster on people who change cities late. Sample questions that feel right to me but that you should rewrite or replace: in the plain case, is there anything you can do that raises your chance of being alive Friday, or does it not matter? If it doesn't matter, is that because you're doomed either way or because it's a straight coin flip? Would you pay for the randomizer, and does your answer flip between the two randomizer variants? Your buddy offers to tell you, after you've decided but before dawn, whether Death guessed right — do you want to hear it? And for the leaves, I need at least: the causal-style view, the evidential-style view, something that looks for a decision you can still endorse once you know you've made it (an equilibrium or ratifiability-flavored leaf), the 'case is underdescribed, tell me how the prediction is generated first' position, a resigned 'you're dead either way so decide on non-decision-theoretic grounds' leaf, and whatever cells come out empty marked 'unnamed'.

Two more things. First, build it from the logical space rather than from the list of names: lay out the questions and the answer-combinations they actually permit, then place the real positions into the cells, and for any combination nobody occupies give me one line on whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate — I'm fairly sure there's one pair that ends up giving identical advice ('doesn't matter, pick either') for completely opposite reasons, and I'd rather the handout admit that than pretend the tree cut finer than it did. One thing Marco said I couldn't place anywhere and want to see located: that once Death's prediction is produced by running a simulation of your deliberation, you aren't really choosing between cities, you're choosing what the simulated version of you does, and that this changes which options are even live.
