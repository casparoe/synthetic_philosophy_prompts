I'm 68, retired — spent my career in physiology, mostly motor control and a lot of time building little feedback rigs in the lab — and I've started going to a monthly philosophy reading group with five other retirees. We got badly stuck last week on sphexishness.

You know the case: the digger wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out and hauls the cricket in. Move the cricket a few inches while she's down in the hole and she comes up, drags it back to the threshold, and goes in to inspect all over again — and you can apparently do that to her dozens of times. The point it's used for (I think this is Dennett's word, or possibly Hofstadter's — our notes are a mess) is that the wasp is going through the motions with nothing home: rigidity as the outward sign that no understanding is driving any of it.

Here's the move that half the room makes, and it seems to be the standard one. The wasp is just a machine executing a fixed program. We are machines executing an enormously bigger program. So the gap between us is one of size only: what the wasp shows is the nature of the machinery, and our flexibility is more steps of exactly the same kind. One guy pushed it further and said the human cases prove it — you drive home on autopilot and remember none of it, you tie your shoes without a single thought — so we're sphexish nearly all the time and just tell ourselves a story about the parts where we aren't. Conclusion: free will is either an illusion or just a polite name for "complicated."

That argument feels like it's leaning on a badly outdated picture of what a machine can be, and I think I can say why in my own vocabulary, so let me put my cards down. In control work you meet two things that both look "mechanical" in the loose sense and are not remotely alike. There's the open-loop routine: a fixed sequence triggered by a sign, running off to completion without checking anything, like a washer's cycle or a lookup table that pairs stimulus with response. And there's the closed-loop controller: one fixed rule, keep the error at zero, which copes with an open-ended range of disturbances nobody ever wrote into it, because what it does next is a function of what the world is doing right now. The first can hold ten thousand entries and still be brittle; the second can be written on an index card and do things no fixed sequence could. If that's right, "how many distinct behaviors does the system have" and "how sensitive is it to what's actually going on" are separate axes, and the sphex argument looks like it's sliding between them. But I'm out of my depth on the philosophy side and I can't tell whether my distinction is doing real work or just relocating the bump — a control law is fixed too, after all; the flyball governor doesn't pick its own setpoint.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I'd like the correspondence laid out explicitly — in "the creature is a machine running a program," what plays the program, the input, the clock, the output device, the programmer? Then where the mapping genuinely holds, where it comes apart, and which features of a program the argument is quietly importing that the biology doesn't have: that a program has to be a sequence, that it's specified case by case, that it can't respond to anything outside its table, that someone wrote it knowing what it was for. And the part I actually care about: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table — that a flexible agent is only ever a longer program, and that the wasp shows us our own case? Is my open-loop/closed-loop distinction the right repair, or does the bump just move? If there's a better analogy for what flexibility consists in, give me that too, including what it can't cover.
