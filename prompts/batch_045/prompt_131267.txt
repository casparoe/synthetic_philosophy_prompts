Hey — I'm the lead writer on a scripted audio drama set in first-century Galilee, twelve episodes a season on a public broadcaster, and basically every script meeting hits the same wall: do we put this in Jesus' mouth or not. It's about forty of these calls a season, I get one afternoon per episode to make them, my historical consultant is on retainer for two hours a month (I read Greek badly and no Aramaic at all), and everything I decide then gets read twice — once by the broadcaster's factual-standards person, once by a faith advisory board we have to keep reasonably happy. So I want a procedure, a thing I run, not an essay about how hard this all is. No bibliography and no citations please, I can't look anything up and I don't need to cite anyone in the writers' room.

What I want is: the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to me (or to the board) because the question is really about values — do we offend believers, do we mislead the audience, do we lose the scene. For each test tell me which kind of test it is: a gate that can kill an item, a tie-breaker I only use when I'm stuck, or a one-way filter that can only ever disqualify and never confirms anything.

Some things I want built into it rather than re-litigated every episode:

My consultant keeps saying the standard criteria grade the tradition, not the event — so I want separate verdicts for "something like this happened", "he said roughly this", and "it went down the way we're staging it", and I want the procedure to be able to return mixed ones, because that's most of my job.

He also says nothing in the kit can return a verdict of inauthentic — failing a test means 'no verdict', not 'false'. I believe him but I don't know what that does to my workflow, so make it explicit.

There's an asymmetry I've started to suspect and want you to sharpen or kill: "this fits a first-century Palestinian setting" looks like a great filter for throwing out later legends, but a nearly useless confirmer, since anyone making something up a generation later would make up something that fits. If that's right, say what it's allowed to do in the procedure.

The dissimilarity worry: a Jesus assembled only out of material that passes because it's unlike both the Judaism around him and the church after him is, by construction, a Jesus who is neither of those things. I want a cap — no more than some fraction of the season's Jesus material may rest mainly on that one test. Give me a number and defend it, and tell me what to do when two great scenes compete for the last slot under the cap.

Coherence: to me it just looks circular, you may only add what fits what you've already accepted, so it can never correct the picture. Is it a gate, a tie-breaker, or a trap?

Multiple attestation needs an independence step that I can actually apply without a synopsis of source criticism in front of me: when two sources are in a literary relationship they count once, and "two gospels say it" is not two attestations. Give me a crude version of that test that a non-specialist can run and won't get badly wrong.

And one idea of my own I want you to try out, and tell me honestly if it's junk: the "explanatory orphan" test — the item is worth keeping if it explains something about the movement that nothing else in our picture explains, and if it isn't itself better explained by some later need of the movement. Where does that misfire?

Then run the whole thing on three cases:

1. The baptism by John. I assume this one sails through; if it doesn't, tell me what the weakest link is.

2. The cry from the cross. It's embarrassing, it looks Aramaic-ish, but the line is straight out of a psalm — so what survives, the fact of a cry, the exact words, the bystanders misunderstanding it?

3. The crowd choosing Barabbas and Pilate washing his hands. The writers' room loves this, it's our act-two climax, and the board is already nervous about the blame-the-Jews thing. I fully expect you to tell me to cut it or change it a lot; run the procedure honestly anyway, tell me the strongest version of the case for keeping it, and what I'd have to change to make it defensible. We do have a narrator for hedging, but the standards person hates it when we use the narrator as a fig leaf, so be specific about when hedging is legitimate.

Give me a one-page version I can actually work through at my desk between meetings, the reasoning behind the ordering so I can defend it when someone upstairs asks, and a short list of the calls this procedure simply cannot make.
