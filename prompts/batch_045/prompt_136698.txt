I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and next week I'm taking one ninety-minute session of our MA seminar on formal epistemology. There will be about fourteen people — mostly philosophy MA and PhD students, plus two from economics and one from computer science — who have all seen Bayes' theorem but none of whom have seen a Dutch book argument. I don't want to put the synchronic argument on the board and walk through the algebra; I want them to hand me a set of numbers and then watch a package of bets they themselves called fair lose whatever happens. No real money: points, with a small prize (a book token) for whoever ends up richest and for whichever bookie extracts the most.

What I need is the activity itself. Each student should post, for each proposition on a menu, the price at which they would be willing either to buy or to sell a ticket paying a fixed stake — crucially a single number, not a bid and an ask — and a rotating subset of students should play bookie against them, free to take either side of any posted price and to combine the resulting trades into one package. Then I want a diachronic round on the same model: before anyone learns anything, each student fills in a short table saying what their fair prices would be if they were to learn each cell of a partition, and each receives a sealed envelope that determines which cell they actually learn; the bookies trade against those pre-commitments first, the envelopes are opened, the students re-post their prices live, and any gap between the live price and the pre-committed one is traded against too. Two things I would also like squeezed in if you can manage it: a brief variation in which I let them post a spread instead of a single price, so that the one-shot package no longer works and they can see what the sequential version of the argument is supposed to buy; and one or two propositions whose truth nobody at the table can work out by hand (primality of a six-digit number, some combinatorial count), so that the exploitation turns on facts no one present can settle on the spot.

Alongside the rules, materials and payoff scheme, what I most want is the debrief: which moments in the session correspond to which formal assumption — that prices are two-sided, that the trades are taken at the agent's own numbers so the loss is guaranteed rather than merely probable, that the diachronic version only gets a grip if the agent's updating plan is fixed in advance, and that the argument requires the students to be logically omniscient about what they are pricing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect someone posting 0.5 on everything, someone refusing to trade out of loss aversion, and at least one student who tries to beat the bookie by misreporting their real fair prices) and how to handle each. And be explicit about what the activity cannot deliver: the part where I have to step in myself and say that coherence is not the whole of rationality, that exploitability may be a symptom rather than the ground of irrationality, and that the whole construction assumes stakes small enough for the points to be treated as linear in value. Please keep the answer under 900 words, with the timeline as a compact table and the debrief as short bullets mapping each beat onto the assumption it isolates.
