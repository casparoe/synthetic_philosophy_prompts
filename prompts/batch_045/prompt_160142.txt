For the past year I have been working through the ethics-of-belief literature on my own — James, Clifford, and the more recent work on pragmatic reasons for belief — because of an argument I keep having, and I have come to think that the argument is not really about what either of them was arguing about.

Three instances. A teacher is told that a randomly selected pupil is about to spurt; she gives that child harder work and more attention; the child improves. Someone decides that a new colleague is trustworthy and hands over something risky; the trust draws out the reliability. The participants in a campaign become convinced that winning is possible; the conviction recruits the people who make it possible.

In each case the belief causes the state of affairs that makes it true — but only while the believer is not treating the belief as an instrument for producing that state of affairs. Tell the teacher the selection was random and she cannot sustain the expectation. Tell the trusting person to trust in order to elicit reliability and the stance collapses. A conviction adopted openly as a recruiting device recruits no one. Contrast an ordinary self-fulfilling prophecy — a run on a bank, a collapsing currency — where full publicity about the mechanism dissolves nothing and may make things worse: there the belief survives being seen through.

Is there an established name for the first configuration, or for the contrast between the two? I am not after a bare label. I want to know how the first differs from James's genuine options, where the evidence cannot settle a forced, live and momentous question and where the truth of the matter does not depend on how one bets, and how it differs from claims about practical stakes raising the standards for knowledge; whether the difference I am pointing at is one distinction or several; and what an ethics of belief should say about the first kind, given that the belief is true, that its fruits are evidence of it, but only after the fact, and that telling the holder how it works seems to damage something.

Please answer in four titled sections, in this order: "Name, if there is one" (about 100 words); "Neighbours and non-neighbours" (about 200 words); "The distinction, defined, with boundary cases" (about 250 words — include beliefs held by a group rather than a person, and beliefs whose opacity can be restored by self-deception or deliberate ignorance); "Verdict for an ethics of belief" (about 150 words).
