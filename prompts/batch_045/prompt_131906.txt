I've been going in circles trying to figure out what I actually think about whether pleasure and pain are tradeable against each other, so I'm going to just list my verdicts on cases and ask what principle, if any, they add up to.

Setup so we use the same words: by the Cliff I mean the lexical threshold idea — that above some level of suffering, no quantity of pleasure anywhere can outweigh it, while below that level everything sits on one scale and trades off normally. By the Drip Problem I mean the question of whether the Cliff applies to momentary experiences, to episodes, or to whole lives.

Here's where my gut lands:

1. An hour of pretty severe pain this afternoon in exchange for a genuinely great month afterwards, in my own life — I'd take it.
2. The exact same trade imposed on a stranger who never agreed to it — not allowed, and it isn't only the consent that bothers me; the trade itself feels illicit. But the same surgery the stranger asks for and is grateful to get — obviously fine.
3. One person tortured for fifty years versus dust specks in the eyes of an astronomically large number of people — I take the specks, and I do not change my mind as you keep adding people.
4. Sixty years of mild but unremitting nausea for one person, versus a bad-but-ordinary week of flu for a million people — and here I wobble. Every single moment of the nausea is below the Cliff, so by clause (ii) plus a per-moment Cliff it shouldn't be weighable at all, yet I can't bring myself to say it's the worse option. This is the case I keep going back on.
5. A one-in-a-billion chance that someone is tortured for a year versus a certainty that one person has a bad year of moderate pain — I take the risk. So the Cliff seems to survive being spread across persons but not being multiplied by a tiny probability, and I have no idea why it should be one and not the other.
6. Pains of very different kinds — grief, toothache, humiliation — seem to me to sum into one another much more readily than any of them trades against pleasure. Two mild pains make something genuinely worse. Mild pleasures never seem to make anything that can stand up to a severe pain.
7. Packaging doesn't help: the pleasure of relief after pain, or of a cold swim, or of very spicy food, doesn't let the painful part hop the Cliff by being bundled with something pleasant in the same experience.
8. But sensory intensity isn't what's on the pain side either: the same sensory state, in someone who savours it and sought it out, doesn't seem to go on the pain side at all.
9. Painless bads — a death with no suffering in it, a betrayal the victim never learns of, a capacity lost that would never have been exercised — matter to me, matter more than mild pains, and don't seem to belong on the pain side of the ledger at any intensity.

My rough stab, for you to break: call it the Silo View. (i) Below the Cliff, pleasure and pain share one scale with an exchange rate. (ii) At or above the Cliff, pain is lexically dominant over any amount of pleasure. (iii) The Cliff applies to episodes, not moments. (iv) Pain-pain and pleasure-pleasure comparisons are always permitted, so pains of any kind aggregate and so do pleasures. (v) What goes on the pain side is felt aversiveness, not intensity of sensation.

What I want: test that against the cases above and repair it or replace it; state the finished principle precisely; tell me which clause each case is doing the work for; and give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed — a trillion people each getting one second of just-below-Cliff pain versus one person getting a year of just-above-Cliff pain, and whether creating a suffering and failing to prevent an identical one come out the same. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best version of the view can't accommodate, and whether it's my verdict or the view that should give.
