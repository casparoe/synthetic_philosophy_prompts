I teach a binational summer-school seminar on everyday moral reasoning: twenty undergraduates, roughly half from American universities and half from German ones, one room with movable chairs, a single 100-minute session that falls in the middle of a short module. This session is on the contrast between two folk-moral styles. In the first, the question of what a situation calls for is answered by locating the applicable rule and doing that, and order, predictability and checkability are themselves counted as moral goods rather than as bureaucratic overhead. In the second, the same question is answered by asking what this situation demands of me in particular, where the answer may not be utterable by anyone else, and where an act done merely because it was required looks hollow, or worse, like a way of not being present.

The room is the point of the session: about half the participants grew up hearing the first style praised and half the second, and both groups arrive with a ready caricature of the other. So I do not want the session to be a discussion in which people report their intuitions. I want an activity in which the difference shows up in what people actually do and in what they find they are able to say, before it is named. And I want neither style to come out of the session looking like a parody of itself: not rule-following as timidity, not conscience as a licence for self-dramatization.

Three distinctions from my own notes should be load-bearing in the design; I want participants to hit them, not to be told them. First, detachable versus attached reasons: some reasons keep their force when they are separated from the person who offers them, since anyone in that position could have given them, while others go dead when detached, because they depend on a history only the speaker has. Second, the licence gradient: some arrangement has to decide who may depart from a rule without paying for it, and the two styles answer differently, the one concentrating that permission in an office and the other distributing it to everybody, which sounds egalitarian and in practice distributes it to whoever is most at ease speaking in public. Third, procedural residue: after a rule has been broken for an excellent reason, what is left behind is not a memory but a precedent, available to be cited next time by someone whose reason is contemptible, and the residue is the same no matter how good the original breach was.

Here is what I have settled on and what I want you to design around. No lecture up front; I will not say the words conscience, rule-following, duty, obligation, or their German equivalents, and no jargon of the -ism variety, until the final fifteen minutes. Participants should spend the first stretch authoring, themselves, a short written code for a small shared enterprise they are imagined to be running together, so that the conflict later in the session is not between my will and an alien rule but between what I now want and what I myself wrote down an hour ago. That seems to me the only way to get the interesting version of the conflict rather than the cheap one.

Then a sequence of incidents, each requiring an individual decision under a hard time limit and before any discussion, made publicly by moving to a marked part of the room. Compliance should be silent and unremarked. Departure from the code should require standing up and giving a reason in the first person, and I want a speech constraint: the words one, you, anyone, people, everyone and nobody are disallowed, as is any sentence about what the rule is for. Only I-sentences about what the speaker is doing and why. The asymmetry of cost between the silent option and the spoken one is not a bug to be corrected; it is the engine. Somewhere in the sequence I want one incident for which the code is simply silent, with a very short clock, because I suspect that what fills a gap is not twenty individual consciences but rapid imitation of whoever moved first, and I want the room to discover that about itself. I also want departures to be entered in a visible log that later participants can cite as permission.

Please choose the shared enterprise and give me the incident cards in full, and tell me why the choice of enterprise matters: it needs a purpose the participants can name and actually care about, since at least one incident has to be a case where a rule is followed perfectly and its point is thereby defeated, which only lands if there is a point to defeat. Give me a minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list, the exact wording of the instructions participants hear, and the rules of the speech constraint.

Stakes should be social rather than financial, though I am open to something tiny and real. I have been toying with two things and want your judgement on both. One: assigning a few participants positions that weaken their stake in the enterprise's future, such as the person who is leaving at the end of the week, because I suspect that temporariness dissolves loyalty to a rule faster than bad consequences do, and I would like that to be visible. Two: a closing in which everyone seals a card at the start naming a rule they actually live by, and chooses at the end whether to read it aloud.

The debrief needs to map specific moments I will have watched for onto the three distinctions above, and to say which observable event is evidence for which. It also has to handle the evaluative question, since the module promises a verdict on which style has the better of it. I do not want the session to end on either of the two stock conciliations, that it depends on the case, or that we need a bit of both; both are ways of not having the argument. Tell me how to stage the close so that the question of which style has the better of it is forced to reformulate into something the room can actually argue about, and tell me what I must not let them conclude.

Be specific about what will go wrong. In particular: what I should do if nobody departs from the code in the first two incidents; how to stop participants from performing their nationality instead of their disposition; how to keep the two or three most fluent speakers from setting the norm for everyone; and how to handle the speech constraint when someone experiences it, correctly, as a restriction on what they are permitted to say.

Finally, be frank about what this kind of activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to say myself. My candidates are that real rule-following is mostly invisible and unchosen, in that one rarely decides to comply and rarely notices having done so; that the payoff of a stable rule is spread over years of coordination and no 100-minute session can show it; that the failures of the conscience style are systematically harder to see than the failures of the rule style, because a bad appeal to conscience leaves no audit trail; and that in ordinary life agents usually cannot tell which of the two styles they are currently using. Tell me which of these I am wrong about and what else is missing.
