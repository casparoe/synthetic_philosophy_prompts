Here is Question 3 (worth 25 points):

A three-judge appellate panel must decide whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract. Under the applicable doctrine, liability holds if and only if (p) a valid contract was formed and (q) the defendant breached it. Call the liability proposition r, with r ↔ (p ∧ q). The judges' individual views are: Judge A accepts p, accepts q, and therefore accepts r; Judge B accepts p, rejects q, and therefore rejects r; Judge C rejects p, accepts q, and therefore rejects r.

(a) Show what propositionwise majority rule (decide each proposition separately by majority) delivers here, and explain why this is called a *dilemma* rather than merely an unexpected result. Be precise about the level at which the inconsistency arises.

(b) Set out the premise-based procedure and the conclusion-based procedure: what each would decide in this case, and what each gives up. Use the conditions as defined in class: *universal domain*; *collective rationality*; *independence* (the collective judgment on a proposition depends only on individuals' judgments on that proposition); *unanimity* (if everyone accepts a proposition, the group accepts it too). Then explain how the general impossibility result from the readings bears on the choice between the two procedures — roughly, that no aggregation function satisfying universal domain, collective rationality, independence, and unanimity is available except rules that effectively hand the decision to a fixed individual or a fixed oligarchy, and that adding anonymity collapses these to dictatorship.

(c) The legal version of this phenomenon is known as the doctrinal paradox; the generalized version, applying to any group whose judgments must be logically coherent, is the discursive dilemma. Is the discursive dilemma simply Arrow's theorem in another notation, or does it show something the Arrovian framework does not? Take a position and defend it.

Here is one student's answer, reproduced verbatim:

> (1) Under propositionwise majority rule, Judges A and B accept p, so p is accepted; Judges A and C accept q, so q is accepted; but Judges B and C reject r, so r is rejected by a majority. So the court is collectively committed to p, to q, and to not-(p and q), which cannot all be true. That is the dilemma: the majority's reasons and the majority's verdict come apart. Notice that majority rule is itself incoherent here, so it isn't really an aggregation function at all — a genuine aggregation function has to output a consistent set. In the legal setting this is the doctrinal paradox, which is just another name for the discursive dilemma, and the legal case is vivid because a court has to state reasons and so cannot quietly drop either the premises or the verdict.
>
> (2) There are two standard ways out. The premise-based procedure has the judges vote only on p and q and then derive r: 2–1 for p, 2–1 for q, so the defendant is liable. The conclusion-based procedure has them vote only on r and say nothing about p and q: 2–1 against r, so no liability. The premise-based route is clearly the better one. It satisfies independence, unanimity, and collective rationality, so it escapes the impossibility result entirely; the conclusion-based route throws away the reasons the judges actually give, and it can't be right for a court to reason its way to a verdict while refusing to endorse the premises of its own reasoning.
>
> (3) Really, though, I think the whole thing dissolves once you see where it comes from. Majority rule is perfectly consistent when applied to any single proposition; the inconsistency only appears because we apply it twice over, once to the premises and once to the conclusion, and then complain that the two don't match. If the court announces in advance which procedure it is using, there is no paradox left — it is an artifact of asking the same question at two levels. And even apart from that, raising the threshold would fix it: replace majority rule with a two-thirds supermajority and the court will never be driven into an inconsistent set. So the dilemma is an artifact of majority rule rather than a deep fact about collective judgment.
>
> (4) On part (c): I don't think any of this is new. The judgment-aggregation impossibility result is just a corollary of Arrow's theorem — Arrow already showed that you cannot aggregate individual inputs into a coherent collective output while respecting independence and unanimity, and the discursive dilemma is the same result with propositions in place of preferences. The right conclusion is therefore that the discursive dilemma adds nothing to what Arrovian social choice had already established; the legal case only supplies a nice illustration.
