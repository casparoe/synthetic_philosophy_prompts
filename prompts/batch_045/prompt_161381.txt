ok so my friend and I got into a genuinely stupidly long argument about Ulysses contracts last night (we got there from constitutional entrenchment, because nothing kills an evening faster) and Ive been lying awake turning it over. His argument, cleaned up a bit:

(1) The standard story about why tying yourself to the mast can be rational is just welfare arithmetic: you know your judgment later will be impaired, so you remove or reprice the option now, and the test for whether to set the thing up is a straight expected-cost comparison over your own life.

(2) Nothing in that test mentions who installs the restraint. Only two things matter: that the impaired choice reliably gets blocked, and what the blocking costs. The identity of the binder does no work.

(3) So if the arithmetic clears, it clears just as well when a third party does the installing: the friend who hides the bottle, the doctor who fits the implant without asking, the legislature that restricts the option for whoever is likely to be impaired. Third-party versions might even score better, since a stranger is less likely to cave in the moment.

(4) And it doesn't have to be a restraint in the narrow sense. If all that matters is that the impaired action gets less likely, then deception, sedation, or quietly arranging things so the option never comes to mind are all on the table, and irreversible beats reversible, since reversible leaves impaired-you a way back in.

(5) So the standard story licenses paternalism in general: by strangers, by deceit, without consent — and the version it ranks highest is the most coercive one.

(6) But the only reason anyone cares about Ulysses contracts — the reason people reach for them as the model for constitutional precommitment, supermajority amendment rules, independent central banks, all of it — is that they're supposed to be the one species of paternalism that doesn't insult autonomy: the binder and the bound are the same person at different times, and the constraint was agreed to in advance.

(7) So (1) and (6) can't both stand as they are: read honestly, the standard story turns the Ulysses contract into ordinary paternalism with extra hardware, and destroys the feature that made it the respectable case.

My move against him was an escape hatch: don't remove the option, just make it expensive to reach — 48-hour wait, notarized form, public posting — so that the version of you who still wants out after the delay can get out, and autonomy is preserved because the door exists even though temptation can't use it. He said that doesn't help, and I think he's right that it doesn't settle it: either the delay is short enough that the impatient version of you sits through it, or it's long enough that the person who genuinely and calmly changed their mind is stuck too, because a waiting period screens for impatience rather than for impairment of judgment, and those come apart — the convert and the addict both show up at the window. And then he ran the same point on the constitutional case: an amendment threshold screens for how many people want the change, not for whether they've thought about it, so no threshold both blocks the panicked majority and lets the deliberative one through.

I'd rather keep (1) — the arithmetic really does feel like the whole explanation of why these things work — and I really don't want to end up saying that constitutional precommitment is just paternalism with better branding. So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the answer is (2), doesn't whatever principle replaces it run straight into the dead-hand objection anyway, since the people who do the binding aren't the people who end up bound?
