I spent forty years as a field biologist, most of it on breeding-bird surveys, and in retirement I have been reading my way into philosophy of science. I have reached the raven paradox that goes under Hempel's name, and I want something more useful out of it than the puzzle itself.

The setup, as I understand it, rests on two things that each look compelling. First, a condition I would have endorsed without thinking: an object that is both a raven and black confirms All ravens are black; a raven that is not black disconfirms it; anything that is not a raven is irrelevant to it. Second, whatever confirms a hypothesis confirms every statement logically equivalent to it, and All ravens are black is equivalent to All non-black things are non-ravens. Together they entail that a grey pebble on the beach confirms the hypothesis. I gather the standard response is Bayesian: yes it does, by an amount too tiny to be noticed, and the tininess tracks how improbable it was that a randomly encountered non-black object would have turned out to be a raven.

That response strikes me as right as far as it goes and useless as stated, because it imagines evidence arriving from one protocol sampling one undifferentiated pool. No survey I ever ran worked like that. Detection is imperfect, samples are clustered, the domain is usually restricted, and what a method could have found matters as much as what it did find. So let me state my own verdicts and ask what principle, if any, holds them together. I am not asking for a summary of the literature or for names and citations; I can read those myself. I want a principle stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a field assistant.

1. One black raven, seen well at close range, in a valley where ravens breed. Confirms, but only modestly.
2. Three black nestlings from a single nest. Confirms, and noticeably less than three black ravens from three territories ten miles apart.
3. A large black corvid glimpsed badly at distance and recorded as probably a raven. Confirms, clearly less than case 1.
4. Two hundred black ravens recorded by a method that would not have registered a pale raven against snow, on ground where a pale raven could have been standing. My verdict: barely confirms at all, and far less than two hundred independent sightings would.
5. A grey-and-black crow, a non-black bird in the same genus as ravens, definitely identified. Confirms a whisper.
6. A grey heron, a non-black bird from a distant family. Confirms less than case 5, though I cannot say why, since both are simply non-black non-ravens.
7. A grey pebble from a beach where ravens never occur. Confirms nothing at all.
8. A grey pebble from the scree under an occupied raven nest. Confirms a whisper, and more than case 7 — again with no difference in logical form to point to.
9. An island small enough that a team examined every non-black object on it, stones and shells and seabirds included, and found no raven among them. This settles All ravens on this island are black outright; no number of black ravens could have, and a black raven seen after the census adds nothing.
10. A black raven in the valley confirms All ravens in this valley are black more strongly than it confirms All ravens are black.

My tentative principle is that an observation confirms a hypothesis to a degree fixed by two quantities: the probability, under the procedure that actually produced the observation, that the procedure would have turned up a counterexample if the hypothesis were false; and the extent to which the observation shifts the odds between the hypothesis and the alternatives that are still live. The first clause is meant to carry cases 4, 7, 8, 9 and 10; the second is meant to carry 5 and 6 by noting that a non-black corvid threatens hypotheses in the neighbourhood of mine in a way a heron does not.

I would like you to test that against the cases and repair it where it breaks. State whatever principle you arrive at precisely, and tell me which case motivates each clause. Then give me its verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a trail-camera photograph of a black bird that an automated classifier assigns to raven with 0.92 confidence; a single black raven, and then a run of sixty with no pale one, as evidence for the statistical claim that at least ninety-five per cent of ravens are black; and a black raven on the mainland, as evidence for All ravens are black against the rival All ravens are black except in one island population where a pale morph persists. Finally, tell me honestly which of my ten verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which of the two clauses has to give. Where the principle has nothing to say about a case, say so rather than stretching it.
