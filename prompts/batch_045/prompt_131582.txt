For the past two quarters I have been running the internal forecasting pool that feeds into our organisation's decisions about where to pre-position emergency supplies — a few dozen analysts, roughly forty questions per quarter, a handful of them about low-probability, high-consequence events such as a border closing or a warehouse flooding. I inherited the scoring spreadsheet, which grades every forecast with the Brier score, and I have been asked to decide within the month whether to keep it or switch to something else. Trying to understand what the Brier score is actually for led me to a long article in a science magazine about the "accuracy-first" turn in epistemology, and I have no way of telling how much of that article's version of the view to trust. As best I recall it, the central passage ran roughly like this:

"Epistemologists used to think that evidence is what makes a belief rational: you should believe what your evidence supports. Accuracy-first epistemology reverses this. The only thing that fundamentally matters about a belief is how close it sits to the truth, and evidence matters only as a useful instrument for landing near the truth, never as a standard in its own right. Since closeness to the truth is a quantity, the whole subject becomes a branch of decision theory: you grade a degree of confidence by how far it falls from the truth value, and the scoring rule you have to use — the Brier score — is the unique one that is 'proper', meaning simply that it is the honest rule, one that can never reward you for reporting a confidence you do not really have. Once that is fixed, the rest is mathematics. Any system of partial beliefs that violates the probability axioms is guaranteed to be beaten for accuracy by some coherent rival no matter how the world turns out, so probabilism is not a norm of evidence at all but a theorem; and conditioning on your new evidence is likewise provably the one uniquely accuracy-maximising way to update, so Bayes' rule is not a sensible policy among others but the only rational one. The upshot, the article concludes, is that a man who takes his beliefs from the astrology column is in exactly as good an epistemic position as a careful investigator so long as his numbers happen to land near the truth — which shows that the whole programme is only a dressed-up version of 'whatever works'."

Would you work through this more or less claim by claim and tell me which parts someone actually working in this literature would accept, which are the simplifications a popular piece can reasonably get away with, and which are straightforwardly wrong? I am unsure about three things in particular.

First, the astrology conclusion is the part I am most inclined to disbelieve, but I cannot locate where the article goes wrong, and I would like to know both whether the framework really has that consequence and what a defender would say instead about a belief that gets the numbers right by luck.

Second, the uniqueness claim about the Brier score is the one I would be acting on, so I need to know whether the choice among strictly proper scoring rules is arbitrary or whether it makes a real difference for someone in my position — I am scoring rare events, I care about the difference between an analyst who says 3 percent and one who says 0.5 percent on an event that then happens, and I score both individual analysts and an aggregate figure for the group.

Third, one of our more experienced analysts has recently started reporting intervals — "somewhere between 30 and 50 percent" — rather than single numbers, and I do not know whether this literature gives me any verdict on whether she is being irrational, or whether the verdict depends on the scoring rule in a way the article's talk of theorems conceals.

Finally, I would be grateful for a verdict on the last move: does the article's closing jibe follow from the parts of its summary that are correct, or does it depend on the parts that are not?
