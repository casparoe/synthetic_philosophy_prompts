I'm a high school senior putting together an application for my school's alumni-funded student research grant, which would support one semester of independent work in philosophy (about four months, eight or so hours a week, alongside a normal course load). The application wants a two-page proposal — roughly 1,000 words — and I'd like help turning a half-formed idea into one. I don't need any bibliography or literature review with citations; I'll fill those in myself, but the proposal should name the positions and arguments it's responding to clearly enough that a reader who knows the material can place them.

The area is Scanlon-style contractualism — the family of views on which an act is wrong if it would be forbidden by a principle that no one could reasonably reject — and in particular the problem of aggregation. As I understand the debate, it's almost always framed as a question about numbers of people: one person suffers something serious, a very large number of people each suffer something trivial, and the question is whether the sheer number of trivial complaints can ever outweigh the single serious one. Contractualism is supposed to say no, and that answer is treated as one of its distinctive commitments.

My half-formed idea is that there's a prior question here that gets treated as background but shouldn't be: what is the unit whose complaints are being weighed? Contractualism seems to already permit aggregation *within* a single person's life. To compare what a principle would cost one person, you have to add up the different ways it affects them across time and across the different things they care about — otherwise you can't say how badly off they are under it at all. So intra-personal aggregation is built into the machinery, while inter-personal aggregation is banned. What I want to know is whether that asymmetry can actually be defended, given that we can redescribe many inter-personal cases as intra-personal ones and vice versa. Call it the slicing problem.

Two directions I have in mind: taking a case where one person bears many small burdens and another bears one medium burden, and comparing it to a case where the very same small burdens are distributed one each across many people — the complaints are qualitatively identical, and the only difference is whose they are; and going the other way, imagining the many people replaced by a single long-lived subject, or by a collective agent like a firm or a state that bears all of the small complaints at once. If summing is permitted inside a subject, the ban on aggregation starts to look like it depends on who or what counts as a subject.

What I need from the proposal: a precise statement of the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a slogan; an explanation of why the existing contractualist positions leave it open; a method — specifically, whether the right approach is to construct matched pairs of cases that are structurally identical except for how the complaints are distributed across subjects, and to test candidate explanations of the asymmetry against them, including the awkward version where the small complaints differ in strength from each other, so that no simple tie-breaking story covers them; why that method rather than the obvious alternatives; the possible conclusions and what would decide between them; what a finished deliverable looks like (I was imagining a 6,000–7,000 word paper plus a one-page summary for non-specialists, and maybe a table sorting kinds of aggregation by whether contractualism can allow them); and honest scope risks and failure conditions — what would it mean for this project to fail, and what about it might be too big for a high schooler in one semester.

Begin the proposal itself as the very first line — no "here is a proposal," no preface, no restating my idea back to me, no headings before it — and make that opening sentence state the question and the case for funding it both at once. Then give the rest of the proposal in order.
