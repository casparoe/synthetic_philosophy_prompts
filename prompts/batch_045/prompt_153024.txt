You are a professional philosopher who works on metaphysics and the philosophy of science, and I would like you to work carefully rather than quickly. Before you propose anything, please tabulate my verdicts one by one, flag any place where I may be answering two different questions with the same word, and only then look for the principle. If you find a tension, say plainly which verdict has to go.

I ran a biophysics lab for a little over thirty years and have started reading philosophy in retirement; the old puzzle about Theseus's ship has been nagging me, and I find I have firm gut reactions to concrete cases even though I can't state what I believe.

Our circular-dichroism spectrometer: across thirty years every module was replaced one at a time — lamp, monochromator, detectors, electronics, finally the optical bench — always in the same room, always in service, always the same inventory number. Same instrument: yes. Then it was moved to a new building: dismantled into crates, out of service three months, reassembled by the same technician from the same parts. Same instrument: yes, and oddly I feel no hesitation at all. A wooden research vessel: every plank replaced over forty years, the old planks stacked in a shed and at the end reassembled into a hull of the same design that now floats in a marina. The sailing ship is the original; the reassembled hull is a replica: no. My grandfather's axe — father replaced the head, I replaced the handle: yes, same axe. A cell line from my own lab, split into two aliquots in 1987, one passaged continuously and one held in liquid nitrogen for thirty years and then thawed: both are the same line, and I notice that here same is not one-to-one, which I am willing to accept. The cases I cannot square are the crate-moved spectrometer and the reassembled hull: both are disassembly, a gap, and reassembly from the original matter, and I answer yes to one and no to the other without being able to say why.

My tentative principle is that a thing persists as the same thing so long as an uninterrupted career continues — a causally connected run of the activities that make it the thing it is — that matter is irrelevant, that where a career has been interrupted identity goes to whichever candidate has been carrying it forward, and that where careers branch the relation becomes lineage rather than identity. Does that survive my cases? It seems to get the spectrometer, the axe, the sailing ship and the branching cell line, but it appears to deny that the thawed aliquot is the same line, since the continuously passaged aliquot has been carrying the career all along, and my gut says the thawed one is the same line. Is the repair to distinguish an interruption that is abandonment from one that is arranged in advance as dormancy, and can that distinction be drawn without smuggling in intentions? And does the clause about competing candidates mean that in the ship case both hulls have an equal claim and the tie is broken only by our practices of custody — that is, that identity here is not a fact of the matter? I would like a principle, or two rival candidates, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdicts on cases I have not listed (a codebase fork, a person all of whose cells have turned over, a restored painting), and a verdict on the one I go back and forth on: a dataset from my lab, forty years old, every record re-measured or corrected at least once, migrated across four storage media, with the original tapes preserved and still readable — the same dataset for purposes of citation, I think, and not the same dataset when I think about the stored values themselves.
