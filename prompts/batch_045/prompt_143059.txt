I would like a decomposed credence, not a verdict, on a causation-by-omission case that my co-author and I have been arguing about for two months.

The case. A patient dies of a drug interaction. There are three candidate omissions, and no one of them is but-for necessary, because any one of the three agents acting would have intercepted the order: (i) the clinical informatics team shipped a release in which the interaction alert rendered in a sidebar that was collapsed by default; (ii) the reviewing pharmacist did not open the sidebar; (iii) the renal-dosing nurse was on an unscheduled break at the time. There is a fourth candidate omission, the admitting physician's failure to read the alert, but it is screened off: she credibly testifies, and the log data support her, that she was dismissing even interruptive alerts unread throughout the shift, so a hard stop would not have altered her behaviour.

The wrinkle I actually want adjudicated is the choice of baseline for identifying what was omitted. Call an omission *specification-relative* when the unperformed act is picked out by a rule, protocol, or design specification in force, and *envelope-relative* when it is picked out by what the system as deployed actually delivers under realistic load. On the specification-relative reading, all three are omissions: the alert was specified to fire interruptively, the protocol required the pharmacist to review it, and the nurse was rostered. On the envelope-relative reading, the deployed system delivered no effective interruptive alerting during night admissions at all, and the staffing envelope contained no coverage at that hour, so it is arguable that (i) and (iii) are not omissions at all but features of the actual baseline, leaving only (ii).

The proposition I want a number on is this: in this case, at least one of (i), (ii), and (iii) is an actual cause of the death, where actual causation is whatever relation inquiry of this kind is after, as opposed to mere membership in the causal history or a convenient locus for blame. I will count the question as resolved affirmatively if any reasonably worked-out account that admits omissions among the causes at all would return at least one of the three, without having to stipulate a baseline gerrymandered to get that result.

Please give me your credence, decomposed over at least these: that omissions can be genuine causes at all rather than dependence-shaped shadows cast by absent positive events; that symmetric overdetermination among preventers does or does not defeat causation by any individual omission; that the screened-off fourth omission does not show the remaining three are causally inert too; that the correct baseline for specifying the omitted act is specification-relative rather than envelope-relative; and that a causal finding here is not covertly a responsibility finding, i.e. that there is a fact of the matter about (i) to (iii) that inquirers could converge on independently of who ought to have done what. Add whatever further terms you think the decomposition needs, give a number and a one-line justification for each, show the combination, and then give me your headline number conditional on each of the two baseline readings separately.

My own number is about 0.5; my co-author's is about 0.12. Tell me which of the two of us is most likely wrong about which sub-question, which term your final number is most sensitive to, and which single change to the facts, such as deleting the third preventer or making the nurse's break unauthorized, would move it furthest and in which direction.
