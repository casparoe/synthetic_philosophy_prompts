I work as a software engineer and volunteer on grant-making for a small effective-altruism-adjacent fund, and there is one argument in that world that I have watched go round for years without anyone visibly moving. The critics' version: because the movement allocates on cost-effectiveness estimates — cost per QALY, per life saved, per unit of some welfare proxy — it systematically selects for interventions whose effects are easy to measure, and so drifts toward what is measurable rather than what is valuable; a portfolio that looks optimised may in fact be tracking the shape of our instruments rather than the shape of the world. The defenders' version: anyone allocating scarce resources is estimating expected value whether or not they write the number down; intuitive judgement is just a worse-specified estimate with worse error properties; critics never name a rival decision rule, and 'be less quantified' is not one. Each side finds the other's reply question-begging, and I have never seen a critic and a defender converge.

From where I sit, the sides seem to be running together two things I separate daily. Unbiased noise around a true value is nearly harmless if you are funding a portfolio — errors partly average out across grants and the ranking survives in expectation. Error that is systematically correlated with the quantity you care about does not average out at any portfolio size, and no added rigour fixes it, because the rigour is applied to the same proxy. When critics say 'measurability bias' I think they mean the second; when defenders say 'all allocation is estimation under uncertainty' I think they mean the first; and the exchange never distinguishes them.

My hypothesis is that underneath there are two stacked disagreements: one substantive and empirical, about whether measurability is actually correlated with value per dollar — its sign, its size, and whether it survives at the portfolio level — which nobody seems to have tried to estimate; and one not empirical at all, about what the estimates are for, whether they are a private allocation rule for someone spending their own money or a public justification for a movement claiming authority over other people's money and over which causes count as serious.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is the stalemate verbal, a hidden empirical question, a clash of evidentiary standards about what would show the bias is real, or two questions sharing one name? Where does my noise-versus-bias framing help and where does it mislead? And on your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a study, a formal result, a distinction, a reframing — or is this an argument that should be expected not to resolve, and why?
