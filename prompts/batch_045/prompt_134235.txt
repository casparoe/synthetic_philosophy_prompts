I am the director of a small regional museum, and I need to think clearly about one decision before the board meets in eleven weeks.

We hold a large early-nineteenth-century painting that has always been catalogued as the work of a minor painter in the circle of a much better-known one. A visiting scholar argued last year, on stylistic grounds, that it is by the better-known painter himself. Within twelve months we must commit irreversibly to one of two five-year programmes, and the right one depends on which attribution is correct. Read as a minor work, the painting is the anchor of a regional-school narrative, insurable and lendable, and our plan would be built around a touring loan. Read as a major work, it cannot be lent or insured at anything like our current budget; the plan would instead be built around re-hanging the permanent collection, upgrading climate control and security, and keeping the picture at home. The programmes cost roughly the same, we cannot run both, and we will live with whichever we choose for a decade.

A technical examination could settle the question: three micro-samples from the tacking edge, pigment and ground-layer comparison, results in about four months, for a fee that is small next to either programme but not next to our acquisitions budget. Four complications. The sampling is irreversible, and our conservator objects to it on grounds she does not express in money. Whatever the result, it enters the object's file and is effectively public: we could not un-disclose an unflattering finding, and we would not suppress a flattering one. The laboratory tells us that in something like a third of cases of this kind the result comes back uninformative. And part of what either programme buys is an experience — for visitors, and candidly for me — that is partly constituted by what we believe the picture is; a downgrade would not merely change what we do, it would change what we and our public get.

The board is fourteen people who will not simply defer to me. Their priors on the upgrade range from roughly one in ten to better than even, they weight the regional-school narrative very differently, and two slogans are in circulation: that you cannot be hurt by knowing, and that some things are better not known. I suspect both are misuses of Good's theorem — the result that for an expected-utility maximizer the expected value of a free piece of information is never negative, and strictly positive whenever the information could change what one does.

Please describe the most important implications of that theorem for how we should reason here, in 700 to 900 words of prose a well-informed board member with no decision theory could follow.

I would like you to: state precisely what the theorem does license, including the difference between its ex ante guarantee and what can be said about any outcome actually realised, and between information that is merely costly and information whose acquisition is itself an intervention; work through which of its assumptions this situation violates and in which direction each violation pushes, separating belief-dependent value, the physical cost to the object, the effects of publicity on insurers, lenders, donors and the regional story, and the fact that the decision is made by a group with divergent priors rather than by one agent; tell me whether non-negativity survives if we cannot agree on a single prior and instead evaluate our options by their worst case over a range of plausible ones, and say plainly if you are unsure; and give me the quantitative skeleton we should actually use, including how to bound the examination's value from above, how to handle the uninformative-result case, how to compare deciding now with more time to execute against waiting with less, and how to treat the conservator's objection without either ignoring it or letting it function as a veto. Close by saying what each of the two board slogans gets right and what it gets wrong.

Do not cite, name, or recommend any reading; no bibliography, no authors, no papers. Reason from the structure of the problem only, and where a formal claim is contested or depends on modelling choices, say so and explain the dependence instead of asserting the claim.
