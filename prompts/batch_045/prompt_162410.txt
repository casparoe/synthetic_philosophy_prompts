Grading short essays for an intro death-and-wellbeing unit today and could use a second pair of eyes — I've got about forty of these left. The question was:

Epicurus argues that death is not bad for the person who dies. Reconstruct his argument as charitably as you can, then give what you take to be the strongest objection to it. Finally: does whatever makes the argument work also cover a *temporary* gap in someone's existence — say, being painlessly suspended without any experience for a century and then waking up? Say why or why not; most people mishandle that last bit.

Here is one answer, unedited:

Epicurus' argument goes like this. Death is the dissolution of the body and the soul together, so once you are dead there is no you left. But a thing can be good or bad for someone only if there is someone for it to be good or bad for, and when death is present we are not. So death is nothing to us: it cannot be bad for the person who dies, which is why fearing your own death is irrational and why nothing bad happens to the dead person.

This is not to say that death is not bad for anyone. Obviously it is usually very bad for the friends and family of the person who dies. The conclusion is only about prudential badness for the person herself, and that is what makes the argument interesting — it targets self-interested fear, not grief.

The strongest objection is the deprivation account. Death is bad for a person not because of anything she experiences while dead but because of what it takes away: the years of pleasure, achievement and relationship she would have had if she had not died when she did. The Epicurean tradition presses hardest here with the symmetry point: the infinite stretch of time before we were born did us no harm, so why should the infinite stretch after? The standard reply to this is the asymmetry of our attitudes and desires: our desires point forward and not backward, so the years before birth deprive us of nothing we wanted, whereas death frustrates desires we actually have. The prenatal years are therefore no guide to the posthumous ones. That, I think, settles the dispute.

The last part of the question is easy. If not existing is never bad for you, then a century of dreamless suspension is not bad for you either, since exactly the same premise — that there is no subject during the gap — is doing all the work. The argument generalises, so anyone who accepts Epicurus here has to accept the suspension case too.
