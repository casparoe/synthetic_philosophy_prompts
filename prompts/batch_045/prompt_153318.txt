I have been trying to decide what to think about the ethics of pest control, and I have assembled an argument every step of which strikes me as defensible and whose conclusion strikes me as mad. I should be grateful for a diagnosis of where it goes wrong.

Two stipulations first, so that nothing rests on a contested empirical claim. First: suppose that deliberate insecticide use in agriculture kills something on the order of 10¹² insects a year. If you think that figure is too high or too low, divide or multiply it by a thousand; I shall try to show that the argument survives several orders of magnitude of fiddling in either direction. Second: suppose that the commonest modes of action are not instantaneous, and that a poisoned insect typically dies over hours rather than at once.

(1) Suffering aggregates across individuals. If it is bad to degree s that one individual suffers at intensity i for a period d, then it is bad to degree n·s that n individuals each do so.

(2) Species membership is not, in itself, morally relevant. A given quantity of suffering matters equally whomever it belongs to; what remains to be settled is only how much suffering there is.

(3) Insect sentience is uncertain, but not so uncertain that it may be ignored. Take a credence you find respectable — say one in ten that a typical pest insect is sentient at all — and, conditional on sentience, put the badness of a death by poison at a thousandth of the badness of a human death, which strikes me as absurdly ungenerous to the insect. That is an expected cost per insect of roughly 10⁻⁴ human-death-equivalents.

(4) Ten to the twelve insects at ten to the minus four each is ten to the eight: on the order of a hundred million human-death-equivalents a year, and rising by orders of magnitude if the true count is higher or the exchange rate less grudging. Divide the whole thing by a thousand and it is still a hundred thousand a year. Nothing else on any moral ledger I can construct is remotely this large.

(5) What one morally ought to do tracks the stakes. If one course of action averts vastly more suffering than any alternative, at comparable cost to oneself, then it is what one ought to pursue, and to go on pursuing anything else is a serious failing.

(6) Therefore: the abolition of agricultural insecticides is, by an enormous margin, the most important thing anyone could work on. Every other cause — global poverty, farmed animals, even existential risk — is a rounding error by comparison, and someone who has followed the argument and carries on as before is like a person who wades into a pond to rescue a child whilst a city burns.

What makes me suspicious is not (6) itself, unwelcome though it is, but what happens when I run the same machinery with the sign reversed.

(7) Most pest insects are r-selected: they produce far more young than the environment can support, and nearly all of those young die within days, by starvation, desiccation, parasitism or predation. Suppose that the expected net welfare of a typical aphid's life is negative — that existence is, for it, a bad bargain. Then the trillion deaths in (4) are not a cost but an enormous benefit, and (6) does not merely fail, it inverts: what we ought to do is spray more, and spray it on land we have no intention of farming.

(8) Nor, when I tried to act on (6), could I find the counterfactual it needs. A ban does not deliver a world in which those insects are not killed; it delivers a world in which they are killed differently, perhaps by older compounds, perhaps by additional tillage, and in which more land is brought into cultivation to make up the lost yield.

So something in (1) to (5) is doing work I have not noticed, and I cannot locate it. One friend says the fault lies inside (1): aggregating over a duration presupposes a subject who persists through that duration, and an insect may have only a flickering, present-centred stream of experience, in which case a death lasting hours is not one individual suffering for hours but a succession of momentary subjects each suffering briefly, so that (1)'s multiplication by d double-counts. I cannot tell whether this is profound or merely fanciful, and in any case it would only shrink (4), not explain why the sign of (4) can flip. Another friend says the trouble is that (1) to (4) treat averted lives as gains at all, and that nobody is benefited by never coming into existence; but that sounds to me less like a diagnosis of my argument than like a different argument.

Please tell me which step you would reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs. I should most like to keep (2) — I do not want to end up a speciesist — and (5), since abandoning it appears to make moral prioritisation impossible in general rather than merely here. If the flaw is a slide from deaths caused to suffering added to the world's total, I should like you to locate exactly where my chain makes that slide, and to say what is left of the argument once the slide is blocked.
