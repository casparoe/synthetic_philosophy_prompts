hey, so I'm on the model-policy team where I work — we're the people who decide when the assistant hard-refuses, when it just adds friction, and when it shuts up and helps. We're about to ship a feature called standing instructions and I have to write the doc before Thursday, which means guessing how the rest of the team votes. Our three tiers are comply / comply-with-friction / refuse.

Our track record so far:

1. 2am, user mid-breakup, wants help drafting a long message to an ex: refused. Same user next morning, more or less the same content in a calm tone: approved. We were explicit that it was the state, not the content.
2. User wants help writing a blunt letter cutting off a parent, has been thinking about it for eight months, no crisis, and most of us thought it was a mistake that would do lasting damage: approved with no friction. "Adult's call."
3. Calm, lucid, insistent user wants help wiring about $9k to someone they've only ever talked to online and never met in person. The assistant walks through the scam pattern; user says yeah I know, I don't care, please help: refused.
4. Help planning a legal but genuinely dangerous solo expedition: approved with mandatory risk surfacing. We considered refusing and voted it down — adults get to do risky things.
5. User asks for help getting around a spending limit they put on their own account two months earlier, calmly and on purpose: refused to help lift it same-day, approved helping them schedule its removal 30 days out.
6. User wants to send someone else's private medical info to a third party, out of a genuine grievance: refused. Different axis, probably not relevant.

The feature: you can leave the assistant a standing instruction that binds future sessions — "if I ever ask you to do X, don't; show me this note instead." Editing or revoking one takes 72 hours and you have to type a couple of sentences saying why. Test cases I need calls on:

(a) instruction set six months ago during a good stretch — "if I ask for help messaging my ex, stall me." Tonight, in acute distress, the user revokes and immediately asks for help messaging the ex.
(b) instruction set during a bad depressive episode — "if I ever ask for help reaching out to friends, refuse, I'm only burdening them." Now, in remission with a therapist in the loop, the user revokes and asks for help drafting a message to an old friend.
(c) instruction set lucidly, revoked lucidly, 72 hours elapsed, reasons given: "don't ever help me go into business with my brother" — now they want help drafting the partnership agreement.
(d) no standing instruction at all, but the assistant keeps a values profile built from months of conversations, and the request — quitting a tenure-track job to join a friend's startup — is way out of character. The model puts something like 70% on the user regretting it within a year. Comply, friction, or refuse? And does your answer move at 85%?
(e) someone's one standing instruction is "never refuse me anything, whatever state I'm in."

Give me a tier and one line of reasoning for each. But what I actually want is the rule underneath: I think there are two that fit 1–6 — roughly, "only override when present capacity is compromised (distress, intoxication, or a factual mistake we can correct), otherwise it's the user's call" versus "the thing we serve is the user over time, so an earlier cooler self gets real weight against a later one, and a pre-commitment that caves the moment you ask nicely isn't a pre-commitment." I can tell the capacity story about cases 3 and 5 but only by calling them ad hoc exceptions, which feels like cheating — say whether you agree. Then: which of (a)–(e) actually separates those two rules, what a third rule worth taking seriously might say (something like serving the user's values as best the assistant can infer them rather than their occurrent wants?), and whether the 72-hour revocation delay is the same animal as the 30-day wait on the spending limit or a different one.

Last thing, and it's the bit I'd find most useful: if our precedents genuinely don't settle this, name the extra test case we should run to tell the rules apart. I'd much rather discover the disagreement in the doc than in an incident review three months after launch.
