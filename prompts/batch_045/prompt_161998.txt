I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a large teaching hospital trust — four years now, as one of the two lay members — and I've also spent an embarrassing amount of time over the years on the rationalist blogs, where "pain that isn't unpleasant" is a stock intuition pump everyone deploys as though it settled something. It has now arrived as a real case, and it is far messier than the thought experiment. Mr H is 58, six weeks into recovery from a right-hemisphere stroke with insular involvement, admitted with deep partial-thickness burns to his left forearm and hand after falling onto a heater while hemiplegic. He needs daily dressing changes and debridement and then a split-skin graft. On formal testing he localises pinprick and discriminates temperature normally and rates the debridement at 8 out of 10 for intensity, but he reports no unpleasantness whatsoever: he chats through the procedure, says the pain is "there but completely uninteresting", and has twice re-injured his graft donor site because nothing tells him to stop leaning on it. He has refused paracetamol, NSAIDs, ketamine and opioids, on the stated grounds that they fog his thinking and he wants to stay sharp for the decisions about his rehabilitation. The burns team say they will not take him to theatre without analgesia or anaesthesia; two of the anaesthetists and the ward sister have said they would conscientiously refuse to participate in a painful procedure conducted without any analgesia at all; our legal officer's note says a capacitous refusal binds us and that best-interests reasoning belongs to patients who lack capacity; and our own policy uses the phrase "unnecessary suffering" without ever saying what suffering consists in. Mr H has also signed a statement asking us to give him analgesia "the moment I say it bothers me", which several of us find worthless as a safeguard precisely because the thing that would tell him it bothers him is the thing he lacks.

What I think the committee is actually arguing about, underneath the procedural noise, is this: take the textbook decomposition of pain into a sensory-discriminative component, an affective-motivational one and a cognitive-evaluative one, and ask which of them carries the badness. If the badness lives in the aversiveness — in the hurting, or in the imperative that commands you to stop and withdraw — then Mr H may have nothing we are obliged to protect, and pressuring him into analgesia he has capacity to refuse starts to look like paternalism about a state that is not bad for him. If the badness lives in the sensory state itself, or is at least partly independent of whether the patient minds, then a trust that knowingly debrides a burn on an unanaesthetised patient is doing something it should not do, whatever he says, and the staff refusals are the soundest moral data in the room. I don't think we can dodge it by saying pain is subjective and self-reported, because we also have Ms R on the chronic pain service: minimal findings on imaging and a low intensity rating, but she describes her pain as catastrophic and is asking for dose escalation that the pain team thinks will kill her. Any principle we write for Mr H has to come out the other end saying something coherent about her, and I'd rather the memo say plainly if our principle treats them differently and why that isn't just inconsistency. One of our members has already circulated the line that Mr H's pain is like a fire alarm with the bell disconnected — the signal is still running, and damage is still being signalled, whether or not anyone hears it — and I suspect that framing is carrying more weight than it deserves, but I'd like it addressed rather than ignored.

So: please draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the committee before we meet on the 14th. It should be a document the committee could actually vote on — a clear recommendation for Mr H with a time frame and a named fallback if he threatens to self-discharge, the general principle behind it stated tightly enough that the pain service can apply it to Ms R, and a note on how we handle the conscientious objection from the clinical staff if we go the other way. It has to be readable by a consultant, a ward sister and a lay member in one sitting, with no jargon I'd have to explain, and it must not lean on any published literature — nobody on this committee wants a reading list, and we couldn't follow one up anyway. Most importantly, whichever way it comes down, I want the strongest version of the losing view stated as well as or better than its advocates would state it: if the recommendation is to respect his refusal, give the best case for the view that a hospital which inflicts unanaesthetised pain on a patient is doing him a wrong he cannot waive by indifference; if it is to treat him against his wishes over analgesia, give the best case for the view that we are overriding a lucid, capacitous man's judgement about his own mind on the basis of a theory about what pain really is.
