I'm in a small reading group that's working through Hobbes's system more or less in the order he built it — De Corpore first, then a bit of De Cive — and I've got stuck on something I thought I understood. The outline everyone repeats is: philosophy is knowledge of effects from causes and causes from effects, gotten by reasoning from definitions; geometry is the model science because its first principles are definitions we make ourselves, so we know the causes of the things it's about, so it's genuinely demonstrative; natural philosophy can't be demonstrative because we didn't make nature and have to guess at hidden causes from observed effects; and civil philosophy is demonstrable after all because we make the commonwealth ourselves by covenant. That all sounds beautifully tidy when I say it fast, and then falls apart as soon as I try to say any part of it precisely.

Here's where I lose the thread. Hobbes is a thoroughgoing nominalist — truth and falsity belong to speech, not things, and there's nothing universal but names — but he also says geometry is the one science God has seen fit to bestow on humankind, and De Corpore treats it as the science of body and quantity generally, i.e. as genuinely about the physical world, the one part of his system that isn't guesswork. So: (1) If definitions are stipulations of names, what stops a physicist from stipulating definitions and getting a demonstrative physics too? My own stab at an answer is that for Hobbes a real definition has to state the generation of the thing defined — the motion that produces it, like a circle being traced by a line swung about a fixed end — so a definition isn't arbitrary at all, it's constrained by how the thing can actually be brought about. I've started calling these generation-definitions as opposed to inventory-definitions that just list marks and properties, and I want to know whether that's actually his constraint, whether he's consistent about it, and whether it's what's supposed to carry the whole weight of the geometry/natural-philosophy asymmetry. (2) Relatedly, what connects the stipulation to the world? Two of us disagree: one says Hobbes is basically doing axiomatic/implicit definition the way a later mathematician would — you lay down constraints and everything that satisfies them is your subject matter, and applying it to real space is a separate, empirical business — and I think that's anachronistic and quietly drops the causal and constructive stuff he seems to care about, but I can't say exactly what it leaves out. Is there a third option, e.g. that the definitions are legitimate precisely because the generating motions are ones we can actually perform, so that geometrical practice and physical possibility are tied together from the start? (3) A worry I can't shake: if the theorems follow from our own stipulations, is Hobbes committed to saying something like the Pythagorean theorem holding only given our naming conventions, in a way that makes it contingent? That seems like the wrong thing for him to be committed to, but I can't see how he's entitled to anything stronger.

Two more things, in case they help pin it down. First, my own little test case: suppose a Hobbesian geometer stipulates a definition of some figure whose specified generating motion no body could ever perform — a line of no breadth, or a motion completed in an instant, that sort of thing — and then proves elegant theorems about it. On the permissive reading of stipulation he's got a science; on the restrictive reading he's got a fiction, or a name with nothing under it. Which does Hobbes's own apparatus say, and does he have resources to distinguish the two cases, or does the distinction smuggle in something his nominalism can't have? Second, does any of this explain his actual mathematical career — the repeated attempts to square the circle and the long public fight with Wallis? I don't want the gossip version of that quarrel; I want to know whether a philosopher with his theory of definition and of knowing-by-causes would be systematically inclined to think he'd established something when the calculation said otherwise, or whether his geometric blunders are just orthogonal to the theory. Ideally I'd come away knowing what Hobbes takes a definition to be, what makes one definition scientific and another not, and why the we-make-it-ourselves criterion is supposed to be the hinge of the whole system — and also which parts of my paraphrase above are already distortions.
