Hey — I need a class activity, not a lecture, and I've been going in circles on this one.

Setup: 12–14 third-year undergrads (mostly philosophy, a few psych majors), one 75-minute seminar slot, one room, whiteboard, no tech beyond that. They'll have done the reading before class: Hacking on looping effects and on making up people. And they will have already had the thought everyone has, which is that this is just labeling theory or self-fulfilling prophecy with fancier vocabulary. I want them to lose that thought, and I want them to run into it rather than be told it.

Here's where I keep getting stuck. It seems to me there are at least three different things people collapse into one word, and I want an activity that separates them:

(a) the one everybody stages — a label becomes available, people re-experience and re-present themselves in its terms, and the kind and the people co-evolve;
(b) a second one: the *classifiers* move too. The criteria get revised because of how the classified responded, so the science is aimed at a moving target and things like prevalence figures, course, and cause start drifting in ways that aren't just measurement error;
(c) a third one I don't have a name for: institutions — insurers, schools, disability systems, courts — pay out on the label, so presentations gradually come to fit whatever the forms can recognize, clinicians get trained to read exactly those presentations, and the category slowly becomes the thing the paperwork can see. Call it the administrative loop, or whatever you'd rather call it.

What I want is a runnable activity for that group and that time budget that gets them to actually run into (b) and (c), not just (a). I can already picture the obvious version — hand out a made-up sorting quiz, watch people act the part, reveal the trick — and I think it mostly just re-derives stereotype threat and lets everyone conclude they already knew the reading. Please dont give me that one. I'd like them to build the loop themselves across the session and not hear the phrase until the debrief.

Specific asks:

- Actual rules, roles, materials, a rough minute-by-minute timeline, and what small stakes you'd use that are real enough to bite but don't involve money or anything that touches their grade.
- Where the fake category comes from, and — I keep going back and forth on this — whether it should sound psychiatric at all or be something deliberately boring like a typing of learning styles or handwriting. Argue me into one.
- How (a), (b) and (c) each show up as a distinct moment they can point back to afterwards, and what you'd do in the room to make them notice each one as it happens.
- There's no insurer in my seminar room. What plays the institutional role, and how do you make the payout structure felt rather than explained?
- The ethics of doing this to your own students: what are you actually doing to people by sorting them under a kind you invented, do you owe them a debrief that fully unpacks the deception, and is there a version where they consent up front without the whole effect evaporating? Be concrete, I have to defend this to someone.
- A debrief that maps moments in the session onto the three loops, and that's honest about which part of the picture the activity just cannot stage. My guess is time-scale — the real loops take decades and generations, and the people classified sometimes campaign *for* the label rather than against it — but say what you actually think, and say what I'll have to supply by talking instead.
- What will go wrong the first time I run it. Including the version where one student says something like 'so you're saying my diagnosis isnt real' and the room goes quiet.

And if my three-way split is wrong, or there's a fourth loop I'm missing, or (c) is really just (b) viewed from the billing department — say so and design around your version instead. I'd rather have the better activity than be right about the taxonomy.
