I'm marking a take-home for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar and would like an independent read on one answer before I finalize the mark. The question and the answer are reproduced below. In setting the question I wanted students to keep three things apart: the time at which an experience occurs, the time that its content represents the change as occurring, and the time at which the subject later reports having had it.

Question 3 (25 marks). In the color phi phenomenon a red spot is flashed briefly at one position and, a fraction of a second later, a green spot is flashed at a nearby position. Subjects report a single spot travelling from the first position to the second and changing color partway along its path — and they place the color change earlier than the second flash. Dennett uses such cases to contrast Orwellian revision, in which a version of events is laid down and then retrospectively rewritten, with Stalinesque revision, in which the doctored version is what gets laid down in the first place.

(a) State what each hypothesis claims about when the subject's experience occurs and about what it is an experience of. [8]

(b) We discussed a third option in the seminar: "antedating without revision." On this option there is exactly one relevant experience; it occurs after the second flash; its content presents the color change as occurring midway along the path; and no earlier experience is laid down, edited, or overwritten. Is this a genuinely distinct option, or only Stalinesque revision described differently? Defend one answer. [9]

(c) Does the Orwellian/Stalinesque contrast, as Dennett draws it, presuppose what his multiple-drafts model denies — namely that there is a determinate time at which a given experience occurs? [8]

Student's answer:

(a) On the Orwellian hypothesis the subject first has the experience the stimuli would lead you to expect — a red spot at the first position, then a green spot at the second — and only afterwards, when they recall or report it, is that record rewritten so that they describe a single spot that changed color as it moved. On the Stalinesque hypothesis the rewriting is done in advance: the content that gets laid down already contains the moving, color-changing spot, so the subject never has the unedited experience at all and nothing is revised later. The two accounts agree about the data: the subject has an experience as of a spot changing color midway along the path, and they have it before the second flash is presented — that is simply what they report, and their reports are the data that any hypothesis has to accommodate. The accounts disagree only about how that experience acquired its content, whether by later editing of an earlier record or by being written with that content to begin with.

(b) Antedating without revision is not a third option; it is Stalinesque revision under another name. In both cases the subject's only experience has the edited content, and in neither case is there an earlier experience that gets corrected. Anyway, the two hypotheses Dennett contrasts are not really two hypotheses at all. They are indistinguishable from the subject's point of view and from the experimenter's — nothing the subject can say or do, and nothing an experimenter could record, would tell them apart — so they are two descriptions of one and the same neural process, and the question "when did the experience occur?" has no answer.

(c) Yes. The contrast only makes sense if we can ask, of a given experience, when it occurred and whether it was subsequently altered; that is to assign experiences determinate dates and to treat one of them as the authoritative record. That is just the Cartesian theater Dennett rejects. On the multiple-drafts model there is no single place or time at which content becomes conscious; there are only many content-fixations with various fates, and no privileged one among them. So Dennett's own view dissolves the question rather than answering it, which is why he holds that no experiment could ever decide between the two hypotheses.
