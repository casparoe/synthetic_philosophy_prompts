Hey — I'm a PhD student in robotics (HRI-ish) and I've somehow become the person in our lab who writes the weekly behaviour report on our fleet of hospital delivery robots. It's about 12-15 logged episodes a week, roughly 15-20 minutes each, and the report goes to the hospital's clinical safety committee: smart people, not technical at all, and they will definately read a sentence like "the robot wanted to avoid the glare" as a claim about the robot's mental life, or worse, about who's liable. Right now I write whatever comes out fastest, which is usually intentional-stance soup, and my supervisor and I keep having the same argument about it.

So: I want a procedure, not a discussion. Something I can actually run on a logged episode, in order.

The stuff I'm trying to operationalise is Dennett's three stances — physical, design, intentional — together with the idea that a pattern is real when adopting a stance buys you compression: a description of the behaviour that beats the raw frame-by-frame description. That's the bit I want turned into a checklist, and I want the simplifications to be honest about which distinctions they're dropping.

One wrinkle I keep tripping over and that I want the procedure to handle explicitly: as I understand it the compression test is a test on the data. Which means a pattern that lives in my logging pipeline rather than in the robot still counts as real by that test — the sampler generates it. I need a step that separates pattern-in-the-log from pattern-in-the-thing, and I don't know how to do it without opening the box, which I can't: vendor-supplied, black box, no internals, and I can't just re-run the episode either, these are one-offs in a live corridor.

What I need from you:

- The questions to ask about an episode, in order, doable in ~15-20 min with no access to internals.
- What counts as a sufficient answer to each one. What I usually have is: the log, a video, the ward map, and the planner's published spec.
- Where the procedure stops with a no — i.e. hard rule, do not use intentional vocabulary here.
- Where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and which person, given that the committee is the audience and my supervisor signs off before it goes out.
- What to do when I can't get anything resembling a compression number: one-off episode, no baseline, no repetition to speak of.

Then run it on three episodes from last month:

1. A robot takes a reliably longer route that avoids a stretch of corridor with afternoon sun glare. Everyone on the team says "it avoids the glare." There's no glare sensor and nothing about glare in the cost map as far as the spec shows; the route just correlates with time of day.
2. A robot "hesitates" for about 3 seconds at one particular doorway, roughly 60% of the time, before going through. We found a fixed 3-second planner timeout in the spec. It feels like the design stance should kill the intentional story here, but the intentional story is by far the fastest thing for me to write and by far the easiest thing for the committee to read.
3. A robot waits near the nurses' station until a nurse swipes, then follows behind them through a door it can't open on its own, at the two times of day when nurses are actually moving. I love describing this as "it waits for a nurse," and I suspect your procedure is going to take it away from me. If it does, say so straight. If it doesn't, tell me what I'd have to go check in order to earn it.

Two more things. First: sometimes two stances compress the same episode about equally well and give conflicting write-ups. I need a tie-break rule, and I'd rather it be explicit than be left to my mood on a friday afternoon. Second: be honest about where this procedure misfires. My guesses are cases where the intentional stance predicts beautifully but is useless or actively misleading when you want to intervene on or fix the system, and timescale problems — patterns that only show up over months or over days. Say clearly what the procedure cannot do.
