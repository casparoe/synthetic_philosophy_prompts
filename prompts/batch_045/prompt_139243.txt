I am drafting a two-page pitch for internal seed funding: one researcher for twelve months, no teaching, to work on a single question in collective decision-making. The topic is safe Pareto improvements, and I want the money to go to a question I think the literature has left in an awkward place.

The notion as I am working with it: there is a status quo policy, a feasible set of alternatives, finitely many people, and finitely many states of the world. Each person has a utility function over outcomes and a probability distribution over states. A move from the status quo to an alternative is a safe Pareto improvement when, for every person, that person's own expected utility — computed from their own probabilities and their own utility function — is higher under the alternative than under the status quo. Its appeal is that it needs no interpersonal comparison and no social welfare function: a planner who has no view at all on the merits can apparently still make everyone better off, where better off is spelled out in terms each person would accept.

My half-formed worry is that the guarantee is conditional on people's actual beliefs, and beliefs are precisely what the planner does not observe. If the planner has to ask, then anyone who expects to dislike the proposed change can report probabilities under which it looks bad, and reporting pessimism is costless; symmetrically, people can report confident beliefs to push through changes they favour. So safety may turn out to be safety relative to reported beliefs only. Behind that strategic point sits a structural one: the status quo is the disagreement point, no change is available for free, and anyone can in principle secure it by reporting suitably unhelpful probabilities. I suspect some kind of impossibility is available — that a rule which (i) selects a safe Pareto improvement whenever one exists relative to the true beliefs, (ii) never gives anyone an expected gain from misreporting their probabilities, and (iii) is neither a rule that always keeps the status quo nor one that consults a single designated person, either cannot be had or can be had only under a characterization so narrow that it shows the notion is not institutionally usable. But I do not know whether the honest conclusion is pessimistic about safe Pareto improvements or reconstructive of them, and that ambiguity is partly why I want the project scoped properly.

What I need is a proposal that does the following. State the question so that it has a determinate answer — a theorem, a characterization, or a clearly described counterexample space — rather than a topic. Fix and justify the modelling choices: whether utilities are taken as observable while beliefs are private, or both are private; whether the people are assumed to share a prior; whether the planner can commit to a rule in advance. Explain why the existing answers leave it open: say what the standard treatments of safe Pareto improvements are doing when they help themselves to each person's beliefs, and say why the familiar impossibility results about strategy-proofness and about the tension between incentive compatibility and efficiency do not simply settle the matter, given that here the private object is a probability rather than a preference over outcomes and the default is the status quo rather than an arbitrary alternative. Argue for one approach over the obvious alternatives — in particular over the move to a robust or ambiguity-set version, where a change must raise each person's expected utility under every probability in a set attributed to them, and over a purely dynamic treatment in which each step is an improvement but the sequence may be exploitable — and say which of these belongs in the twelve months and which has to be left for later. Give a month-by-month plan for the year, including one minimal worked example with two or three people and two or three states that fixes intuitions and that the project would actually solve early on. Say what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them. Say what would count as the project having failed, and what would count as a disappointing but still useful result. Finally, describe a concrete deliverable and make the case, in a sentence or two, for why twelve months of one person's time is the right size for this and not for something larger.

Describe positions and arguments by their content, not by author or paper: I will fill in the references myself, and I do not want the proposal's plausibility to rest on a citation list. Be straight about what could make the project too big for the time available.
