I'm writing the third section of a dissertation chapter on the dogmatism paradox, where I want to argue that the argument's plausibility is sensitive to the kind of would-be defeater involved. I need a matched pair of cases built to order, not illustrations.

The argument as I will be using it: take an ordinary empirical p that a subject knows — nothing stage-managed, just something someone knows by perception, memory or testimony — and let e be something the subject has not looked into which, were it true, would count against p. Since knowledge is factive, the subject can infer: p; I know p; so e is misleading; so there is no need to investigate e. The inference appears to yield knowledge of not-e, and license to ignore e, without the subject's ever having examined the evidence — that is the scandal. The three replies I am taking seriously are: deny the closure principle the inference needs; grant the inference but insist it delivers no knowledge the subject did not already have, because knowing p already requires that one's total evidence outweigh e; and accept it, treating the scandal as a feature rather than a bug.

What I want is a pair of cases, as closely matched as possible — same protagonist, same p, same grounds for p — differing in one structural respect, such that in the first the dogmatic response is not merely permissible but better than investigating, while in the second, with the subject still genuinely knowing p, the same response is clearly bad epistemically: someone who reasoned that way and turned out to be right would still have reasoned badly.

Constraints: (i) ordinary and non-sceptical — a lab, a clinic, a newsroom, a department meeting; no demons, no brains in vats, no fake barns, nothing I could not present in half a page of a journal article; (ii) the subject really does know p in both cases, so the bad case cannot work by making the subject merely believe p or by making p false; (iii) the difference must not be one of degree — not that e is stronger, p more important, or investigation costlier in the bad case; (iv) the good case must not be propped up by a defeater-defeater: the subject may not independently know that e is misleading or that its source is unreliable, since the licence to dismiss has to come from knowing p plus the shape of the situation; (v) one paragraph per case.

Then I want: the argument run on both cases with the exact point of divergence located; the single feature doing the work and why it is not strength-of-evidence in disguise; an ablation, i.e. what is left when that feature is removed — the pair should collapse into one uninteresting case, and I want you to say so if it does not; which of the three replies above fares best and worst against the pair; and the strongest objection an outright dogmatist can make to your bad case, with your view on whether it lands. Cheating I will be watching for: a bad case in which the subject does not really know p, a good case in which the dismissal is independently justified anyway, or a structural difference that turns out on inspection to be a difference in how much evidence the subject has.
