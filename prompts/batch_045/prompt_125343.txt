I've got a 90-minute slot next week on Otto Neurath and Isotype in a second-year data-viz / information design studio, and I need the plan for the session, not the content. I know the material reasonably well; what I keep failing at is the order.

Who's in the room: about twenty second-years who can put together a perfectly good chart in Figma or d3, have sat through exactly one lecture of the "maximise data-ink" variety, and switch off the moment something smells like theory. Their reflex going in will be that Isotype is icons — nice little pictograms, airport-sign energy, the point being to make boring numbers friendlier. I want that reflex broken by about minute twenty.

The angle I'm teaching (this is my own framing, argue with it if it won't hold up in front of students): Isotype isn't a visual style, it's an argument plus an institution. My claim is that the actual invention isn't the pictogram — Gerd Arntz drew those, and the museum in Vienna that Neurath ran is where it all happened — it's the "transformer," the person whose job it was to turn a table of numbers into a picture sketch, which was Marie Reidemeister's role from early on. On that reading Isotype is a procedure with a division of labour and a house rulebook, not a look, and the famous design rules (bigger quantities shown by more same-sized symbols rather than one fat symbol, no perspective, no decorative colour) aren't aesthetic taste at all: they drop out of wanting the reader to count instead of squint and compare areas.

So: give me the plan. Specifically —

- The order the ideas have to go in, and what depends on what. I genuinely don't know whether the "count, don't estimate" point has to land before the rules make sense, or whether you need to show them the rules broken first and let them feel the failure. Say why, and give me rough minute marks.
- The one example that should carry the whole session. I've been using two countries' populations shown two ways — one big figure vs. a row of identical little ones — but tell me what that example must have and must lack to do the job (does it need to be a quantity they care about? does it need a third case? should it be something they can't already imagine?)
- The misconceptions, in roughly the order they'll actually surface, and the point in the sequence where I head each off. My guesses: "so it's just emoji" or "we already do unit charts"; "so he wanted pictures to replace words entirely" (I'm pretty sure he didn't — it was a helping language, always with words — but I want to know whether to use that to close the loop on why pictures can't say "not" or express doubt, or whether that's over my students' heads); "so it's just Red Vienna propaganda" from the one politics-y kid, and "so it's neutral science" from everyone else; "so the perspective ban is just because the symbols were woodcuts."
- What I should flat-out refuse to get into in ninety minutes and say so out loud. I'm tempted by the Vienna Circle stuff, the protocol sentence dispute, the unity-of-science encyclopedia, the later Mundaneum years. Which of those do I name-and-drop in one clause, and which do I cut entirely?
- One thing to put in front of them near the end — a question or a tiny exercise — that would actually tell me whether they got "it's a procedure and a set of commitments, not a style," rather than just that they enjoyed the pictures. I don't want a reading list, I've got a one-page handout of Neurath's own rules already.

Write it in American English, as a plan I can walk in and teach from, and close with one last line that starts "One line:" and gives the whole arc of the session in a single sentence.
