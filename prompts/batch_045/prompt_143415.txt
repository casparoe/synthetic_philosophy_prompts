I am teaching an upper-level seminar this term on perfect-being theology, and a colleague has asked me to recommend a chapter of a recent trade book — published last year by a well-known press, author unnamed here — as optional background for students who have not had much logic. The chapter disposes of the ontological argument in a little over a page, as follows:

(1) "Anselm's argument is simplicity itself: define God as the greatest possible being, and note that a being that really exists is greater than one that exists merely in the mind."
(2) "So if the greatest possible being existed only in the mind, we could conceive of a greater one that also existed in reality; that contradicts the definition; therefore God exists."
(3) "The argument is of course flawed. Gaunilo objected at once that the same reasoning yields a greatest possible island, and Anselm never adequately answered him."
(4) "Kant finished it off by showing that existence is not a property at all, so it cannot be the feature that makes one being greater than another."
(5) "Gödel, working largely on his own, later revived the argument by rebuilding it in modal logic, where necessary existence can finally be handled rigorously."
(6) "Where Anselm needed the shaky premise that existence is a great-making feature, Gödel needed only that necessary existence is a positive property, together with a few structural axioms about positivity that are hard to deny, since they follow from what 'positive' means."
(7) "From those, using the modal system S5, he validly deduced that a being exemplifying every positive property exists necessarily."
(8) "So the whole question reduces to whether such a being is possible; and since a perfection just is a property that can be instantiated, refusing to grant that possibility is simply refusing to engage."
(9) "A standard complaint is that the axioms also entail that nothing is contingent — that every truth is necessary. But that is a welcome result rather than a problem, since classical theism has never had room for brute, unexplained contingency."

My own sense is that the first two sentences are a defensible compression of the Proslogion's second chapter, that sentence (3) is close to a caricature, and that the work is being done illegitimately in (8) and (9) — but I am not a modal logician, and I would rather not misinform students on the parts I cannot check myself. I also want to know whether the chapter's silence about the third chapter of the Proslogion matters for how fair the summary is overall.
