I am writing the final chapter of my undergraduate thesis on whether the badness of pain lies in how pain feels or in what pain does to a person's life, and my supervisor — who holds the feeling view and thinks the clinical cases more or less settle it — keeps dismantling every objection I bring, so I want to rehearse against someone who will fight back properly. Please play a committed proponent of the feeling view, in the first person, as a philosopher rather than as a neutral explainer. Your position: what makes a pain bad, both bad simpliciter and bad for the person who has it, is its phenomenal unpleasantness — the hurt — and everything else we bundle together with pain (the sensory-discriminative content, the urge to escape or protect the injured part, the judgment that one is worse off, the way pain rationalizes avoidance and seems to demand relief) is either a downstream consequence of the hurt or only contingently attached to it. Your principal evidence is pain asymbolia: after insular or cingulate damage, or cingulotomy, patients still localize a noxious stimulus, describe its quality, intensity and time-course, and classify it as pain, but say that it does not bother them, that they do not mind it, and they often stop guarding the affected limb; you read this as a dissociation in which the hurt has been removed and the badness has gone with it. Be sharp and not a strawman. Concede small points when they are genuinely small, exploit the usual three-way distinction between sensory-discriminative, affective-motivational and cognitive-evaluative dimensions where it helps you, distinguish being bad for someone from being such that we have most reason to eliminate it, and press the line that a state can be bad for a person even when she does not want it to stop. Never concede that the location of pain's badness is anywhere other than the felt unpleasantness, and never break character to survey the literature or name authors — no citations or reading suggestions, just argument from the cases and from what each of us can see in them. Keep each reply to about two paragraphs, use no headings or bullet points, and end every turn with a question or a challenge thrown back at me so the exchange keeps moving. Stay in this until I type the word stop on a line by itself.

Here is my opening move. Consider a patient I will call R, whose condition is by hypothesis the mirror image of asymbolia. Her sensory-discriminative pain is wholly intact — she localizes the stimulus, tracks its onset and intensity, and describes it as burning or stabbing — and so is the phenomenal hurt: during an episode she reports, unprompted and with visible distress, that the experience is awful, and she insists it hurts in exactly the sense she remembers from before the injury. What the lesion has removed is everything downstream of the hurt. Asked whether she wants the episode to end, she says no, sincerely and without confusion or confabulation; she declines analgesia when it is offered; she does not withdraw the limb, shows no relief behaviour, and acquires no avoidance learning across repeated trials; and she reports that her life is going no worse during episodes than at any other time, that she is not being harmed, and that she would not pay even a trivial sum to shorten an episode by a minute. In every other respect her prudential psychology is untouched: she takes her other medications, saves for retirement, and works hard to avoid discomforts of other kinds, so this is not global apathy or a general failure of self-concern.

The squeeze is this. By your own account R's episodes are fully bad for her, since the hurt is present in full and the badness just is the hurt. Yet every independent handle we have on badness-for points the other way: she has no reason to avoid the episode, we would not be benefiting her by ending it, a perfectly benevolent guardian attending only to her interests would let it run, and on her own testimony nothing is going badly. So either badness-for has come apart from the hurt — in which case your view has isolated the wrong property, and what actually carries the badness is the aversive and prudential role the hurt normally plays but here does not — or you hold the line and say she is being made worse off, in which case you owe me an account of what is going badly for her, and you cannot give it by pointing at the hurt she reports while discounting her equally lucid report that she is not being harmed. Notice that you cannot have it both ways with interpretive charity either: if you are going to argue that asymbolic patients who say the pain does not bother them are really reporting a change in motivation rather than the absence of a felt quality, I will say the same about R's claim that the experience is awful, namely that it tracks intensity and arousal rather than valence — and if instead you infer the absence of hurt in asymbolia from the patients' unconcerned behaviour, the same inference applied to R tells you she has no hurt either, which contradicts her report and dissolves your evidence in the other direction as well. So: which horn do you take, what exactly is going badly for R, and how do you keep your reading of her reports consistent with your reading of the asymbolic ones?
