hey — im working some rough notes up into an essay on the inefficacy objection and i'd like comments before i commit to writing the whole thing. i already know the basic dialectic (the Parfit style no-difference move, expected-difference replies, the gap between imperceptible-harm cases and threshold cases, participatory/expressive views), so please don't explain the background back to me and don't restate my notes. what i want is whether the central move holds up. notes first, then what i want from you.

setup. inefficacy: my skipping this chicken, or my staying home on election day, makes no difference, so i've got no reason to do it. i think two very different shapes of "makes no difference" get run together:

- diffuse: each act adds a tiny imperceptible sliver of harm, no thresholds anywhere (driving, household emissions). aggregation is roughly linear and the expected-difference reply is clean — real, small, add it up.
- spiky: there's a threshold, so my act does literally nothing in almost every nearby world and something big and lumpy in a vanishing few (the purchase that trips the next order to the supplier; the pivotal voter — think Florida 2000, a few hundred votes deciding a presidency). here the expected difference is a tail expectation, dominated by worlds i can barely describe, and its variance dwarfs its mean.

claim 1: the inefficacy intuition is much stronger in spiky cases than diffuse ones, and not because the expected harm is smaller — it can easily be larger in the spiky case. it's the shape. in spiky cases nearly all the expected difference sits in a measure-tiny set of worlds where i happen to be pivotal, and whether i'm pivotal depends on facts about thousands of other people's acts that are practically inaccessible to me. so the problem isn't that the difference is small, its that i can't locate it: in diffuse cases i can have ordinary knowledge that i'm doing a small harm; in spiky cases the expected-difference reply asks me to act on a quantity i can't point to. call this the locatability problem.

claim 2 (the practical move): if the reason against participating traces to expected difference, then it isn't specifically a reason to abstain — it's a reason to get the most difference-reduction per unit of cost. so: buy the chicken, and spend the price gap on whatever has the highest expected-difference-per-dollar against chicken production. if the abstainer's own numbers are right, abstention is just one item on a menu with a bad exchange rate, and refusing the whole menu while insisting the numbers are what matter looks inconsistent. call it leverage transfer. note it isn't offsetting: you're not cancelling a harm with a benefit, you're trading low-pivot-probability exposure for high-pivot-probability exposure at the same price.

claim 3 (where it eats itself): leverage transfer looks fine in the consequentialist currency and awful in the participatory/expressive one. on those views what's wrong is joining in — being one of the people who does this together — and buying the chicken while funding the advocates is if anything worse, since you've taken the role and then tried to buy your way out of what the role means. so the two currencies don't merely come apart here, they point opposite directions, and i don't think the essay can keep both. right now i lean toward arguing that leverage transfer is what you get if you take the expected-difference reply seriously, and that this is a reductio of that reply as a complete answer to inefficacy — the essay's real conclusion being about the limits of consequentialist replies, not about what to do on saturday.
