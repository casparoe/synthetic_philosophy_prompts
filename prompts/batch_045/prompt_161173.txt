Hi — I'm putting together a laminated one-pager for our hospital's clinical ethics consult service (I'm on the committee, and also, annoyingly, the person who ends up writing most of the consult notes). Here's the thing I keep running in to: two consultants write notes on basically the same case, both say something like "this is letting die, not killing," and then come out opposite ways on what we should actually do. So presumably we're all using "letting die" to mean different things and not noticing.

What I want from you is a decision tree — call it 7 to 9 branch questions and 8 to 10 leaves — that takes someone from a concrete ICU case to a position about what the killing / letting-die distinction is supposed to consist in. It should NOT be a tree about whether euthanasia is permissible or about any jurisdiction's law; I want the moral structure. The point is diagnostic: a consultant should be able to hold up their own gut judgment on a case and see which account of the distinction they're implicitly committed to.

Cases that must show up somewhere in the tree (add others if you want, but these are the ones we actually argue about):

- withdrawing the vent on a dependent patient vs. giving a lethal dose at the same family request — the baseline pair everyone already has an opinion about
- a treatment that has to be actively re-armed or reset (ventilator alarm, infusion pump, a 24-hour order) and nobody re-arms it: omission, or the last step of an ongoing doing?
- a handoff: one clinician writes the order and sets everything up, another actually disconnects; or one disconnects and a second declines to reconnect
- the surgeon who creates a temporary dependence (device, graft, whatever) and then declines the follow-up that would end it
- a competent patient refusing dialysis or refusing food and fluids, with the team going along
- the same external action done in order that the patient dies vs. done merely foreseeing death while aiming to stop burdensome treatment
- death that follows in about three minutes after extubation vs. death that takes three weeks after feeding-tube withdrawal — I want a node that forces people to say whether the length of the dying does any work
- a threat the patient arrived with vs. a threat we caused iatrogenically, where in both cases we simply don't fix it
- at least one case where letting die plainly looks worse than killing (protracted, ugly dying) so the tree has to say what happens when the asymmetry seems to flip

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: do the questions first and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space. Mark the unoccupied combinations and give me one sentence each on whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody's bothered. Name the leaves where you're actually confident, and write "unnamed" where you're not — I'd rather have an honest "unnamed" than a misattributed label, since residents are going to read this thing. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate instead of pretending it can.

Two more constraints: no node may just ask "is killing worse than letting die?" — every node has to be a case or a claim the views really diverge on, phrased so a nurse or a resident could answer it without knowing any theory names. And at the end, one short paragraph on which single node does the most work in a setting like ours, i.e. which question is actually generating most of our disagreements. That paragraph is what's going at the top of the handout.
