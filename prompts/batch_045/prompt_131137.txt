I am finishing a novel whose last section is set on a small fictional island, where the narrator has come to clear out the field station of her great-aunt, Ilse Vane, an ornithologist who spent forty years there studying one species: the lesser night rail, a flightless, nearly blind, nocturnal bird with no predators on the island and, so far as anyone can tell, nothing much looking at it. In the scene I can't get right, the narrator finds a single poem in Ilse's hand pinned above the workbench, and I've concluded it has to be a sestina: six unrhymed stanzas of six lines, roughly iambic and of comparable length, plus a three-line tornada, with the six end-words being six features of the bird — the turquoise throat patch, the fused ankle bone, the crest she never once saw raised, the claw on the first finger of the wing, the stones the bird swallows, and the song it sings only in the dark (single-word variants are fine if a better word serves the line, but keep them anatomical or material, and keep them six). I want the rotation itself to carry the argument: because the sestina returns the same six features in a different order each stanza, it can show what Ilse came to think was wrong with her own life's work — that an organism carved into separate traits will take a separate adaptive story for each, in any order, and that a programme which can always find a story is not thereby finding anything. So let the first three stanzas be the confident voice she used for thirty years, each trait given a clean for-the-sake-of-which explanation, and let the turn come in the fourth with the split she insisted on late in life: a feature's present usefulness is one question and the history that put it there is another, and some things are not built at all but are simply what is left where two other things meet.

The distinctions I need the poem to make without ever naming them are these, from Ilse's notebooks: first, the strict spandrel — a feature forced by the geometry of something else that was selected, the corner left over when two arches meet, selected for nothing at all. Second, exaptation — material built for one purpose or for none and later taken up for a use, so that its current job is real but its origin lies elsewhere. Third, a category Ilse coined for herself and that no one else uses, which she called a rafter: a feature whose contribution to survival is real and measurable, so that if you removed it the animal would die, but which was never selected to hold anything up — it was scaffolding once, and everything has since been built to depend on it, which is exactly why its history keeps getting misread as a design brief. Give the throat patch three competing stories in the early stanzas (display, though there is no audience; species recognition, though there are no congeners; a byproduct of the pigment that also hardens the eggshell, in which case it may later have been taken up by the chicks, who peck at it), and let the tornada, which gathers all six end-words into three lines and thus refuses to consider them one at a time, end on a line that settles which of the three the throat patch actually is. One of the six must be given no story anywhere in the poem, and the refusal has to read as rigor rather than laziness. Somewhere in the last two stanzas Ilse should say, in plain language and with no jargon, what observation she would have had to make to abandon one of her own accounts — and the poem should let us understand that she never made it.

Two things I'd ask you to hold on to. Ilse is not a hostile witness: she thinks most of her adaptive explanations were correct, and the poem shouldn't turn into a sermon against selection or a shrug about how everything is a bit of everything — the complaint is about a method that cannot fail, not about the mechanism. And it should work twice: someone who has never heard of the adaptationism dispute should read a good poem about a blind bird on an island, while someone who knows that dispute should find the structure — the origins-versus-current-utility distinction, the complaint about atomizing the organism, the possibility of a feature that is load-bearing but never chosen — genuinely rendered rather than name-dropped. Please don't put the names of any scientists in it, don't include a preface or notes on the form, and use American English throughout. Give me the sestina and nothing else, then, as the last line of your reply, the machine-readable verdict on the throat patch in exactly this form: \answer{spandrel}, \answer{exaptation}, or \answer{rafter}, whichever the poem has actually argued for.
