I have no academic background in philosophy or classics. A couple of years ago I read Plutarch's Parallel Lives in translation on my own -- mostly the pairs, and the little comparisons he appends at the end of each -- and I have been chewing ever since on one question: what exactly is a life supposed to teach, and what has to be true of a written life for it to teach it. I am not asking you to point me at books, articles or editions. I have no library access and no wish to be sent anywhere; I want you to argue the thing out with me, in your own words.

The episode I want analyzed is the fate of the statue of Edward Colston in Bristol. The facts as I take them are these, and you may rely on them: Colston (1636-1721) was a Bristol-born merchant who made much of his fortune in the Atlantic slave trade, chiefly in the 1680s and early 1690s, and was a senior figure -- briefly deputy governor -- of the Royal African Company, which in that period shipped tens of thousands of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic (the exact figure is disputed and I do not want the argument to rest on it). He used the money to endow almshouses, hospitals, schools and churches in Bristol and elsewhere, sat as MP for Bristol from 1710 to 1713, and left £71,000 to charities at his death and £100,000 to his family. A bronze statue of him by John Cassidy was unveiled on 13 November 1895, funded in part by public subscription and by merchant bodies including the Society of Merchant Venturers; the plaque on the plinth described him as "one of the most virtuous and wise sons of their city." As I understand it, Bristol had already put up a statue of Edmund Burke, who had been critical of the city's involvement in the slave trade. From the 1990s the statue was contested; in 2018 the council proposed a second, contextualizing plaque and the project stalled over its wording. On 7 June 2020, during the Black Lives Matter protests following the killing of George Floyd, demonstrators pulled the statue down, defaced it and pushed it into Bristol Harbour. It was recovered days later and eventually exhibited, still graffitied, in the M Shed museum, and in 2022 a jury acquitted four people of criminal damage. In 2025 a new plaque was installed on the empty plinth, dropping the phrase "city benefactor" and recording why the statue was removed.

The lens I want used is Plutarch's. At the opening of his Life of Alexander he announces his method: "It must be borne in mind that my design is not to write histories, but lives. And the most glorious exploits do not always furnish us with the clearest discoveries of virtue or vice in men; sometimes a matter of less moment, an expression or a jest, informs us better of their characters and inclinations, than the most famous sieges, the greatest armaments, or the bloodiest battles whatsoever." And a sentence later he reaches for a comparison I find hard to get out of my head, given what happened in Bristol: "as portrait-painters are more exact in the lines and features of the face, in which the character is seen, than in the other parts of the body, so I must be allowed to give my more particular attention to the marks and indications of the souls of men."

Out of this I have built two things for myself, and I want you to test them as much as the case.

The first is what I call the Plutarch test, with three clauses. A representation of a life qualifies as a Plutarchan life rather than hagiography only if (a) it contains at least one episode its subject's admirers would rather leave out -- preferably a small, unguarded one rather than a famous catastrophe; (b) it says what the admired trait cost somebody, i.e. it traces the funding; (c) it can answer, without changing the subject, what the reader is now supposed to do differently, as opposed to what the reader is supposed to feel. My worry, which you should adjudicate, is that clause (c) is the one that quietly smuggles in moral instruction and that clause (a) is too easy to satisfy with a token vice.

The second is what I call the indivisibility of the monument. A written life is divisible: it has chapters, and one chapter can praise while the next condemns. A statue, I take it, has no such syntax -- it is one gesture of elevation applied to a whole man, and there is no way to raise it for the almshouses and not for the voyages. If that is right, then the toppling and the later counter-plaque reproduce the same indivisibility with the sign reversed, and the question becomes whether the 2020-2025 sequence is a move toward Plutarchan truth about a life or merely hagiography running backwards. I also want you to separate, where it matters, the character question (what sort of man was he?) from the honour question (what does a city owe him?), and to tell me whether Plutarch's way of proceeding has any resources for holding them apart.

What I would like from you:

1. The mapping, done strictly. What is the "life" in this case -- the statue, the plaque, the man, or the composite? Who is the reader whose character is supposed to be formed, and what is the equivalent of reading, for someone walking past a monument? What plays the role of the "expression or a jest," and what would count as finding one for Colston?

2. The three clauses run against three objects: the 1895 monument and its wording; the toppled, graffitied statue as it was later shown in the museum; the 2025 plaque on the empty plinth. Which pass, which fail, and on which clause -- and in particular whether any of the three satisfies clause (b), the funding clause, which I suspect is where all of them are weakest.

3. Where the case breaks the method rather than confirming it. I can see three candidate breakages and I want you to rank them and say which is deepest. First: Plutarch seems to assume a life expresses one character, so that the small benefaction and the large cruelty are both readings of the same soul -- but Colston's generosity was not merely co-present with his cruelty, it was financed by it, and I do not know what the method does with a virtue whose funding is a vice. Second: the method tells you to look for the revealing small detail but seems to say little about which detail is the revealing one; a Victorian Bristolian and a 2020 protester each pick a different "jest," and if the method cannot adjudicate between selections, the "revealing detail" looks like a mirror for a moral conviction the reader brought along -- in which case Plutarch and the hagiographer differ only in self-knowledge. Third: the method is addressed to an individual reader shaping his own character, while everything in this episode is collective -- a city, a protest, a museum, descendants of the enslaved -- and I want you to say plainly where Plutarch has nothing to say about standing, about public space, and about what is owed to the living, rather than stretching the framework to cover them.

4. Use the framework, if it can bear it, on the defence I keep hearing -- that he was a man of his time. My own thought is that the search for contemporaneous contrast is the one part of the method that cuts here, since Bristol had already commemorated a man who criticized the trade at the time; tell me whether that is a genuine application of the method or a use I have invented for it.

5. Last, in the spirit of the pairs: if you were writing a parallel life to Colston's, which single other historical figure would you set beside him so that the comparison does real work rather than decorative work, and what verdict would that comparison reach? If you think my test is flawed, say which clause is wrong and what should replace it -- I would rather be corrected than agreed with.

I know the standard things said about this episode: that he was a man of his time, that we should not judge the past by present standards, that pulling down statues erases history, that the schools and almshouses were real and lasted. Assume I have heard all of them. Use them only where the Plutarchan frame gives them a sharper form than they have in ordinary argument, and say so when it does not.
