Everyone argues about "are supertasks possible" as if it's one question, and I think it's at least three that keep getting run together: (a) whether the schedule can be *prescribed* consistently at all — the Thomson's lamp case, where the description of what happens on the interval up to the limit time doesn't seem to settle what's true at the limit; (b) whether some actual finite physical arrangement could *carry out* the schedule — the infinite-collision setups, where a handful of balls produce infinitely many collisions in finite time and there's no first collision to trace the state back to, and the relativistic setups where one observer's finite lifetime contains another worldline's infinite proper time; and (c) the bit I think might be new: whether the terminal state is *robust*. Define a supertask's outcome as well-defined only if every admissible regularization converges to the same limit — a regularization being: truncate the schedule at N steps, or impose a minimum finite switching time epsilon, then take N to infinity / epsilon to zero. Thomson's lamp looks like the pathological case: different regularizations give different limits, or none, so the puzzle isn't about possibility at all but about limit-fragility, the way a singular differential equation or that dome example (ball at the apex, starts sliding at an arbitrary time with nothing triggering it) isn't about what's possible but about the dynamics failing to pick out a unique continuation.

The question I actually want to defend: is (c) the right diagnosis of what goes wrong with the lamp, or is it just the Benacerraf-style "the description is incomplete, no state is specified" reply dressed up in calculus vocabulary? And if it is the right diagnosis, does it explain why the collision and relativistic cases feel different — they seem to have determinate answers even though the same infinite structure is sitting right there?

What I need in the proposal: the question sharpened until it has a determinate-ish answer; why the usual positions (logical-possibility-first, or "check whether physics forbids it") leave it open; why the regularization angle beats the obvious alternatives rather than just being more work; what the two or three possible conclussions are and what would decide between them; what would count as the project failing (my worry: it collapses into plain old continuity of the state as a function of time, or the prescribed-vs-carried-out distinction turns out to be doing all the real work); and an honest scope section — what gets cut if this is too big for one semester.
