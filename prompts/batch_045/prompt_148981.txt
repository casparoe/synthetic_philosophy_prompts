I've pretty much signed on to illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the Frankish-style version: no qualia, just sensory and attentional states plus an introspective system that misrepresents them as having intrinsic, ineffable phenomenal properties, so the hard problem is a problem about that misrepresentation rather than about the world. Tell me what should move me off it.

Concretely: what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? I want the defeaters its own defenders concede count against them (not just what realists say), and I want to know how much of the view is insulated by the move "apparent datum of qualia = just more illusion," plus what, if anything, it predicts that its rivals don't. Two things I personally find most persuasive, and I'd like them kept seperate from the rest: (1) a resolution worry — if fine-grained introspective reports track sensory discriminability in a stable, lawful way (a perfumer's similarity judgements lining up with neural similarity structure, say), then either the illusion inherits all the structure realism wanted, and the view is realism with the vocabulary swapped, or illusionism is doing no explanatory work; is that fatal, or does it just force "illusionism plus"? (2) if the unpleasantness of pain is itself part of the misrepresentation, pain stops being intrinsically bad in the way we normally assume — consequence to swallow, or reductio? Also: does the fact that carefully attending to an experience doesn't dissolve the seeming count as evidence at all, or is that exactly what the theory predicts?
