Dear Dr Vance,

I am writing to ask your advice about a small practical difficulty which I have come to think is not really practical at all, and I should be grateful for your view of it before our next meeting.

Some background. I spent my working life in geochemistry, and in retirement I have been attending a continuing-education class in philosophy — my first sustained reading of anything outside the natural sciences. I have acquired just enough to be suspicious of my own intuitions and not enough to replace them.

The immediate matter concerns a small charity of which I am a trustee, the Marchwood Naturalists' Trust. We give perhaps forty small grants a year to amateur naturalists for fieldwork and for the cost of publishing their results. I have served for six years and this year find myself on the committee revising our governing document, a four-page deed last revised in 1974. Two of its clauses matter here. Clause 2 states what the money may be spent on. Clause 14 provides that the rules may be amended by a resolution of not less than two-thirds of the trustees present at a quorate meeting and entered in the minute book, and that every such amendment takes effect on the first day of the financial year next following the meeting at which it was passed.

Three proposals are before us, and I must vote on each.

The first is our chair's. She proposes a new clause providing that Clause 2 may not be amended except by a resolution of all the trustees. She says this protects the objects of the Trust. I asked what is to prevent a future two-thirds majority from repealing the protecting clause and then amending Clause 2 the same afternoon, and she replied that this would obviously be against the spirit of the deed. I do not think the word "obviously" is doing any work, but neither can I say what does the work in its place.

The second is our treasurer's, and consists of a single amendment which would lower the threshold for altering Clause 14 from two-thirds to a simple majority, and which states in addition that this amendment itself may be carried by a simple majority. It was carried by five votes to four. Under Clause 14 as it stands that is not enough; under the amendment as proposed, it is. But the amendment, if it takes effect at all, does so on the first of April, some months after the vote. One trustee says the amendment authorises itself, and that this is either harmless or nonsense depending on whom you ask. It seems to me that the validity of the vote depends on a rule that will not exist until after the vote that was supposedly taken under it, and I cannot tell whether that is a defect or merely an oddity.

The third proposal is my own, and I am no more confident of it. I proposed a clause to the effect that no amendment shall take effect if it would leave the deed without any procedure for its own further amendment. A younger trustee pointed out that one could satisfy this while making amendment practically impossible — by providing, say, that future amendments require the assent of everyone who was a trustee in 1974, all of whom are dead. I had assumed that forbidding the removal of a power was a different thing from forbidding the frustration of it. I now wonder whether that distinction can be drawn without going round in a circle.

One further thing. While going through the minute books I found that the 1991 revision, which among other things increased the number of trustees, appears to have been passed by a simple majority rather than two-thirds. Nobody now living remembers the meeting. If that revision was not properly made, a good deal of what has been done since may rest upon it, including the appointment of some of the present trustees. I do not know whether a fault in the chain makes everything downstream faulty, or whether there comes a point at which the chain simply stops mattering.

My instinct, from forty years of measuring things, is to treat the deed as a set of constraints to be satisfied together and to ask which arrangement is stable under its own rules for changing it — the way one looks for a fixed point. I suspect this is exactly where the analogy with the laboratory fails, and I should like to know where and why. Do the three proposals stand or fall together, or are they three separate problems? Is there a distinction I am failing to draw — between a rule and a rule about rules, for instance — that would settle any of them? And if my own proposal merely pushes the difficulty up one level, is there anything honest to say at that level, or does one simply stop? I should add that I am not asking for legal advice; I know that some of this may be settled by charity law, and it is the structure of the argument I want, not the outcome in court.

Two requests about your reply, if I may. First, tell me how confident you are in what you say, and single out the one claim in your answer that you would most expect a philosopher who works on these matters to quarrel with; I would rather be shown where the ground is soft than be handed a tidy answer. Second, I read more comfortably without headings, bullet points, bold type or tables, so continuous prose, as in a letter to a colleague, would suit me best. And please do not send me to the literature: our library has nothing, and I would rather have your reasoning than a list of names.

Yours sincerely,

Aubrey Wren
Emeritus Professor of Geochemistry
