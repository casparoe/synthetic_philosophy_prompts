i've been reading Tolstoy's What Is Art? on my own — no philosophy or lit-crit background, I just got sucked into it after an argument with a friend — and I keep flip-flopping, so I want to hear the disagreement played out properly.

Write me a dialogue between two people who've both read the book closely and disagree about whether his core test actually holds up. Call them M and R or whatever. Neither should be a pushover: give each the strongest version of their side, and make them answer what the other one actually says instead of just restating a position.

Where I got stuck was a case I made up. A studio makes a nine-minute short film, funded by a streamer. Nobody involved felt anything while making it. They ran a couple of hundred variants through focus groups with skin conductance and self-report scales, and kept whatever scored highest on one target: viewers end up feeling tenderness and solidarity toward a stigmatized group — say, people serving long prison sentences. It works. Reliably, across countries, across education levels, no explanation needed. The editors say they were unmoved, and that they'd have optimized just as happily for contempt if the numbers had pointed that way. The feeling also decays in about an hour and never shows up in how anyone votes or treats anyone.

M's job is to run Tolstoy's machinery on that case: art as the deliberate transmission of a feeling the artist once had, the sincerity condition, the four markers of counterfeit art (borrowing, imitation, effectfulness, diversion), the idea that real art infects ordinary people without needing an expert to explain it, and the bit about art being judged by whether it serves the consciousness of human brotherhood. R's job is to press on whether that machinery discriminates at all: whether infectiousness is doing any work once sincerity and brotherhood are in play; whether an audience can ever tell sincerity from the receiving end, which is supposedly where Tolstoy's test lives; and whether his trashings of Wagner, Beethoven, Shakespeare and most of his own novels come out as predictions of the theory or as personal taste in a definition's clothing.

Then have R flip the case: a work that infects nobody today without a lot of scholarship behind it — a Noh play, a fifteenth-century mass — and push M on whether needing explanation is really evidence of counterfeit or just evidence of distance.

I want both of them to concede something real. Don't tie it up neatly either. I'd rather they end by locating the one crux they'd have to settle — something like whether transmitting a feeling is a psychological/causal claim or already a success-and-value claim — and disagreeing about what kind of evidence would even count as settling it. Around 900 words, and definitly no asides about what Tolstoy historically really meant; keep it as two smart readers arguing.
