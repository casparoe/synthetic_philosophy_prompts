I'm on a standing electoral-law review commision for our region, which is basically the body that gets handed every proposal about who gets to vote and then has to send a recommendation to the legislature a few months later. We do this maybe two or three times a year, the recommendations are public and get picked apart in the local press, and half the members are lawyers and former councillors with no philosophy background at all — so whatever we use has to be runnable in a meeting, in plain language, without anyone looking anything up. The same three proposals keep coming back: lower the voting age (usually to 16, someone always floats 15), give the vote to non-citizens who've been living and working here for years (often local elections only), and make voting compulsory, at least in the weak sense of a modest fine for not turning up. And every single time we have the identical argument and split the identical way: some of us think the test is whether your life is materially shaped by the decisions, some think it's whether you're bound by the law and subject to coercion, some go on about competence and whether someone can form a view of their own, and the compulsory-voting people insist that compulsion is just what you do once you've got a franchise worth having. Nothing ever gets resolved.

What I want is a procedure, not another position paper: the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each one, where the procedure stops and returns a clear no, and where it has to hand the decision back to us as a values call we own instead of dressing up as a technical finding. It should be grounded in something better than 'balance the interests' — the thing I keep circling is that the all-affected idea (everyone whose interests are affected by a decision should have a say) is plainly too wide, since it seems to enfranchise tourists, trading partners and future generations and nobody wants that, while the 'you're subject to the law' test is too narrow in a way I can't quite articulate. My own half-baked suggestion, use it or bin it: what actually tracks my instincts is how costly it would be for someone to exit the jurisdiction — the people who can't leave without wrecking their lives are the ones who must be in on the decision, which would explain why a long-term resident non-citizen has a stronger claim than a citizen who moved abroad thirty years ago and can just stay away. Maybe there's a better variable and I'd rather have that than my hunch. Two more things I think but can't defend: (a) 'may they vote' and 'must they vote' are different questions, and even if someone has a claim to be allowed to vote you'd need a separate and stronger argument before you could fine them for staying home; (b) whether compulsion is defensible might depend on who else is already in the electorate — if the people who don't turn up are a systematically biased sample rather than a random one, compulsion seems to do something quite different. Tell me if either of those is confused.

Then please run whatever you build on three cases: lowering the age to 16 for regional elections; votes for non-citizens of five years' residence, local elections only; and compulsory turnout with a small fine. I'm personally sympathetic to all three, so I'd like at least one of them to come out somewhere I don't like — don't just hand me my own politics back, and tell me honestly if the right response to an unwelcome verdict is to change my mind rather than patch the procedure. Also put it through the hard cases it would have to survive in real life: prisoners, people with advanced dementia, dual citizens who have never lived here, sixteen-year-olds who are also non-citizens, undocumented residents, people who moved away but kept the vote. And finish with what the procedure can't do: where it will misfire, what it stays silent about, and where we'd have to overrule it by hand. Please don't cite anything or point me at reading — it has to stand on its own in the room.
