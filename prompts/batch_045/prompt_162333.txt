I'm writing a short position paper for a graduate seminar on the Vienna Circle, on Otto Neurath's picture-language project — the system developed at the Social and Economic Museum in Vienna in the late 1920s, later called Isotype, in which quantities are shown by repeating identical stylized pictograms rather than by enlarging a single one, in which the figures are deliberately flattened and stripped of perspective and incidental detail, and in which a chart is never presented alone but always accompanied by a small amount of verbal text. Neurath himself described the result not as a full language but as a "language-like technique" and a "helping language," and his museum's catch-phrase was "To remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures." I'm working somewhere without library access, so please don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations I can't verify: reason from the description I've given and from what you know, and give me arguments I can reconstruct rather than references I can't check.

What I want you to compare for me is two readings of the claim Neurath had displayed on his office wall, "Words divide, pictures unite," because I can't get them to sit together. Call the first the *transparency* reading: pictures unite because depiction rests on resemblance and on perceptual capacities all human beings share, so a well-made pictogram is understood without instruction, and the teaching Neurath cared about is only needed at the margins. Call the second the *conventionalist* reading: pictures unite only because Isotype is a small, explicitly specified, explicitly taught set of conventions that can be adopted internationally without being embedded in any particular natural grammar or literary tradition; the gain is institutional and pedagogical, not perceptual, and "visual education" is not a supplement to the system but the thing that makes it work at all. Please compare them along three axes: what grounds the intelligibility of a single pictogram; what explanatory work is left for visual education and for the verbal elements that always accompany an Isotype chart; and what becomes of the idea that picture statistics are more objective, or less rhetorical, than prose — notice that if the transparency reading is right, the apparent self-evidence of a chart may be a manufactured effect that conceals the contestable choices involved in deciding which quantity to show and what counts as one instance of the kind depicted, whereas if the conventionalist reading is right, "pictures unite" starts to look like a merely sociological remark about which conventions happen to be widespread, and I don't see what is left of the argument for pictures *over* words. My provisional sense is that the conventionalist reading fits the actual practice far better, but that it quietly deflates the slogan, and I suspect Neurath wanted both. One further wrinkle I can't place: is any of this connected to his anti-foundationalism elsewhere — the rejection of incorrigible protocol sentences, the famous image of sailors rebuilding their ship at sea — and, if so, does that connection help the conventionalist reading or make the neutrality claim look even harder to sustain?

Please also be explicit about the epistemic status of what you say. Separate, clearly, anything that is settled or uncontroversial among people who work on Neurath and Isotype from what is a live interpretive dispute, and from what is your own reconstruction where the texts you know don't settle it; don't present a contested reading as the consensus one, and say plainly where you are inferring from the practice rather than reporting a position anyone has defended. If you think one of my two readings is a straw man, or that the distinction between them collapses under pressure, say so and tell me what the better contrast would be.
