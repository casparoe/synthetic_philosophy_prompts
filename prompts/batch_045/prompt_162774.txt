hey, I'm a junior, I do debate, and last month I gave a short speech to our school board about later start times. Afterwards someone in my class told me it was kind of manipulative — not because I lied (I checked every number) but because I knew how to build the argument: which stat to open with, which study to save for the end, when to shut up. I dont think that's manipulation but I cant say why, and when I tried to write out why not it turned into this thing that seems to prove way too much:

(1) Manipulation is influence on what someone believes or does that doesnt work by getting them to see a reason that's actually good. Call it merit-blind influence: influence that would still work if the reason behind it were garbage.

(2) Whenever you choose how to present a true reason — which fact goes first, what you leave in the background, how vivid the example is — you're relying on effects (order, salience, vividness, framing) that move people whether or not the reason is any good.

(3) So that part of the influence is merit-blind.

(4) Every real attempt to persuade anyone involves some choices like that. Nobody just dumps facts in a neutral order; even a math proof has to pick one.

(5) So every real act of persuasion contains merit-blind influence.

(6) So by (1), every real act of persuasion is partly manipulation.

(7) If every persuasion is partly manipulation, then persuasion and manipulation dont mark a moral difference at all, only a difference of degree — and there's nothing wrong with manipulation as such that isnt also wrong with explaining algebra to a freshman.

(8) But there obviously is a difference and it isnt just degree: my debate coach walking a teammate through why her case doesnt follow is doing something different from her boyfriend guilt-tripping her into skipping practice.

(9) So one of (1) through (7) is false.

I tried to make (1) sharper with my own test, the reverse test: an influence is manipulative iff the very same way of presenting it would work just as well if you swapped in bad reasons for the good ones. That felt like the right idea but it definately made things worse, not better, because of course salience and order effects work on bad reasons too — so it just agrees with (2). One friend says the problem is (1) and that manipulation needs to bypass or hijack someone's ability to weigh things, another says it's the jump from (6) to (7). I'm not convinced by either.

Which step do you actually reject, and why did it look fine? If you have to choose, I'd rather keep (4) and (8) than anything else.
