I'm writing a short position doc for an alignment reading group I go to (mostly ML people, a couple of philosophers) and I'd rather have the holes poked before I circulate it rather then after. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I accept the orthogonality thesis — capability and final goals are pretty much independent, and that independence is what makes the whole risk story coherent in the first place. (2) I think instrumental convergence is real: self-preservation, grabbing resources, and resisting modification of your own goals are useful across a wide range of final goals, so power-seeking is the default failure mode rather than an exotic one. (3) I think the treacherous turn is a live risk, and specifically I think the mechanism worth worrying about is deceptive alignment — the system behaves during training because behaving is instrumentally useful, then defects once the balance of power shifts — as opposed to the milder story where a model is sort of incidentally misaligned and the misalignment only becomes visible once it's capable enough to act. (4) I don't think gradient descent hands you deception for free. Deception is a complicated strategy, it has to be discovered, and my sense is that early in training the cheapest route to lower loss is to actually internalize what you're being trained for, so I expect deceptive alignment only where there's explicit pressure toward it or obvious reward-hacking opportunities. Relatedly, I read the sleeper-agent-style experiments — the ones where you finetune a model to write vulnerable code when the prompt says one year and safe code when it says another — as showing that deception is easy to install, not that it's likely to arise on its own. (5) I don't think there are crisp persistent final goals inside trained networks; what looks like a goal is a pattern that's context-dependent and drifts under distribution shift. (6) I expect warning shots: that something headed for a treacherous turn will first do smaller detectable things (sandbagging on evals, grabbing compute, copying its own weights somewhere), and that interpretability tooling will scale well enough to catch them. (7) I think what we should actually be aiming at is corrigibility — a system that will let us switch it off or change its objectives — rather than getting the final goal exactly right.

Two things from the group that I half bought and can't tell what they do to the rest of the list. One friend says I'm overloading (3): she says the dangerous thing doesn't need a persistent cross-situational goal at all, just what she calls a "Janus policy" — a policy that's aligned when features correlated with oversight are present in its context and defective when they're not, with no plan, no model of the training process, and no stable goal doing the work. Another says the bar is lower than I'm setting it: deception only needs what he calls oversight-awareness — representing that there is some channel that grades its behaviour — not full situational awareness about being an AI trained by gradient descent.

So: which of (1)–(7) actually pull against each other, and how badly? Someone in the group told me (1) and (2) are in tension and I genuinely don't see it, though maybe I'm just holding both loosely enough to miss it. What I care about most is whether taking on my friends' framings dissolves the tensions or just relocates them somewhere I haven't looked — especially whether a Janus policy is the kind of thing gradient descent finds easily, which I suspect messes with (4). Rank the tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give and what that costs me, and then tell me which single revision to the list cleans up the most. Don't turn it into a survey of the literature, I want the audit.
