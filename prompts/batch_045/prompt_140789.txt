I run the induction afternoon for new volunteer care companions at a hospice: about sixteen people at a time, mixed ages, mostly no background in philosophy, a few retired clinicians, a couple of students, and usually one or two who volunteered because someone they loved died here and who will be quietly furious if the afternoon wastes their time. For the past year I have also been the person who coordinates care for my mother, who has vascular dementia, so I am no longer reading this material from the outside. My slot is 75 minutes, late in a three-hour session, immediately after the safeguarding module — which means they arrive already in the frame of mind where the point of a scenario is to locate the correct procedure and not get into trouble. That is the frame I want broken, and I do not want to break it by announcing that it is the wrong frame.

What I need is a structured activity — a game with rules and some payoff, a role-play, a staged deliberation with real if small stakes — that makes them run into three things before anyone names them.

First, what I have started calling continuity cost. Two arrangements can deliver exactly the same acts, to the same standard, at the same times, and differ only in whether it is the same person who comes back. My mother's carers are all competent; the one I dread losing is the one who knows that she will refuse the second half of a sentence before anyone has finished the first. Is there a real loss when the identity of the carer changes while the content and quality of the care are held fixed — and if there is, is it a moral loss or merely a preference? I want them to discover that they think something here, not to be told what to think.

Second, what I have been calling the uptake condition. A technically flawless piece of care that the person receiving it experiences as an intrusion is, it seems to me, a failure of care, and not only because of its downstream effects: care that does not land is not care. But the cared-for may be unable to say so, may not know it themselves, or may actively reject what they need, and a hospice is full of exactly those cases. I want them to feel the pull of the condition and then feel the places where it cannot be applied.

Third, the noticing deficit. A need nobody has noticed is not a need that goes unmet; as far as any rota is concerned it is not yet a need at all. Noticing is work, it cannot be delegated downstream to whoever is on shift, and no audit counts it. The working assumption of the rota — that two hours from anyone is two hours — I call the substitutability assumption, and I want them to watch it fail in something they are doing, not in a story I tell them.

Constraints, because they decide whether I can actually run this:

No lecture at the front, and the name of the tradition should not be spoken until the last ten minutes. The insight has to arrive as something they did. No trolley problems, no dramatic dilemmas about stealing a drug to save a spouse, no case that turns on life and death, and no case whose answer is obvious: those all import the very frame I am trying to unsettle, the single decision taken from nowhere with a right answer waiting. Care in this building is mostly the sustained maintenance of dependency, and the activity should be about that. I have no real patients and no actors; the participants are the participants, nobody should have to disclose anything about their own experience of illness or caring, and nobody should have to play a dying person if a proxy or a written role will do. Materials are index cards, pens, a whiteboard, a phone timer and the room, because that is what the room has. One colleague can help for the first ten minutes and not after. It must work with ten people as well as with eighteen.

The stakes have to be real but small, since there is no money and no mark to give. My own instinct is that the currency should be something the participants actually possess — their attention, their promises, a budget of minutes they have to allocate — and that whatever score the game keeps should systematically fail to record what turns out to have mattered, since that mismatch is most of the point and is what an institution does to care. But I would rather see your mechanic than impose mine.

What I would like from you: rules, roles, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; the scoring or payoff scheme and why participants will care about it; and where the activity is designed to go wrong for them — the specific moment at which each of the three things above should land, and what you expect people to say at that moment. Then a debrief that maps those moments onto the three ideas, and that includes the honest part, which is what the picture costs: care indexed to a relationship is finite, it does not reach strangers, it can curdle into possessiveness or into the carer's disappearance into the cared-for, and an institution that runs on personal relationships is running on the labour of people who are usually unpaid and mostly women. I want them to feel that too, and to leave with one thing they can do on the ward tomorrow that is not just another rule. Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the retired nurse who wants the protocol, the person who treats it as a puzzle to be won, the person who goes quiet because it is close to home, the group that colludes in being nice — and how to handle each. And last, which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce at all and which I will therefore have to say in so many words. My suspicion is that it is duration, that real care is what happens on the four hundredth visit with no debrief at the end of it, but tell me what you think it is.
