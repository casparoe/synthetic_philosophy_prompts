I sit on the technical review panel for a small private foundation that funds high-upside, long-shot basic science — the kind of place that will happily put $300k into a tabletop experiment with maybe a 3% chance of finding something. We've got a proposal I can't get a grip on and the decision meeting is in ten days, so I need to circulate a written opinion beforehand. The proposal (the PIs call it a "supertask readout", internally we've been calling it Project Achates) is to build an accelerating machine: a device whose nth operation takes half the time of the (n−1)th, so that infinitely many steps complete inside a bounded window of lab time, with the answer read off at the end by a bit — a lamp, essentially — that is on iff the encoded Turing machine halts. Their pitch is explicitly about the Church-Turing thesis: they say that read as a claim about what physical systems can do it's an empirical claim, that nobody has tested it directly, that this is about the cheapest experiment that could refute it, and that even a null result teaches us something about which precision, energy, and time bounds actually bite.

The panel is split roughly down the middle. Half wants to desk-reject: no finite observation could ever certify that the thing computed the halting function, because you can't finitely check a "doesn't halt" verdict — there's nothing to point at — so the proposal is unfalsifiable in principle and is really asking us to take "we built an oracle" on faith, and anyway the mundane physics kills it long before the philosophy does. The other half says that objection proves way too much: it's the underdetermination of theory by evidence in a lab coat, no novel instrument could ever meet it (you can't check the first atomic clock against a better clock), and we fund instruments, not theorems. I'm stuck in the middle and annoyingly underqualified — one computability course as an undergrad, and mostly what I retained is the diagonal argument and the fact that the busy beaver numbers outrun anything computable. Two things I've been chewing on from arguing about this at my local rationalist meetup, which I want the memo to use if there sound and to kill outright if they aren't, rather than politely routing around them. First, a "certification condition": require any hypercomputation proposal to name a finite procedure a Turing machine could run to check the device's verdicts on a test battery. My worry is that this smuggles the conclusion in reverse — for a halting-type target it can't be met as a general guarantee, since any finite battery can be aced by a lookup table, so making it a rule quietly adopts the thesis as a constraint on what counts as evidence, i.e. settles the philosophy by comittee. Second, the "implementation dodge": suppose the lamp flipped correctly on every case we can independently check. A defender can still say that emitting the right outputs isn't computing — a lookup table assembled by a lightning strike isn't computing either — so this is a readout and not a counterexample, and the thesis survives on the strength of what "computation" demands. If that move is always available, then the thesis-as-definition and thesis-as-empirical-law readings might both tell us not to fund the oracle part while telling us to for entirely different reasons, and which reason we give matters a lot for what we'd fund next time.

What I actually need is the memo, in American English (half the panel is British and I don't want a style fight on top of everything else). Addressed to the panel, bottom line up top, 800–1000 words: recommend one concrete disposition — decline, fund, or fund with conditions — with milestones and a time frame the PIs could genuinely respond to rather than just appeal; say explicitly which reading of the Church-Turing thesis the recommendation depends on and what would happen to the recommendation under the other reading; and give the strongest version of the objection on the losing side, in the form we could defend if someone leaked it. Please don't pad it out with citations or a reading list — none of us are going to chase references, and I'd rather have the argument than the apparatus.
