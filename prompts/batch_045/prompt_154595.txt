My friend and I got into it for like two hours after a dinner party last night about AI labs' voluntary safety commitments — the big multi-company letters, the "we won't train the next thing until we can do X" pledges, the third-party audits a couple of them signed up for. He ended basically every point with "talk is cheap": nothing a company says about its own safety means anything, because saying it costs them nothing and they'd say it whether or not it was true. I pushed back with the handicap-principle thing I half remember from an undergrad bio class — signals can be honest precisely because they're expensive, a peacock's tail is only affordable if you're a healthy bird — and he said fine, but then show me the tail: most of these pledges are drafted by lawyers, they get quietly watered down, no one has ever been punished for missing one, and a lab that wanted to cheat could sign and ignore it. I partly conceded that, and then we hit a weirder case and went in circles, which is actually the part I want help with. Alot of these commitments are expensive to make — standing up an eval team, giving auditors access, agreeing to a pause that hands your lead to a rival — but the cost doesn't really land on whoever is doing the signaling: it gets passed to customers, or to the next model, or to a subcontractor who can take the blame later, or it's just a number on somebody's budget spreadsheet. He says that makes it worse, a costly signal that costs you personally nothing proves nothing; I said that can't be right either, because then nothing any institution does would ever count as a signal, and besides, sometimes the point is that the money actually gets burned. We also never settled the opposite case: whether something can be credible precisely because it's free, because our interests happen to line up, or because it's checkable, or because saying it out loud invites a penalty if you turn out to be lying. I'm helping put together an informal scorecard that rates these things for a small newsletter, so I need something better than vibes.

So: "talk is cheap," and its cousin "actions speak louder than words" (which he also used). I want the menu, not a verdict: what are the distinct, precise things that one-liner could be asserting — from the trivial to the strong — about what it takes for a message to be trustworthy? For each reading say whether it's basically a platitude, whether it's substantive and actually plausible, or whether it's straightforwardly false, and say plainly which reading my friend would need in order for it to do the work he was making it do, i.e. dismissing the pledges outright.

Two things I especially want sorted out. First, whether what's supposed to matter is cost as such, or cost that differs between a good sender and a bad one, or cost that the sender can't shove onto someone else or just pay off with money — including the case where the signal is expensive but also pays for itself, and the case where it's expensive but the sender is rich enough that the expense is basically a price. Second, whether cost is required at all, or whether there are other routes to credibility that the slogan ignores, like being physically tied to the quality in a way you couldn't fake even if you wanted to. Then run the readings on three concrete cases: a vague multi-company letter that everyone signed; a lab publishing its internal eval methodology including its own failure rates; and a pledge backed by a contract with a monetary penalty paid to a third party. For each, which readings say "ignore it," which say "update on it," and where does the slogan genuinely run out — the case where it's true but doesn't get him his conclusion?
