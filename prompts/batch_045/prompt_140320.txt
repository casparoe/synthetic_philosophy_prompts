I'm a PhD candidate in public policy (decision-science track), and I'm writing the final exam for the MPP seminar I TA this term, "Global Priority Setting". The students have had cost-effectiveness analysis and the importance–tractability–neglectedness (ITN) heuristic, and most of them can recite the three factors and the instruction to multiply them. I want one 90-minute, 30-mark question that gives nothing to that recitation: it should force them to say what single quantity the framework is trying to estimate, what each factor is a stand-in for, and what goes wrong when the stand-in comes apart from the quantity. An answer that only restates the framework should land in the B range at best.

I've been toying with a stylised case, in welfare units (WU), which I'd like you to use — or to tighten so that the arithmetic is unambiguous and no alternative reading yields a different ranking. If you adjust numbers, please keep the two structural features that make it bite: one cause has constant, non-diminishing returns, and another's best opportunity is lumpy and sits at a very high level of existing funding.

Treat WU as fully comparable across causes and ignore timing and discounting.

The setup. A foundation has a single non-recurring grant of $50M to allocate this year. Its trustees' fallback use is Cause C, whose return is known exactly.

Cause A — control of a chronic parasitic infection. Fully solving it is worth 12,000 WU. Returns are constant: every additional $1M solves 1% of the problem, i.e. yields 120 WU, right up to complete solution at $100M of cumulative funding. Cumulative funding to date: $30M. Current annual funding: $10M. No other funder is expected to change its behaviour in response.

Cause B — the eradication endgame for a vaccine-preventable livestock disease. Fully solving it is worth 1,000,000 WU, but 99% is already solved. If eradicated this year, the remaining benefit is 10,000 WU (the residual 1%) plus 15,000 WU from permanently avoiding resurgence. Eradication requires an indivisible one-off $50M campaign; any smaller contribution adds nothing at all. Current funding: $2,000M a year of routine vaccination, which continues until eradication occurs.

Cause C — an established scholarship programme, the trustees' default. Worth 500,000 WU if fully solved; each additional $1M solves 0.06% of the problem, i.e. yields 300 WU, over the relevant range. Current annual funding: $500M. Divisible, no crowding out.

The parts I have in mind: (a) rank the uses of the $50M with numbers, and say how the ranking changes if the foundation had only $1M to give this year; (b) apply the ITN heuristic the way it is usually applied in this seminar (each factor rated, then multiplied), compare its ranking with yours, and explain precisely why the two differ and what that shows about what ITN is estimating; (c) say what neglectedness would have to mean for it to be more than a crude proxy — redefine it, or argue the framework should drop it; (d) in a sentence or two, if eradication happens the $2,000M a year of routine vaccination stops — should that be counted in Cause B's benefit, and if so, how?

What I want from you:

1. The question exactly as it should appear on the paper: parts (a)–(d), stated time (90 minutes), a word cap (1,200 words), and the mark total (30). Lead with this, as it would be handed to candidates — no preamble, no commentary on how you constructed it, and no repeat of this email's discussion.

2. A model answer of roughly 800 words whose first sentence states the bottom line — the ranking with its numbers — and which then gives the reasoning. No restatement of the question, no citations, and no reference to any organisation's write-up; the question has to be answerable from the information given and nothing else.

3. A marking scheme: five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (totalling 30), and one line on each about how candidates typically get it wrong — the specific error or the specific recitation that should cost them the marks.
