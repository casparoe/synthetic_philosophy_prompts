I work on reward modeling for RLHF, and a philosopher friend has been telling me that my group's setup is a special case of the problem Harsanyi's aggregation theorem settles. The setup, as we actually run it: thousands of annotators give pairwise preferences over model outputs; we fit one reward model to the pooled data; before pooling, we normalize each annotator's implied reward scale, roughly zero-mean and unit-variance per annotator, in the spirit of the normalization tricks we use in multi-task RL; and the policy is then optimized for expected return. One wrinkle that seems to me important and that she waved away: the annotator pool is different for every prompt, a fresh sample of people for each decision, so the set of individuals being aggregated is not fixed from one decision to the next. Her statement of the theorem: if each individual's preferences over gambles satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, the social ranking of gambles also satisfies them, and the social ranking never overrules unanimous preference (or at least preserves unanimous indifference when one person strictly prefers), then the social ranking must order gambles by a weighted sum of individual expected utilities. She concludes that some version of utilitarian aggregation is forced on us, and that alternatives we have actually implemented — max-min fairness across annotator groups, lexicographic priority for the worst-off group, per-group constraints enforced by constrained optimization — are either weighted sums in disguise or incoherent.
