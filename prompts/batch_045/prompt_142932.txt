I am a PhD student in philosophy — my dissertation concerns the distinction between reasons that bear on whether an attitude fits its object and reasons that bear only on the value of being in that attitude — and for the past two years I have served as the graduate member and, informally, the minute-writer of my university's Research Integrity Review Panel. I would like a decision procedure the panel could actually run, written so that people who have never encountered that distinction can apply it correctly.

The setting: the panel has eight seats — two senior scientists from different departments, a statistician, a research-compliance officer, a non-voting attorney from the Office of General Counsel, a rotating faculty member, me, and a chair who is an associate dean. We meet monthly for ninety minutes and handle ten to fifteen matters a year. We have no power to compel raw data or to interview unwilling parties; the statistician is the only member who can competently read the analyses in front of us; and the attorney screens every sentence for defamation exposure, so we cannot say anything we could not defend. Our output is a two-page recommendation to the Vice Provost for Research, who signs off on nearly all of them. The options in front of us are: close the matter with no public action, request materials from the authors, place a note on the publication record, recommend retraction, or refer the matter to the federal research-integrity office. The stakes run from a postdoc's career to a center's funding to the credibility of the institution's record.

My sense is that the panel's real question is an attitude question in administrative costume: do we have a reason of the right kind to reduce confidence in this finding? Most of what actually moves the panel is not of that kind — it is the trouble a complainant can make, the existence of press coverage, the funder's mood, the size of the citing literature, the career damage a note would do to a junior author, the desire to be seen as responsive. Some of those are perfectly good reasons to do something. None of them, as far as I can tell, is a reason to doubt the finding, and the panel slides between the two constantly. I want a procedure that stops the slide.

Please give me the following.

An ordered set of questions, each with an account of what counts as a sufficient answer, including where the procedure stops with a no and where it hands the decision to a vote because the question is one of values rather than facts. It must be runnable inside a ninety-minute meeting, by the eight people described, and it must generate the two-page record as a by-product rather than as extra work.

The procedure has to preserve a set of distinctions I keep watching the panel collapse. First, the basic one: considerations that bear on whether the finding is reliable, versus considerations that bear on what it costs or gains the panel, the institution, or the authors for us to be in a state of doubt. Second, reasons to investigate versus reasons to doubt: most of what reaches us justifies the first and is then treated as though it justified the second, and I want that gap to be explicit and thresholded. Third, the asymmetry of verdicts: our not finding evidence of fabrication, and the authors' answering our questions cooperatively, are not reasons for confidence, and the procedure must not let a written record imply that they are. Fourth, the stakes question, which I want flagged rather than settled: a finding that underwrites a clinical guideline, or that has hundreds of citing papers, raises the cost of being wrong — say whether that lowers the bar for reducing confidence, what the procedure assumes, and what would change under the view that it does not. Then the mirror image, which is the panel's most common failure: the damage that flagging would do to a junior author is not a reason for confidence either, and I want that as its own check rather than a footnote. Fifth, source-relative reasons: an allegation from a former lab member in a bitter dispute, or from an anonymous dossier since picked up by a science news outlet — distinguish what the complainant's motives do to the reliability of their testimony from what the trouble they can cause, or the coverage they have generated, does to us.

Two further wrinkles that I have not seen handled anywhere and that I think matter more than the tidy cases. The first is what I have started calling laundered reasons: considerations that exist only because the panel or another body previously acted on reasons of the wrong kind, and which return to us later disguised as reasons of the right kind. A flag issued two years ago mainly to placate a large foundation now appears in a General Counsel memo as part of a documented pattern. The procedure must say whether such a consideration has any weight, and if it has none, what the panel is obliged to do when it shows up in a new file — including whether it owes a written correction of its own prior action. The second is that a committee is arguably not the kind of thing that has attitudes at all, and its output is a public statement that will function as evidence for readers who were not in the room. So the procedure should say whether it is trying to bring the panel to the fitting attitude, or to give readers a reason of the right kind to revise theirs, or both — and what breaks if it is the second, since a statement aimed at other people's attitudes makes almost every consideration look like a state-given one from our side.

Please also mark, at each step, which parts are genuine procedure and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and close by saying what the procedure cannot do: the questions it cannot answer, the cases it will systematically mishandle, and the points at which my distinction simply runs out.

Then run it on three files.

One: an anonymous dossier alleging image manipulation in a paper from six years ago. A forensic image review came back inconclusive; the authors produced part of the raw data and said the rest was lost in a lab move; the field is polarized; the dossier's author is identifiable as a former lab member with a documented grievance; a science news outlet has written about the allegations twice; and the chair wants a note on the record to show we responded.

Two: an undisputed analytical error in a paper whose central result underwrites a clinical guideline. The statistician says the claim does not follow from the analysis anyone could run; the senior authors concede the error but say the conclusion survives; the error was found by a graduate student in the lab who is about to go on the job market and has asked not to be named; and the compliance officer notes that a note on the record would trigger a funder review of the entire center.

Three: the laundering case. A new allegation against a lab the panel flagged two years ago, on a file I now think was driven almost entirely by reasons of the wrong kind and for which I drafted the minutes. The new file's summary cites our own earlier flag as corroboration. Run the procedure honestly and tell me what it commits us to, including whether it commits us to stating in writing that the earlier flag carried no evidential weight. If it does, say so plainly; that is an answer I will not like, and I would rather have it from the procedure than discover it myself in the meeting.

I know the philosophical literature on this; I do not need citations, references, or a reading list, and the attorney will not read them anyway. What I need is something eight people with different training can run in a room in ninety minutes. Please write in American English, roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, in a form I could circulate to the panel with only the identifying details changed.
