I work as a data analyst at a mid-sized online grocer — me and two others, none of us with any economics training, we mostly write SQL and build dashboards. Around thirty times a week someone in product asks us what customers actually want and expects a number back, and my boss has started calling these "utility scores". A mate of mine read a bit of philosophy at university and told me, in the pub, that the whole idea of revealed preference means utility isn't a hidden feeling inside the shopper at all, it's just a way of numbering their choices — so on that view we can apparently never be wrong about what someone wants, they bought the thing, so that's what they wanted, and asking whether they really wanted it is nonsense. That definately sounds like it proves too much to me but I can't say why, and it matters because if utility is only a numbering of choices I don't know what we think we're reporting to the board, and half our decisions are about cases where the shopping behaviour obviously isn't a straight expression of a want.

Here's the bit that really broke my brain. Last quarter we launched a "weekly plan": customers build their basket on Sunday, then on the morning of delivery they get a chance to edit it, and anything out of stock we auto-substitute for a similar item — sometimes they notice, often they dont find out until the van arrives. So for a single shop we now have two recorded choices, a plan and a checkout, and they disagree something like a third of the time, and on top of that there's everything they scrolled straight past without buying. I'd like a procedure my team can actually run, ideally in ten minutes before a product meeting, for deciding what (if anything) a given piece of behaviour tells us about preference: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point where the procedure just says "we can't tell from this" and stops, and the point where it has to hand over to a person because the question is about what we ought to do rather than about facts. It has to work for people who've never opened an economics textbook, and whatever it says has to survive being read by our product director and, for anything that changes a default, by our legal and ethics reviewer.

Please run it on three real-ish cases. (1) A customer plans porridge oats every single week and then, at checkout, swaps to a branded sugary cereal about two times in three: do we restock and default them to the oats or to the cereal? (2) We auto-substituted a cheap own-brand pasta for a pricier one during a stock outage, four in five shoppers accepted it, and plenty of them were still buying the pricier one a month later — is that a revealed preference for the expensive pasta, or just what was on the shelf? (3) This is the one I think I'll hate the answer to: we're about to launch a "healthy swap" where full-fat items are quietly swapped for lower-fat ones at checkout unless the customer opts out, and the business case rests on us reporting afterwards that customers chose the healthier option; I suspect your procedure is going to tell us we can't use that data as evidence, and if so I'd rather hear it now than after launch. Be straight with me about which distinctions I have to keep to make this usable and which ones I can throw away, and about where the whole thing misfires.
