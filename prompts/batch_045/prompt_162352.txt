My sister just quit being a hospice nurse to take a data job she hates, because a spreadsheet convinced her she'd do more good donating a third of a bigger salary. She sent me the spreadsheet and wants to discuss it at Christmas. I dont want to lecture her, I want to hand her something that makes the point by being funny.

So: write me a ~600-word op-ed, deadpan and completely straight-faced, in the voice of a senior analyst at an invented philanthropic consultancy — call it something like Ledger & Co. — proposing the obvious fix for the movement's measurability problem. Since only outcomes you can count can be compared across causes, unmeasurable goods (dignity, grief, being cared for by someone who actually wanted to be there) keep losing out to interventions with a clean number attached. The consultancy therefore proposes a single new instrument: the Certified Beneficiary Programme, which pays people a published hourly rate to be, and to remain, beneficiaries, so that every intervention can finally report an auditable figure per dollar.

The laughs have to come from getting the real critique right — measurability bias, treating units of good as if they were fungible, counterfactual credit (who gets to book the good), and the way the highest-ranked item in any ranking turns out to be the ranking organisation itself — and it should end by following its own logic somewhere my sister will actually recoil: the analyst's own salary line, the audit of the audit, people paid to be counted twice. No winking at the reader, no real people or real organisations named anywhere, invented names only, and it should stay readable for someone who's never heard of any of this. Also please dont let it slide into a rant about rich people — the target is the style of reasoning, not the bankrollers.
