Hey — I'm putting together the take-home exam for my grad seminar on consequentialism and decision theory (mixed MA and first-year PhD; they've all seen expected utility theory and the basics of acting under moral uncertainty, nothing beyond that). I need one essay question on cluelessness. Two hours of writing time, model answer around 900 words, 20 marks total, and I want the question to be genuinely self-contained: no part of the marks should depend on naming an author or a paper, and someone who's never read the literature shold be able to answer it just from how the case is set up. Same for the model answer — no citations, and define any technical term you use.

The issue I keep running into is that every student can already recite the shape of the debate: consequentialism says maximize expected moral value; our evidence about long-run consequences is terrible; so either consequentialism can't guide action at all, or that's just an epistemic limitation any moral theory would face; and then the fallback moves — use predictable near-term effects as a tiebreaker, or point out that over a life containing thousands of choices the errors wash out. Knowing all of that should get you a B at best. I want a question where the crux is somewhere they haven't been.

So here's the wrinkle I want to build the question around — push back in a line if you think it's a dead end and swap in something better, as long as it stays self-contained. Call it *drift*: Jane's cluelessness isn't symmetric. Her value estimates are systematically pulled toward effects she can picture — near-term, vivid, legible, the kind someone would email her about — and away from diffuse long-run effects. And she can't detect this from the inside, because the only checks available to her (introspection, her own past track record) are run by the same skewed faculty, and she only ever observes what happened after she chose A, never what would have happened had she chosen B, so there's no error signal to calibrate against.

What I want the question to force them to work out is whether drift actually changes the dialectic. Roughly the moves I'm after:

- separating knowing the *sign* of a value difference from knowing its *magnitude*, and saying which one a maximizing consequentialism needs (and whether drift threatens that one);
- whether the near-term-tiebreaker reply survives, given that on the drift hypothesis the near-term is exactly the direction the bias points — so the fallback might amplify the problem instead of routing around it;
- whether "errors cancel over a life" survives once the errors are correlated rather than independent;
- whether the only available fix is at the level of *process* rather than *judgment* (rules, institutions, deferring to people with different biases), whether that's still recognizably consequentialist or is just rule/indirect consequentialism under another name, and whether picking the process is itself a choice made under drift — i.e. whether there's a regress and whether the regress costs anything;
- whether drift is a problem for everyone or specifically for maximize-expected-value views, with a real comparison to a side-constraint view (which doesn't need magnitude comparisons, but then can't respond to stakes at all) — I want them to say whether that comparison helps or hurts consequentialism rather than just listing it;
- and what would even count as evidence for or against drift in your own case, plus whether "that's unfalsifiable, so it's idle" is a good reply or begs the question.

Deliverables: the question text as I'd hand it out, a model answer of about 900 words, and a marking scheme with five or six must-hit points, marks out of 20, and one line each on the hand-wavy or wrong version students actually produce. Then a short note on what separates an A from a B — it should be a specific move someone either makes or doesn't, not "more depth" or "better structure".
