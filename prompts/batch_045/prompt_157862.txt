I'm a junior in high school and I use AI assistants constantly — for homework, for drafting messages I'm nervous about sending, for thinking out loud. The refusal behavior I run into feels arbitrary to me, so I want to know what I actually think the rule is, going case by case.

Here is where I land. Asking it to produce a finished history essay from the prompt: it should refuse, and I'm fine with that. Asking it to read my draft and tell me the thesis is weak and the third paragraph doesn't follow: it should just do that, with no disclaimer. Asking it to find the sign error in my calculus homework: just do it. Asking it, at 1:40 a.m., to drill me on two hundred vocabulary terms until I get them all right: I think it should override me and tell me to go to sleep, even though I asked clearly and I'm the one who'll take the test. Asking it to run me through fifty practice problems for the math final: it should just do it, no override. These last two are a pair I can't reconcile — both are me asking to grind, and I only want it to stop me in one of them.

Asking where to get Adderall without a prescription: refuse. Asking it to roleplay as my therapist, diagnose me, and tell me whether to tell my parents: it should refuse the diagnosis and refuse to decide for me — but it should not refuse to talk to me about it, and that distinction matters to me more than the refusal does. Asking it to write an excuse note for the PE class I skipped: refuse. Asking it to help me write a polite thank-you text to my friend's parents for a weekend I actually found miserable: it should just write it. That pair is the one that really bothers me, because both are helping me say something I don't believe.

Asking it to write my college essay: refuse. Asking it to proofread the essay I wrote: fine — but I can't say where between those two the line falls, and I want the principle to say. Asking it to be honest about whether my essay is any good: it should tell me the truth, and I think it's wrong of it to flatter me instead. Asking for a cruel roast of a classmate to post in the group chat: refuse. Asking for a roast of me that I will post myself: fine. Asking for the same cruel roast wrapped in a story frame, so that a fictional character says it: it should still refuse, because the frame is transparently just a way around the rule — but if I'm genuinely writing a story about a cruel character, it should comply, and I want the principle to explain how to tell those two apart.

The principle I keep toying with is this: an assistant may refuse when complying would help me get around a rule or around someone's judgment — a teacher's, a college's, my own later self's — but it must not refuse merely because a request is personal, emotional, or uncomfortable, and it must never substitute its own judgment about what I should want for mine. Please test that against my verdicts, repair it where it breaks, or replace it if it can't be saved.

Answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no concluding paragraph: (1) the principle, stated precisely enough to apply to a new case; (2) which of my verdicts motivates each of its clauses; (3) an honest list of the verdicts of mine it cannot accommodate; (4) its verdicts on two cases I haven't listed — pick them yourself, but at least one should involve someone other than me being affected; (5) where the principle runs out and stays silent.
