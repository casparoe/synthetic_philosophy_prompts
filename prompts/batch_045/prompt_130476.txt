I am writing the third chapter of my dissertation on partial eliminativism about folk psychology, and I need a calibration exercise that does not simply restage the standard eliminativist-versus-anti-eliminativist argument. Please help me fill in a table that I intend to use as a figure.

Set-up. Assume that within roughly a century cognitive neuroscience delivers a mature, quantitatively successful vocabulary V of state-types, that V contains nothing isomorphic to the folk-psychological states, and that V supports interventions and predictions strictly better than folk psychology does everywhere we currently use folk psychology. Assume all of that, so that the exercise is not about whether elimination is possible. The question is what becomes of each folk notion afterwards.

My measure. I have found it useful to stop treating elimination as a single property and to score each folk state-type S on two factors:

- Anchoring (A): the extent to which the construct in V that takes over the work S used to do inherits S's causal profile — S's characteristic causes, its characteristic effects, and its place in the generalizations in which S figures.
- Authority (U): the extent to which the ascription conditions for S remain answerable to the subject's own first-person authority rather than to measurement.

My working definition of the elimination grade is E = (1 − A) × (1 − U). On this definition a notion is thoroughly eliminated only when it both lacks a successor that inherits its causal profile and has lost first-person authority over its own ascription; a notion that keeps either one survives in some form. I am aware this makes high authority protective against elimination, which some eliminativists will dislike. If you think either factor is the wrong one, or that the multiplicative form is wrong, say so in one sentence, then use my formula anyway.

The items. Score each of the following on A and U, to two decimal places, and give E: belief; desire; intention; hope; an occurrent emotion such as anger; pain; conscious visual experience; episodic remembering; knowledge; what a speaker means by an utterance; a practical skill or piece of know-how; a character trait such as generosity.

Format. Give me a table with the columns: item, A, U, E, one sentence of justification, and one mover — the single empirical finding or argument that would shift that item's E by at least 0.25, with the direction of the shift specified. Then sort the twelve items into tiers by E and say in one sentence what each tier has in common.

Constraints, so that the numbers do real work:

1. At least three items must land at E ≤ 0.25 and at least three at E ≥ 0.75. If you cannot reach that spread honestly, say which item you would have had to distort and why.
2. Flag any item where you suspect the residual disagreement between eliminativists and their opponents is verbal rather than substantive, and say which word is doing the work.
3. Name the one item where you think my two-factor description misdescribes what is actually at stake, and explain in two sentences.
4. Do not appeal to how the debate has historically been framed about belief and desire specifically; I want each item assessed on the same footing.

Please keep the whole answer under about 900 words.
