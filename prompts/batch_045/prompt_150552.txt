I work on reward inference and value learning — inverse reinforcement learning and preference-based reward modelling. The vocabulary I actually use: trajectories and feature expectations, Boltzmann-rational policies, maximum-entropy and Bayesian IRL with a posterior over reward weights, feature-expectation matching, the fact that reward is identifiable only up to potential-based shaping transformations and only under assumptions I know are strong, the degenerate solution in which a constant reward makes every policy optimal, complexity penalties on the reward representation of the MDL or BIC sort, off-policy evaluation, and multi-agent settings in which part of the dynamics is another agent's policy.

I would like the problem of evil and the main theodicies translated into that vocabulary, one at a time, as faithfully as you can, and then a residue report.

The positions to translate: the evidential argument from evil — that the suffering we observe includes instances that look gratuitous, and that this is evidence against a perfectly good creator; skeptical theism — that our epistemic position gives us no warrant for judging any instance gratuituous; greater-good and soul-making theodicies — that some suffering is necessary for goods that cannot be had without it; the free-will defence — that certain world-histories are simply unavailable to the creator because they depend on what free creatures would choose; and the move that there is no best possible world at all, only an unbounded sequence of better ones, so that creating "the best" was never an option.

Before translating, please state explicitly which of the following mismatches you are absorbing into the model and whether each one breaks the identifiability results I normally lean on: creation looks like a single choice of an entire world-history rather than a policy I can query at many states, so I observe one trajectory, no actions, and no counterfactual trajectories; the dynamics are unknown and partly constituted by the policies of other agents the creator does not control, making this closer to a Markov game in which the principal chooses initial conditions and the other agents' reward functions; the reward apparently ranges over whole histories rather than states, since theodicies invoke narrative and holistic goods, which is non-Markovian reward that I can only represent by inflating the state with its own history; and the demonstration contains states that look bad on every feature basis I would natively reach for, so my prior over reward functions, not the data, will carry the argument.

Then give me the residue in three buckets: what translated cleanly, what translated only with distortion you had to introduce, and what could not be expressed at all. Four residues I suspect, which I would rather have tested than confirmed. First, I have one object where the theology needs two: the function being maximised and the good. My framework can stipulate that they coincide, but it cannot represent the claim that the creator's will is good as a substantive claim, and stipulating it is exactly the smuggling I want flagged. Second, symmetry: if the feature basis is unknown and reward is non-identifiable, whatever blocks the inference from observed suffering to the absence of a benevolent creator should equally block the inference from observed goods and apparent design to a benevolent one. In my own work a flat posterior is a familiar outcome and I respond by adding a prior; say what the analogue of that prior would be here and where it could possibly come from. Third, "gratuitous" is a normative notion with no counterpart in reconstruction error: an instance can be instrumentally necessary for a greater good and still be morally gratuitous, and I suspect my vocabulary cannot hear the difference. Fourth, identification in IRL comes from comparing trajectories under fixed dynamics, and here there is one trajectory and no possibility of intervention, so tell me honestly whether my sample-complexity intuitions imply that the entire debate is under-determined by a factor of roughly one world, or whether modal counterfactual trajectories — hold the purported good fixed, delete this instance of suffering, ask whether the history's value drops — are a usable substitute.

I also want to try two diagnostics on the theodicies and would like your verdict on whether they survive translation. The first is a complexity penalty on the reward representation: a theodicy that adds unobservable features, or worse, a reward defined by a lookup table over whole histories, should be penalised the way I would penalise a reward model fitted to a single demonstration, and I want to know whether any of the standard greater-good theodicies does better than that baseline or whether all of them collapse into it. The second is a counterfactual necessity test: for a given instance of suffering, does the good the theodicy cites actually depend on that instance, or does the same feature obtain in nearby histories without it? If the feature is invariant under the deletion, my instinct is that the theodicy does not purchase that instance. Say whether that test is fair to the position as its defenders state it, whether it is merely an instance of the general problem of feature inflation in reward modelling that I should already be worried about in my own work, and whether it has bite without the ability to run the world again.

For each residue, tell me whether it indicts my vocabulary or the original claims. Where it indicts my vocabulary, say what I would have to add to it — a second object alongside the reward function, a normative primitive, a notion of feasibility over histories — to express what it cannot currently say. What I do not want is a paraphrase that quietly substitutes reward-maximisation for divine goodness and then reports the problem solved or dissolved.
