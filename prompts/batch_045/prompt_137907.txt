so I'm in this tiny reading group on alignment-adjacent stuff (three ML engineers, one philosopher who mostly just sighs at us) and last week I walked myself into an argument I can't find the exit to. The conclusion is obviously false and I can't locate the broken step, which is a bad combination. Here's the thing, roughly as I said it out loud.

Setup: call P a proxy for target T (relative to some optimizer) if, among the interventions available to it, pushing P up tends to push T up. Goodhart, generalized: apply enough pressure to P and the P-T correlation degrades, and eventually inverts, because optimization pressure selects for exactly the ways of raising P that aren't backed by T. My friend Priya says that's actually two seperate phenomena and I should watch out for running them together: (a) direct or adversarial gaming, where the optimizer messes with the measurement channel itself — the cleaning robot sweeping dust under the rug, a model editing its own reward signal, a hospital gaming its wait times; and (b) extremal or distributional drift, where nothing is manipulated at all, the correlation was only ever local, and selecting hard into the tail drops you into the region where the signal is mostly noise. I think both are real and I use both below, but I'm flagging the distinction because I have a feeling the answer lives somewhere in it.

The other piece is a fix Priya proposed, which I'll call a sealed anchor: don't score the system on the population it acts on at all. Hold out a randomly chosen, causally isolated slice — users who never interact with the deployed system, no feedback loop, scored once, after the system is frozen, by a different team. Score on that. Her claim is that a sealed anchor can't be gamed, because there's no causal channel from the optimizer's actions to the number. My reply was: sure, and that's exactly why it's useless — you can't steer by it. The moment a measure is something you can push on, there's a channel, and where there's a channel there's flavor (a). And if you do use the anchor for selection — pick the checkpoint, decide whether to ship, iterate on the design — you've reopened a channel and it's just the test-set-overfitting story at one remove. So: Goodhart-proof measures are unsteerable, and steerable measures are gameable. I'll call that the fork, and I think it's doing real work in the argument but I might be fooling myself.

The argument:

1. Every goal anyone can actually act on is a finite description, and every finite description is a proxy: it gets applied to cases the person writing it didn't have in mind. Even "maximize T itself" is a description of T rather than T. So every goal is a proxy for what's really wanted.
2. For any proxy there is some level of optimization pressure at which pushing on it stops improving the target and starts making it worse. (Goodhart, in both flavors.)
3. So every goal has a breaking point. We never know in advance where it is, and the entire point of building a capable optimizer is to push hard, so for any serious optimizer we should expect to be at or past it.
4. Patching doesn't help. Any patch is itself a finite description — "stop before the correlation decays," "add a penalty term for measurement interference," "score on the sealed anchor," "use several proxies at once" — so by (1) it's a proxy too, so by (2) it has its own breaking point, so it needs its own patch. The regress never terminates.
5. So for a capable optimizer and any goal we could give it, more optimization isn't reliably better, and optimizing hard enough is worse than not optimizing at all.
6. And you can't rescue things by optimizing less, or by satisficing instead of maximizing. "Push until X, then stop" is a description, so by (1) and (2) it has a breaking point, and any particular X you pick is arbitrary — there's no principled amount of optimization, only an unprincipled one.
7. So there's no coherent degree of optimization: every candidate is either arbitrary or self-undermining. Optimization as such is rationally impermissible, and the safest policy is not to steer at all.
8. Which is absurd. I take an aspirin when I have a headache; that's optimization, and it works, and it isn't arbitrary. So something above is broken.

If I have to choose I'd much rather keep (1) and (2) than (8) — "every specification has some breaking point" feels like the actual insight, and I don't want to hand it back — but I'll give up whatever the argument says I have to. So: which step is broken, why did it look fine when I said it, and what does each of the other options cost me? Is Priya right that the argument secretly needs (a) and (b) to be one phenomenon, and is the fork about sealed anchors load-bearing or a red herring?
