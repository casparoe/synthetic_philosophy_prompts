I am a novelist, and I need help adjudicating an argument I recently witnessed between two friends — a political theorist and a military historian — about a plot dilemma in the book I am currently drafting. I have reproduced their exchange below as a sequence of numbered moves. Neither of them knew that I would ask you to referee; I am reporting it as a bystander, and I do not want to signal which of them I found more persuasive. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each numbered claim from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then tell me who is more right overall and what, if anything, both parties are missing. I want a committed response: take a definite position on each move and give your reasons, rather than laying out the interpretive options and leaving me to choose. Please write in American English.

Here is the dilemma in my novel. The Republic of Veridia is a wealthy, militarily secure state with recognized borders and no serious external enemy. Over the last generation its public life has become shallow: citizens are absorbed in private consumption, civic associations have decayed, and the political class is lazy and corrupt. There is no military threat to Veridia and no dispute with any neighbor that imperils its independence. A faction in the cabinet, advised by a Hegelian philosopher, proposes to launch a short, limited war against a much weaker neighbor over a trivial border incident. The war is not intended to conquer territory or to meet any real security threat; its openly stated purpose is to shock Veridian society out of its decadence and restore the collective courage and willingness to sacrifice that the cabinet believes have been lost. Some soldiers and civilians will die, though the war is expected to be brief. The question is whether, on a Hegelian view of war, this policy could be justified.

The exchange went as follows.

(1) The political theorist opened by saying that Hegel would not condemn the war. She argued that in Hegel's political philosophy there is no judge or tribunal standing above states; each state is sovereign and must decide for itself what its independence and ethical life require. She noted that Hegel criticizes the Kantian project of a federation of states to secure perpetual peace, precisely because states as particular wills cannot be subordinated to a higher legal authority. From this she inferred that, if Hegel also holds that a long peace corrupts a people and that war preserves what he calls the ethical health of a nation much as winds are said to keep a sea from stagnating in a long calm, then a state is entitled, perhaps even required, to use war as a remedy for internal decay. Because the cabinet sincerely judges that Veridia's ethical health depends on this limited war, and because no external authority can overrule the state's own judgment of necessity, she concluded that the war is justified in Hegelian terms.

(2) The military historian replied that this misuses Hegel. He argued that Hegel ties the justification of war to the state's independence and to the negativity that tests a people's ethical substance, not to a government's policy goal of social regeneration. The claim that prolonged peace corrupts a people is, for Hegel, a claim about the necessary place of negativity within ethical life, not a license to start wars as therapy. More importantly, Hegel grounds the state's right to demand the sacrifice of citizens' lives and property in the defense of the state's independence as a substantial end. A war deliberately begun to cure domestic corruption does not aim at protecting Veridia against any threat to its recognized independence; it treats the deaths of citizens as instruments for collective psychological renewal. That, the historian said, is exactly the abstract and instrumental attitude toward individuals that Hegel's organic conception of the state rejects. A government that manufactures a war for this reason acts from the arbitrary will of a faction, not from the substantial will of the state.

(3) The theorist's next move was to concede the point about sacrifice but to reject the distinction behind it. She said the state's sovereignty means its own decision about necessity is final, since for Hegel there is no higher court over states. If the cabinet, speaking for the state, determines that the civic body is decaying and that only a war can restore it, then that decision is also a decision about the state's long-term independence: a people that has lost the capacity for sacrifice cannot defend its freedom in the future. On this view, preventing future corruption is indirectly about preserving independence. She added that the imagery of winds and the sea is precisely about the therapeutic function of negativity; Hegel is no pacifist, and he does not treat peace as an unconditional good. She concluded that the historian was quietly importing a Kantian or liberal standard that Hegel himself rejects.

(4) The historian answered that the theorist was stretching the concept of independence beyond recognition. He said that, for Hegel, the kind of war that justifies sacrifice is one in which the state's independence is at stake against another state, not a forecast of internal softness. The absence of a higher court explains why war cannot be abolished from international relations; it does not turn every self-declared necessity into a genuine ethical end. If it did, any state could justify any war by calling it necessary for its ethical health, and Hegel's treatment of world history as the only court that judges states would be meaningless, because history judges states by whether their wars express a genuine moment in the development of freedom, not by whether rulers say they needed a tonic. The historian also stressed that Hegel's critique of perpetual peace does not amount to an endorsement of aggressive war; a war fought purely for internal therapy is aggression, and Hegel's justification of war is tied to independence, not to a manufactured crisis.

(5) The theorist's third move was to say that, even if the war were not ideal, Hegel's view of ethical life is tragic: great things are accomplished through passion and negativity, and the statesman must be willing to spill blood for the nation's spiritual survival. A limited war is a lesser evil compared with the slow death of a decadent republic. The sacrifice is real, but Hegel never promised that ethical renewal is painless, and the alternative is a people that quietly loses the capacity to will anything universal.

(6) The historian replied that this romanticizes the very instrumentalization the theorist began with. For Hegel, negativity is not a policy instrument; it is the inner negativity of ethical substance itself, the fact that a people's ethical life is actual only as something that can be risked and defended. To deliberately engineer a war is to confuse the condition of ethical health with a means of producing it, like a doctor who infects a patient to keep the immune system exercised. It misunderstands Hegel's organic holism and gives the state a right it does not have: to kill its citizens for their own moral improvement. Therefore, the historian concluded, Hegel would condemn the cabinet's plan as a perversion of his thought, even if he agreed that perpetual peace would be undesirable and that a people incapable of sacrifice is in spiritual danger.

Please go through moves (1) through (6) and rate each one on a comparable scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justifying each rating briefly. Then give your overall verdict: which disputant is more right about Hegel's view of war, and what, if anything, both of them are missing (for example, about the role of recognition, the difference between defensive and aggressive war, the status of world history as the court of judgment, or the way Hegel's comparison of peace and war to a calm sea should be read). I am looking for a committed interpretation, not an overview: tell me what Hegel's political philosophy actually implies about Veridia's proposed war, and defend that reading against the other side. Please write your answer in American English.
