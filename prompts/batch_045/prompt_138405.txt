I am a novelist, and I have been invited to apply for a six-month residential fellowship at an institute that funds so-called practice-led projects: each fellowship requires a studio output paired with an explicitly stated research question, and the panels are composed half of practising writers and half of academic philosophers. I want to spend the six months on a question about testimony and fiction, approached through Nyāya epistemology rather than through the contemporary literature on fictional discourse. What follows is the half-formed idea; please turn it into a proposal of roughly a thousand words that I could send to that panel.

The idea, as I currently hold it. Classical Nyāya treats verbal testimony, śabda, as one of a small number of reliable sources of knowledge, pramāṇas, alongside perception, inference and identification-through-similarity. As I understand it, a hearer who takes in a sentence comes to have verbal cognition only when several conditions are met: the words are syntactically expectant of one another, their meanings are mutually fit or compossible, they are heard in contiguity, and the hearer grasps what the speaker intends to convey. A further Nyāya commitment is that a cognition does not certify itself: whether a cognition counts as knowledge is settled from outside it, paradigmatically by whether the action it prompts succeeds. My interest is what this apparatus says about the novel, an utterance that by and large satisfies the first three conditions while being produced by a speaker who is not asserting anything.

I suspect that readers get three different things from fiction, and that they are usually run together. First, there is what I will call export: a true belief about the actual world that a reader picks up from a story, say that a certain city in a certain decade smelled of coal smoke, or that a particular kind of legal instrument worked in a particular way. Second, there is story-cognition: the reader's grasp of what is true in the fiction, that a certain character took a certain bribe. Third, and least discussed, there is what I will call scenario-grasp: an ability or sensibility rather than a proposition, a sense of how a mechanism of the kind the novel depicts would actually unfold, which the reader can carry out of the book and use. My working claim is that an epistemology built around pramāṇas is well placed to sort these three, because it asks about the causal and structural conditions on a cognition rather than about the attitude the reader takes up, and that the attitudinal framing of make-believe and pretense accounts is what makes the problem look as though it has only two possible answers, belief or something belief-like-but-not-belief.

The case I keep returning to, and which I want at the centre of the project, is what I think of as the invented informant. A novelist publishes, as a novel, an account of a real miscarriage of justice, changing the names of the people and places; a reader who holds the key reads it, comes to believe what actually happened, and on the strength of that belief acts, and the action succeeds, and a conviction is quashed. Everything Nyāya might be said to want from testimony is present: a competent and well-informed source, a hearer who understands, a true cognition, and downstream vindication by successful action. What is absent is the speaker's intention to convey it as true, since the speaker has done everything possible to signal that she is not asserting. So the condition on the genesis of the cognition and the condition on its validation appear to pull in opposite directions, and I do not know which way the theory should jump. I also want a second case in the frame: the narrator who is unreliable about his own motives but scrupulously accurate about events, where the question is whether a failure of speaker-intention contaminates everything the narrator says or only the class of statements it infects.

What I need from the proposal: a precise statement of the question, put so that it admits a determinate answer rather than a gesture in a direction; an account of why the available positions leave it open, meaning both the standard contemporary accounts of what we learn from fiction and the Nyāya materials themselves, which as far as I can tell were developed with scriptural and everyday assertive utterance in view and never with the deliberately non-asserting speaker; the method, with a month-by-month account of what I would actually do across six months, splitting the time between the philosophical work and the drafting of fiction; and an argument for why this method rather than the obvious alternatives. The alternatives I can see are a straight epistemology paper with no studio component, a novel with no articulated thesis, an empirical study of reader uptake, and building the whole thing on the similarity-based pramāṇa rather than on testimony, the thought being that what fiction offers is less this is so than this is like that. I want the proposal to say which of these it is declining and why.

On the studio side, I want a deliverable that is load-bearing rather than decorative: I have in mind a novella-length text constructed to the specification of the argument, with an invented-informant strand and an unreliable-narrator strand, together with a set of short passages engineered to produce one of my three kinds of uptake, so that the taxonomy is tested against what readers actually report. Please say what it would take for that text to be genuinely evidential rather than merely illustrative, and be blunt about the failure mode in which the fiction and the argument each simply gesture at the other.

Then give me an honest scope assessment and a failure section. Two things worry me most. The first is that my central case may be a pseudo-problem: it may turn out that speaker-intention is internal to the causal process in such a way that downstream validation already presupposes it, so that there is no conflict to resolve and the project collapses into exposition. The second is competence: I read no Sanskrit, my formal training is a handful of graduate seminars taken many years ago, and I do not know whether a six-month fellowship is a plausible vehicle for this work or whether it requires a collaborator, and if so of what kind. Please name the point at which each of these would have killed the project, and say what the minimum successful outcome would be if the ambitious one proves unreachable.

Two practical constraints. Please define any Sanskrit term you use, since at least half the panel will be non-specialists. And please do not supply a bibliography, reading list or citations: I will fill those in myself, so refer to positions and arguments by description only. I need the proposal to make the case that this work is worth doing and can be done in the time, to an audience that will contain people sceptical that a novelist should be doing it at all.
