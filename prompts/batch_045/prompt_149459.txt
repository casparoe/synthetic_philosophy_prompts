I'm a high school senior applying to college this fall, and over the past year I've ended up defending a position in arguments with my friends, my economics teacher, and my parents: that the thing worth fighting for is not free tuition for everyone but heavily subsidized or free attendance for students from low-income families, with income-contingent repayment for everyone else. This is mildly against my own interest — my family would probably qualify for nothing under a means-tested scheme and would save real money under a universal one — so I want to know whether I'm being principled or just posturing.

The version I actually hold is narrower than the soundbite that free tuition is regressive. The comparison everyone reaches for, Germany and the Nordic countries on one side and the US and the UK on the other, strikes me as confounded in ways that cut both directions, so I've tried to build the case out of mechanisms instead of national anecdotes. I rely on three. First, an enrollment-composition claim: the students who change their behavior when tuition goes to zero are drawn disproportionately from families that were already likely to send their children to college, so a large share of the public money buys enrollment that would have happened anyway. Second, a displacement claim: when tuition is abolished but housing, food, and transportation are not, borrowing does not fall one-for-one with tuition, because students borrow instead to cover living costs and consumption, so the anti-debt rationale for universalism buys less debt relief than its advocates imply. Third, a positional-good claim: because the earnings premium attached to a degree depends partly on where the credential threshold sits, expanding enrollment raises the private return to the marginal student while diluting the social return, and universalism is precisely the policy that expands enrollment most.

Two further things I hold more speculatively, and would most like tested. The first is what I've started calling the deadline effect: any per-semester price, tuition included, does useful work by making it costly to drift — to add a fifth year, a second minor, a semester of figuring things out — and I suspect that part of what gets counted as a benefit of free tuition is really students substituting time in college for time in the labor market, which raises attainment without raising lifetime earnings. The second is that the framing of the whole debate is wrong: income-contingent repayment is already close to a graduate tax, so "free versus debt" is not the real axis, and the honest question is only who absorbs the shortfall left by the graduates who don't earn much. If that's right, my opponents and I are mostly disputing the size of a parameter rather than two visions of society.

What I want is a map of my exposure. Which findings — about displacement, about who the marginal enrollee actually turns out to be, about non-take-up and administrative burden in means-tested programs, about time-to-degree, about what becomes of the low earner's residual balance under income-contingent repayment — would count against my position, and which would merely be inconvenient? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which of them someone who had argued my side in public would concede as a genuine defeater rather than explain away. Tell me specifically what my view predicts that universalism does not, so I can judge whether I've built something testable or something I can always redescribe after the fact; if it's the latter, say whether that is a real problem for a view of this kind or whether policy positions are allowed to be that slippery. And tell me where to place my own tripwire: what single result, if it came in tomorrow, should make a seventeen-year-old abandon the targeted-aid position and start arguing for universal free tuition?
