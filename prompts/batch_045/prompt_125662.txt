For a unit on AI policy in my debate class, I have been reading about the question of whose values an aligned AI system should serve, and one explainer I came across argues roughly as follows. Liberal democracy has already solved this problem: because the state may not favor any particular conception of the good life, it stays neutral among citizens' values, and an AI serving a pluralistic public should do the same. Concretely, that means training it on the overlapping consensus, the values everyone in the society already shares, and then, wherever people's values genuinely conflict, splitting the difference by averaging them. Averaging is needed rather than voting, because Arrow's theorem shows that any voting rule either installs a dictator or produces inconsistent results; but if you add up how strongly each person cares about each outcome, the dictator problem disappears and everyone's stakes get counted equally, which is just what treating people as equals requires. The article even names a training procedure for this, veil-of-ignorance fine-tuning: have the model rank outcomes the way it would if it did not know which of the affected people it would turn out to be, on the grounds that this is Rawls's veil of ignorance and will therefore make the system give priority to whoever ends up worst off. It concludes that an AI built this way would be genuinely neutral, and that its objective should be to maximize total satisfaction across everyone it affects.

I am suspicious of how neatly this hangs together, especially the slide from being neutral to adding up everyone's preferences, and the way the veil of ignorance is supposed to yield a special concern for the worst-off. But I do not know enough to say where it goes wrong. Which parts would someone who works on this actually accept, which are ordinary simplifications, and which are real conflations or overstatements? And does the final conclusion about maximizing total satisfaction survive once the mistakes are corrected?
