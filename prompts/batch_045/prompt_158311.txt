Hi — quick question from a novelist who's about two thirds of the way through a book that rests entirely on one analogy, and I want to know whether it can hold the weight before I write the last hundred pages.

The book: in a fictional German-speaking duchy in 1791, a copying clerk in the chancellery mis-files a single routine letter about a delay in a tax collection. The reader sees the consequences forty years later — a different ruling family, a different city, an industrial canal that never gets built. The thing I'm leaning on is the old rhyme: lose a nail, lose the shoe, the horse, the rider, the battle, the kingdom. One small thing, a whole world.

The trouble is my writing group has an actual historian in it, and she says this is storyteller's vanity. Her counter-picture is that history is more like a river finding its channel, or a glacier: slow structural pressure — demography, grain prices, state finances — and the nail is just the detail you reach for because you already know where the water ended up. She also says that even granting the butterfly thing people attribute to Lorenz, all it gives you is that you can't predict the weather, not that a letter changes the shape of a century. I half think she's right and half think she's answering a question I'm not asking, since I'm not claiming my clerk caused anything. I'm making something up.

So please audit the analogy. Walk me through the correspondence in plain sentences — what plays the nail, what plays the initial condition, what does the amplifying, what counts as the outcome, and who or what is in the position of trying to predict it. Then take it apart. In particular:

One — I suspect two different machines get run together under this rhyme. There's a brittle chain, where each link has no slack and the failure gets transmitted because nothing catches it; and there's chaotic amplification, where the same mechanism gets iterated over and over and nearby paths peel apart. The rhyme sounds like the first, the butterfly sounds like the second. Are they really different, which one would a chancellery in 1791 actually be, and does the difference change what I'm allowed to claim?

Two — the attractor worry. My understanding is that chaotic systems give you wildly unpredictable trajectories that nevertheless stay inside a bounded regime. If that's right, the honest version of my premise might be that nails decide who sits in the palace and never whether there's a palace, and my unbuilt canal is the wrong kind of divergence. Is that right, and what would a divergence that respects the regime look like instead?

Three — the hindsight objection. The nail gets picked because the outcome is already known; ex ante there were thousands of mis-filed letters that changed nothing. Does that objection even bite someone who picks the nail first and invents the outcome afterwards, or does it come back inside the book as a fact about my characters, who live forwards and can't know which letter matters?

Four — whether I should relocate the letter to a genuine knife-edge: contingency seems highest near a tipping point and close to zero when things are settled. Is putting my duchy at a near-tie the fix, or is it a cheat that just relocates the contrivance?

Five — is the river really competing with the nail, or is it a claim about a different level, about which facts are counterfactually robust? If both can be true at once, what's the honest way to dramatise that: nails changing the names while the grain prices grind on unchanged?

And tell me if there's a better analogy for what I'm actually doing, because I suspect there is.

Around nine hundred words would be perfect, and please no headings, no bullets, no bold text, no tables — just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email to a friend. No citations needed, I can't check them anyway.
