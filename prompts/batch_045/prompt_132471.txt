i'm in a small reading group working through stuff on blame and punishment, and one of the other members keeps ending arguments with a single line: "You do what you do, in any given situation, because of the way you are." He used it twice last week, and both times it was doing very different work.

First use: nobody ever deserves blame for anything, because of that line. Second use — the one I can't get my head around — the line shows that it doesn't even matter whether determinism is true. Even if determinism is false, he says, you still don't get moral responsibility out of it. When I objected that I spent about four years deliberately training myself out of being a selfish person, and surely I get some credit for that, he said: yeah, but you wanted to train yourself because of the way you were then, and that's the whole point. He says the line is the first premise of "the Basic Argument."

I feel like one sentence is being made to carry several different claims and I can't prise them apart. So: what are the distinct precise things that sentence could be asserting? I have in mind two axes you should probably separate out (but add others if I'm missing them). On "the way you are": does it mean your occurrent state at the moment of acting, your standing traits and dispositions, your temporally extended history including the parts where you shaped yourself, or the full causal ancestry of the action, environment and all? On "because of": does it mean mere causal influence, full causal determination by you-as-a-whole, full determination by something you didn't choose, or something non-causal, like "that's the explanation of why she did it" in a reasons-giving sense?

Give me the result as a menu, ordered from the trivial readings to the radical ones, and for each entry say: is this a platitude that no compatibilist would ever deny, a substantive claim that's actually defensible, a substantive claim that's shaky, or just false? Then, for each of his two conclusions separately (nobody deserves blame; determinism is irrelevant), tell me which readings would actually deliver it and which leave it hanging — I suspect at least one of them needs an extra premise that the sentence doesn't supply, maybe something about having to be the cause of yourself, but I can't say what.

Two cases I made up that I think should discriminate between the readings, but I can't work out what they show:

1. The bootstrapper. At 20 Ada hates that she's a coward. Every year from 20 to 50 she deliberately reshapes herself — exposure, taking harder jobs, cutting off people who make her avoid things — and by 50 she is reliably brave. Each year's reshaping was itself done because of the way she was the year before, and the series starts with a character at 20 she had literally no hand in. My intuition is that she's closer to being responsible at 50 than at 20. Which readings of the sentence allow "closer," and which force the all-or-nothing verdict that she's responsible for none of it?

2. The coin-flip shaper. Same setup, except that some of Ada's bigger character shifts are initially produced by a genuinely indeterministic event — say a random neural tie-break that could have gone either way — which she then notices and deliberately consolidates over following years. Does the sentence even apply to her on the readings where "because of" is doing real causal work? And if the indeterministic case is the one where it stops applying, what happens to his claim that determinism is irrelevant?

I want something I can bring back to the group as a list with verdicts, not an essay on whether free will exists.
