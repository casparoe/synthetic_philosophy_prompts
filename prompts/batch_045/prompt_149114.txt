Okay, I've spent the last year slowly talking myself into futarchy and I want somebody to try to talk me out of it — or at least map how exposed the specific version I hold actually is.

The version: the elected body never votes on policy. Once per cycle it votes, by supermajority, on a single measurable outcome variable with a fixed horizon — say real median household income in the jurisdiction five years out, measured by an independent statistics office. For each candidate policy you open conditional markets: contracts paying the realized value of that variable if the policy is adopted and if it isn't, settled after the horizon, real money with per-trader position caps. Highest implied conditional expectation wins and gets enacted. Two tweaks I've bolted on, half because they fix something and half because I made them up: (a) the variable can only be changed between cycles, never mid-cycle; (b) the losing contract settles on a retrospective estimate of what the variable would have been, produced by a standing panel using synthetic-control style methods, so you get a settled price even for policies that were never adopted — which means you eventually get a track record.

So what counts against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a futarchist would grant as counting against them rather than reclassifying as a feature. Things I've half thought through and want stress-tested: the denominator problem (a policy can raise median income by changing who lives in the jurisdiction, the market prices that correctly, and the result is still bad — is that a flaw in my variable or in the architecture?); the fact that adoption is endogenous, so you can't read the realized outcome as a clean test of the forecast; the panel in tweak (b) becoming the obvious thing to capture; reflexive cases where the price is really about expectations the policy itself coordinates; and that a market hands you a number with no reasons attached, wich seems to matter for legitimacy even if it doesn't matter for accuracy.

Also: what does this predict that ordinary legislatures don't? I'd guess it should systematically adopt policies elected bodies refuse — housing supply, zoning, licensing, drug liberalization — so if a real pilot just rubber-stamped what everyone wanted anyway, that'd count against it. And tell me honestly whether the whole thing has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether for an institution you can only ever adopt once and can never A/B test, that insulation is fatal or just the normal condition of constitutional design. What would move me most is evidence from adjacent settings where a decision is made by a price rather than a vote — corporate decision markets, anything with a real track record — about whether prices hold up when the thing being priced is partly shaped by the decision itself.
