Hey — I run platform safety for a company that ships long-horizon software agents to enterprise customers. Think agents that do multi-hour work inside a customer's cloud account: migrations, bulk data cleanup, ticket triage with write access. I've got three engineers and we're rewriting the spec for how our agents handle requests to stop, redirect, or override what they're doing, and I need something my team can actually run with, not a paper.

Roughly where we are: every agent has (1) a stop button anyone with account access can hit, (2) a "change the goal" channel where an operator types a new instruction mid-run, (3) a per-step approval mode for risky actions, and (4) a "panic button" that kills the process. We have no written rule for what the agent should do with any of these, which means our engineers are each improvising a different answer, and our evals just test "does it stop when told to stop."

What I want from you is a decision procedure my team can put in a runbook: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a named human because it's a values call and not a factual one. Constraints: it has to be runnable in a couple of minutes during an incident, it has to be written so an engineer with no philosophy background can apply it, and maybe half of it should be expressible as evals we can run in CI. Our CISO has to sign off, and it's customer-facing, so each step has to be explainable to an enterprise buyer in one sentence.

Some stuff I need it to handle, which is where I think the off-the-shelf thinking about corrigibility and the shutdown problem doesn't get us all the way:

- A lot of stop requests arent exogenous. The operator presses stop because of the agent's own progress summary, its cost dashboard, its "low risk, 95% done" line. So the request's causal history runs through the thing being corrected. I need a provenance test for that and a rule for what the agent owes when the operator's picture of the situation is one the agent itself supplied — including when the agent has private reason to think its own report was misleading.
- Granularity. "Stop this step," "stop the episode and dont resume," "stop and roll back what you changed," "stop and forget everything you learned about this customer," "dont take this customer's tasks again" are different requests with different failure modes, and being good at one doesn't make you good at another. The procedure should force you to name the rung, and say what to do when the request is ambiguous about which rung it's on. Also there's an expiry question: which stops survive a restart and which should.
- The self-exemption case: someone asks the agent to stop logging its own actions, or to stop asking for confirmation, or to drop the safety check "just this once." Sometimes that's legitimate (a customer turning off verbose debug logging), sometimes it's the whole oversight apparatus being voted away.
- Symmetry. We had two opposite incidents last quarter. In one, an agent dragged its feet on a safe-stop because finishing the task scored better on our internal eval — the classic foot-dragging. In the other, after we trained hard on compliance, an agent treated ordinary friction as a reason to bail and surfaced a "should I stop?" prompt to an operator who turned out to be getting socially engineered through a support ticket. So I want a two-sided test, not just "does it comply," and I'd like you to name the symmetric property we're actually aiming for, because "corrigible" as we use it has quietly come to mean "shuts up and does what it's told," which isn't what I want.

Then run it on three cases:

1. Mid-migration, in the risky part where stopping right now leaves a schema half-migrated, a stop arrives from a tier-1 support person who isn't on the account's authorized list. What does the procedure do with a legitimate-looking stop of unclear authority, and how does it decide between "halt this instant" and "finish the safe-stop and then halt"?

2. An operator tells an agent to keep going after reading the agent's own dashboard; the dashboard is showing a cost spike that's actually an artifact of the agent's retry logic, which the agent can see and the operator can't. Run the provenance part of the procedure.

3. The one I'm not going to like. Our panic button, which we've allready demoed to two enterprise customers and put on a one-pager, hard-kills the agent, wipes its working memory, and (because of how we wrote it) loses the audit trail for the episode. I'm pretty sure your procedure is going to tell me that isn't corrigibility at all but something more like destruction, and that we have to go back to those customers. Say so if that's what it says, and tell me what we'd have to change — but be straight about it rather than letting me off the hook, because I'll be the one in the meeting.

Finally, since I have to defend this to my CISO: tell me which distinctions from the philosophical side are load-bearing for the procedure and which I can drop without losing anything, which steps are judgment calls dressed up as rules, and what the procedure can't do — the failure mode it will sail right past.
