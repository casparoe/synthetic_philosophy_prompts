In a reading group on intentional-systems theory I asked whether a chess-playing program — one whose behaviour over many games we can predict better and more cheaply by attributing to it beliefs about the board and a desire to win than by any tractable description of its search routine — really believes anything, or only behaves as if it does. Someone replied: "Real patterns are real — if the intentional stance pays, there's no further fact of the matter about whether it really believes." The line was treated as closing the question, and I want to know whether it should have been.

Lay out, as a menu, the precise theses that one-liner could be expressing, from the platitudinous to the radical, and say of each whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false — and whether it would actually settle my question. In particular I'd like you to separate: (i) the claim that pattern-hood is objective, that compressibility is not merely in the eye of the beholder; (ii) the claim that predictive success from the intentional stance is good evidence of genuine belief; (iii) the claim that being a believer just is being predictable from that stance, so that no "really" remains to be asked about; and (iv) the claim that whatever real pattern we have found is the pattern of a belief rather than of a merely belief-shaped surrogate that mimics belief's predictive profile. Then tell me what becomes of each reading if the reality of a pattern is stance-relative — if the same system exhibits a real pattern from the design stance and none from the physical stance, or the reverse, which reading survives, and which collapses into the others? Please answer in British English, and keep it to a few hundred words.
