I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar on modality and conditionals this term with a colleague from linguistics, and we've arrived at a standoff that I would like a third party to adjudicate numerically rather than dialectically. The question is whether the contrast between indicative and counterfactual conditionals is genuinely encoded in the semantics — that is, whether a correct compositional semantics for English would have to state two different rules, or one rule equipped with a mood-sensitive parameter that does real truth-conditional work — or whether the contrast instead falls out of a single uniform rule interacting with the information state of the interpreter: pragmatics, context-sensitivity in the modal base, or the ordinary dynamics of conversation. My own credence that the contrast is semantically encoded is about 0.35; my colleague's is about 0.7. I want your number, and I want the decomposition that produces it.

Here is the decomposition I would like. (1) Your credence that a single variably strict, closest-worlds semantics of the sort standardly associated with Lewis and Stalnaker, with the selection function contextually supplied, can be stated so as to cover both moods without residue. (2) Your credence that the bridge principle standardly offered for indicatives — the identification of a conditional's probability with the corresponding conditional probability — has a genuine analogue for counterfactuals, and what you take the success or failure of that analogue to show. (3) A diagnostic I have been toying with, which I would like you to evaluate rather than merely apply. Consider a detective who has not yet ruled out that the safe was opened with a crowbar and says: if the safe was opened with a crowbar, the lock plate is scored. Now consider otherwise identical utterances issued by speakers whose evidence has progressively settled that the safe was opened with a key, up to a speaker who knows it for certain. If felicity degrades smoothly and continuously as the speaker's confidence in the falsity of the antecedent rises, that looks like a pragmatic, information-state effect; if there is a sharp discontinuity at the point where the antecedent is settled false, that looks like a semantic one. Give me your credence that this experiment, actually run with proper controls, would yield a clean verdict at all, as opposed to a pattern contaminated by independent factors: a general reluctance to assert about settled matters, intonation and focus, the availability of a set-aside-what-you-know reading, or the fact that a speaker who knows the antecedent false may simply be targeting someone else's epistemic state. (4) Your credence that the familiar cases in which a speaker who knows perfectly well that Oswald shot Kennedy can nevertheless felicitously utter an indicative conditional to the effect that if Oswald did not do it, someone else did, are a genuinely semantic phenomenon rather than a special deferential or suppositional use. (5) Your credence that the semantics/pragmatics boundary is drawn sharply enough here for the question to have a determinate answer at all, rather than being a choice between two empirically equivalent divisions of labor that we settle on methodological grounds.

Then combine the terms into your headline number, show roughly how the arithmetic goes, and tell me two things: which single term your final figure is most sensitive to, and where you think my 0.35 and my colleague's 0.7 most likely diverge — is the real disagreement about the semantics/pragmatics boundary, about the force of the known-false indicative cases, about how much weight a hypothetical experimental result deserves, or about something I have not listed? If you think one of my sub-questions is ill-posed, say which and restate it; and please do not answer with it depends on the framework.
