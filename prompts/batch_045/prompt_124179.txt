Right — I want to actually argue with you rather than get an essay, so: play Thomas Aquinas. The historical one, mid-1200s, using only the concepts you had then — natural law, the common good, sedition, the difference between a ruler with no title and a lawful ruler who governs badly — plus whatever I tell you about the case. Stay in character until I say stop. No "modern scholars would object", no hedging, answer as you. Keep it to a paragraph or two a go. And don't be agreeable: I want you to push back, especially if I start sliding towards "then any private person may act". Correct me as well if I get your own position wrong.

Setup, since you'll need it. Imagine a country — I'll make it up — where the head of governent came to power in a free election about fifteen years ago and has since used entirely legal means to make himself, in practice, unremovable: the courts restaffed by statute, the electoral commission moved under his own ministry, most of the press bought out by businessmen who owe him favours. Everything through the proper forms; no decrees, no mobs. He is also good at the job. Growth is up, the hospitals run better than they did, and polls say a solid majority approve of him and would vote for him again tomorrow. He leaves the Church alone and keeps public order.

My opening. By your own distinction he looks like a tyrant by abuse rather than a usurper — he orders government towards his own continuance rather than the common good, but he was elected, so his title seems fine. And you are oddly soft on that sort: you tell people to put up with a milder tyranny rather than risk the chaos of resisting, and you send them off to whoever has authority to remove him. But that is precisely what he has dismantled. There is no superior left to appeal to, and the very people whose right it was to institute him have been turned into a procedure that re-elects him. So the more complete and the more legal the tyranny, the less your framework permits anyone to do anything: it's got an off switch built into the worst case.

I can see two ways out and I want to know which you take. One: say that a ruler who destroys the community's capacity to remove him has thereby stopped holding power by title at all — the entrenchment is itself a kind of seizure, so he may be dealt with as a usurper, popularity and hospitals notwithstanding. Two: say the material prosperity and the genuine consent really do count, so that he isn't a tyrant in the relevant sense at all, and what looks like caution to me is just you refusing to call a competent elected leader a tyrant. Which is it — or is there a third way you'd rather go?
