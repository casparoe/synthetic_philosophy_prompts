Me and two friends got stuck on this for like two hours last night and I couldn't referee it. Setup is a doctored version of counterfactual mugging: Omega has already predicted whether you'll say yes to its request, and is right 90% of the time. It then flips a fair coin but hides the result, and asks you: will you pay $100? If the coin came up heads and Omega predicted you'd say yes, it hands you $10,000 after you answer. If the coin came up tails, nothing happens either way. Your answer only moves the $100 — the $10,000 depends solely on the prediction. Crucially you can't tell which branch you're in.

Sam says pay. Your expected return as someone who says yes is about 0.5 × 0.9 × $9,900 − 0.5 × 0.9 × $100, versus roughly 0.5 × 0.1 × $9,900 for someone who says no, so the "say yes" policy wins by a mile, and the coin result can't be what decides it since you're not allowed to know it.

Dana says refuse. The coin has landed and the prediction is on the books; conditional on those two facts, saying yes costs you $100 and changes nothing, in either branch. Sam's arithmetic is really a calculation about what you'd have wanted to decide *before* the prediction was made, and that decision isn't available to you anymore.

Sam: "before the prediction was made" is doing all the work. The prediction is accurate because it tracks what you'd do, so it isn't the kind of already-fixed fact that a coin flip is. And notice your answer doesn't change if Omega tells you the coin came up tails — you'd still refuse — so you're being insensitive to evidence about the very thing you call decisive.

Dana: of course I'm insensitive to it, because on my view it was never the point. Everyone grants that a policy can look better in advance while the particular act is worse now; the question is what to do now, and "what I'd have wanted to commit to" only reasons if I can still affect something, which I can't. If Omega ran this on me a hundred times with observers keeping score, I'd pay every time — reputation makes the causal link real again, and that's my point, not yours.

Sam: repetition just makes the policy reasoning visible, it doesn't create it. And your view is the same one that refuses the XOR blackmailer, which is right there but for a bad reason: if your policy is "never pay," receiving the letter means you have termites, so you get the warning for free, whereas paying just bleeds $1,000 in both worlds. Policy-thinking refuses there and pays here; you refuse both.
