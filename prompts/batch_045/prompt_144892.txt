I'm a graduate student in psychology writing up a study on procrastination, and I keep running into philosophers who tell me that what my subjects are doing isn't really irrational at all because of something called hyperbolic discounting. I want to check whether I've understood the dialectic before I cite it. Here's my reading: the akratic agent is the one who, at the time of action, does something she herself judges worse, and the standard explanation is that her discount function is hyperbolic rather than exponential, so preferences reverse as the smaller-sooner reward gets close. But then I don't see what's left to explain. If the discounting story is right, then at the moment of action the earlier abstaining is genuinely what she most wants, and her earlier resolution was just a prediction she was entitled to make at the time and that has since been falsified by a change in her own tastes. That looks like plain old preference change over time, which happens to everyone and isn't a failure of rationality or of will at all. Yet the literature treats weakness of will as a genuine normative problem, and people like Davidson apparently thought it needed something as baroque as partitioning the mind to explain. So: what is the mistake in my argument? Is there a reading of the discounting model on which it explains the phenomenon rather than dissolving it into preference change, and has anyone proposed a way of distinguishing the two empirically — say, a signature in the choice data that separates a preference reversal caused by hyperbolic discounting from one caused by the agent's tastes genuinely changing? Please tell me where my reconstruction goes wrong rather than just laying out the standard view, and if you think the akrasia/discounting debate has been conducted on a confusion, say so and say what the confusion is. Also flag how confident you are in each part of your answer, and single out the claim you're least sure of.
