I teach an advanced undergraduate metaphysics seminar, and I am assembling a two-page handout whose purpose is diagnostic rather than expository: a decision tree that takes a student from their verdicts about concrete cases of composition to a named position on when, if ever, some objects compose a further object. I need the tree itself, not prose about the debate.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of names. Start from five or six separating questions, each phrased as a yes-or-scale question a student could answer without already knowing which position it supports — for instance: whether the things in question compose something only when the result can do something its parts cannot; whether spatial contact or fastening is required; whether composing anything at all ever requires life; whether the relation can hold as a matter of brute fact, ungoverned by any qualitative feature of the candidates and their arrangement; whether composition is unrestricted when the world bottoms out in simples, and what is to be said if instead every part has a proper part; whether the composite is one of its parts' sums, identical to them taken together, or something over and above them. Then lay out which combinations of answers the questions permit, place the actual positions in the resulting space, and mark the combinations nobody occupies.

Cases to be used as the nodes' content, at least most of them: two hydrogen atoms bonded in an otherwise empty gas cloud; a cat that loses the tip of one whisker; a termite mound with its queen, workers, and fungus garden; the Eiffel Tower together with your left kidney; a pile of sand whose grains can be rearranged to make a barely different pile; a vortex in a river that persists for a minute; a stone arch; a cell; an atom-for-atom duplicate of your cat formed in a distant galaxy by chance and never touched by any organism; and a social club that gradually replaces every member while keeping its charter. Where you add a case of your own, make it a case rather than a label.

Constraints: seven to ten leaves, each with the standard name of the position or else the word "unnamed"; one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment; the organism-only answer and the unrestricted answer and the never answer must all appear as leaves; at least two unoccupied combinations, each with a one-line diagnosis of whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; and at least one pair of leaves that your questions cannot separate, flagged as such rather than forced apart with an extra distinction. Add a short note on what the tree should say to a student whose answer pattern fits no leaf. Assume the reader has had exactly one prior session on the question and no appetite for jargon introduced for its own sake; do not cite or recommend readings.
