You are a moral philosopher who works on population ethics and suffering-focused ethics. Think carefully before you commit: start with a short, candid brainstorm of the candidate mappings (including ones you will go on to reject), then give me your considered verdict.

In my research group, "it's just triage" has become the standard move for settling funding disputes. A medic with one dose and twenty wounded does not ask whose pain is most undeserved or who has the best life ahead; she ranks by urgency and by how much loss she averts per unit of scarce resource, and writes off the rest without guilt. People here use it to license three conclusions: (1) mild suffering drops out of the picture entirely; (2) averting catastrophic future suffering and reducing existential risk swallow nearly the whole budget; (3) causing or funding new happy lives is at best a wasted dose and at worst a distraction.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on those conclusions in the abstract. Give me the correspondence table — medic, patient, injury, prognosis, the single dose, the golden hour, writing someone off, the fixed budget — and say what each stands for here. Then give the disanalogies, and separate the cosmetic ones from the ones doing argumentative work: triage takes the baseline as death, a bad outcome to be averted at almost any cost, rather than as nonexistence; it has no category for patients who do not yet exist; its budget really is fixed and its horizon short, whereas spending here can build or consume future capacity; and it ranks by averted loss under a single agreed aim, while we have rival aims (least total suffering, least worst suffering, least suffering weighted by how much it matters to the subject). Call the variant in which the medic also decides whom to admit, including people who do not exist yet, "generative triage": does triage reasoning extend to the admission decision, or does it fall silent there?

Finally, say which of (1)–(3), if any, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other comparison would carry the argument better. Mark clearly what you take to be settled, what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and what follows only from a substantive commitment — about the asymmetry, lexical priority, or population axiology — that a reasonable person could reject, and do not present a contested claim as the consensus.
