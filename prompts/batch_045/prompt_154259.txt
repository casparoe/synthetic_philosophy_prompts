Hey — I'm a TA for an intro to ancient philosophy course (mostly first-years, usually 18-ish people actually show up) and I've got one 75-minute slot next week on Aristotle's account of exchange and money. They'll have read the exchange chapter in book 5 of the Nicomachean Ethics and the stretch early in Politics book 1 where he splits household management from wealth-getting, but I really don't want to just talk at them for an hour, and I can't print anything fancy — index cards, maybe dice, a whiteboard, that's it. Could you design a runnable activity: a trading game or little market simulation with cards for goods and services, payoffs in points, no real money, where they run into two things on their own instead of hearing them from me? First, why a pile of stuff nobody can compare directly — shoes, grain, a house, someone's labour, a flute lesson — seems to need a single conventional stand-in before it can be traded at all, and why need by itself can't do that job even though Aristotle clearly wants need to be the thing that really holds the exchange together. Second, the slide from acquiring things because you need them to wanting the stand-in itself without any limit: the moment the token stops being a yardstick and becomes the object of the game.

Here's the angle I actually care about, and it's a bit in the weeds. A friend of mine (econ PhD, a bit of a pest about this) calls it the two-hat problem, and I'd like the activity built so students feel it: money has to wear two hats at once. As a measuring rod it ought to be stable, external to the things being measured, and not itself something anyone consumes. As a hostage against future exchange — a claim on other people's cooperation later, when I need something I can't name now — it has to be worth holding and worth wanting. Those two job descriptions pull in opposite directions. So I want a round where the thing that works beautifully as a measuring rod breaks as a stored claim (needs change between rounds, or some goods are perishable, or the person you'd have traded with is gone), and a round where whoever just accumulated tokens ends up better off than anyone who produced anything. And if you can set the incentives up so that some student proposes lending tokens out and getting back more than they gave — Aristotle's complaint about money breeding money — and the rest of the room has to react to it without me prompting, that would be perfect. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the Greek labels until afterwards.

What I need from you: the full rules and materials list, a round-by-round timeline with roughly how many minutes each bit takes, the scoring, and then a debrief that maps specific moments that will have happened in the room onto the specific claims in the texts — including where a student's objection is actually Aristotle's own worry and where it isn't. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I'm guessing someone hordes, someone refuses to trade on principle, and the scoring gets gamed somehow) and how to handle each. And be honest about which part of the view the game can't reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud — my guess is the bit where the common measure is only conventional but need is somehow doing the real work underneath, since in the game I'll be the one who decides what the tokens are worth, but tell me what you think.
