I write literary fiction — mostly close third person, mostly what my writing group calls psychological realism — and for the past two years I have been working on a novel built around a structural device I have started calling the production register. Every scene in which the protagonist appears to deliberate and choose is printed twice on the page: once as the character lives it, in free indirect style, with the deliberation rendered as an unfolding present that terminates in a decision she takes herself to have made; and once, in a narrow column to the right, written in a flat, impersonal voice that describes the same moment from the outside — rival action-tendencies competing, one narrative line winning promotion to the version the character can later avow, the others surviving only as a mood she cannot account for. My own craft vocabulary is the vocabulary I would like an answer in: point of view and the discipline of staying inside it, free indirect discourse, the unreliable narrator and the gap between what a narrator says and what the text shows, the implied author, scene versus summary, character as a useful posit rather than a person, drafts and revision history, and the difference between what made it into the narration and what was left on the cutting-room floor. I have read a few popular books about consciousness and some essays years ago, but I do not know the technical literature, so please define any term of art as you go rather than leaning on it.

At a residency last winter a philosopher I trust defended, over several long walks, roughly this package of claims: that what a person calls her stream of consciousness is a heavily edited presentation rather than a faithful record; that there is no inner place where the presentation is assembled and shown to anyone; that the self to whom it is addressed is itself part of the presentation, a posited character rather than the thing doing the perceiving; and that first-person reports should therefore be treated as data about that presentation rather than as authoritative verdicts on what is really going on. I want that package restated in the vocabulary above, as faithfully and as charitably as you can, and then I want to know what the translation cost me. Specifically: what carries over cleanly onto the page; what carries over only after I have quietly changed its meaning; and what cannot be said in craft terms at all. Three places I keep tripping over, and would like you to address head-on: in fiction every illusion has a reader, and an illusion addressed to nobody is not an illusion — so what becomes of the claim once I ask who the user is supposed to be; whether the view is best read as a claim about narration only or as a claim that there is no further fact of the matter; and whether the quarrel between someone holding this view and someone who says that she knows perfectly well what it is like to taste coffee and that this is not an illusion is, in my terms, a verbal dispute — two editors disagreeing about whether a scene is in the book, one meaning the published text and the other meaning the drafts.

Please give me two sections, headed Translation and Residue, and keep the whole answer under 600 words: no more than 300 for the translation and no more than 300 for the residue. In the Residue section, sort your points into what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary, and close with a single sentence on whether the residue indicts my vocabulary or the theory. Use numbered or bulleted entries rather than flowing prose wherever you can. No footnotes, no citations, and no suggestions for further reading — I am working in a cabin without reliable internet and would not follow them up even if I could.
