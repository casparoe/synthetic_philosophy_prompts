I'm a novelist, and I want to know whether the position I've worked out for one of my characters actually hangs together. In the draft, a furniture restorer is drawn by a charming rival through a cycle of swaps — her workshop for a better-lit one, that one for a cheap place with a garden, that one back for her original — paying a small fee each time, and genuinely pleased with each trade as she makes it. I don't want her to be a sucker; I want her to be someone a decision theorist could respect. Reading about money pumps and diachronic rationality, here is where I've landed:

(1) An agent can rationally have cyclic preferences — A over B, B over C, C over A — when the comparisons turn on different considerations. There needn't be a single ordering, or a utility function, standing behind them.

(2) If each trade in such a cycle is genuinely preferred at the moment it is made, accepting it is not irrational. Being exploitable is not an additional defect, and the pump adds no reason that was not already present in the individual trades.

(3) Rationality does have a diachronic requirement: it can be rational to pay now to bind your later self — to make the will irrevocable, to destroy the letter you know you will post drunk — because you can foresee that later self acting against what you now have most reason to do.

(4) But a choice is not irrational merely for belonging to a sequence that foreseeably leaves you worse off than you began. That is why I don't think the character in the self-torturer case does anything wrong at any individual increment: the predicament is tragic, if it is anything, and not a failure of rationality at a step.

(5) In the final chapter she declines the third swap and keeps the garden studio, purely because she has seen the shape of the sequence she is in. I want that to be the moment she gets something right — a kind of practical competence, not weakness of will.

Audit this for me: which pairs genuinely conflict, and which only look as though they do? Rank the real tensions by severity, say for each what I would have to give up, and tell me whether there is one revision that would repair the most at once — and what it would cost me in the story.
