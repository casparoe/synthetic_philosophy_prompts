I'm putting together a short anthology of poems about concepts in moral philosophy — one poem per concept, each in a form whose mechanics do philosophical work — and mine is supererogation. I want a sestina.

Take the six end-words from the concept's own vocabulary; my suggestion is owe, give, praise, blame, grace, enough (replace any of them if you have a better set, but say why). The form has to earn its place: the rotation should leave the reader feeling that these six words are defined only in terms of one another, and the envoy, where all six crowd into three lines, is where the pressure should tell — the point at which the space for more-than-duty either holds open or snaps shut. Grace is in the set deliberately: the category comes out of a distinction between what is commanded and what is merely counselled, and the poem should know that without turning devotional.

Two things it has to get right rather than gesture at. First, the asymmetry — the same act is praiseworthy if done and not blameworthy if omitted, and that gap just is the category; don't let praise of the saint quietly convert the omission into a fault. Second, what I'll call the ratchet: the suspicion that the category is self-eroding, that someone who keeps doing more than she owes shifts the baseline of what she is owed and of what everyone now expects, so that grace hardens into debt. Commit to a verdict on whether the ratchet is a real feature of the practice or a confusion about it, and let the poem enact that verdict rather than assert it.

Close with a paragraph saying which contested reading of supererogation you've committed to and where the verse takes liberties with it.
