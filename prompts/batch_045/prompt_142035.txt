I've been chewing on something for our reading group on Strawson's Freedom and Resentment and I think I've built an argument that eats its own tail. I'd rather keep both halves of what it attacks, so I want to know which step is doing the damage.

The setup, briefly so we're on the same page: the participant reactive attitudes are things like resentment, gratitude, guilt, hurt feelings — responses to the quality of will other people show toward us; the objective attitude is the stance you take toward someone you see as a thing to be managed, handled, trained, rather than someone you can relate to as a person. Strawson's claim, as I read it, is that we can't adopt the objective attitude toward everyone always, that the reactive attitudes aren't a policy we chose, and so the metaphysical worry about determinism never gets a foothold on the practice of holding people responsible.

The argument:

(1) Holding someone morally responsible just is being prone to the reactive attitudes toward them and regarding them as apt. There's no further fact behind the practice that could make responsibility real or unreal.

(2) The reactive attitudes aren't adopted for reasons and can't be given up at will. Taking them up isn't a decision, and there's no coherent general adoption of the objective attitude — only local, temporary withdrawals in particular cases.

(3) So there's no standpoint outside the practice from which "is the whole practice justified?" could be asked or answered. The determinist's global question has nothing to stand on. (Note this is stronger than compatibilism: it's not that determinism is compatible with responsibility, it's that the question doesn't bite.)

(4) If there's no outside standpoint, then nothing external can be a reason to revise the practice. Criticism can only come from inside, from the practice's own standards of when resentment and gratitude are fitting.

(5) But those internal standards only ever issue verdicts on individual cases — did he mean it, was she coerced, was he in a position to know. They tell you when to withhold resentment from this person, and in doing so they presuppose the practice rather than assessing it. So internal criticism can't reach the global question either.

(6) Still, we take ourselves to have made real progress in how we deploy these attitudes: resentment has been substantially withdrawn from people with severe mental illness, from very small children, from people acting under duress. And we don't treat that as a shift in fashion; we think the older pattern was getting something wrong.

(7) And here's the bit that worries me most. The practice's own standard says resentment is fitting only toward someone whose will manifested disregard. Now suppose it turned out that no one ever had that kind of control over their will. Then, by the practice's own lights, no resentment would ever be fitting — a global verdict generated entirely from inside, with nobody having to step outside the practice and nobody having to actually stop resenting anyone. The counterfactual is enough; Strawson's point about the psychological impossibility of universal objectivity doesn't touch it, since nobody has to do anything.

(8) So the Strawsonian package either makes the history of moral reform unintelligible, or it contains a standard that can return a global no — which is the metaphysical verdict Strawson was trying to make impossible.

(9) Therefore either there is an external standpoint after all, or the price of denying one is that we can't say withdrawing resentment from the mentally ill was an improvement.

I dont want either horn. If I have to choose I'd rather keep the claim that the reforms were genuine improvements than keep the dissolution of the free will debate, but I want to know what giving up the dissolution actually costs me and whether I really have to. So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does each alternative cost?

And please be blunt about confidence. If the argument as I've set it up is muddled, or steps (5) and (7) are quietly equivocating, say so plainly instead of picking the politest step to reject. If you think the whole thing rests on a misreading of Strawson, I'd rather be told that than get a tidy answer to a broken question. Flag which parts you're confident about and which you'd want to think harder about before staking anything on them.
