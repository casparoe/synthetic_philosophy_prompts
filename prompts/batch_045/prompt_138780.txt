I write and edit a column on science and ideas for a magazine with roughly 200,000 readers, and an embarrassing share of the copy that crosses my desk turns on one word: random. Press releases tell us the chance that a randomly chosen patient, exoplanet, tweet or chord has some property. Our own writers say a typical one will. A monthly box prints odds in large type with no hedge. Most of the time this is harmless. Sometimes I have come to suspect the number is not a number at all.

For about two years my working rule has been a single question. I ask the writer or the press officer: random how — what procedure picks out the thing we are talking about? If they can name one, I let the sentence stand. I now think the rule fails in exactly the cases I invented it for. Told that a randomly chosen chord of a circle has a one-in-three chance of being longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, I ask how the chord is chosen; I am told, perfectly reasonably, that its two endpoints are picked independently and uniformly on the circumference; and my rule waves the sentence through. But picking its midpoint uniformly along a radius is an equally good answer and gives one-half, and picking its midpoint uniformly inside the disc is an equally good answer and gives one-quarter. My question always has an answer and almost never has a unique admissible one, so it lets through precisely the claims it was meant to stop. I gather this is an old chestnut in probability theory, and I have published at least one column built on the mistake.

So I want a procedure rather than a tutorial. Please write something my team can actually run.

What I am asking for:

1. An ordered set of questions — few enough to run in ten minutes — that takes a sentence containing random, typical, chance or odds and returns one of three verdicts: print; print with this specific amendment; do not print. For each question say what counts as a sufficient answer, and say where the procedure stops rather than continuing.

2. A distinction I think has to survive the simplification but that I cannot state cleanly: between a chance-fixing procedure that the world or the study design hands you, and one that the writer or analyst simply stipulates. The first makes the number report something; the second makes it report a choice. If that is the wrong cut, say so and give me the right one — but commit to one.

3. A step that catches the failure above: something like trying two or three equally natural ways of parameterising the same object and seeing whether the number moves. Be definite about how much movement is disqualifying and how many alternatives have to be tried before you stop.

4. The hard cases it must handle, at minimum: a finite population that is real but not enumerated; a population that is infinite; random attached to an event rather than an object; a study that genuinely did have a randomisation procedure but whose press release dropped it; a number produced by simulation, where the randomness lives in an algorithm; and a passage where the vagueness is deliberate and pedagogically fine, in a piece for children.

5. The points where the procedure has to hand the decision back to a person because the residual question is editorial rather than conceptual. Mark them; do not smooth them over.

Then apply it to three live cases. (a) A city forestry department: if you picked one of the city's street trees at random, there is a one-in-four chance it is a maple. (b) An astronomy release: for a planet on an Earth-like orbit around a Sun-like star, there is roughly a half-percent chance that a randomly oriented orbit happens to be aligned well enough for us to see transits. (c) A feature I have already commissioned: in an eternally inflating universe, the chance that a randomly selected observer finds herself in a galaxy like the Milky Way this long after the Big Bang is some very small number. I expect the procedure to clear one of these, amend one, and kill one, and I would rather you tell me straight if it is the piece I have paid for. I also want it run on my own chord column, with a verdict on whether I owe readers a correction.

Constraints you should write to: the people applying this are me (ten minutes, one methods class many years ago), freelance writers with no statistics training, and two fact-checkers with about forty-five minutes per piece. House style bans formulae and most jargon. The numbers box prints without hedges, so anything printed there is a commitment.

Two last things. First, where a default has to be chosen — which measure to assume when a paper says something was chosen at random and the methods section is silent, say — pick one and defend it in a sentence; do not give me the menu. I want your position, not the space of positions, including where you think my current practice is wrong. Second, two of my writers will say that every probability is conditional on something, so that singling out random is arbitrary and the whole exercise is pointless; give me an answer to them in a paragraph that concedes what is right in that and still leaves the procedure standing. And tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — including whether the honest upshot is that our style guide should simply ban the construction.
