I am a junior at a public high school, and I am one of two student members of my district's Committee on Naming and Acknowledgment. Roughly ten times a year we receive petitions asking the district to say something officially about a historical wrong connected to the schools: a campus named for a superintendent who resisted desegregation, a tracking policy that steered Black students away from college-preparatory coursework, a building that was constructed on land cleared by an urban renewal project. For each petition the school board expects a one-page recommendation choosing one of four dispositions: remain silent, acknowledge the harm without apologizing, apologize, or apologize together with a named act of repair. We have been deciding by instinct and the inconsistency is now visible to the community: last spring we issued a full apology over a comparatively minor matter and said nothing about a much larger one.

Please write a decision procedure that a committee like ours can actually run and defend. The constraints matter: we meet for ninety minutes, none of us has training in philosophy or law, we have no research staff and can work only from the district's own records and whatever the petitioners submit, and anything carrying a price tag must be routed to a separate budget process. Our recommendations are public and are usually reported in the local paper, and a board of seven elected members votes on them.

What I want most is the architecture: the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure returns a no and stops early, and the point at which it stops because what remains is a disagreement about values rather than facts and has to go to a vote of the full committee. Please also say which distinctions have to survive being simplified for lay people and which you would leave out as too fine for our purposes.

My faculty advisor sketched some distinctions that you should use, revise, or reject as you see fit: whether our standing to speak comes from institutional continuity with the body that acted or from the district's present enjoyment of what the wrong produced; whether a sizable minority within the district who publicly reject the apology defeats the collective 'we' it is offered in; whether an apology that the wronged party refuses is an apology at all or only a statement of regret; whether it is permissible to apologize while leaving material repair unspecified, or whether that amounts to using words to settle a claim for repair; and whether we are obligated to name the specific act rather than the harms of an era.

Then run the procedure on three petitions: one asking for an apology and a scholarship fund over a segregated annex the district kept open into the late 1960s; one where the people affected have told us they do not want an apology and would rather have records corrected; and one concerning the school I attend, built on a neighborhood cleared by a city redevelopment authority before our district existed. I expect the third one to come out somewhere I will not like, and I would rather have that stated plainly than have the procedure quietly bent to reach the answer I would prefer. Please also include the case where the petition asks us to apologize for one of our own earlier statements that was itself meant as an apology, and close by saying what the procedure cannot tell us and where a committee simply has to decide.
