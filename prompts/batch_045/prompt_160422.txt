I'm putting together a two-hour seminar on anthropic reasoning for a graduate reading group. We have already done Sleeping Beauty and the presumptuous philosopher, and I want one new case that makes a point neither of those makes. Here is the point I think is there. The standard framing treats the disagreement between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption as a disagreement about whether your own existence is evidence for hypotheses that contain more observers: SIA multiplies the likelihood by the total number of observers, SSA does not, so the two come apart exactly when the hypotheses differ in how populous they are. But if that is the whole story, then holding the total number of observers fixed across the hypotheses should make the two principles collapse into each other, since the factor SIA adds is precisely cancelled by the factor by which SSA discounts each individual observer. My hypothesis is that the dispute does not vanish when you do this; it relocates. Once totals are fixed, what does the work is the choice of sampling unit: whether the things you sample are observers, counted once each, or observer-moments, counted once per episode or per unit of subjective duration, so that a subject who undergoes ten indistinguishable episodes contributes ten times as much weight as one who undergoes a single episode. I want a case that isolates that second fault line and shows it can flip the verdict that the first two principles jointly deliver.

So please build me a case to the following specifications. Use these definitions throughout, because I know the labels are used loosely: under SSA the likelihood of your evidence on a hypothesis is the fraction of observers in the reference class whose evidence matches yours; under SIA it is that fraction multiplied by the total number of observers in the class, i.e. just the number of observers whose evidence matches yours; and under what I will call moment-sampling (Bostrom's own refinement of SSA, I gather, replaces observers with observer-moments, so this may not be a third view at all — tell me if so) the same two formulas are applied with observer-moments in place of observers, so that longer or repeated stretches of experience get proportionally more weight. The case needs: equal priors, set by a coin flip or the equivalent, with no rigged priors; finite numbers throughout, no infinite populations, no branching or duplication of a single person, no Boltzmann brains, nothing exotic — either a protocol a human subjects committee could in principle approve, or an empirical hypothesis about finite populations that could in principle be checked against a record; one and the same reference class for all the rules, so that the reference-class problem is not what does the work; SSA and SIA agreeing with each other, and agreeing on something other than one-to-one, so that their agreement shifts the posterior off the priors by a definite ratio rather than being degenerate; and the moment-sampling version dissenting, ideally in the strong sense of favouring the opposite hypothesis, and ideally under both the SSA-style and the SIA-style treatment of moments — if only one of those can be made to work, do that one and say which. It should not be a re-run of Sleeping Beauty or of the presumptuous philosopher. After the scenario, show the arithmetic for each rule explicitly, with the actual numbers, then state which single feature of the design is doing the work, what happens to all four rules if that feature is removed (equalize the number of episodes or the duration per observer, say), and how a defender of the moment-sampling rule would attack the case.
