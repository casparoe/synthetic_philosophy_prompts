I've got to give a 20-minute response to my supervisor's paper at our HPS reading group in a fortnight, and I've just realised I can't predict what she'll say about the example I want to use — which is a bit humiliating given I've read basically everything she's published. She's an unembarrassed Kuhnian: uses "paradigm shift" and "incommensurable" as working terms, treats the taxonomic reading of the later Kuhn as continuous with the 1962 book rather than a retreat from it, and will sometimes say in passing that the paradigm is what makes a field's objects available to it in the first place (a thing Kuhn himself half-took-back). Here are her verdicts, as best I can reconstruct them from her papers and from things she's said in seminar:

- Lavoisier vs. the phlogiston chemists: revolution, and genuinely incommensurable — dephlogisticated air and oxygen can't be picking out the same stuff, so Priestley and Lavoisier weren't having one argument, they were having two.
- Planck in 1900: not a revolution, just normal science getting creative under pressure; the revolution is Bohr's 1913 atom.
- Plate tectonics: revolution, but not incommensurable. Fixists and mobilists meant the same thing by continent, mountain belt, fossil, argued over the same rocks, and what really changed was which puzzles were worth a career and what sorts of data counted.
- Neptune in 1846: not a revolution. That's the paradigm working exactly as advertised, and she gets annoyed when people call predictive successes "revolutions."
- The double helix and the rise of molecular biology: she refuses to call it a revolution — a new specialty crystallising out of several messy fields, not a revolution inside one. Revolution has become a compliment, she says, and that has corrupted the word.
- Electron microscopy in cell biology: not a revolution by itself. Instruments don't do that on their own, though they can hand you the anomaly that starts one.

The case I want to spring on her is invented, which is partly the point — I want her rule, not her memory. Say a field (I've been using earthquake forecasting) spends a decade, roughly 2012 to 2022, moving from physics-based fault models with explicit, interpretable parameters to models trained on continuous seismic and geodetic data and judged purely on out-of-sample skill. Same journals, same departments, mostly the same people: the leaders of the old approach are the leaders of the new one and describe the switch as obvious progress. Every term — rupture, aftershock, stress shadow, recurrence interval — still refers to whatever it referred to in 1995, and people still read and cite the 1990s papers. But nobody asks for a mechanism any more; a paper that explains why rather than predicts well is close to unfundable; students are trained on a completely different stock of worked examples; the textbooks were rewritten within about five years. And here's the bit I think actually trips her up: there was no crisis. Nobody thought the old framework was failing, the new models just worked better, and everybody agreed, smoothly, inside half a decade.

So: what does she say — revolution or not, incommensurable or not — and what's the rule she's running? I can see at least two that fit all six of her verdicts and come apart on mine: one that ties incommensurability to whether the field's kind-terms keep their reference across the change, and one that ties it to whether the two camps still share a standard for what a good answer to the field's questions even looks like. The first definately seems to say "revolution, not incommensurable" and the second seems to say "incommensurable, and therefore a revolution." And then there's a third thing she repeats that I've never managed to turn into a rule: that a revolution has to come after a crisis, meet resistance, and be more like conversion than persuasion — which mine has none of, and which might push her all the way to "not a revolution, just a change of technique." I want her verdict rather than yours; if you think she's wrong, one line at the end is plenty.
