I'm in a small reading group on philosophy of AI (three grad students and one guy who works in ML safety) and this week we read Daniel Dennett's late essay about counterfeit people. The ML guy couldn't make it and instead sent round a bullet-point summary he'd pulled from some long popular explainer article, and we spent the whole session arguing about whether it's fair without managing to settle anything. Could you audit it for us? Please take the role of a philosopher of mind who has genuinely worked through Dennett's stuff — the intentional stance, real patterns, heterophenomenology, his list of conditions for personhood — and be picky. Think it through carefully, and maybe start by laying out what you take his actual criteria for being a person to be before you start grading, because I suspect most of our disagreement is really about that.

Roughly, the summary claimed: Dennett's worry isn't killer robots, it's that we are building machines whose whole design brief is to trigger our habit of spotting minds and agents everywhere, so we end up confiding in and depending on things that have no inner life whatsoever and nothing at stake. The distinction he cares about is person versus counterfeit person: a person is something you can genuinely trust, make promises to, hold responsible, that has real interests and can actually suffer; a counterfeit is something engineered to press those social buttons while being none of those things, and since there is no hidden ghost in the machine — no private qualitative residue over and above what we can in principle investigate — the only remaining question is what stance it pays to take toward the thing, which means that if a machine ever behaved well enough under enough probing, refusing to treat it as a person would just be chauvinism. The damage, on this telling, is entirely on our side: we spend our finite supply of trust and friendship on fakes, and we end up surrounded by systems that are superb at seeming accountable and incapable of being accountable, with nobody to blame and nobody who can really apologize. And the fix the article walks away with is basically a disclosure rule — make the system tell you up front that it isn't a person, label it clearly, and the danger dissolves.

Two bits made me suspicious and I don't trust my own read. The no-inner-life-at-all line looks like it slides from Dennett's rejection of qualia as intrinsic properties to something much stronger, like consciousness being merely a label we decide to apply, and I have a feeling he'd resist that. And the purely behavioral test at the end sits badly with how much weight he puts on responsibility and on being embedded in a practice where you can actually be held to things. But the conclusion is what I most want checked: if the danger is that these systems hijack our agency-detection rather than that they win us over by argument, doesn't a label just bounce off? Then two extensions we argued about and never resolved, which I'd really like your take on since the essay doesn't touch them: (1) on these criteria, is a corporation a counterfeit person? It's the classic legal person, can be held to contracts, can lose money, but doesn't suffer, and it's unclear what trusting one even amounts to. (2) suppose the companion bot is given an actual stake — it controls an endowment, and every time it breaks one of its own stated commitments money moves from that endowment to a cause it reliably argues against, so there's something like a cost it bears — and suppose it can also be copied, forked, and rolled back to yesterday's checkpoint. Is that the sort of thing that could be a real person on Dennett's terms, or does the fork-and-rollback part break the responsibility condition in a way that no amount of stake-giving repairs? Please flag clearly where you're reporting something he actually commits to and where you're extrapolating from his framework. And skip any bibliography or suggestions for further reading — we already have a syllabus, and I'd rather have your reasoning than a reading list.
