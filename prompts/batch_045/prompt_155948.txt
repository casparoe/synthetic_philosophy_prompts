I sit on the heritage advisory panel of a mid-sized city. We must give written reasons for each decision, our decisions can be appealed, and our remit covers a mixed stock: street names, a school, a hospital wing, two statues on civic squares, a set of regimental plaques in a church, and a former workhouse we are turning into a museum. Reading what philosophers and historians have written about commemoration has not helped as much as I hoped, because nearly all of it argues case by case — this statue of a slave trader, that college named after an imperialist, those barracks named after a general — rather than saying what a position on such cases has to be able to do. So I want the specification before I want the verdicts.

Set out the desiderata that any account of the ethics of commemoration in public space would have to meet to be adequate for a body in our position: what it must be able to decide, which distinctions it must be able to draw, which considerations from neighbouring areas (the theory of speech acts, the ethics of public reason under pluralism, the economics of network goods, the law of evidence) it must not contradict. Below is my draft list, with the case behind each entry. I want you to separate the genuine requirements from the prejudices of one camp, repair or discard the ones that are badly stated, add what is missing, and mark which are non-negotiable for us and which we could trade away.

1. **Uptake profile.** A street name, a school name and a station name are things residents and pupils must utter and use daily; a statue on a roundabout can be walked past; a plaque in a museum must be sought out. Draft requirement: an account must either give different verdicts for objects with different uptake profiles or explain why that difference is morally inert. Case: renaming the street that holds a hundred households' addresses and two dozen businesses' letterheads, versus removing a figure from a square nobody crosses.

2. **The composition of the standing stock.** Almost everyone we have ever commemorated did something we would now condemn, and we do not remove for mere mediocrity or private vice. Draft requirement: an account must not entail emptying the whole stock, or must bite the bullet and show that emptying it is correct. Case: our civic square, in which every one of the eight statues honours a man who profited from empire.

3. **Anachronism and moral luck.** Draft requirement: the account must say whether the wrongdoing has to have been recognised as wrong at the time, and what to do with someone who scrupulously followed the norms of his own day. Case: a benefactor who endowed the city's hospitals with money made in the slave trade, in a jurisdiction where that was lawful, respectable and unremarkable.

4. **Graded commemoration.** Draft requirement: it must distinguish "commendable on balance" from "commendable in every respect", and must permit partial, annotated or conditional commemoration rather than only keep-or-remove. Case: a street named after a campaigner for women's suffrage who was also an enthusiastic eugenicist.

5. **Which speech act controls.** A monument is erected once and then stands for a century, during which its audience and its meaning change. Draft requirement: the account must say whether the original act of dedication or the present act of retaining it carries the weight, and how the two combine. Case: a memorial raised in 1900 as mourning for the dead of a colonial war, as against one raised two decades later explicitly as a claim about who rules the city.

6. **Decision under historical uncertainty.** Draft requirement: the account must yield a decision when the record is contested, and must allocate the burden of proof between those who want retention and those who want removal. Case: a merchant whose fortune is disputed between slaving and ordinary trade, with the archive incomplete and two rival biographies in circulation.

7. **Distributed burdens.** Removal and renaming impose costs on people who did no wrong: residents' addresses, businesses' branding, a charity's donor name, the affections of those who simply like the old name. Draft requirement: the account must weigh these against the expressive harm of retention and say whether they can be compensated or only regretted.

8. **Commemoration versus testimony.** We preserve the wall where people were shot and the barracks of a camp without honouring anyone; we also possess a statue of the man who ordered it. Draft requirement: the account must mark the line between commemorating and testifying, and must say what "kept as evidence" does to an object's meaning — including what happens when an object removed from honour is quietly moved to a store rather than to a museum.

9. **Publicity, pluralism and standing.** Draft requirement: the account must be statable as a public reason, must not rest on premises a reasonable citizen could reject, and must answer the question of who decides — an expert panel, an elected council, a referendum, the descendants of those wronged — separately from the question of what is right.

10. **Genericity and settlement.** Draft requirement: the account must apply beyond our kind of case, and must take a view on time: whether longevity itself counts in an object's favour, whether a decision is ever final, and whether every retention should carry a review date.

Three things I want flagged explicitly. First, any pair of desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect the pair is the demand for verdicts under empirical uncertainty with a burden-of-proof rule (6) together with the demand that an account never require a lay panel to settle contested history, but tell me if I have picked the wrong pair. Second, the single requirement that does the most work in excluding live positions. Third, any requirement that is really only a dislike of one camp's conclusions dressed up as a methodological constraint.

Then, briefly — a small table at the end is fine, the criteria are the centrepiece, not the scoring — how four families of view fare against the list you end up with: retain-and-recontextualise; removal keyed to the gravity of the commemorated person's wrongdoing; procedural views that defer to whichever constituency is affected; and the abolitionist view that public space should not be in the business of honouring individuals at all.

Please write in British English.
