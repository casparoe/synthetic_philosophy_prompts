I'm a senior taking an elective in bioethics, and I've been stuck on a question about something that happened last year: a friend told our school counselor she had a plan to kill herself, and within a few hours she was in the back of a police car and then on a psychiatric hold for several days, against her will, in a hospital gown, with her phone taken away. She was released saying nothing had changed except that she had learned never to tell anyone again, and she was angrier at the adults than she had been at anything before. Separately, for the philosophy half of the course, I read Hume's essay defending suicide, the one where he mocks the idea that we are like sentries posted by God who need permission before leaving our post, argues that a person who has become a burden to others does them no injury by withdrawing, and says that when continued life has become worse than death suicide can be a duty rather than a crime. Both of these things have been sitting in my head at the same time and I can't get them into one picture.

What I actually want to know is what view of persons, rationality, or reasons our suicide-prevention institutions presuppose, because the pieces don't look consistent to me and I can't tell whether that's my confusion or theirs. In almost every other corner of medicine a competent adult's refusal is binding even when it leads to their death: you can refuse chemotherapy, you can stop dialysis, you can decline a blood transfusion on religious grounds, and the hospital has to let you die. You can also give away a kidney, which is irreversible and of no medical benefit to you, after a short consent conversation. You can enlist in the military at seventeen. You can smoke, or free-solo, or eat yourself into a heart attack, and no one restrains you. But if what you want is death and you say so, the state may restrain you and it appears to treat the fact that you want death as part of the evidence that you are not competent to want anything — and, as far as I can tell, the one direction in which the law will let an earlier self bind a later one is when the earlier self asks to be stopped during a future episode. So: what theory is this practice committed to? That death is a harm of a different order because it is irreversible? That there is a "real" or future self whose will outranks the present one? That a desire for death is, as such, a symptom rather than an evaluation? That autonomy protects the *source* of a decision but not its *content*?

My own guess, which I can't make work, is that the practice is really committed to a claim about the reliability of the process rather than about the content of the judgment — that acute crisis and depression distort reasoning in a way that refusing chemotherapy does not — but if that were right, the trigger ought to be a diagnosis or a measurable cognitive deficit rather than the fact that what you want is to die, and it looks to me like the trigger is the outcome, not the process. So please do three things. First, reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me honestly where it is underdetermined and several different theories (a Humean one with a reliability caveat, a value-of-life view, a temporal-self view) would all license what we do. Second, tell me what would have to change in the practice if the Humean picture were the right one, or if we replaced the implicit view with a content-neutral competence test. And third, and this matters most to me: tell me where your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong and what would change your mind — which argument, or which consideration about how these institutions actually operate, would make you say the practice rests on something other than what you've claimed.
