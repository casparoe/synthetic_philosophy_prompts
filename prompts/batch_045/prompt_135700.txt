I'm on the committee that maintains the habitat classification our conservation agency uses for statutory site designation — about ninety types, revised every six years or so, and every revision ends in the same three-hour argument about whether a category is a real thing or just a box we find convenient. It matters because designation triggers legal protection and money, because the ecologists and the landowners' lawyers both get to appeal, and because every time we align our scheme with the neighbouring countries' one we have to say of each pair of categories whether it's one thing under two names or two things that happen to overlap. On the last field trip I got into an argument with a colleague about whether "unimproved", which is defined by the absence of any history of fertiliser, is even a property, and we got nowhere, partly because neither of us could say what we were requiring of a theory of properties.

So before the next revision I want the spec, not the theories. What would a theory of universals — properties, repeatables, whatever you want to call them — have to get right for it to be usable for what we do? Give me each requirement together with the case from our work that makes it one, and mark which are non-negotiable for this purpose and which are just what one camp insists on. Here's my starting list, which you should add to and cut down where something isn't really a requirement: why a hundred sites can all be of one type at all, and how two sites with almost nothing in common physically can both be of it; whether a type has to earn its keep by doing causal work, given that the whole point is predicting what happens if we drain or graze or burn, and what to say about a type none of whose instances anybody has ever disturbed; what we're doing when two of our types overlap without either being a subtype of the other; what it takes for a predicate in our scheme and a predicate in theirs to pick out the same property, and whether the theory has the resources to keep apart two properties that apply to exactly the same sites in every circumstance; whether properties that hold by absence or by disjunction count; what happens with categories fixed by a cut-off somebody chose, where forty percent cover is in and thirty-nine is out — is the property vague or only our word for it; whether the levels of our hierarchy are genus and species or something more like determinable and determinate, and whether the top level is anything over and above the bottom ones; and, for "even-aged", "viable", "connected", which belong to the stand or the population and not to any individual plant, what instantiation by a collective even requires.

The one I most want an answer on is something I havent seen discussed. Maybe half our categories seem to carve at the joints in one part of the country and to be an arbitrary slice of a continuous gradient in another: same predicate, same written definition, real and repeatable and predictively powerful in the uplands, gerrymandered in the lowlands where the gradient runs differently. Is that a coherent thing to demand that a theory of universals accommodate — naturalness that is local, or that comes in degrees — or does it just show we've confused one joint-carving property with two, one of which is abundant junk? If a theory admits it, what does it cost elsewhere? If none does, say so and tell me which of my other requirements is doing the damage. Also: name any pair of requirements on your own list that you think can't be had together, say which one you'd drop and why, and tell me which single requirement rules out the most contenders. A short score at the end for the usual families would be useful — transcendent/Platonic, immanent or in re, tropes, resemblance and class nominalism, and the ostrich view that there's nothing here to explain — but keep it to a line or two each and put the weight on the criteria, since the criteria are the part I'll actually use.
