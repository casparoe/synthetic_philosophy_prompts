ok so I had a long argument with a friend after our team offsite last night and I'm still chewing on it, partly because I think she might be half right and I can't fully say why.

Context: I'm ops lead at a ~120-person nonprofit that builds research software, and we keep a public "commitments register" — every promise we make to partners, funders, users, and each other is supposed to go in it. Two things have made it weird. First, it's now partly auto-populated: a model reads our meeting transcripts and Slack threads and files anything that looks like an action item into the register, so we have entries nobody ever actually meant to commit to ("we should probably try to get to that this quarter" now sits there looking like a promise) and, separately, plenty of real commitments made in hallway conversations that never got logged at all. Second, a big chunk of what we've committed to is owed to diffuse groups rather than to anyone in particular — we told a user community we'd never train on their data, we said the API would stay free forever — where there's often no measurable reliance by anyone and no single person who could release us. Team leads come to me about twice a week with "are we actually bound by this?", and the situations that come up are pretty repeatable: the person who made the promise has left, or we got acquired, or the cost of keeping it has ballooned, or two commitments have turned out to be incompatible, or the promisee never followed up and may have forgotten.

What I want is a decision procedure they can run themselves, in about fifteen minutes, in plain language, with no philosophy background, that outputs a short written justification we file next to the register entry so we can audit ourselves later. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands the decision up to me because it's a values call rather than a factual one. I especially want it to separate out: whether a promise was made at all as opposed to a prediction or a statement of intention or enthusiasm; what exactly it binds us to in scope and time; whether it survives nobody having relied on it, or nobody having even read it, or the register having logged it wrongly in either direction; who has the standing to release us when the promisee is a crowd; and what we owe when the honest answer is that we should go back and renegotiate openly rather than quietly let the entry lapse. Where the answer depends on which view of promising you take — her line was that a promise nobody heard and nobody relied on, which would now cost us a lot, simply doesn't bind, because the whole point of promising is the assurance or the reliance, and quietly dropping it deceives no one — say so explicitly and flag it, don't pick silently. Then run the procedure on three cases: a loud public promise in a blog post that nobody in particular relied on and that now costs real money; an auto-logged register entry that began as "we should probably try to get to that this quarter"; and two incompatible promises to two long-standing partners, one of whom is much more dependent on us. Include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I definately won't like, and tell me plainly what the procedure can't do. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — the one-sentence version of the procedure plus your verdict on the three cases — then give the reasoning and the full procedure; no preamble and no restating my question back to me.
