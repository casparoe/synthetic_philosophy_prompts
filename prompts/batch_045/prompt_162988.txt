I'm on a tiny heritage committee in the town I grew up in (not going to name it) and we've spent about six months arguing about one object: a granite obelisk in the market square, put up in 1898 to a local man who made his money importing sugar from slave-run plantations in the Caribbean. One faction wants it taken down, one wants a plaque bolted on, one wants it left exactly as it is, and none of us are philosophers. A friend of a friend — she writes a philosophy blog and is definately cleverer than me about this sort of thing — heard about the argument and sent us a two-page note saying Nietzsche had basically sorted this out a hundred and fifty years ago, and that we should stop floundering and use his three kinds of history. I can't get hold of the essay anywhere and our library doesn't have it, so her note is all I've got. It reads:

"In On the Use and Abuse of History for Life (the second of the Untimely Meditations, 1874), Nietzsche argues that history is worth studying only insofar as it serves life rather than truth, and he sorts it into three kinds. Monumental history is the history of great men and great deeds, kept alive so that we have models and examples to copy; it belongs to rulers and ambitious people who need role models. Antiquarian history is the history of ordinary people and their origins — local customs, old buildings, family graves — kept out of a kind of piety, and Nietzsche thought it was psychologically necessary for the weak, the humble and the conservative, who cannot face creating anything new. Critical history is the mature stage: here you stop using the past and start judging it, setting aside your own present interests and asking, as impartially and rigorously as you can, what actually happened and whether it was justified. Since Nietzsche thinks history has to serve the living, and since the living need to be free of whatever oppresses them, critical history is his actual recommendation — the other two are stages a culture passes through on the way to it. Applied to your obelisk: monumental history just flatters the donor, antiquarian history venerates him because he's local, and only critical history does the honest thing, which is to weigh the man dispassionately, find him condemnable, break with him, and take the monument down."

Something in me balked at the word impartially, because the sentence just before it says history is there to serve life, so how can the best kind be the disinterested one? And I have a vague memory from somewhere that Nietzsche's remedy for being crushed under the weight of the past wasn't more judging of it but something about forgetting, and about art — though I might be mixing that up with something else entirely. But her note is confident, it has a date and a German-sounding subtitle in it, and it tells our committee to tear the thing down, which happens to be the outcome I'm least comfortable with, so I don't trust my own reaction either way.

Can you audit the note for me? Go through it more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who has actually worked through the essay would accept, what they'd call a fair simplification, and what they'd say is a straight misreading — I'm particularly suspicious of the way she maps the three kinds onto three sorts of people, the idea that they're developmental stages with critical history at the top, and that impartial-judging characterisation. Then, the bit I actually care about: once the misreadings are corrected, does the note's conclusion about the obelisk still follow, or does a corrected Nietzsche leave our committee with a different set of questions to argue about? Please write in British English.
