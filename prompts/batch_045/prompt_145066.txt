I teach a first-year seminar on language and cognition, and every year the discussion of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis collapses into the same two ditches: the strong version is obviously false, the weak version is obviously true, and nobody is any wiser by the end. The readings are part of the problem — they argue where they should embarrass. So this year I want to open with something that does by ridicule what the syllabus does by argument, and I'd like you to write it.

What I have in mind is a Swiftian modest proposal cast as an internal policy memorandum of roughly 800 to 900 words: an invented "Director of Applied Linguistic Design," writing from a fictional Office of Cognitive Infrastructure to a cabinet secretary, proposing a phased programme of compulsory grammatical reform of English. The centrepiece should be the abolition of obligatory future-tense marking — retiring "will" and "going to" in favour of present-tense-plus-adverb constructions — sold with a straight-faced cost-benefit table on the strength of the much-cited finding that speakers of "futureless" languages save more, smoke less, use condoms more, and hold more retirement assets. Around it, the memo should bundle three further reforms, each drawn from a real result in the literature rather than invented: the removal of grammatical gender from nouns (with the adjective-elicitation finding about bridges and keys doing the work), the mandatory introduction of absolute cardinal-direction spatial marking modelled on Guugu Yimithirr or Kuuk Thaayorre — "the cup is north of the plate" — piloted through the driving-licence test, and a procurement standard for safety signage keyed to the Russian light-blue/dark-blue distinction and the faster colour-discrimination it supposedly buys.

The jokes have to come from getting the linguistics and the methodological worries right, so that someone who knows the literature laughs rather than winces while a student who has only seen a TED talk still follows. In particular, the memo should exaggerate real features of the position: the slide from "this grammar makes this discrimination faster or more salient" to "this grammar makes this thought unavailable"; the confound problem, where the causal variable might not be the grammar at all but what it travels with — subsistence pattern, schooling, climate, ancestry — including the awkward datum that some of these effects survive into second-generation speakers who no longer speak the heritage language; and the bootstrapping problem of who is qualified to design a new grammar when every candidate's thinking is already shaped by one. Somewhere the memo must also explain, in perfectly ordinary English prose, exactly what it is that speakers of other languages cannot think — and should come within a hair's breadth of noticing the difficulty, then file it under implementation risk. Give me one short quote from a "concerned" colleague in the psycholinguistics unit who raises the obvious objection and is answered with appalling bureaucratic competence, and let the piece end deadpan: since grammar-only transmission turns out not to be doing the work, the programme should escalate past grammar to something a reader recoils from — and the final phase should include a design feature of the reformed English that makes the reform itself unreviewable, closing with a dated implementation timetable.

A few constraints, please. Invent all the internal apparatus — office names, committee numbers, working papers, pilot sites — and don't attribute anything to a real paper or author, so that nothing hinges on citation. Stay deadpan throughout; no winking at the reader, no authorial aside that lets the reader off the hook. Do not build any joke on the "hundreds of words for snow" trope or on invented exotic vocabulary: a linguist should be able to read this without wincing, and the target is the argumentative move and the policy genre, never the communities who speak the languages in question.
