Here's my opening, and I want a real answer to it, not a recital of the BHK clauses:

You ban excluded middle in general, but you don't actually treat all sentences the same way. For a decidable predicate A you're perfectly happy with "for all x, A(x) or not A(x)", because a decision procedure just is a construction. So the ban isn't really about evidence in general — it's about which sentences happen to have evidence, applied by a rule that can't see the difference. Now take a Pi-1 style statement like Goldbach, or "this Diophantine equation has no solution": a finite search would refute it if it's false, but nothing finite would ever confirm it if it's true. Call that half-searchable. My offer to you is: adopt excluded middle for half-searchable sentences only. That looks more faithful to your own motivation than a flat ban, since it tracks exactly the structure that makes evidence available. But I think the offer poisons itself, and that's the bit I want you to deal with: whether an arbitrary sentence is half-searchable usually isn't decidable either, so to apply the restricted principle you first have to settle a question of just the sort the principle was meant to settle. So it seems like either your flat ban is a blunt instrument that isn't tracking evidence at all, or the restricted version can't even be stated. Which horn do you take? And if you take the second and do grant the restricted principle, tell me straight whether you've just swallowed Markov's principle — and if you reject it, what actually breaks, because I've never actally seen the argument that it dies on contact with choice sequences and I suspect I'm parroting something.
