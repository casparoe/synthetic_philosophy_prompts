Hi — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond some blog posts, so please keep it concrete and don't send me a reading list; I can read blog posts, I can't get at paywalled journals, so argue from the structure of the thing rather than pointing me at papers.

Context: I maintain a scheduler that hands out a shared pool of GPUs between about forty internal teams. Each team files a cardinal utility function over allocations (we made them report numbers because everyone just said they needed it more). Right now I aggregate by summing with a hand-tuned per-team weight. It works okay-ish, but the weights are basically vibes and every reorg restarts the argument.

Someone on our internal ML forum pointed me at Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and said it "proves" any sane social welfare function has to be a weighted sum of individual utilities. I read the summary and i have a few things I dont get, and I suspect at least two of them are the same thing.

(1) As far as I can tell, the theorem only says there exist weights; it doesn't say what they are. And worse: each team's utility function is only defined up to a positive affine transform, so if team 7 doubles all their reported numbers and I halve their weight, I get the exact same ranking. So "weight" cant mean "how much we care about team 7" — it looks like caring multiplied by an arbitrary unit. Is that right? And if so, in what sense did the theorem tell me anything about aggregation at all, other than ruling out shapes like max-min?

Here's my fix, which I want you to poke holes in: normalize every team's utility to [0,1] over the allocations we could actually make this quarter, then use equal weights. When I tried it, two things happened that freaked me out. (a) The ranking of allocations A and B flipped when a third allocation C got added to the menu, even though C was never chosen and nobody's preference between A and B changed. (b) A team whose utility barely moves across any of the options on the table — easy to please either way — ends up counting exactly as much as a team whose whole quarter is on the line, because both got stretched to [0,1]. Are (a) and (b) the same bug wearing two hats, or genuinely different? Would anchoring on two fixed reference allocations instead of the min and max over the current menu dodge (a), or does that just move the arbitrary choice to the anchors? I sort of expect there's no normalization that's both menu-independent and doesn't throw away intensity info, but I can't see why yet, and I'd like the gut-level reason rather than a proof.

(2) The summary said the theorem also needs the social ranking itself to satisfy the vNM axioms over lotteries. I don't understand what a lottery is even doing here — nobody is rolling dice on GPU allocation, we pick one allocation. And it feels like if you assume the aggregate ordering is linear in probabilities you've already assumed utilitarianism in disguise and the "theorem" is just unpacking that assumption. Is that the standard complaint, or am I misreading what's being assumed about the social side versus what's being assumed about the individuals?

(3) Someone else said the real fix is the impartial-observer version, where you pretend to be equally likely to be any team and then maximize your own expected utility. That reads to me like it just relocates the arbitrary bits: who says the probabilities are equal, and why should the observer be risk-neutral about which team they turn out to be? If the observer is risk-averse over that, I think I get something closer to "priority for whoever's doing worst", which is a totally different scheduler. But also — and this is the part I keep going in circles on — the teams already know which team they are. A team that lost the coin flip is not going to be consoled by "you might have been team 7." Does that matter for whether the veil version is usable here, or is it just a separate complaint that doesn't touch the math?

Please give me the intuition-first version of what the theorem is actually doing, what it's smuggling in, and where the real choice is hiding. Equations are fine after the intuition, not instead of it.
