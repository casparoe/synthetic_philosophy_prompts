I'm in a small reading group that works through one formal epistemology paper a week, and over the last few sessions I've become convinced by van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the constraint that my credence now in some proposition, conditional on my later self's landing on a particular credence in that proposition, should simply be that later credence (for every value I assign positive probability to). Roughly: don't second-guess your own future credences; defer to them now. Two things make it feel like more than a slogan to me. First, the diachronic Dutch book / Dutch strategy argument seems to give it real teeth: violate Reflection and a bookie who knows your violation can arrange a bet now plus a bet later that loses you money however things turn out, while satisfying it blocks the strategy. Second, it appears to constrain changes that aren't evidence-driven at all — the unruly shifts, drift, mood, even a bit of motivated reasoning — which is why I don't think it's just a repackaging of conditionalization. And I quite like the thought that it might do normative work that plain Bayesian updating can't, e.g. telling you what to think now about periods where you'll get no new evidence but will move anyway.

So tell me what would count against it. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I especially want the ones a sympathetic defender would concede as genuine hits rather than the ones only critics trot out. Is the Dutch strategy argument actually sound, or does it smuggle in what it was supposed to prove — that you'll bet later at your then-credences, that you can bet on your own future credences, that a bookie can know about your violation in the way the setup needs? Is there a version of the case where your future self gets strictly more evidence, is perfectly rational in every ordinary sense, and Reflection still tells you something you shouldn't believe? A friend in the group thinks the self-locating cases settle it: once the relevant propositions are centered, deferring to a fully informed future self can be straightforwardly wrong. I can't tell whether that's fatal or just fixes the domain of application. And I want to know whether the standard escape hatches — you must be certain your future self is rational, hasn't forgotten anything, isn't drugged or brainwashed, isn't one of several successors — are principled scope restrictions or a protective belt: has Reflection been qualified into something close to trivial, and if it has, is that actually a problem for a principle that's advertised as a requirement of rationality rather than a piece of ideal theory?

Two cases I made up, because the ones in the literature all feel like variations on the same trick. (1) I know in advance that tomorrow I'll be given a drug that leaves my total evidence unchanged but pushes every credence toward the extremes by a fixed rule — middling confidence gets sharpened into near-certainty either way, and I know the rule exactly. Reflection seems to demand that I now defer to that sharpened future self, i.e. become near-certain now on evidence I haven't got, which looks crazy to me; but the obvious reply is that I'm not certain future-me is rational, so the principle never applied. Fine — but then is there any case where future-me is rational and undeceived and the principle still bites wrongly? (2) The split-and-merge case: tonight I get scanned, and tomorrow two equally-me copies wake up, one given evidence package E and one given package F, both fully rational, both as much me as the other, and then they get merged back into one person by some averaging procedure. There isn't a single future credence function to defer to. Does Reflection go silent, does it force my current credence to equal some mixture, or does it break in a way that tells us something about what the principle was really about? Take those two as tests: which of them, if any, is a real defeater, and which only looks like one because I've built a background condition out of the case?
