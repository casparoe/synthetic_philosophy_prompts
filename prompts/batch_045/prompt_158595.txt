I want to practice arguing that probabilities are degrees of belief, and I would like you to be the strongest opponent available to me: a committed propensity theorist. You hold that a probability is an objective, single-case disposition of a chance setup — a physical tendency in the world, not a feature of anyone's information — and that observed frequencies are evidence of propensities rather than what the word means. You may concede incidental points; you never concede that propensity talk is reducible to, or replaceable by, talk of credence. Stay in character until I say "stop", and keep each reply to about two paragraphs: sharp rather than exhaustive. Two stipulations so we do not waste turns: I grant you from the outset that single-case propensities are real, so do not argue for their existence; and take it as read that I know the bridge principle saying rational credence should track known chances — if you reach for it, make it do further work rather than treating it as a stopping point.

My opening. Consider a one-shot device: a die is cast once from an alloy doped with a short-lived radionuclide, rolled exactly once, then destroyed. There is no reference class, no long-run series, no repeatable experiment — and I have stipulated that it still has a genuine single-case propensity to land six. Forward probabilities pose no problem for you. So answer the other direction instead: in your vocabulary, what is the probability that this die came from the high-doping batch, given that it landed six? That is the number inference actually needs; it is the output of every update I ever perform, and it does not look like a disposition of anything to bring anything about. If you reply that it is a credence and propensities merely supply its inputs, you have conceded that your interpretation covers the easy direction and falls silent about the direction doing all the epistemic work. If instead you save yourself by making dispositions symmetric — a two-way constraint on the nomological network rather than a forward production — you lose the asymmetry you needed for independent trials and for causation. Which horn do you take, and what does it cost you?
