So I somehow ended up on the planning commision for the small city I grew up in, and it's turned into the most morally annoying thing in my life. We hear maybe thirty variance and rezoning proposals a month, forty-five minutes each, one-page staff report, and public comment that is mostly people being furious about parking. Votes get appealed, so whatever I do has to be defensible in writing afterwards. I want a decision procedure I can actually run, in about half an hour, from a one-page report plus whatever gets said in the room, and that my fellow commissioners — a retired contractor, a high school principal, two realtors — can follow without any theory background.

What I want from you is the procedure, grounded in whatever political philosophy actually has to say about who has a right to the city. No reading list, no names, I just want the distinctions. The one I keep coming back to is that "we have a right to this neighborhood" seems to be three different claims shuffled together: a right of access (to be allowed to move here at all), a right of authorship (to have a say in what the place becomes), and a right of continuity (to not have the ground shift under you because of other people's choices, and to not be handed the bill for the region's growth). Almost every objection I hear is one of those wearing the costume of another. I'd like the procedure to start by sorting which one is actually being claimed, and I'd like it to say whether length of residence strengthens the claim genuinely or is just a proxy for something more defensible — I honestly don't know and I've gone back and forth.

Three things it has to handle. First: the people most affected usually aren't in the room — the family that would live in the building, the person who'd use the shelter, and, weirder, the fact that what we decide partly determines which people end up existing here at all, so "who is harmed" can't just be read off the folks at the microphone. Give me a rule for who speaks for them, and be honest that it's a rule you chose rather than one you derived. Second: sequencing — refusing a building is reversible next year and approving a demolition isn't, and I want to know whether that asymmetry is a real moral reason for a default or just status quo bias in a nice coat. Say which, and build it in or leave it out accordingly. Third: the cases where the objection is real but the thing being asked for is a veto rather than a fix — how do I tell those apart, and where does the procedure hand the decision back to a human because it's a values call and not a factual one? Also fix the scope if the procedure needs it fixed: I never know whether the relevant "city" is my city or the whole metro area, and I suspect the answer changes the outcome.

Then run it on three: (1) a six-unit building on a corner lot currently zoned for one house, (2) a sixty-unit supportive-housing project on a commercial strip two blocks off a single-family street, and (3) something I'm personally inclined to approve — a small apartment building a friend wants to put on the lot behind his house — where I'd rather find out I'm being inconsistent than get my way. And tell me plainly what the procedure can't do: which inputs it can't manufacture, when it will misfire, and what it says nothing about at all.
