I've been reading the rationalist blogs since back when LessWrong was still a subfolder of Overcoming Bias, and I have never once seen the fanaticism fight actually get resolved, so I'm dumping this one on you. Two people from my old EA house — Wes and Talia — got into it after dinner about whether the simulation argument should make you less of a fanatic about the far future. I told them I was staying out of it. I've been Turning it over since though, and I definately want a verdict.

(1) Wes opened with: the reason longtermism goes fanatic is the size of the tail. If the future holds something like 10-to-the-fortyish morally relevant lives, then a one-in-a-million shot at nudging the trajectory beats anything you could do in the near term, and it gets more extreme the further out you look. But conditional on Bostrom's trilemma landing on its third horn, that tail probably isn't there. Ancestor simulations are run to study the past; the cheapest thing for whoever is running one to do is terminate it right around the point where the simulated species would itself start building simulations. So the far future inside the sim is short, the stakes are roughly the people alive around now, and the astronomical tail evaporates. He said this is the best anti-fanaticism proposal going, because it's a bound with a mechanism behind it instead of just stipulating that utility is bounded because unbounded utility makes people uncomfortable.

(2) Talia: you're taking the many-sims step and then dropping it the moment it's inconvenient. The only reason you get to conclude we're probably simulated is that there would be vastly more simulated observers than base-reality ones. But if there are zillions of ancestor sims, your decision is very likely duplicated across a huge number of them, and if what gets evaluated is your decision procedure rather than your particular token, you should count all those copies. Simulation doesn't shrink your impact, it multiplies it. Fanaticism gets worse, not better.

(3) Wes: okay, but that multiplication only bites if the copies diverge after your choice and if your decision theory aggregates across them, and you settle neither of those by thinking about simulations. Then he said there's a bigger point he should have led with: conditional on being in a sim, our cosmological evidence is rendered, not measured. You can't read off the carrying capacity of the real far future from a sky that was drawn for you. So the tail isn't truncated, it's unmeasurable — and the precise-looking numbers that fanatical expected-value calculations run on were never real inputs to begin with.

(4) Talia: unmeasurable isn't small, and if anything it cuts the other way. Whatever the simulators run on could support enormously more computation than our physics suggests — faster clocks, bigger memory, maybe accessible infinities if the substrate is weird enough — so the tail gets fatter and the unbounded-stakes problem comes back worse than before. Also: if our evidence is screened off, then the astronomical waste argument is screened off with it, and Wes doesn't get to turn around and use it in the next breath to say that delaying is costly.

(5) Wes: that's not symmetric though, and the asymmetry is anthropic. Most simulations are cheap, and cheap simulations are short, low-resolution, and shut down early, so the median sim you should expect to find yourself in is a small one. Not that the tail is zero, but that you should expect the tail to be unremarkable — which is exactly the kind of modest, centered prior that defuses fanaticism without needing to prove anything about simulator motives or simulator hardware.

(6) Talia: typicality reasoning about sims is precisely where anthropic arguments eat their own tail, and anyway, if we're in a sim the highest-leverage move might not be steering the far future but keeping the sim running — staying interesting enough to whoever's watching that they don't pull the plug on a species that just discovered agriculture. Which would reorient cause prioritization toward something near-term and legible, i.e. anti-fanatical in shape, but for a reason neither of them started with — and you can't estimate that either, so it collapses straight back into cluelessness.

(7) Wes: fine, then the honest upshot is that the simulation argument doesn't hand you a number, it hands you a reason to distrust every number, and whether that dampens fanaticism depends on whether fanaticism is driven by stake size or by confidence about stake size, and those pull in opposite directions. Talia: no they don't — if you're a fanatic because you chase tiny probabilities of huge payoffs, then making your estimate diffuse should make you more hesitant, not less, since you can't even say which option has the big tail.

They both looked at me. I went and made tea instead.

So: adjudicate it move by move. Rate each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification, tell me who's more right overall, and tell me what both of them are missing. The thing I especially want: is dampens fanaticism even the same question as shrinks the expected value of the far future? I have a suspicion they talked past each other on exactly that for twenty minutes without either of them noticing.
