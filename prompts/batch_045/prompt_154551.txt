The situation: I am applying for a nine-month doctoral fellowship. The deliverable is a single paper of about 10,000 words, plus a one-page practitioner's annexe for engineers and product leads who have to classify systems in practice. Working title: "Tools, agents, and the interruption condition."

My half-formed idea is this. Most attempts to draw the tool/agent line look for a property *inside* the system — goal-directedness, planning, a world-model, the capacity to respond to reasons, some threshold of generality. But in practice the line seems to be drawn by something relational, and I think that something is what happens when a human principal intervenes mid-task. With a tool, you can suspend the operation, redirect it, and resume it, and the resulting sequence is still redescribed as *one action of yours*; nobody has to renegotiate what the process is for. With an agent, an interruption is something the system has to accommodate among its own ongoing concerns, and after the interruption there is a question about whether the resumed sequence is still your action or now partly its own. Call this the interruption condition. My suspicion is that it does real work that the internal-property accounts do not, but that it is not a single condition: interruptibility in fact, the need to *justify* an interruption to the system, and the redescription of post-interruption behaviour as the continuation of the principal's action can all come apart. A batch job nobody can now stop is uninterruptible but has no ends; a system with genuine goal-directedness that has been heavily shaped for compliance is trivially interruptible but arguably still has ends that must be overridden. So perhaps the tool/agent distinction is really two or three distinctions that we have been running together, and the interesting philosophical pay-off is a map of where they diverge and which one(s) our practices — blame, explanation, delegation, liability — actually track.
