hi, looking for a hand with a case study I keep going in circles on.

I want you to read the two entrenched bits of Article V of the US Constitution as Ulysses contracts, properly and a bit pedantically. You know which ones: the clause saying no amendment before 1808 could touch the importation of slaves, and the clause saying no state can be stripped of its equal vote in the Senate without that state's consent. Both were placed inside the amendment rule itself, so they were shielded from the ordinary route, and neither was a pure supermajority requirement — they were outright prohibitions on a certain content.

Here's roughly what I want, though push back on the framing if it's wrong:

1. A disciplined mapping rather than a vibe. Who is Ulysses here, who or what are the sirens, who actually ties the rope, and who is supposed to hold the other end? The whole point of the Ulysses story is that the binding is autonomy-enhancing because the agent binds itself against its own predictable future weakness of will. But the guys writing this in 1787 were binding a population twenty and two hundred years down the road. So my first question is whether the standard justification survives that: if the binder and the bound aren't the same agent in any interesting sense, are we looking at a Ulysses contract at all, or at something else that just looks formally like one?

2. The valence flip, which is the bit I can't get straight. In the usual story the sirens are the agent's own bad impulses. Here, at least from where I'm sitting, what the clause blocked was the *better* impulse — abolishing the slave trade earlier. Whatever else it was, it was a moratorium on moral improvement bought with a deadline. Does the Ulysses framework have any way of handling a precommitment against your own future virtue, or does it just quietly assume the untethered future choice would be worse? My hunch is that that assumption is doing enormous covert work in the whole self-binding literature, but tell me if that's a cheap point.

3. What actually happened, and whether the thing worked. Congress moved against the slave trade essentially as soon as the 1808 window closed; the Thirteenth Amendment lands in 1865; and the Senate clause is still sitting there, unamendable in practice. Say plainly whether the precommitment succeeded on its own terms, and be honest about what success could even mean, since I obviously don't get the counterfactual of 1787 without the clause. I'd like you to name the counterfactual you're implicitly relying on.

4. Where the theory goes quiet. Two places I have in mind, add your own. First, enforcement: a Ulysses contract needs someone to hold the mast, but if the agent is a sovereign people then every candidate enforcer is an institution that agent itself created, which looks circular. Second, and I think this one is a genuine gap: the Senate clause is formally indistinguishable from self-binding but functionally operates as a permanent minority veto. Hostage-taking and self-restraint wear the same clothes. Is that an anomaly the framework should have predicted, a case it never contemplated, or am I overreading?

If it helps to have a contrast case where the rope just got untied: the UK's Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 said elections needed a two-thirds vote of the Commons or a particular confidence route; in 2019 a government got round it by passing an ordinary short statute with a simple majority, and in 2022 it was repealed outright. Compare the two and tell me which variable in the theory the difference actually tracks — enforcement costs, whether a decisive majority ever really wanted out, whether the binding was bundled into something people independently valued, or nothing the theory predicts at all.

One thing I don't want: a tour of the literature, or a list of names and books. I can't check them and I don't need them. Work it through from the case.

And please do the following: say how confident you are in the main claims you make, in rough terms, and single out the one specific thing you're least sure of. My guess is that the weakest link is the union-preservation counterfactual in point 3, but you tell me where you actually feel the floor is thinnest. I'd much rather have one sharp claim with an honest confidence tag on it than five paragraphs of hedged mush.
