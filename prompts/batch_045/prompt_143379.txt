I am finishing a position paper on practical reasons for a proseminar, and I would like the set of commitments I have ended up with audited before I circulate it. Stated as neutrally as I can, I hold the following.

1. Internalism about reasons, of the sort associated with Williams: an agent has a reason to act only if there is a sound deliberative route from that agent's subjective motivational set, as it actually is, to her acting. No route, no reason.

2. Some claims about reasons that no such route supports are nevertheless straightforwardly true. Two cases I care about: a man who has lost every project he once had and now wants nothing still has a reason to get help; and a company director who is entirely unmoved by the fate of her workers has a reason not to shut the plant and destroy their livelihood. In neither case, as I see it, is there anything in the agent's motivational set from which the act could be reached by deliberation.

3. The internalist is not entitled to repair 2 by idealizing the agent: if the route is allowed to run through what the agent would want if she were fully informed and perfectly rational, then the condition in 1 comes out vacuous and the view has simply become externalism under another name. I have been toying with a narrower device, what I call reasons of upkeep, meant to cover the first case, where the point of the act is to restore an agent's very capacity to be moved by reasons at all; I am genuinely unsure whether it is anything more than a label.

4. Reasons themselves are facts about the world rather than mental states. When I say that the state of the bridge is a reason for you not to cross, the reason is the condition of the bridge, not your desire to get home.

5. Motivational externalism about moral judgment: a person can judge in full sincerity that she has conclusive reason to act and feel no pull whatsoever, and where moral judgment does move people, that is explained by a contingent desire to do what is right. I also want to say that rationality itself requires responding to one's own judgment about what one has most reason to do, and that failing to do so is a rational failing.

6. Blameworthiness requires reasons-responsiveness: an agent is blameworthy for what she has done only if she could have recognized and responded to a reason against it. I want to go on blaming the director in 2.

7. Ends are themselves answerable to rational criticism, not merely the fit of means to ends. The director's projects can be criticized as such, and not just as incoherent or self-defeating.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey of the literature: which of these genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look as though they do, and for each real tension, what exactly has to give. Please rank the tensions by how much of the set they cost me, and for each, say what the cheapest repair would be and what that repair costs elsewhere in the set. I would rather be told that one commitment has to go than be offered three quiet revisions.
