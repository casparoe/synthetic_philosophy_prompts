"Look — the common prior assumption, or the Harsanyi doctrine [he might have mangled that name], says that before any of us got our private evidence we all had the same probability distribution over how the world might be. Rational differences of opinion, on this picture, come entirely from different information, never from different priors. Once you grant that, Aumann's result is basically arithmetic: if two people share a common prior, and each one's posterior probability for some event is common knowledge between them — I know yours, you know mine, I know that you know mine, and so on without end — then those two numbers have to be equal. You literally cannot agree to disagree. And you don't need to pool all your evidence either; just swapping your current posterior numbers back and forth a few times forces convergence, and it still goes through if the numbers are only approximate, say to within a percent. So a disagreement that survives full disclosure of each other's reasoning is proof that somebody isn't being rational, or has an interest in not agreeing, or that you weren't really disagreeing about the same proposition in the first place. The same thing kills trade: if I'm willing to sell you a share at a price you're willing to pay, we must disagree about what it's worth, and two rational agents with a common prior wouldn't, so every voluntary trade shows that somebody is making an error — which is basically the efficient market hypothesis written small."
