I'm in a small reading group on mental causation — six of us, half philosophy and half cognitive science — and last week I tried to reconstruct the exclusion argument in what seemed to me its strongest form, deliberately without leaning on any particular author's version, because I wanted to see whether the machinery works on its own. What I got looks valid and looks like it proves considerably more than I can accept, and I'd like help locating the seam. Here it is, with my own labels:

(1) Closure: every physical event that has a cause at all has a sufficient microphysical cause — some complete microphysical state of the world at a time, together with the laws, is enough for it.
(2) The Ledger Principle (this is the bit our group invented, and I like it): causal contribution to a single effect is like a fixed budget. If one event already accounts for all of the causal work leading up to E, then any distinct candidate cause accounts for none of it, and saying otherwise is double-booking the same work.
(3) No Systematic Overdetermination: we should not posit widespread, coincidental overdetermination as a general policy about the world; overdetermination is something that happens occasionally, not everywhere at once.
(4) Distinctness: for any mental event M and its microphysical base P, M is not identical with P. Even granting that M supervenes on P, the mental property and the physical property are distinct properties, and events understood under distinct properties are distinct causes.
(5) No Downward Shortcut: M does not causally produce P. Supervenience is a constitutive or grounding relation, not a causal one, so M is not upstream of P in the causal order.
(6) So no mental event is a cause of any physical effect, except in the stray overdetermination cases.
(7) Generality: nothing in (1)–(5) is specific to the mental. Every special-science candidate stands to its realization base exactly as M stands to P — the gear's rotation, the neuron's firing, the pressure of the gas, the keystroke, the erosion that breached the levee. Each is distinct from its base, none causes its base, and each has a complete microphysical cause.
(8) So only microphysical events cause anything. All special-science causation, including all of biology, geology and engineering-talk, is epiphenomenal.
(9) Evidential self-undermining: our evidence for (1) is entirely macro-level. We learned closure by intervening on macro variables — cooling gases, stimulating neurons, pressing keys — and finding that macro manipulations suffice for macro effects. If (8) is true, none of those manipulations were causes of anything, and the empirical support for (1) drains away.
(10) Doxastic self-undermining: if (6) is true, then my coming to accept (1)–(5) was not caused by my taking the steps to be good reasons for the conclusion. So if the argument is sound, there is no route by which anyone could come to accept it rationally, which is not a position one can occupy.

I want to keep (1) — I have no interest in denying closure — and I want to keep (4), because I don't want to be a type-identity theorist about the mental, and I'm perfectly happy to see (8) go: I want the special sciences to be in the causation business. My guess is that the trouble is in the move from (1) to (2), or in the way the effect E is held fixed as one and the same item across (2) and (7); my two philosopher friends each picked a different one of those and neither could tell me what the other option would cost me.
