Last night my housemate Priya (she's in ML, I'm finishing a philosophy of science PhD) and I got into about a three-hour argument that started after a departmental seminar and ended with us drawing diagrams on a pub napkin, and I'm still not sure who won. The view I ended up defending — let me call it access-bound deflationism about ontology — is this: you can only identify which ontology of a domain is the true one if the competing candidate ontologies are distinguishable using information that is at least in principle available to us; where two ontologies differ only in what they posit in a region we could never aquire evidence about, there is no fact of the matter about which is correct, and the "debate" between them is a choice of bookkeeping, not a discovery waiting to happen. (I kept leaning on a distinction between access-equivalence — no possible observation could tell them apart — and something weaker I called trace-difference, where the two stories do differ in what they say about the inaccessible region itself, just never in anything reachable. I think I need that distinction, but I'm not sure it's doing real work.) Priya's line was that this is just non-identifiability from latent-variable models, and that nobody in her field concludes the latents aren't real: either you work with the whole equivalence class, or you add constraints that aren't in the data — sparsity, independence, simplicity — and sometimes you thereby get identification up to permutation and rescaling, which she says is arguably all "identifying the ontology" ever needed to mean.

What I want from you is the exposure map for my side, not a neutral summary of the dispute. Rank the defeaters from would-be-fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones a committed access-bound deflationist should concede as genuinely counting against them rather than just what critics say. Her two best candidates, as I see them: (a) cases like independent component analysis, where extra-empirical constraints plus structure in the data really do pin down hidden structure without anyone "observing" it; and (b) the darkness-later-lifted cases — atoms, germs, other minds' interiors — where it looks like people identified an ontology correctly well before they had access, which suggests I've made facthood depend on our epistemic situation in a way that retroactively makes past scientists wrong when intuitively they were right. I also want to know whether my view collapses into a rival I'd hate — global anti-realism or expressivism, or a quietism that can't say anything substantive at all — and whether I've qualified it into unfalsifiability by getting to decide unilaterally what counts as "in principle accessible," and if so whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or just what metaphysical views are like. Be blunt about which of my distinctions are load-bearing and which are relabeling. For what it's worth, the thing that would move me personally most is a case where the information stays permanently inaccessible and we still want to say we got the ontology right; if you think no such case is convincing, say that plainly.
