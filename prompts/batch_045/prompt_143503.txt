Hi — novelist here, so sorry for the setup-in-costume, but I've got a scene I can't write and it turns out to be a philosophy-of-law problem in a wig.

My book: protagonist is a third-year law student clerking for a federal judge who is a cheerful, genuinely formidable originalist — original public meaning, "history and tradition" as the test for unenumerated rights, the whole apparatus, and he's good enough at it that she keeps half-converting. She starts reading Douglass's speeches for reasons that don't matter and ends up arguing with him in her head for the last third of the novel, at the same time as she's arguing with her judge. I need Douglass to be a real interlocutor, not a quotation dispenser who shows up to be right.

So, what I actually can't work out: what would Douglass make of originalism as it's practiced now, in 2025, not as a slogan?

The bit that got me stuck is this. Douglass broke with Garrison and his disunionist friends by arguing that the Constitution was, properly read, an anti-slavery instrument — no word for slavery in the text, the language about persons held to service pointed the other way once you stopped assuming the conclusion, an abolitionist lawyer's tract helped convince him, that sort of thing. That sounds an awful lot like an originalist move: beat your opponent on the founders' own ground. But then the Reconstruction amendments happen and he more or less says the document has been remade, and that it's finally a liberty document. Which means the thing he spent fifteen years arguing was already true only became true by being rewritten. So which Douglass walks into my scene — the one who found the founders usable, or the one who concluded they had to be overridden? And is that even a tension, or did he never think the 1787 text was the real object of loyalty in the first place?

Which leads to the question I'm almost afraid to put in a character's mouth: was his reading of the Constitution load-bearing in his thinking, or was it a rhetorical wrapper around a moral conclusion he'd reached on entirely independent grounds? I need to know because I need to know whether he'd be insulted by the question or whether he'd have an answer for it.

Two more things, both specific.

First: my judge's method, in a lot of cases, is to ask what was protected by history and tradition and stop there. Douglass lived at a time when the tradition pretty unambiguously included chattel slavery, so my instinct is that he'd find that method self-refuting on its face. But he also won his argument with Garrison partly by appealing to the founders' own words, which means he thought the founders could be turned against the practice they protected. Is there a version of the history-and-tradition test he could endorse, or is the method the problem rather than the outcomes?

Second, and this is the part I invented, so tell me if it's rigged: there's a fictional case in the book where a state uses a risk-scoring algorithm to decide who gets parole, the scores come out systematically worse for one group, and the state's defense is basically that nobody intended that, so there's no equal-protection problem. Would Douglass care about the intent, or about the fact that a set of arrangements keeps reproducing a caste-like outcome regardless of anybody's intention? I want the reasoning, not the label — and if the honest answer is that his own rhetorical habits were pointed at intentions, motives, and the moral character of the law rather than at statistical patterns, say so, because that's a more interesting scene than him being right on modern terms.

One last thing, which is my own dumb theory and I want you to knock it around: it seems to me there's a difference between being bound by the *meaning* of an old text and being bound by the *deal* that text was — and the 1787 Constitution was, among other things, a bargain with slaveholders. Maybe Douglass's real objection to originalism wouldn't be that it's backward-looking but that it's contractarian: it grounds legitimacy in the consent of people who consented to his enslavement. Is that anything, or am I putting 21st-century philosophy in his mouth?

Give me the strongest version of the Douglass side, the place where he'd get stuck or have to concede something, and the thing my judge would say back that would actually sting. And no footnotes or citations, please — I can't check them, my readers won't, and I'd rather have a scene that's true than one that's sourced.
