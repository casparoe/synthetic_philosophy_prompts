I'm a novelist, not a philosopher — I did a philosophy minor about fifteen years ago and mostly remember loosing the thread in seminars — and I'm stuck in the middle of a scene I can't make work. The book is set in the Dutch Republic in the late 1630s; one viewpoint character is a Leiden-trained physician who is a convinced Cartesian and does public demonstrations on living dogs in an anatomy theatre, and the other is his daughter, who helps him and hates it. Every time I draft their argument it comes out as the cartoon version: he thinks kicking a dog is like kicking a doorbell, she thinks he's a monster, end of scene. I want the real argument. So I've been reading the Discourse (in translation), plus a pile of secondary stuff I can't keep straight, and arguing with my brother about it for a year, and what I actually want from you is the document two philosophers who'd been forced into a good-faith adversarial collaboration would produce: call one side the beast-machine camp in its modern form — the people who think the evidence doesn't license attributing felt experience to non-human animals — and the other the continuity camp, who think the best explanation of what animals do, and of how their nervous systems are built, is that at least some of them have experiences. No verdict, no adjudication, and please don't send me to any papers or give me a reading list: my physician can't look anything up and neither should you.

Here's what I need in it. First, a numbered agreed-claims section — things both camps can sign that aren't vacuous, at least six of them. I've drafted candidates and I want you to either tighten each one into something both sides would accept or say which camp would refuse it and why: (1) the Discourse's two famous tests — the one about using words or other signs arranged to declare one's thoughts to others, and the one about a machine's acting by the disposition of its organs so that it can't handle every circumstance the way a creature with reason can — target general-purpose, language-using intelligence, not felt pain, and so can't by themselves show that animals feel nothing; (2) "animals are machines" plus all the behavioural evidence still doesn't entail "animals have no experiences," because you need the extra premise that no arrangement of matter could think or feel, which makes that premise, not the anatomy, the real battleground; (3) it is genuinely unsettled what Descartes himself held — whether he denied that animals have sensation at all or only that they lack thought and an immaterial mind — and no single famous sentence settles it; (4) the evidence is one step removed in a way it isn't for other humans, since for other humans we have their reports and for animals we never will; (5) there are two separable questions, what animals are and how we may treat them, and neither settles the other, so my daughter's horror at the demonstrations isn't by itself an argument that the dog feels anything; (6) if we met a creature that matched us behaviour for behaviour, injury-reaction for injury-reaction, and learned from the same rewards and punishments, but never spoke, the pressure to attribute experience would be enormous, and the whole fight is about whether that pressure can ever become entitlement. Second, a disagreements table, with a column for the topic, a column for the wording the beast-machine side would sign for its own view, a column for the wording the continuity side would sign for its own view, and a column marking whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal, with a sentence of justification — don't let anyone win on a borrowed premise or a slanted formulation.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. I suspect three: what it takes for "best explanation" to license attributing felt pain to a subject that can never report; whether "it hurts" does any explanatory work over and above a purely functional description of avoidance learning, nursing behaviour and the rest; and whether the whole thing is really the dualism-versus-physicalism fight in disguise, i.e. whether part of it is verbal. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, and pre-register predictions in this form: here's what each side predicts the case will show, and here's what each side says in advance it would conclude if it comes out the other way. Two of the cases I want to supply. One: a species I've invented for the book, the "echoes" — no central brain, a distributed nerve net, nothing homologous to ours, and yet it avoids for months the place where it was injured, reroutes when the old path is blocked, and spends days tending a wounded conspecific; I want each camp's verdict and, honestly, whether the continuity camp splits internally over it. Two: a hypothetical human case in which the full behavioural and autonomic pain profile is present but the patient sincerely reports no felt pain — what does each side say that tells us about how much the non-testimony evidence is worth? Add a third case of your own, and say for each crux what each camp would count as losing. Then, for the scene: give me two lines of dialogue I can actually use — one thing my Cartesian could concede that a real Cartesian would definately concede, and one hard line he'd say that would make a modern reader's blood boil and is nevertheless defensible. Use the headings Agreed claims (numbered), Disagreements (table), Cruxes, Dialogue. The very last line of your reply should be \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the single crux both camps agree would actually move the debate, or \answer{none} if the joint statement concludes nothing would.
