i'm working on a paper about "collapse" as a concept and i keep going in circles because everyone using the word seems to be measuring something different. so before i commit to a definition i want the spec. i need a definition i can actually apply to archaeological sequences — settlement sizes, layers of burning, how many tiers of administration you can still see in the record, diet and health from skeletons, whether the script keeps getting used — and say "that one collapsed, that one didn't." but i also want the word to keep meaning something worth caring about: collapse should be the sort of thing it's rational to try to prevent, not just "change" with a gloomy name.

so: write me the desiderata. for each one, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp. the headaches i can't get past, and that i want your list to deal with:

- my own made-up case, bear with me. call it Kavos: inside two generations the palace, the script and the tax system are gone, population drops by maybe 40%, but the skeletons get taller, fewer kids die, there's no evidence of mass violence, the pottery styles and the gods stay the same, and 300 years later people build something pretty similar on the same spot. the complexity measures say collapse, the bodies say improvement, and the "nothing failed, it just changed" people say neither. what should a definition say here, and what does answering it cost you?
- collapse for whom. the same event is a catastrophe for the palace household and a pretty good century for the villages. does a definition have to fix a subject (household, city, polity, whole interaction network) and a stratum?
- collapse vs the ordinary death of a polity: conquest, a dynasty ending, a city abandoned because everyone moved two valleys over. if all those count, the word does no work.
- rate and grain. is a 200-year slide that ends in the same state the same thing as a 30-year one? and can we even call anything a collapse if the best data we have is 100-year resolution?
- trigger vs fragility. the eruption was genuinely huge, but a society that a modest drought wrecks was arguably already brittle. does the size of the outside shock belong in the definition at all?
- who's doing the noticing. if the people living through it had no before/after to compare and didn't experience it as anything in particular, is it still a collapse, or does the concept need an insider phenomenology? (and note that our evidence is mostly written by the literate class that tends to vanish, or by whoever came later and had an interest in saying the place had been worthless — so think about whether any criterion needing stated intent is usable at all.)
- recovery and ending. if something similar gets rebuilt three centuries on, was that a collapse or a sag? and if you answer "a collapse followed by a recovery," tell me when the collapse ended — an actual rule for dating its end, not a gesture.

then: is any subset of your requirements jointly unsatisfiable? my suspicion is that "measurable from the record" plus "thickly bad, i.e. something to be avoided" plus "not merely transformation" can't all hold, but you tell me. and which single requirement does the most work ruling candidates out? after that, one line each on how the three broad families do — the complexity-and-marginal-returns one, the wellbeing-or-lived-experience one, and the resilience/transformation one — just where each one breaks against your list. skip the scorecard beyond that, and please skip citations and author names entirely; i've read enough of that and it's only made it worse. a table with columns requirement / motivating case / non-negotiable or preference is fine if that's easier than prose.

final line, in exactly this form, with the one desideratum that does the most exclusionary work, five words or fewer: \answer{...}
