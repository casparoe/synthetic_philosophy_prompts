hey — odd request, but you seem like the right tool for it. I'm in a small reading group on formal epistemology (we just did Aumann's agreement theorem — the result that if two people start from a shared prior and their posteriors are common knowledge, those posteriors have to be equal, so in a sense they can't agree to disagree) and someone said offhandedly that the common prior assumption is basically untestable, and that's been rattling around in my head ever since. I also write fake-bureaucracy humour for a satirical zine, so I want to mash the two together.

Write me a public-information leaflet, roughly 700 words, issued by an agency in a jurisdiction where the common prior assumption isn't a modelling convenience but is legally enforced — something like a Bureau of Common Priors (invent your own name and officials, I don't care). The premise: citizens are presumed to have shared a prior before acquiring any private evidence, and disagreement that survives contact is a compliance matter. Format it as deadpan officialese: a short statement of purpose, a set of numbered clauses, one worked example (two neighbours disagreeing about whether the river floods, or two radiologists reading the same scan — your pick), and a brief FAQ at the end. The target is the assumption itself and the use it gets put to in making disagreement theoretically impossible — not any real person, and please make the agency competent and well-meaning, since that's what makes it frightening.

What I really care about is that the jokes come from getting the doctrine right rather than from making the bureaucrats stupid. Things I'd hope find their way in: the prior is counterfactual, so the Bureau has to ask what you believed before you had any evidence that distinguished you from anyone else, which should produce some gloriously absurd filing requirement about a pre-incarnate or at-birth prior return; the gap between full common knowledge of posteriors and what actually happens when two people swap numbers a few times (if I remember right, when the set of possible signals is finite, repeatedly announcing posteriors back and forth does converge in finitely many rounds, but that's a stylised process, and it assumes the information partitions are themselves common knowledge); the Harsanyi-flavoured move that a difference in belief has to be traced to a difference in information rather than to differing dispositions, which forces the Bureau to adjudicate whose evidence counts as evidence; and the nasty one — a common prior can never be falsified, because any stubborn disagreement can always be explained away by positing sufficiently exotic private information, so enforcement must collapse into either a rubber stamp or a licence to declare one party irrational, and which it becomes depends entirely on who holds the pen. There's also the standard no-betting result, if you can make it funny: a shared prior plus common knowledge of rationality and of the voluntary character of a trade is supposed to rule out purely informational gains from trade, so the Bureau's sibling agency could be voiding bets. Stay deadpan the whole way — no winking, no asides to the reader — and let the last line be the reductio. It should be funny to someone who knows this literature and still readable to someone who doesn't; I'd say about 600–800 words, and feel free to invent procedural detail wherever it helps.
