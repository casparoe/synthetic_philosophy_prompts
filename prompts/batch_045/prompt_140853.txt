Suppose a corpus that does not exist in the archaeological record but is a cleaned-up composite of many that do: some three thousand small fired-clay objects, roughly lentoid, from forty settlements along a single river valley dated 1200–800 BCE. Their weights cluster tightly (mean 8.4 g, standard deviation 0.4 g) through 950 BCE, and more loosely thereafter; about a fifth are pierced as if for suspension; about a hundred carry one of six simple impressed marks; they turn up disproportionately in doorway foundations and in children's graves, absent from rich adult burials, and in the storerooms of the two largest sites only in small numbers. After 950 the marks stop, the weight distribution widens, and the settlement hierarchy flattens from three tiers to two.

Two archaeologists have argued about this corpus for a decade and have agreed to write up their disagreement as an adversarial collaboration. Camp A (regularity-and-context) reads the corpus as the residue of a redistributive institution: a standard statutory measure used to assess and ration staples, administered from the two largest sites, whose post-950 loosening tracks an institutional breakdown. Camp B (meaning-and-deposition) grants the standardization but treats it as a fact about manufacture rather than function: the depositional profile fits apotropaic or dedicatory objects, the record preserves disposal rather than use, and ascribing rules, roles and sanctions to it outruns what the material can support.

Write their joint statement: a numbered section of claims both can sign, including claims about what the corpus does and does not establish and about what would have to be true for anything to count as an institution; then a table of residual disagreements, each stated in neutral terms alongside the wording its own side would accept; then the cruxes — for instance, whether the metrological pattern discriminates between administrative and dedicatory use, whether doorway and grave concentrations signal primary use or discard, whether the post-950 change is contemporaneous with or offset from the flattening of the settlement hierarchy, and whether "institution" should be reserved for cases with evidence of norm enforcement — with, for each, a test, comparison or piece of further evidence both parties agree would bear on it and each party's prediction recorded in advance. Say explicitly which disagreements are substantive and which, if any, were only about words. Reason from the evidence described above rather than by appealing to literature or to particular excavations. In the agreed-claims section, distinguish what is settled from what remains contested among people who work on this kind of material, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
