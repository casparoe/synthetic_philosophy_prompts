I was at a dinner party where two friends of mine — neither of them an academic, though both are the kind of people who read philosophy blogs — got into an argument I've been turning over ever since. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. I have no background in any of this; I work in freight logistics and think about these things on my own, so I'd be grateful for a careful verdict rather than a crash course.

The argument started from a real decision. Marcus had been in a one-shot, fully anonymous situation: he and a stranger he would never meet each had to choose, with no communication and no possibility of reputation or repeat play, whether to cooperate or to defect, on roughly the usual payoffs — a decent sum each if both cooperate, a small sum each if both defect, and a large bonus for anyone who defects while the other cooperates. He cooperated. Elena said he had simply given money away. Here is roughly how it went.

1. Marcus: I don't think the standard verdict survives contact with how large the world actually is. In a universe this big there are almost certainly many other agents somewhere — outside one another's light cones, no communication, no reputation, no repeat play — who face a decision problem that is, for every predictive purpose, the same as mine: same payoff structure, same evidence, the same kind of deliberative machinery. Such agents will very likely decide as I do. So my deciding to cooperate is strong evidence that they cooperate too. My own cooperation costs me exactly one cooperative act; what I get in exchange, evidentially, is the cooperation of all of them. If there are enough of them, that trade is obviously worth taking. The usual argument for defecting quietly assumes a small world, in which I am the only token of my decision problem anywhere.

2. Elena: Two problems. First, correlation isn't manufactured out of resemblance. Two copies of the same program running on two planets produce the same output, but they don't produce statistically dependent outputs unless something ties them together — a shared cause, a shared environment, an actual signal. My token action and their token action are independent events; saying "we're the same computation" doesn't make them dependent. That's sympathetic magic with mathematical vocabulary. Second, and more deeply: even if the correlation were real, you'd be booking other people's acts on your own ledger. Grant that, conditional on your cooperating, a thousand distant agents cooperate. Those are a thousand acts by a thousand other people. Unless your choice brings theirs about, their value isn't a reason for you. Otherwise anyone could benefit the world by staying home and counting everyone else's good deeds as the fruits of their own imagination. Call it benefit inflation.

3. Marcus: On the first point: the tie isn't resemblance, it's indexical uncertainty. Until I have actually decided, I don't know what the agent in my situation is going to do — and from the inside, neither do they. If we really are the same deliberative process, that isn't two uncertainties but one, and a shared uncertainty just is what correlation consists in. You don't need a causal link; you need someone not to know something, and self-locating ignorance generates correlations all the time. On the second point: "their acts are theirs, not yours" is causal decision theory wearing a disguise. Of course evidence about independent acts is worthless on a causal view. But whether causal influence or evidence is the currency of rational choice is precisely what's in dispute. If good news is worth having, then the fact that my action would be good news is a reason to perform it.

4. Elena: On the first point, you're sliding between two kinds of not-knowing. Genuine self-locating uncertainty exists when your evidence doesn't distinguish the candidates — that's why the coin-flip and sleeping-beauty style cases work. But you know perfectly well which agent you are: you're Marcus, in that chair, in this city. Uncertainty about what you will freely decide isn't self-location at all; it's just your deliberation not being finished yet, and every agent has that whether or not they're correlated with anyone. If that were enough, you'd be correlated with everyone, including agents with the opposite dispositions. On the second point, I'm not assuming causal decision theory. I'm saying that on your own evidential view you have to take the news whole. A world large enough to contain your friendly duplicates contains agents correlated with you in the unfriendly direction too: agents whose situation is like yours except that their cooperation pays out exactly when you defect, and agents similar to you in every respect you'd cite except that they're disposed to choose the opposite. If the reference class is wide enough to generate the good news, it's wide enough to generate news that cancels it. Narrow it until only the friendly correlations survive and you have chosen your conclusion rather than derived it.

5. Marcus: That's the objection I take seriously, and I think it fails, because the class isn't picked by hand — it's fixed by predictive similarity, and predictive similarity has a direction. Agents disposed to do the opposite of me aren't predictively like me; I can predict them perfectly, and the relation runs the wrong way to be what my argument trades on. Agents with mirrored payoffs aren't like me in the decision-relevant respect either. So the cancellation doesn't come for free.

6. Elena: Two replies. First, "predictively like me" is either causal or it isn't. If it is, you're back to needing a real tie between my token decision and theirs, which is exactly what a large world doesn't hand you. If it isn't, it only means "similar in the respects I'm choosing to call decision-relevant" — and similarity is description-relative, so I can always redescribe you and your opposite as a pair whose decisions track each other. Either the filter smuggles causation back in, or it's a choice dressed as a discovery. And by your own criterion the opposite-disposed agent is correlated with you: conditional on your cooperating, you know they defect. On an evidential view that's bad news about the world and it has to be weighed; you can't screen it out by calling it the wrong kind of similarity. Second, separately: even granting you the correlation, the arithmetic isn't stable. I don't know how many agents are in my reference class, and in a very large world my uncertainty about that number is enormous and heavy-tailed — a small credence on astronomically many copies. Then the expected total is dominated entirely by the tail, and your argument returns no stable verdict about how much cooperation is worth buying, or any verdict at all if the expectation doesn't settle down.

7. Marcus: Maybe. But there's a difference between an argument that's hard to compute and one that's wrong. Do the sum with a bounded, reasonable estimate of the size of the class — which is what a very large but finite universe would actually give you — and the cooperative side comes out far ahead. And the instability you point to is a problem for anyone who adds up value across an enormous world, including you.

I've read a little about evidential and causal decision theory, so you don't need to explain those from scratch, but please don't assume I know any of the specialist literature on large worlds, and don't send me to read anything — I'd rather have your own reasoning.

Please go through the exchange move by move and tell me who is right about what, not merely who wins. For each of the seven moves, say whether it is basically right, half right, or wrong, and what exactly is right or wrong in it. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing: is there a distinction neither drew, or a consideration that settles more of the dispute than they realised? Finally, I'd like to know how confident you actually are — which part of your verdict you would most readily give up, which argument or consideration would move you to a different one, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be going wrong.
