I'm a staff engineer at a company that builds autonomous coding agents, and I chair the design review that has to sign off on anything that changes what an agent optimizes for, what supervises it, or how its outputs get evaluated. The stakes are real: these agents hold write access to production repositories, and several thousand external users depend on them. We see roughly eight design docs a month, the decision is made in a single 45-minute meeting, both the author and I have to sign, and a separate safety reviewer can veto. Reviewers are backend and ML engineers with no philosophy background whatsoever.

Our docs already require a mandatory "Support Chain" section, in which the author has to state, for every component that is supposed to accomplish something, what does the accomplishing. In practice authors fill it with sentences that have the shape of an explanation without the substance, and I have no reliable way to tell the difference. The distinction I want operationalized is Daniel Dennett's contrast between cranes and skyhooks, together with his complaint about greedy reductionism. As I understand it: a crane gets its lifting power from machinery that is itself already supported from below, whereas a skyhook is invoked as if it lifted from above and is typically defined by the outcome it is supposed to produce rather than by anything that could implement it. Greedy reductionism, as I understand it, is the error in the other direction — dismissing a real higher-level pattern because you can point at the lower-level machinery it is made of. I may have the details wrong; if so, correct me, but keep the correction usable by someone who will never read the book.

Two features of our systems make the translation harder, and I'd like both taken seriously. First, nearly every component in one of our support chains is itself a trained model whose behavior we can only characterize empirically, so our chains do not bottom out in a specification or a piece of hardware; they bottom out in measurements with a stated scope. Second, our docs mix three kinds of claim that I suspect need different treatment: mechanism claims (this component does X by doing Y), guarantee claims (the system will not do Z, or will always do W), and evaluation claims (on this benchmark distribution, metric M improves). My guess is that guarantee claims are where skyhooks hide and evaluation claims are where greedy reductionism hides, but that is only a guess.

Please write me a review procedure my team can actually run. Constraints: it must be executable in a 20-minute pre-read by an engineer with no philosophy background; it must terminate in one of four verdicts — accept, reject, restructure, or escalate to leadership; and it must be explicit about which questions are matters of fact and which are matters of values or risk appetite that no procedure can settle. It should catch errors in both directions: unsupported top-down explainers, and level-collapsing that explains away something real.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. "Vocabulary without the philosophy" (250-350 words). Restate cranes, skyhooks, and greedy reductionism in terms an engineer will recognize, with one concrete example of each drawn from ML systems, and state explicitly where the analogy to ordinary software breaks — in particular what counts as solid ground when the lowest level is an empirical characterization rather than a specification.

2. "The procedure" (800-1,000 words). The ordered questions to run on a single design doc, each with what counts as a sufficient answer, the failure mode the question exists to catch, and the verdict it exits with. Cover how to audit one Support Chain entry, how the three claim types are treated differently, what to do with cranes that are promised but do not exist yet, and the points at which the procedure hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact.

3. "Worked reviews" (three cases, 250-350 words each). (a) A proposal to train a learned verifier that scores the agent's patches, where those scores are also used as the training signal for the agent, and where the only evidence offered is an improvement on a held-out benchmark. (b) A proposal to let the agent rewrite its own system prompt, with a second model acting as a "meta-critic" that approves or rejects edits for fidelity to the user's intent. This one is already deployed to internal users and I would prefer not to be told it is broken; tell me what the procedure says anyway. (c) A proposal to remove intentional vocabulary from our docs and tooling — that the agent "wants", "knows", or "is trying to" do something — on the grounds that it is next-token prediction all the way down, so there is nothing to model at the level of goals. Give the verdict the procedure actually returns in each case.

4. "Where it misfires" (250-350 words). The false positives and false negatives I should expect: legitimate new primitives the procedure will wrongly flag, genuine skyhooks that will pass it, and how quickly it degrades once an author learns to write in a way that satisfies it. Say plainly what the procedure cannot do.

5. "The one-page version" (a compact checklist or table, under 250 words), in a form I can paste into our review template.
