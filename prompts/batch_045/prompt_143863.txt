I spent a career in atmospheric science, the last two decades of it building ensemble forecasting systems, where the whole craft consists of combining many imperfect and partly independent estimates of a field into a single estimate with a defensible error bar, and of knowing when a number coming off an instrument is a measurement and when it is an artifact. In retirement I have been reading philosophy, and I have come across the claim that markets are epistemic instruments: that a price is not merely the number at which supply clears but a measurement of what a dispersed group of people takes to be true. I have also come across the no-trade results — roughly, that parties who begin with a common prior and are rational in a commonly known way cannot each expect to gain from a purely speculative trade, together with the related agreement result about people who know each other's opinions — which, taken at face value, appear to make most real trading either irrational or not about information at all.

I do not want a survey of that literature or a reading list. I want to find out what I actually think, by treating my own reactions to concrete cases as data and asking what curve fits them. Here is my tentative principle, in the vocabulary I trust: a price is a measurement, and it inherits whatever authority it has from three features — the independence of the separate contributions it aggregates, the existence of a settlement that can show the reading to have been wrong, and the absence of any path by which the reading itself feeds back into the thing being measured. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases. Several are confident; at least two I suspect cannot both survive.

1. Futures in a grain that will be harvested and graded on a known date: I trust the price as a forecast, and I would act on it.

2. Two farmers with the same information about the season but different exposure — one has a loan to service, one has reserves — trade a weather-contingent contract: I think the trade is clearly beneficial and that the price tells me nothing whatever about the weather. I do not find this troubling.

3. Two economists, with access to the same data and, as I will stipulate, the same prior, trade a contract that settles on next quarter's published inflation figure, each believing herself the better interpreter of that data: I think at least one of them is making a mistake, and I do not regard the transaction as an epistemic achievement at all. It strikes me closer to self-deception than to discovery.

4. A prediction market on a well-defined question that will be settled soon — say, whether an announced replication attempt will reproduce a published result — in which nearly every participant has read the same preprint and very few hold anything one would call a private signal: I trust the price somewhat. Less than in case 1, considerably more than in case 3.

5. A claim with no settlement date, which trades indefinitely and is never marked against any outcome: the quoted number looks exactly like a measurement and I do not trust it at all. To me it is an instrument with no calibration standard.

6. The same information environment as case 4, except that liquidity is provided by a market maker quoting a bid-ask spread, so that a trader with a small informational advantage cannot profitably express it: I trust the price for large distinctions but not for fine ones. It strikes me as coarse, not as wrong.

7. A market in which a substantial fraction of participants take the price itself as an input — they buy what has been rising — although the underlying private information is, by stipulation, exactly as good as in case 1: I distrust the price strongly. It feels circular, as though the instrument were reading its own output.

8. A market on whether a research programme will succeed, where the price itself influences who funds the programme and therefore whether it succeeds: I distrust the number as a measurement, and yet I also think it is, in some way I cannot state, the most useful number available. This reaction may simply be confused.

9. A corporate officer sells shares into an index fund that must buy mechanically. The price moves toward the truth, and the counterparty's willingness to transact does not depend on being right. I think the price improves, I feel an unease about the transaction, and I cannot tell whether that unease is moral and therefore beside the point of the epistemic question.

Cases 3 and 4 are the pair I keep returning to: in both, no one holds a private signal, and in both the contract settles against a real outcome, yet I condemn one as epistemically idle and credit the other. If diversity of interpretation is what legitimates trade, then the two economists have it too, and verdict 3 should go; if what legitimates trade is that no party's expected gain rests on a claim about her own reliability that the other party would reject, then I need to be told why the many participants in case 4 are not making exactly that claim about themselves collectively.

So: test my three-part principle against these nine cases, repair it or replace it, and state the repair precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which case. Then tell me what it predicts for cases I have not listed — a market whose settlement is a committee vote rather than a physical outcome; the same market run with five participants rather than five thousand; a market in which every participant's past accuracy is publicly displayed; and, the one I care about most, a contract on a quantity that will be measured exactly at settlement but that lies beyond the predictability horizon of the system in question, such as the temperature at a named station on a named day two years hence. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and which of them you would overturn.
