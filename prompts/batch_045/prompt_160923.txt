I'm in a small seminar this term on the ethics of organ transplantation — I'm the only philosophy major in it, the rest are mostly medical students plus a transplant surgeon who sits in — and we have spent the last three weeks on the dead donor rule, the constraint that organ retrieval must not itself cause the donor's death and must not begin before it. I'm writing the final paper, and before I commit myself to an account I've been trying to get my own responses to cases in order, because in discussion I noticed that my gut reactions don't line up neatly with any of the criteria we were handed. Going through them: a patient whose entire brain, brainstem included, has been destroyed, who is being oxygenated by a ventilator, whose heart is still beating and whose pituitary still responds to signals, is dead, and that one feels clear to me; a patient in a persistent vegetative state, cortex largely gone but brainstem intact, breathing spontaneously, with sleep-wake cycles and no evidence of awareness, is not dead, and I resist hard the suggestion that she is, even though I also want to say that something has gone out of her that matters more than breathing does; someone with a high cervical cord lesion, fully awake and dependent on a ventilator, is trivially alive; a donor declared dead after the customary short wait following asystole, with no resuscitation attempted, is dead, and I accept that declaration even though someone could in principle have restarted that heart if they had tried. But then: a patient undergoing deliberate circulatory arrest under deep hypothermia for aortic arch surgery, with no heartbeat, no circulation, and no brain activity for something like half an hour, who is then rewarmed, recovers fully and walks out of hospital weeks later — I don't want to say that person was dead and then alive again. That pair is the one that unsettles me most, because whatever makes me willing to call the donor dead, it isn't merely the absence of circulation or of brain activity. I should also flag three verdicts that feel right but that I can't defend: a woman declared brain-dead in the second trimester and maintained on somatic support with hormonal supplementation for months so the fetus could reach viability is dead throughout, and yet her body was doing something no corpse does, which is exactly what makes me suspicious of talk about the organism as a whole; a protocol in which, after death has been declared on circulatory grounds, perfusion is restarted to the abdominal organs while the vessels to the brain are deliberately occluded so that the brain remains unperfused, is one I want to call permissible, and I can't give a clean reason why that isn't just cheating; and two hypotheticals from a paper we read — a person whose neurons are replaced one at a time over a decade with functionally equivalent synthetic units, awake and psychologically continuous throughout, who I say survives and does not die somewhere in the middle, and a person whose brain is scanned and destroyed slice by slice with a functionally identical simulation then running, where I say the original dies even though the end state is the same thing made of different materials.

The case I keep changing my mind about is a variant I invented to press on that last pair. Suppose the neurons aren't swapped gradually but all at once, during a single period of deliberate circulatory arrest under deep hypothermia: no brain activity of any kind during the procedure, the brain's biological tissue is removed and functionally equivalent synthetic units are installed, circulation is restored, and the person wakes up psychologically continuous with the one who went under, same memories, same projects, same character. Is that a death with a numerically distinct successor, or a survival with a change of substrate? My answer flips depending on which description I use, which makes me think I've been relying on something I haven't stated. So here is what I'd like from you: the two or three accounts of death that best fit the verdicts above — I take the candidates to be something like a whole-brain criterion, a brainstem criterion, a higher-brain or consciousness-based criterion, a circulatory criterion, and a criterion in terms of the organism's ceasing to function as an integrated whole — stated precisely enough that I can see what each predicts; which of my verdicts each one forces me to abandon; what each says about the hypothermic replacement case; and the further case that would separate the ones that survive. I'm also genuinely open to being told that no single principle fits, because I am tracking several different things at once — the organism, the person, the irreversibility of a loss as opposed to its mere permanence — and that the dead donor rule therefore has to be justified separately for each of them; if that's where you land, say clearly what it commits me to.

Two constraints, because I know how these discussions usually go. I have the reading list and the statutory definitions in front of me, so please don't spend words on citations, bibliographies, or on what any particular commission, act, or landmark case held — assume I can look all of that up and that I'm not asking for it. What I need is the argumentative structure: a principle I could state in a single sentence, the case that motivates each clause of it, its verdict on the hypothermic replacement case and on one further case you choose, and an honest note about where the principle gives out or where the clause does the real work is one I'd rather not defend. Assume my seminar leader will press hardest on whichever clause I most want to fudge, and assume the surgeon will ask at the end whether any of it should change practice, so please say where the view you recommend leaves practice alone and where it wouldn't.
