Two people in my reading group got stuck on Mises's old argument that a fully socialised economy couldn't do economic calculation, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.

(1) Jon said the argument is only epistemic, not logical: with no markets in the means of production there are no money prices for factors, so planners have no common unit for comparing alternatives — but that's a contingent fact about how much information can be collected and crunched. Run what he called the omniscience stipulation: hand the planning board everyone's preference orderings, every production function, and unlimited computing power at t0. Then it just solves for the shadow prices and allocates. On that reading Mises only showed that 1920s socialism lacked the hardware, and Hayek's dispersed-knowledge version is the same point updated, so better data and better computation dissolve the whole thing.

(2) Nadia said the omniscience stipulation is itself the tell: the data of the planning problem aren't a fixed list. Which goods are producible at what cost isn't sitting there waiting to be collected; it gets discovered by people trying things under price signals and eating the losses. An optimisation at t0 can only range over goods and techniques somebody has already imagined — the market's output isn't just an allocation, it's a search over a space the planner never enumerated.

(3) Jon: search is computable too. Simulate a million variant economies, including simulated entrepreneurs proposing goods nobody had thought of, score them, pick the best. Real markets are also trial and error with real bankruptcies and wasted factories, so it isn't obvious that decentralised search beats centralised simulation, and whoever is claiming an impossibility carries the burden.

(4) Nadia: the simulation reply has to be scored, and scoring needs a social welfare function and a stopping rule, both of them smuggled in — and skipping that step was supposed to be the whole point of using prices. Also, simulated prices are as-if: they exist only inside the planner's model, so nobody outside can use them to check the planner or mesh their own plans with everyone else's. What's special about a real price isn't accuracy, it's that it's public, contestable, and usable by someone who can't do the math.

(5) Jon: now you've switched topics. That's a point about accountability and legibility, not calculation, and it applies just as well to any big firm, or to algorithmic pricing where a few models set prices nobody can audit. Anyway Lange's play-money factor markets with bonuses for managers already answer the incentive worry.

(6) Nadia: play money only motivates if the score converts into something managers actually want, and if losses are real you need real failure, which needs somebody to own the assets — which is exactly what was abolished.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what, where each of them concedes something they shouldn't have, and what both of them are missing? And separately: is the omniscience stipulation even coherent? It seems to me a planner who models the economy is also inside it, so the model changes what it's modelling.
