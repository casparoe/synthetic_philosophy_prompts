Construct me a decision problem where expected utility theory and risk-weighted expected utility come apart in opposite directions — I mean the rank-dependent version of REU, where instead of weighting each outcome by its own probability you weight it by the difference in a transformed cumulative probability (so the weight on the best outcome is r(p_best), the weight on the next one down is r(p of that or better) minus r(p_best), and so on; r increasing, r(0)=0, r(1)=1, identity gets you plain EU back).

What I'm after, concretely:

- One paragraph, one real person, one actual decision. A farm, a clinic, a small business, a hiring committee, an offer on a house — the uncertainty has to come from something people really face, no lotteries, no urns, no casino frames.
- Two options, A and B, and I want a genuine reversal: EU says A, REU says B. Not "EU shrugs and REU has an opinion."
- Neither option may be risk-free. If you catch yourself reaching for "well, with B she gets a guaranteed X," throw it out and start again — I want the case to work without the certainty effect doing any lifting, since otherwise it's just Allais with a costume on.
- Denominate the payoff in a unit where her marginal value is flat across the range involved — extra patients seen, extra hectares, extra hours on a machine that's the bottleneck anyway — and also give me two or three of her attitudes toward *sure* outcomes so u is pinned. Then show the arithmetic: take the u that fits those sure-thing attitudes, get the reversal, and then show what a u would have to look like for EU to also pick B, and why that u contradicts one of the sure-thing attitudes you gave me. This is the bit people cheat on, so dont skip it.
- Block the "she'll face gambles like this hundreds of times, so she should just maximize expectation" reply, and block it with a reason rather than a stipulation — say why this risk doesn't get smoothed into her portfolio of other risks.

Then: set the outcome table out with numbers, run both theories on it, and do three things. Name the one structural feature the whole case turns on. Ablate it — flatten r to the identity leaving u alone, then flatten u leaving r alone — and tell me which ablation kills the reversal, which is the honest answer to "is this just risk aversion re-described." Finally give me the strongest thing an EU defender says back (I'm expecting something like "your r is just a utility function wearing a hat" or "that's a framing effect, not a rational preference") and say whether you think it lands.

I'm using this in a seminar to make the point that REU accommodates risk aversion in a way that isn't just EU in a trenchcoat, so anything that stipulates the verdicts instead of generating them from the two theories is useless to me.
