I have to give the eulogy on Saturday and Ive been staring at a blank page for four days, so Im outsourcing the part I can't crack.

The person: Dr. Miriam Kolb, 78, emerita professor of historical sociology at the state university, and for fifty years a ruling elder in a small conservative Reformed congregation — real predestination, real church discipline, none of it done ironically. She died three weeks ago. Her life's work was one narrow claim, and I want the eulogy to be built out of that claim rather than be a nice story with a footnote stapled to it. (And please don't cite or recommend anything, or give me a reading list — I can't chase down references right now and neither can anybody in that room.)

Her version of the argument — she got definately annoyed at being called a Weberian, because she thought almost everyone misstated the thesis — had three parts:

1. Culture can be a cause, but not the way a brick through a window is a cause. What values do is change which considerations are available to a person *as reasons*. On her reading, the Reformation doctrines didn't produce diligence directly; they removed the other things that could have counted as evidence of your standing with God, and left only a whole life, so that steady methodical work in a calling became the only thing a person could do *for that reason*. The doctrine doesn't push people; it pries open a slot in the space of reasons and then something ordinary fills it.

2. The consequence she drew from that, which she was insufferable about: you can never test the thesis with aggregate statistics. The same outward behaviour — thrift, punctuality, reinvesting instead of spending — can come out of totally different value configurations: someone trying to read his own election, a widow keeping a shop afloat, a clerk trying not to be disgraced. The causal claim is about which reasons were actually operative, and reasons leave no trace in the denominational averages. She spent a decade saying this to people who kept publishing regressions.

3. The corollary she liked best, and the one that's really about her: if the causal claim is right, then the world-historical effect of that piety was to make itself unnecessary. The diligence outlived the doctrine and ended up inside an arrangement of its own making, one that runs fine without the belief that built it. So the belief gets no credit and no vindication from the thing it built. She used to say you can't argue from the Reformation back to the Reformation.

A few years ago a much-cited paper claimed to have finally settled it with nineteenth-century census and church records, and found that the Protestant wealth gap ran almost entirely through literacy; the authors announced that the values part of the story was dead. She wrote a furious four-page note in reply that almost nobody read, and then never mentioned it again, which was not like her.

The room on Saturday is going to be roughly sixty percent her church — people who heard her preach, who think her faith was the thing that made her who she was, and who want to hear that her faith bore fruit in her work and her students. The rest is her department, a pile of former students, and, in the front row, her oldest friend Ray Delacroix, a labor historian and completely unreconstructed Marxist who has argued for thirty years that no idea ever caused anything and that her career was ideology with footnotes. They fought about this joyfully for decades and she never once converted him. She asked for Ray to be seated in front, and I genuinely can't tell whether that was affection or one last joke.

What I need: a eulogy, seven to nine minutes spoken, that does all of the following and doesn't quietly cheat on any of it.

- The church people have to hear her faith honored as the actual center of her life, without the speech implying that her scholarship showed the faith to be true, or that God rewarded it with a successful career, or that her books were a form of apologetics. On her own view, explaining a life by the values the person avowed is a perfectly good causal explanation and is completely silent about whether those values were true or good. I want that distinction doing structural work in the speech — without being named as a distinction, because nobody there wants a lecture on value-freedom.
- The speech can't do the standard eulogy move of explaining the whole life by its deepest values in the triumphant way, because that's exactly what her own third claim rules out. But it also can't fall back on explaining her by her class, her upbringing, her historical moment, because that's Ray's move, she'd have fought it, and at a funeral it just lands as an insult. Somehow it has to find a third thing to do.
- It has to say something to Ray that isn't a cheap win and isn't a surrender. The argument was unfinished and she'd hate to be used as evidence for either side, including her own. He should be able to laugh without feeling patted on the head.
- No afterlife claims, no watching over us, no settling anything with "she would have wanted" — she was very specific in her last months that her funeral was not to be a recruitment event and that nobody was to use her to win an argument she'd have enjoyed having.
- God should be present in it the way she'd have wanted, since it is her church, but the speech shouldn't do theology on her behalf.

Two concrete things you can use: she taught the same undergraduate lecture for thirty-four years, and she ended the last one, the week she retired, by erasing the board, writing four words on it — "correlation is not a reason" — and walking out without saying anything. And for as long as anyone can remember she kept a 1971 hardware store receipt pinned above her desk at home, and never explained it to anybody.

She was dry and funny and hated being praised for being clever; she liked being praised for being stubborn. Plain language, no jargon, at most one or two laughs, and nothing at the expense of anyone in the room. Don't end with a benediction or with "she lives on in our hearts" — find an ending that comes out of the ideas above, because I think there's one there and I can't see it.
