I sit on a small committee that allocates a fixed health budget, and nearly every disagreement in it eventually gets closed with the same formula: "everyone to count for one, no one for more than one." In a single session last month it was used twice, and as far as I can tell in two directions that cannot both be right. One member used it to stop a proposal to weight benefits to the severely ill: giving priority to the worst off, she said, is counting them for more than one. Another used it to close a proposal that each applicant receive an equal share: no, he said, the formula says only that no one's good is to be discounted because of whose it is, and that is consistent with — indeed requires — sending resources wherever they will do the most good.

To keep the familiar escape route shut, please grant the following stipulation: we already possess an interpersonally comparable cardinal measure of well-being, stated in units per person-year, and there is no dispute about who belongs to the population in question. The disagreement is entirely about what the formula tells us to do with numbers we already have.

What I want is a menu. Set out the distinct precise theses that "everyone to count for one, no one for more than one" could be asserting, and give a verdict on each — platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. In particular, separate at least these axes:

- What the thing that counts is: well-being itself, the fulfilment of a person's interests or preferences, the resources or opportunities she commands, or her standing as a claimant. Say whether the formula is a thesis about what has value or about how value is to be aggregated, since those come apart.
- Whether "one" fixes a marginal weight inside an aggregation function, or instead fixes a status — equal consideration, an equal claim, a veto on being excluded or discounted — that is silent about marginal weights.
- Whether "one" ranges over persons, over person-years, or over whole lives, and how much of the formula's practical content depends on that choice.
- Whom the formula addresses: an individual deliberating about what to do, an institution distributing a fixed stock of resources, or a criterion for ranking outcomes from nowhere. If the last, say whether it is also meant to tell the individual what to do, and what follows if it does.
- Whether "no one for more than one" forbids only discounting — weighing someone's good less because of who she is — or also forbids adding weight, as a prioritarian would; whether it says anything about weight that is zero or negative; and whether it might be a bookkeeping rule against counting the same person's good twice, once directly and once through its effects on others who care about her.

Two cases to make the menu earn its keep. First: one treatment slot remains, and it cannot be divided or shared. Ada, untreated, lives five years at well-being 2, and treatment would raise her to 6 for those five years. Bob, untreated, lives five years at well-being 8, and treatment would raise him to 9. Separately, Ada's recovery would greatly improve the lives of two relatives who love her, worth three units each. Second: everyone concerned faces imminent death, and the choice is between extending the lives of a hundred patients by one year each at well-being 3, or extending the life of one patient by sixty years at well-being 5 — three hundred units either way. For each case, say what each reading on your menu implies, which readings are genuinely indifferent, and which break the tie only by importing something the formula itself does not contain.

Then return to my meeting. For each of my two colleagues, state which reading or readings her or his argument requires, whether that reading survives your verdicts, and whether it in fact delivers the conclusion drawn from it. I would especially like to know whether they can both be right, whether either of them needs a premise the formula does not supply, and whether there is a reading of "count for one" — equal claim to an indivisible good, perhaps — that recommends something like an equal chance rather than either priority to the worst off or the largest total.
