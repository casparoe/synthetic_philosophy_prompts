I convene a weekly seminar on collective decision-making for roughly eighteen advanced undergraduates drawn from philosophy, economics and public policy. I have one eighty-minute session, a projector, index cards, two decks of playing cards, a cloth bag, a whiteboard, and a box of chocolates. I want a fully runnable in-class game that makes participants feel, from the inside, the mechanism behind the unilateralist's curse — the point where a group in which any single member can trigger an outcome for everyone ends up acting on the most optimistic error in the room rather than on its best collective judgment. (If I have just misdescribed the mechanism, say so in a sentence and then design for the corrected version.)

What I need from you:

1. The game itself. Repeated rounds in which there is a hidden true value of a proposed project, each participant receives a private noisy estimate of it, and any one participant alone can launch the project for the whole group. Give me the actual numbers: the distribution the true value is drawn from — I want the downside to be heavy-tailed, with a small chance of a large loss and a modest typical gain — the noise distribution for the private signals, and a cheap, transparent way to generate both with cards and the bag so that participants can see the procedure is fair and I can run it without a laptop. Give a scoring scheme in points, with the chocolates as token stakes, in which refraining is genuinely competitive with acting, so that doing nothing is not a sucker's move.

2. The sequence. Tell me how many rounds, how to vary the number of participants whose signals count across rounds without restarting the exercise, and how to record results on the whiteboard so the pattern is visible. I want two moments engineered to happen rather than asserted. First: in a large round, nearly everyone privately thinks the project should not go ahead, it goes ahead anyway, and the group loses points. Second: the groups with more participants do worse than the groups with fewer, even though nobody has become less well calibrated or more reckless and the signals are drawn the same way. If the numbers I asked for in (1) won't reliably produce both, change the numbers and tell me what you changed.

3. Comparison and wrinkles. Include rounds run under alternative decision rules — majority, unanimity, and delegation to a single designated participant — together with the expected value of each rule under your distributions, so I can check the game is working. Then, in the second half, four complications: a round in which the person who triggers gets a private side-benefit while the losses are shared by everyone; a round in which participants can pay points to force a synchronous group vote instead of acting alone; a variant in which a few participants receive lower-variance signals and only they hold the trigger, where I want your honest verdict on whether that fixes the bias or merely relocates it onto fewer, better draws; and one round with open deliberation, where the design should show when talking helps — because genuine independent evidence gets pooled — and when it makes things worse — because a shared optimistic consideration gets counted many times over as if it were independent.

4. A one-page participant-facing rules sheet, in neutral language that does not name the idea or hint at the conclusion, and no lecture from me before play begins. If possible, end with a round in which the group proposes its own decision rule and then plays one round under it.

5. A debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto parts of the mechanism: which round showed the one-sided filter, what exactly the whiteboard numbers show about the effective evidence threshold for acting versus refraining, and why this is not simply groupthink, not a tragedy of the commons, and not ordinary individual optimism bias. It should also cover why better information and more discussion do not neutralize it while a change in decision rule does, and when the phenomenon does not arise at all because the asymmetry that generates it is absent.

6. An honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time: participants triggering out of curiosity, participants inferring that someone else must know something, attempts to coordinate or signal, unlucky small samples that hide the effect, the chocolates distorting behaviour, and anything else you anticipate — with a fix for each. Close by being frank about what the game cannot reproduce and I will have to say myself, especially anything about irreversibility, unbounded stakes, or the step from the group's decision being biased to a particular individual having a reason to refrain.
