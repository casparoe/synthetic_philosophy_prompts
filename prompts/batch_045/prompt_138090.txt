I work on the evolution of norms, and I keep getting stuck on a dispute that does not seem to have moved in thirty years: whether the Lewisian analysis of convention — a regularity of behavior that is arbitrary, self-enforcing, and held in place by each agent's preference to conform given that the others conform, selected from among alternative equilibria by salience or precedent — can be stretched to cover social norms proper, or whether norms bring in something the framework has no room for: normative expectations, a readiness to sanction, and conformity that survives anonymity. The Lewisian reply is that sanctions and normativity are just extra enforcement machinery bolted onto what is at bottom a coordination problem; the other camp replies that an account on which a norm is merely a self-enforcing regularity cannot tell norms apart from driving-side conventions, and so has not reached the phenomenon. Both sides have been recycling the same handful of cases — driving side, tipping, dueling, queueing — and nobody moves.

My hypothesis is that these are two questions under one heading: how a self-enforcing regularity can arise and persist among agents who begin with no normative attitudes at all, versus what marks off the regularities people hold each other to from the ones they merely coordinate around. If that is right, the programs are not competing answers to anything, and the standoff is an artifact of a label.

The probe I have been trying on colleagues is a population in which no member ever observes another's individual behavior, but everyone sees a reliable public signal of the aggregate distribution — a nightly published percentage, say — and nobody can sanction anybody; the only thing anyone can do is adjust their own behavior. Call these broadcast equilibria: stable patterns that persist until the published number crosses some point and then unravel. I claim they are data for the dispute, since everything distinctively normative is stripped away and a regularity still arises, holds, and collapses; my department is split on whether they touch the question at all.

Diagnose the disagreement, and tell me how the broadcast case cuts given your diagnosis — decisive for one camp, dismissible by both, or evidence that each framework is answering a different question. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a result about dynamics, a distinction between the two questions, a new kind of case, or an honest expectation that the impasse simply persists.
