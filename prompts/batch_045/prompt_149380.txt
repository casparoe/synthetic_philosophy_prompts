I'm taking an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on the measurement of harm, and last week I got into an argument with a classmate who works in injury epidemiology that neither of us could settle. We agreed about the headline empirical claim: that rates of homicide and of death in warfare, measured per head of population, have fallen enormously over the long run, and that a randomly chosen person alive today faces a lower chance of being killed by another person than a randomly chosen person in almost any earlier century. What we disagreed about is whether that headline claim establishes the larger thesis that the world is becoming less violent, or whether the larger thesis is well-posed only after one makes a long series of contestable bookkeeping choices — after which it has as many different answers as there are choices, and no answer that is simply the answer.

Please write an essay of roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words that works through this and then takes a position. Along the way I want you to deal with at least the following four difficulties. First, the currency problem: violence might be counted in deaths, in non-fatal assaults, in threats that never land, in years lived under credible fear, in dispossession or forced relocation, or in pain; and there seems to be no non-arbitrary exchange rate between these, so that a period can show improvement on one currency and deterioration on another. Consider seriously the proposal to collapse them all into a single metric such as person-years of suffering or of autonomously chosen activity foregone, and say whether that proposal actually dissolves the problem or merely relocates it. Second, the denominator problem: per capita rates are not the only option. One could count absolute numbers of violent events, which move with population; or one could count violence per social encounter, which matters because the number of strangers a typical person encounters in a lifetime has grown by orders of magnitude, so that a per-capita rate may understate the civilizing effect while an absolute count may hide it entirely. Say which denominator, if any, the phrase the world is becoming less violent should be read as invoking. Third, what I have been calling the concentration objection: the decline may be a fact about averages rather than about distributions, since violence appears to be increasingly concentrated in a small and partly self-reproducing subpopulation, and in the territories of states too weak to police it. If so, the typical person's risk can collapse while the people who actually absorb the violence experience almost no improvement at all, and it is unclear whether that refutes the thesis, merely qualifies it, or is irrelevant to it. Fourth, the export and measurement problem, which I think cuts in both directions: later periods are far better documented than earlier ones, which should if anything inflate later counts of violence relative to earlier ones and therefore makes the documented decline harder to dismiss as an artifact; but the violence that remains is concentrated precisely where registration systems are weakest, so we may be badly wrong about how much of it is left and about whether it is falling.

Please do not conclude that everything depends on how one defines violence. I want a verdict on two specific things: whether the unqualified claim that the world is becoming less violent is the kind of claim that can be true or false at all, or only true or false relative to a stipulated currency, denominator and population boundary; and — if the latter — which stipulation someone whose aim is to reduce the amount of violence in the world ought to care about, and what the trend actually looks like under that stipulation, including in the concentrated tail. You might test your own answer against what I have been calling the swap test: whether a randomly selected person from a much more violent century, told the truth about both the average and the tail, would be rationally required to accept a lottery ticket that relocated them to a randomly selected life today, and whether that test is a genuine arbiter here or merely smuggles in everything else that has improved. Close with a short paragraph on what follows in practice: given your verdict, would someone trying to reduce violence be making a mistake by focusing on the settings where the headline rates look worst? Please argue from the structure of the measurement problem and from broadly familiar historical patterns rather than from named studies, authors or data sets — I'll be reading this offline and don't want to chase references.
