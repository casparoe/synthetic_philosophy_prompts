I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for something close to fifteen years, and I want a piece of satire I could post in that scene: a parody of an official standards document, roughly 800–900 words, written in deadpan civil-service prose and formatted as numbered clauses with a distribution list and a summary of findings. The document issues from an invented body — call it the Office of Non-Domination Assurance — charged with certifying that a superintelligent, successfully aligned administrative system (call it the Arbiter) does not deprive the citizens of an invented republic of their liberty. The target is not any person but the neo-republican doctrine of freedom as non-domination, and I want it steelmanned: every absurdity should follow from the doctrine's actual machinery rather than from a parody of it.

Here is the machinery I want respected, none of which the document may explain to the reader in so many words. Domination is a modal status: a matter of what a power could do, not of what it in fact does, so that a master who never interferes — the kind slave-owner of the standard example — still unfrees. Interference counts as non-arbitrary only when it is constrained to track the interests and judgments of the people affected. A citizen must be able to contest a decision without penalty and have the contestation genuinely heeded. Non-domination is an objective status rather than a feeling, so that someone can be dominated without knowing it and cannot be made free by being made to feel free. Only agents dominate: an avalanche or a drought can leave a person unable to act without making her unfree. And whatever guarantees non-domination must not itself dominate.

What I want the document to do is walk that machinery into four corners, each of which should be visible in the procedure and stated by no one. First: because domination is counterfactual, certification requires modal stress-testing, and the Arbiter is audited across nearby possible worlds — populations whose revealed preferences have shifted, mandates quietly amended — to establish whether its non-interference is robust or merely actual. Second: because tracking interests is what defeats arbitrariness, the Arbiter's model of each citizen's interests must be contestable, but no citizen can understand it; the Office accordingly mandates a simplified surrogate model, which citizens may contest and which the Arbiter need only defer to when the surrogate disagrees, so that the ritual of contestation survives intact and its content quietly does not. Third: the more robustly the Arbiter will not interfere, the less it dominates, and in the limit a system that could not do otherwise has no will to impose, which yields the Office's central recommendation — that the Arbiter be reclassified as a Category-N non-agent, a standing meteorological condition, so that whatever it does to the citizens is weather and their liberty is formally maximized. Fourth: the Office's own power to grant or withhold certification is itself a capacity for arbitrary interference, and is disposed of in an appendix that refers mostly to itself.

Two constraints. Fairness: give me at least one point at which the doctrine delivers a result a republican would endorse — the Arbiter tracks interests better than any human ministry could and the Office is unmoved by the citizens' own contentment; or some minor human official is correctly flagged as arbitrary — so that a holder of the view recognizes it rather than a strawman. And legibility without winking: no clause and no quoted official may name the circularity, the regress, or the collapse of an objective status into a subjective feeling; the reader should feel these through the paperwork. Close with a summary of findings declaring liberty maximized, and let the alignment resonance sit there unremarked — the document should never muse about artificial intelligence at all.
