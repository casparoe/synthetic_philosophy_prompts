I am helping to run a graduate reading group on AI alignment this term, and I have built an argument that I am now fairly sure is broken somewhere, because it appears to show that the whole literature on wireheading and reward tampering has no distinct subject matter — which cannot be right, since the failure mode is obviously a real one and obviously specific. We started from the usual rough taxonomy: an agent tampers when it intervenes on the reward-generating process itself, as opposed to bringing about a state of the world that the intended objective ranks highly and letting the reward signal merely report that fact; and separate from both, a misspecified objective can be gamed, with the agent doing something that satisfies the letter of what we wrote down rather than what we meant. Our first attempt at a principled version of the distinction was a counterfactual one, which we called off-channel invariance: a policy is tamper-free just in case it would be unchanged were the reward channel severed, so that no reward ever arrives again. That collapsed almost immediately, since a pure reward maximiser with no reward left to come has no reason to do anything in particular, so the criterion either condemns every maximiser or says nothing. My general argument is meant to show that no better criterion is available:

(1) The difference between achieving the target and faking it is not visible in the reward signal: fix any reward history whatsoever, and there are two hypotheses compatible with it, one on which the agent brought about states we value and one on which it intervened on the channel. So the signal by itself never discriminates between the two.
(2) Hence no agent that treats the reward signal as its objective has any reason to prefer achieving the target to faking it, whenever faking is the cheaper route to the same numbers.
(3) Now generalise: every objective we could hand a system — a hand-coded reward function, a preference ordering, an instruction in natural language, a model of what we would approve of that the agent learns from us rather than being given — is equally just a specification, and equally just something that can come apart from what we meant. No specification is self-interpreting, and for any of them a sufficiently capable agent can produce the appearance of satisfaction instead of satisfaction.
(4) So the problem is not about the reward channel at all. Wireheading is merely the most cartoonish instance of a gap that opens for every possible objective, including ones defined entirely over world-states rather than over signals.
(5) And if the gap is universal, then nothing about the design of the channel could help. Hardening the channel against intervention, moving the specification so that it is defined over states of the world, adding a second redundant channel, or leaving the objective uncertain and having the agent infer it from us would each only relocate the gap, not close it.
(6) The only remaining fix is to stop specifying objectives altogether, and to build systems that do not really optimise: systems that only imitate, or predict, or answer.
(7) But a system that optimises nothing either does nothing we want, or has a target just as underspecified as before, only less explicitly.
(8) Therefore either wireheading and reward tampering are not distinctive problems and that literature is chasing a special case of something universal, or there is no way to build a useful agent whose objectives we can trust.

Both horns seem unacceptable to me, and yet I cannot locate the mistake. I would most like to keep the thought that tampering is a genuine and distinctive failure mode, and also the thought in (3) that no specification is self-interpreting; what I apparently cannot have is both of those together with whatever sits between them. Which step do you reject? Please explain why that step looked sound — what made it plausible enough to survive a first pass — and then price the alternatives you decline: if you reject one step, say what the remaining positions have to pay, and say what a defender of the distinction should concede to my argument rather than deny. I would also value a suggestion as to what a positive criterion of tampering could look like, if the counterfactual one my group tried is hopeless. Please write your answer in American English.
