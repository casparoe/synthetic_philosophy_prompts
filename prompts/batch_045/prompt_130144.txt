I help run a monthly evening at my local library, advertised as "decisions, without the maths" — usually 16 to 22 adults, mostly between their thirties and sixties, almost none of whom have studied philosophy or economics, 90 minutes in a room with one long table and a whiteboard. I took the series over last year after making an expensive mess of a renovation decision, and I've been reading about reasoning on my own since then; the sessions go best when people actually do something rather than discuss a case on paper. The one I can't get right is sunk costs. Everyone agrees within about four minutes that money, time, or effort already spent shouldn't figure in a decision — only what's still ahead — and then a third of the room immediately starts producing cases where that verdict feels wrong: finishing the degree because four years are already in, staying in a band or a voluntary project through its bad patch, a shopkeeper staying open on a dead day because she's already unlocked the door, a government refusing to cancel something it has spent billions on. I want an activity where they hit the pull from the inside, actually choosing with something small at stake whether to throw good after bad, and where they produce the pro-sunk-cost reasons themselves before anyone names anything. No lecture up front, and I'd rather the phrase "sunk cost fallacy" not be said until the debrief.

Three wrinkles I've been chewing over, which I'd like built in if they survive scrutiny. First, my sense is that a settled policy of finishing what you start can pay in a way that no single decision to finish does — the person who walks away at the first bad patch never reaches the projects whose returns come late and all at once, and other people can see the pattern even when the individual case looks pointless — so act-by-act reasoning and policy-level reasoning may come apart here. Second, some apparent sunk costs don't look sunk to me at all: the money has left my account, but what it bought is still sitting there doing work — a half-built thing, a skill, a relationship, an option I could still exercise later — and I'm not sure my group will be able to tell that case from the pure one, or whether they even should. Third, in anything social, persisting looks like it could be a signal — "I'm the sort of partner who doesn't abandon things" — worth something to whoever sends it, even if it's worth nothing to the decision in front of them.

So: design me a runnable activity for that room, that table, and that 90 minutes. I need the materials (I have index cards, dice, a whiteboard, and a small budget for snacks or a prize), the rules, a payoff scheme that works without real money but still has enough bite that people hesitate, a timeline with minute marks, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the parts of the idea — including, explicitly, the moments where honoring the sunk cost came out ahead and what made it come out ahead. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it: people recognising what's being tested and playing against the hypothesis, the "it's only points" shrug, the one person who refuses to engage with fake stakes, the loud contrarian. And be frank about which feature of the issue the activity simply can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in words. Please don't give me reading lists or citations — I have no access to a library catalogue beyond the public one, and I'd rather work from the argument than from names.

Where you're guessing, say so. In particular: if my three wrinkles collapse into one, or if the "policy versus act" move is doing more work than it can bear, or if the question "is honoring sunk costs always irrational?" is ill-posed in a way I've walked straight past, tell me that plainly instead of designing around it. Any step in the debrief that depends on how people actually behave in a room, mark as a prediction I should test rather than a fact about the view.
