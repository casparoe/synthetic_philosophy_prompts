I am on the philosophy job market and in three weeks I am giving a job talk at a department that is mostly mainstream analytic philosophers of language, with a strong direct-reference and neo-Fregean majority, two formal semanticists who work in dynamic and situation semantics, and one historian of early analytic philosophy. My advisors think the pragmatic device I introduce is baroque and that the historical section is a hostage to fortune, but I want to keep both, because the historian will be in the room and I do not want to look like I am only doing semantic engineering. My talk defends a version of Russell's theory of descriptions that I call the logical-form-only reading, and I expect a rough and specific question period, so I would like a Q and A prep sheet rather than a defense or a literature review.

The thesis I am defending has three parts. First, definite descriptions are not singular terms and do not contribute an object, or a singular proposition, to the semantic content of the sentences in which they occur; they contribute only Russellian quantificational structure. Second, to explain the apparent singularity of ordinary referential uses, I add a pragmatic device I call the reportative operator, which takes scope over the whole utterance, attributes to the speaker a demonstration, a mental file, or an intention to pick out a particular object, and thereby explains the assertoric force of referential uses without letting that object enter the truth conditions of the original sentence. Third, I argue that the apparatus of primary and secondary scope in Russell already contains the resources for this, provided we reinterpret scope not as syntactic embedding but as a choice of assignment or evaluation circumstance. I have a sketch of the formal semantics, a paragraph on why the classic cases from Strawson and Donnellan are handled by the reportative operator rather than by a semantic ambiguity, and a short section claiming that the arguments from Kripke against descriptivism about proper names do not touch the logical-form-only reading.
