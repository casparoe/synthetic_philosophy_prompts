I teach aesthetics, and I somehow also sit on the university committee that decides what counts as a "work" for our percent-for-art scheme — i.e. what gets paid for out of the building budget, insured as art, and maintained in perpetuity. Our track record so far:

- bronze figurative memorial, cast from a clay maquette — accepted
- the foundry's full-size working plaster, kept afterwards as a record — rejected ("a stage in production")
- hand-painted ceramic tiles for a foyer, commisioned from a local studio — accepted
- a 1930s operating theatre restored in situ in the medical school — rejected ("heritage, not art")
- eight speakers in the law library playing a live feed of the building's own ventilation noise, pitch-shifted, on a fourty-year schedule with no performer after the opening night — accepted
- four hundred glass microscope slides of plant tissue made by a 19th-century botanist, shown as an unlabelled grid because a curator asked us to — rejected ("scientific objects")
- a tapestry woven by six workshop weavers to a cartoon supplied by a named artist — accepted

Here's the new one and I have to guess how we'll rule. The engineering department wants the scheme's money for the nanotech building's facade: three thousand stainless fins whose spacing is set by an optimisation algorithm that minimises cooling load. The pattern gets re-run by the building management system each season, so it drifts from year to year; the engineers are publishing the code; they describe it as "a shading system that happens to read as moiré" and have written an artist's statement because we asked them to; the faculty art committee is enthusiastic.

I don't want a general essay on the definition of art. I want the two or three rules that fit our past verdicts about equally well, what each of them predicts here, your best guess with the reason for preferring it, which of our precedents is the closest analog and why it's genuinely unclear which way it points, and the one further proposal I should put in front of the committee to separate the rules that are still standing.
