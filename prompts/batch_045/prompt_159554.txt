**Two lotteries**

Setup: one dose of a drug. David needs the whole dose to survive; five other people each need a fifth of it. So you can save David or save the five. Taurek's claim, as I understand it: you are not *required* to save the greater number. Each person's loss is, from that person's own point of view, just as bad as anyone else's. There is no perspective from which five deaths add up into something worse *for someone* — nobody suffers the sum. So no one's claim on you gets stronger just because more people happen to be on their side. And as I remember it, in the second half of the paper (the island case with the boat and the volcano) he says that if the islanders have agreed in advance on a policy, the captain should head for the larger group, because that maximizes each islander's chance of being rescued; but if they've made no such agreement, the captain should flip a coin — heads the larger group, tails the smaller one.

I want to set aside the standard ex ante objection (if you didn't know which group you'd end up in, you'd want the captain to save the larger group, since that maximizes your own chance). Both sides have rehearsed that exchange to death, and the Taurekian reply — that this smuggles aggregation back in by summing chances across distinct possible people — is available. My question is different. Grant Taurek everything. You still have to act. And the thing people say at this point is: fine, then give everyone an equal chance. I think that phrase is ambiguous between two procedures that come sharply apart.

(i) *The claim-lottery.* Pick one of the six people at random and treat that person's claim as the decisive one; satisfy it; if there's drug left over, keep satisfying further claims in random order until you can't. Concretely: with probability 1/6 David is picked first and gets the whole dose; with probability 5/6 one of the five is picked first, and once a fifth of the dose is gone David can no longer be saved, so the rest of the five get their fifths too. So David's chance of surviving is 1/6 and each of the five has a 5/6 chance. Expected survivors: about 4.33.

(ii) *The survival-chance-lottery.* Toss a fair coin between give it to David and give it to the five. David gets 1/2, each of the five gets 1/2. Expected survivors: 3.

Both procedures refuse to count the numbers. But with one versus ninety-nine, (ii) still hands David a 1/2 chance and each of the ninety-nine a 1/2 chance — you'd be tossing a coin over whether ninety-nine people die — whereas (i) gives David 1/100 and the ninety-nine a 99/100 chance. So (i) converges on saving the greater number as the imbalance grows, and (ii) doesn't. Similarly in the island case: with eighty on one end and twenty on the other, (i) gives the larger group an 80/100 chance and (ii) gives it 1/2.

Here's the argument. Taurek's *recommendation* is (ii). But (ii) is the procedure that equalizes *prospects*, and equalizing prospects is not what his argument is about. His argument is about the comparative strength of *claims*: no one's claim is beefed up by the number of people standing behind it. The procedure that expresses that is (i), which gives each claim equal weight as a possible tiebreaker — and which, as a kind of side effect that nobody asked for, saves the greater number almost always. So the view has a gap between its argument and its recommendation. Either you give up (ii) and concede that the fair procedure under Taurek's own reasoning looks in practice like saving the many; or you say that what fundamentally matters is equality of prospects rather than equality of claims — but then you owe a story about why prospects matter, and I don't see how that story avoids being the ex ante argument all over again.

Three replies I can think of, none of which I'm confident about: (a) Taurek may not think any lottery is *required*. The claim is permissive: you may save David, you may save the five. On that reading my question rests on a mistake — how to pick between two permissible options is a further question the argument never answers. My attempt at a reply: if both are permissible and you're an impartial stranger with no independent reason to favour anyone, then not picking arbitrarily is itself a reason to randomize, and the which-lottery question comes back; but maybe the honest answer is that any randomization is fine, and then the numbers drop out completely. (b) Maybe (i) isn't really equal treatment of claims at all: it privileges the five because their claims are *cheaper to satisfy*, which is efficiency reasoning Taurek never signed up to. I want to say this is fine because it's aggregation-free efficiency (you're not weighing anyone's loss against anyone else's, you're just not wasting the drug), but I'm not sure that's stable. (c) Maybe I'm over-reading the difference between the drug case and the island case — the boat is a shared institution, everyone knows which group they're in, and maybe the coin flip is doing different work there.
