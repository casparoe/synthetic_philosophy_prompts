I'm doing a final-year option on perception and I got nerd-sniped by something in a seminar discussion, so I've been sat in the library turning it over instead of doing the actual reading. We were looking at the classic argument from illusion, and I tried to write out the strongest version I could, one where nothing rests on a dodgy sense-datum premise and everything looks like plain modal reasoning. Our tutor had set up a case that does a lot of work in it: a cortical stimulator (call it the V-3) that can be switched on without the subject noticing and that produces, by direct stimulation, an experience of the very room she is sitting in, indistinguishable for her from seeing it, while the room plays no causal role in producing it. I've wrote the argument out below. The bit that bothers me isn't the conclusion about naive realism, which I could live with, it's that the argument seems to keep going past it.

(1) Naive realism: when S veridically perceives a mind-independent object o, o itself is partly constitutive of S's experience, so the phenomenal character of that experience is not fixed independently of which object is perceived. (2) Substitutability: for any actual veridical perception E of o, there could occur an experience E* which S cannot, at the time, tell apart from E by reflection alone, and in which o is neither a constituent nor a cause (the V-3 case, or any systematic deception). (3) Reflection is authoritative about character: if S cannot tell E and E* apart by reflection alone, then E and E* have the same phenomenal character. (4) Grounding invariance: if two experiences have the same phenomenal character, then whatever metaphysically grounds that character is the same in both cases, since grounding cannot vary while the thing it grounds is held fixed. (5) So E itself could have occurred, with the very character it actually has, while o played no part in it. (6) Constitution is necessary: if E could have its actual character while o played no part, then o is not a constituent of E. (7) Therefore naive realism is false, and no perceptual experience is object-involving. (8) Generality: nothing in (2) to (6) turns on vision or on o's being a physical object, so run it on apparent memory of a past event (a confabulated memory is indistinguishable from a real one and the past event plays no part in producing it), on reading emotion in a face, on anything you like: no mental state is ever really of anything beyond itself. (9) Self-undermining: but by (7) premise (2) never described a contrast in the first place, because hallucination is meant to be an experience just like a perception except not object-involving, and if no experience is object-involving then every experience is a hallucination and (2) picks out no special class at all. (10) And (8) and (9) can't be right: hallucination is a real, empirically studied phenomenon that contrasts with perceiving, and people do sometimes find out that they were hallucinating, by looking again, by a friend confirming what's there, by the monitor showing the stimulator was on.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? If I have to choose I'd most like to keep (2), (3) and (10); I'm happy to give up naive realism if that's the price, but I don't want a view that also eats memory and other minds, and I really don't want (9). I had one idea of my own while sitting there, which is that introspection might be like a voltmeter with only two decimal places: a difference in what constitutes an experience needn't show up as a difference in what introspection can read off, so maybe (3) and (4) can't be combined the way I've combined them. But I can't tell whether that's a real answer or just a metaphor I'm leaning on, and I can't see what it commits me to if I take it seriously. Ideally I'd like you to name the exact step, say why it looked airtight when I wrote it down, and then tell me what each of the main options — disjunctivism, some intentionalist or sense-datum story, whatever else — has to pay to reject it. Please write your answer in British English.
