Act as a professional philosopher who works on rational choice and the structure of practical reason — someone who has taught the hitchhiker case many times and is bored of the stock moves. Think carefully before you write: open with a few lines of notes (not part of the dialogue) identifying the single strongest objection to each position and one thing each character genuinely concedes to the other, and only then write the scene. Here is the background. My friend Priya and I drove eight hours home from a conference last weekend and spent most of it arguing about Parfit's hitchhiker: the traveler stranded in the desert who promises the driver money to be taken to town, who once in town has no reason to pay, and whose ride depends on the driver being a good enough judge of character to predict whether she will pay. Priya thinks the case supports something like rational irrationality — that it can be rational, at the level of policies, to arrange for yourself to be disposed to act irrationally at the level of individual acts, and that the winning design is to become the sort of person who pays. I think she gets the shape of the problem right and the mechanism wrong: what the hitchhiker needs is not a corrupted decision procedure but a plan adopted at the start and honored afterward, plus whatever cheap external scaffolding — escrow, a third party, a reputation she actually cares about — makes the plan credible. Neither of us budged, partly because we kept arguing about the original case instead of the variant we came up with at a gas station outside Tonopah, which I think is the harder one.

Use my variant. Call it the readout case. Before unlocking the door, the driver can inspect not the hitchhiker's decision but the rule she is running, and his inspection is adversarial: he generates a handful of probe cases in which paying gains her nothing at all — the ride is already over, the driver is beyond being helped by the money, there will be no further interaction, no one will ever learn of it — and he opens the door only if the rule would pay in at least some of those useless-payment cases. The point is that a disposition targeted exactly on the situation where paying is useful looks, under that scrutiny, like what it is: straightforward maximizing. So the hitchhiker has to buy credibility with waste; she needs a rule whose reliability is guaranteed by its firing even where it is pointless, which is another way of saying the disposition has to be coarse enough to misfire. Assume the readout is fine-grained but noisy, and that the driver could in principle inspect not just her rule but the higher-order rule by which she selected it, which threatens a regress. Write a dialogue of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words between two characters, Mara and Ellis, who are themselves on a long night drive around two in the morning with this argument already well underway. Mara defends Priya's side refined to meet the readout case: the rational thing is to design yourself; selective, self-binding irrationality is not a breakdown of rationality but a feature of a two-tier account of it; the wasted money in the probe cases is the premium on the disposition, not a defect. Ellis defends mine: rationality never asks you to act against your own all-things-considered judgment; what does the work in cases that go well is the normative stickiness of a prior intention plus cheap external signals; and if the only way to survive the readout is to adopt a disposition for the sake of the ride, you will not be able to adopt it, because the reason you have for having it is precisely the reason that dissolves it. Make them equally competent and make them actually answer each other. Mara has to say where a finely targeted disposition gives itself away under the readout, and whether she is describing anything more than the purchase of a signal. Ellis has to say what plan-plus-escrow amounts to in the desert, with no third party and no reputation in play, and whether sticking to an intention is anything other than a disposition with better branding. Each should make at least one real concession and neither should win.

Keep it a scene rather than a lecture: no narrator's box explaining the theories, no page-long monologue met with fair enough. Let the ideas arrive through the back-and-forth, through examples the characters invent for each other, through their talking about the hitchhiker they passed an hour ago and did not pick up. Give them distinct voices — Mara quicker and more willing to bite a bullet, Ellis more careful and more irritated by her willingness. You may let them probe the noisy readout if it is useful — a rule that pays only most of the time, a driver who recalibrates — but don't let the arithmetic take over the scene. I'd like it to end without a tidy synthesis: something like Mara arriving at the thought that the disposition has to be adopted for reasons other than the ride, while the only reason either of them has is the ride, and Ellis not quite wanting to grant it, and the road continuing. After the dialogue, add two or three sentences of your own saying who you think has the better of the exchange and which single line is load-bearing.
