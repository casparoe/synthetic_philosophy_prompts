I am writing a seminar paper on whether phenomenal consciousness outruns access consciousness, and on what global workspace theory — the view that a mental content is conscious just in case it is globally broadcast and thereby made available to the consumer systems subserving report, deliberate action, and memory — can and cannot settle. A friend who works on neural correlates of consciousness handed me the following argument, which he says shows that the entire dispute is empty. Every step looks sound to me, and I cannot accept the conclusion.

The set-up. Suppose we had a technique — call it clamping — that transiently prevents the ignition-or-broadcast stage of the global workspace from occurring, or from reaching its downstream consumers, while leaving feedforward sensory processing and the early sensory buffers intact. Assume it can be applied and lifted cleanly, and that we can verify independently that broadcast has in fact failed.

(1) Evidence about whether a subject is phenomenally conscious at t must, in the end, reach an experimenter; and for an adult human the only route by which it can do so is the subject's reports and the subject's voluntary, deliberate behaviour.

(2) On global workspace theory itself, report and deliberate control just are the outputs that require global broadcast. So in a clamped subject, no evidence about its consciousness at t can be produced at t.

(3) Consolidation into a reportable memory likewise requires broadcast. So a report elicited after the clamp is lifted is either a reconstruction from traces that were never conscious or a confabulation; nothing produced after t could tell us whether there was experience at t.

(4) Therefore no possible experiment could distinguish the hypothesis that clamping abolishes phenomenal consciousness from the hypothesis that clamping leaves phenomenal consciousness fully intact while stripping away every route to it.

(5) When no possible evidence could distinguish two hypotheses, there is no fact of the matter as to which holds — or at any rate, the claim that there is one is cognitively empty.

(6) So the Block-style thesis that phenomenal consciousness can overflow access, and could in principle survive the loss of the workspace, is not a substantive claim at all.

(7) But the reasoning generalises past the hypothetical. In the ordinary, unclamped case, the difference between the subject's phenomenology covering all twelve items of a briefly flashed array and its covering only the four or so that were broadcast is also a difference that no evidence could settle, since any report of richness is itself a broadcast-mediated, capacity-limited output.

(8) Hence the access/phenomenal distinction is empty as an empirical research programme — which cannot be right, since global workspace theory's success at predicting which contents become reportable plainly leaves open whether reportable is the same property as conscious.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I am most attached to (1), (2) and (4); I am genuinely unsure about the rest, and in particular I cannot tell whether the break is a single bad step or a premise that is only plausible under a reading the argument does not quite state. Please price every alternative: for each step you consider rejecting, say what I would then be committed to, and why the step looked fine before I noticed the argument needed it. Note that my friend asserts (3) confidently, and I have a suspicion that some consolidation may not require broadcast at all — deal with that wrinkle only if it matters for where the argument actually breaks. Assume I know the access/phenomenal distinction and the bare bones of the global workspace picture; do not give me readings or citations.
