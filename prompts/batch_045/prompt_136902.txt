I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility this spring and want to close it with Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, read against the account Arendt offered in her report on the Eichmann trial. My question is whether Stevens is really an instance of what she described, or only superficially resembles one.

The thesis, as I read it, decomposes into three separable claims: first, that the operative motives were ordinary and career-serving — diligence, ambition, the wish to do one's job well — rather than ideological conviction or cruelty; second, that the agent was to a striking degree interchangeable, in the sense that any competent functionary in the same post would have done much the same; and third, that the root failure was a failure of thought, an inability to carry on the silent dialogue with oneself that would leave one unable to live with the person one is becoming — a failure distinct from weakness of will and from the familiar vices. I want to press on which of these is doing the real explanatory work, and on whether they can come apart: an agent could satisfy the first two while being perfectly capable of self-scrutiny, or fail the third while acting from deep conviction.

Stevens seems to complicate the picture: his motives are professional in the very specific sense of service to an ideal of the good butler, yet the novel labours to make him singular and irreplaceable rather than a cog; and his narration is a tissue of euphemism and self-exculpation, which raises the question of whether Arendt's framework has room for an agent who is not thoughtless but actively engaged in not thinking. I'd ask you to work through three scenes — the dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids, carried out efficiently and later recalled in the language of professional competence; his father's death upstairs while he goes on serving; and the conversation on the pier near the end, where he comes closest to saying what has been lost.

Two questions matter most for my purposes. Is the banality thesis genuinely a claim about a distinct species of moral failure, or does it collapse into the unremarkable charge that the agent failed to consider what he was doing? And does the novel, by making Stevens a figure of pathos rather than a bureaucrat, add something the philosophical literature lacks — the suggestion that there is a failure of loyalty to particular persons, his father and Miss Kenton above all, that the vocabulary of thoughtlessness and conscience does not name? Where the novel resists the reading, please say so plainly; I would rather present a critic who thinks Ishiguro is doing something else entirely than one who makes the fit look tidier than it is.
