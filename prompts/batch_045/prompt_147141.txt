Hi — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I'm working on a piece about the latest revision of the psychiatric diagnostic manual. I've been sitting in on a couple of case conferences at a teaching hospital to get the flavour of it, and I keep getting stuck on the philosophy, so I'd rather get the argument straight before I write something dumb.

Here's my working understanding of Ian Hacking. A psychiatric kind like 'major depression' or 'borderline personality disorder' is what he calls a human kind: unlike a natural kind, the people classified can learn the classification, so the label feeds back onto them, they come to experience and describe themselves under it, the clinicians then adjust the criteria to fit the population they're actually seeing, and round it goes. So the category doesn't discover the condition, it manufactures it — it's made up, in his sense. If that's right, three things seem to follow, and I want to know whether I've got him right or whether I've built something he wouldn't sign: (1) there's no fact of the matter about whether a diagnosis 'tracks something real', so the manual is essentially fashion with better PR; (2) looking for neural or genetic correlates of a manual category is a category error, since the category was constructed rather than found, so there's nothing there to find; (3) the ethical upshot is diagnostic abstinence — if handing someone a label partly creates the condition, clinicians should describe behaviour and symptoms and simply never give the patient the label, and any category we catch looping should be retired. I floated this to a friend who works in epidemiology and she said I was begging the question, which I think was her using 'begging the question' wrong, but it did make me suspicious of my own reasoning.

The concrete case I keep coming back to is a pilot clinic someone told me about: they run a risk-prediction model over adolescents' school and primary-care records and flag kids as 'at risk' for a mood disorder a couple of years before they'd meet criteria. Their own ethics statement says they deliberately don't disclose the flag to the kid or the family, on the grounds that telling someone would cause the very outcome it predicts — they call it a closed-loop protocol, i.e. withholding the label is how you switch the loop off. I find that weirdly compelling, but it seems to rest on my three points, so if I've got Hacking wrong it's probably a bad policy.

So: where exactly have I gone wrong? Is the slide from 'this kind is looped' to 'this kind is invented, there's nothing to find underneath, and we should withhold the label' one mistake or three? I'd especially like to know whether the looping effect is meant to be a debunking argument about psychiatric kinds or just a causal claim about how they behave, and whether there's an important difference between the loop that runs through the patients and the loop that runs through the clinicians' criteria — I keep muddling those two together. Also, can a looped kind ever settle down and become stable enough to study, or does looping mean it's permanently sliding around? No reading lists or citations please, I just want the argument; keep it under about 600 words, and finish with a single line in this exact form: Verdict: <the one mistake I'm making, in ten words or fewer>
