For a little over two years I have run a small Saturday-morning discussion group — six or seven of us, none with any academic training in philosophy. I work in freight logistics and I got into all of this after leaving the tightly knit religious community I was raised in, where we were taught from childhood that wanting a life outside was a sign of a corrupted heart; two of the others left communities of their own, and the rest joined out of interest. There is one argument we have never moved an inch on. Half the group — we have started calling them the deferrers — holds that when a grown woman tells you clearly, repeatedly and without pressure what she wants, and has had at least some contact with the alternative, that settles it: the preference is hers, and for us to go behind her own word and declare it a symptom is to treat her as a child. The other half, the archaeologists, holds that a want can be so thoroughly built by an upbringing in which the alternatives were never genuinely on the table that calling it hers just launders an injustice, and that noticing this is not condescension but the opposite of it.

The case we always come back to is one of my relatives, who I will call Marta. She is in her forties, was raised to believe that a woman's place is the household, was married at twenty, never held a paid job, and now says, when asked directly and at length, that she is glad of how her life went and would choose it again. She is also delighted that her daughter is studying engineering and encourages her constantly — which the deferrers read as proof that Marta's own want is not mere submission, and the archaeologists read as proof that the want was installed rather than chosen, since she wants for her daughter exactly what was never presented to her. There is one more fact we all accept: in her late twenties she spent three months helping a relative run a small shop in another town, doing the books and the ordering, and she came home certain that it was not for her. The deferrers say that is the whole ballgame — she tasted the alternative and spat it out. The archaeologists say three months of bookkeeping in a strange town, with no training and no encouragement, is not the alternative, only a caricature of it. We have gone further and stipulated, just to see what happens, a large long-term study of people who left communities like ours at different ages, in which the great majority of those who left after thirty say within five years that the preference they used to defend was never really theirs. It changed nobody's position: the archaeologists said, there, you see; the deferrers said that this simply shows a new environment producing a new preference, and that if the later want is authoritative then so was the earlier one, and we are back where we started.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I would rather have it than a survey of theories or a list of things to read. My own guess is that we are not disagreeing about any fact at all, but about which counterfactual question the phrase *her real preference* is supposed to be answering: the deferrers, I suspect, mean something like what she would choose now if she knew more, while the archaeologists mean what she would have chosen had the formative conditions been fair — and these two baselines come apart precisely in cases like Marta's, which is why the same case is evidence for both sides. A second guess is that the two camps are doing different things under one heading: one asking a metaphysical or psychological question about which state of Marta is really hers, the other asking a practical question about when the rest of us may override a person's stated wants. Which of these, if either, is right? Are we looking at a verbal dispute, a substantive one about whether autonomy is a property of a person's present state or of the history that produced it, a hidden empirical question about how stable her preference is under changed conditions, or a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis: what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a change of question — or should we expect no progress, and if so, why, and what should we do with that?
