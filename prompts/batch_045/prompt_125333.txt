I am a novelist at work on a book set in present-day Copenhagen, and there is one passage in it that I do not trust myself to write. It is a piece of intellectual ventriloquism, and I need it done with conviction rather than caution.

The chapter stages something I will not ask you to endorse: a séance at a municipal culture house, at which Søren Kierkegaard is induced to pronounce, in a newspaper article of his own composing, on the platform that most of the country now uses. Call it Chorus. Chorus has three rules, and they are the reason I am asking you and not a dictionary.

One: nothing is published under a name. Whatever you write is merged by a model with everything written by the people you follow and issued in the first person plural, attributed to no one. You may afterwards sign it, but the signature is an anonymous mark, identical for every user. Two: your own public feed is written in the same plural and addresses you as we, so that on Chorus no one is ever publicly addressed in the second person singular. Three: the only private channel the service offers, and the one it advertises, is this — once a day you may send a single message, in the singular, to whichever user the service calculates has read everything you have written. It cannot be forwarded, quoted, or captured. The company calls the feature the Single Reader.

The question I cannot settle for him is which thing Chorus is. Is it simply the public — the levelling, irresponsible, nobody-at-all that he described in his review of the present age, the same abstraction that, in the anonymous satirical paper whose mockery he first invited and then could never forgive, turned a whole city into a crowd free to jeer at a man with impunity? Or is it something his categories were not built for: is the Single Reader the parody of the relation he wanted rather than the relation itself, and can a machine that assigns you your one reader do any of the work he assigned to pseudonyms, to indirect communication, to being read by the single individual?

Write me the article, in his voice: the argument, and then a verdict, reached and defended. Does Chorus deepen the sickness of the public, or is it, for all its ugliness, an occasion for the single individual? Choose one. Do not set the two readings side by side and leave me to pick between them.

Beneath it, add a short note addressed to me rather than to his readers. Tell me where you have taken your largest liberty: which of your claims he would most likely have thrown back at you, and which part of his thought — or of his own conduct during that quarrel with the press — you suspect I distort by making him say it. I can work with a wrong answer. I cannot work with a menu.
