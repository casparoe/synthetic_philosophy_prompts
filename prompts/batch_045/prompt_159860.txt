I want to settle something well enough to actually use. When a probability is attached to a single case — this patient, this component, this applicant — which class of cases is it read off from? I take for granted the familiar difficulty that any individual belongs to indefinitely many classes that yield different frequencies, so that the step from a class frequency to a single-case probability needs a rule for choosing among those classes. What I lack is a rule I can state without immediately wanting to break it. My verdicts below come from clinical risk prediction, where the choice is made hundreds of times a day and usually without comment. Please tell me whether they add up to a principle, and if they don't, which of them has to go.

1. A 62-year-old non-smoker with a tumour of a stated stage and grade and no comorbidities, five-year survival: I want the frequency among patients of that stage, age band and comorbidity profile — not among all cancer patients, not among all 62-year-olds, not among all humans. Verdict: narrow, but stop at variables that could plausibly matter.

2. The registry also reports five-year survival among patients who were alive at one year. Used to answer a question asked before treatment, that class is illegitimate to me; asked at the one-year mark, the very same class is fine. Verdict: outcome-contaminated classes are excluded, but the exclusion seems to depend on when the question is asked rather than on the class itself, which I find suspicious.

3. A narrow class defined by a biomarker panel, a polygenic score and postcode, n ≈ 40, gives 30%; a broad class defined by stage and age, n ≈ 4000, gives 52%. Verdict: I distrust the narrow number, but not merely because of sample size. If the panel was pre-specified on the basis of earlier studies and the class has n ≈ 2000 and gives 34%, I take 34%. If someone searched candidate subclasses until one with a striking value turned up, I distrust it at any size — unless the split survives in a fresh cohort.

4. A subclass defined by a trait nobody had any hypothesis about shows a large difference, and the difference replicates in two independent cohorts. Verdict: legitimate class, even though we have no account of why. Replication, on my view, is doing the work that a mechanism would do.

5. "Patients well enough to attend the six-week follow-up" predicts the outcome superbly and replicates everywhere — but attendance is partly caused by the outcome being predicted. Verdict: not a legitimate class; I won't use it, even holding the bed open on its numbers would improve my calibration.

6. A well-understood randomiser: ticket number 17 in a thousand-ticket draw. The narrowest class about which I have any data — this ticket in this draw — would give 0 or 1, and I say instead that the probability is one in a thousand. Verdict: here I go maximally broad and ignore almost everything I know about the individual, which is the opposite of what I do in case 1.

7. Two classes, each legitimate by my own tests, neither a subclass of the other, giving 60% and 35%: one defined by the clinical profile, one by the screening route through which this patient was selected into my clinic. Verdict: I am inclined to say there is no single probability here, only class-relative ones, and that the right question is which decision the number is feeding. But I don't want to say that about case 6, where one in a thousand seems to be simply the probability.

8. Our model emits a continuous score; patients sharing a score form a class whose outcome frequency is well calibrated on held-out data. Verdict: I will act on it — I book the intensive-care bed at 40% — while resisting the claim that 40% is this patient's probability, and I'm not sure what my resistance amounts to. Note also that patients who died before the score could be computed were excluded from training, so the class is defined partly by survival — which by my verdict in case 2 should disqualify it.

9. The case I keep reversing on: a patient who has already received the treatment. Classed with treated patients, her outlook is good; classed with patients like her at referral, it is poor. Which number is hers, and does the answer change depending on whether I am predicting or evaluating the treatment?

What I want from you:

- One or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough to apply, with each clause keyed to the case that motivates it.
- What your principle says about cases I haven't listed — at minimum: a patient belonging to two legitimate cross-cutting classes where the decision has strongly asymmetric costs; a class defined by an intervention; a class defined by a variable whose value you don't know for this patient but whose distribution you do know; and a novel outcome with no history at all, where no class has any data.
- An honest statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, which verdict you would therefore overturn, and why that one rather than another.
- Finally, and most importantly: what would change your mind? Which argument or consideration would lead you to abandon or substantially revise the principle you've offered, and where in the reasoning above do you think the error is most likely to be? Please also flag any point at which you are covertly relying on a view about what probability is — frequency, propensity, degree of belief, something else — that my verdicts don't actually force.
