So I got into a three-hour argument with my friend last night — we started on how a superintelligent system ought to talk to people given that it would be insanely persuasive, she kept saying persuasion that good is just coercion with better manners, I kept saying no, the thing to worry about is who controls the framing rather than how smooth the speaker is, and by 1am I realised that what I was saying is pretty much the argument in the talk I have to give on Thursday, which is why I'm asking for this now instead of sleeping.

The talk is at a small workshop on deliberation and collective agency. Working title: "Factored cognition as an ideal of deliberation." My thesis in two halves. First: the setup that alignment people call factored cognition — you have a question too hard to answer head-on, you break it into sub-questions, you hand the sub-questions to copies of yourself or to other people, they break theirs down too, and the answers get composed back upward until there's an answer to the original question — is actually a better normative ideal of deliberation than the ideal speech situation, because it gets the epistemic goods without the usual pathologies: no node in the tree is charismatic, no node gets tired or interrupted, status buys nothing, and every node owes an explicit justification to the node above it. Second: the standard objection, that this is aggregation wearing a deliberation costume, misfires — in my "second-order factoring" version any node is allowed to spawn a node whose question is "is this decomposition of the parent question the right one?", and that's allowed recursively, so the thing that really determines outcomes, the framing, is contestable at every level in a way that an assembly's agenda never is. There's also this property I've started calling no-node-sees-the-whole, which I want to argue is a feature rather than a bug: nobody in the tree ever faces the full question, and I think that's exactly what makes the process immune to a certain kind of rhetorical capture.

The room, specifically: two senior political theorists who live inside the deliberation literature (ideal speech situation, reciprocity, the transformation rather than the aggregation of preferences); a formal person who does judgment aggregation and will look at a tree and immediately see an aggregation path with path-dependence written all over it; an epistemologist who works on peer disagreement; a self-described realist who thinks ideal theory is a way to avoid saying anything about power; and one junior person who has read enough about alignment to be actively hostile to the whole vocabulary.
