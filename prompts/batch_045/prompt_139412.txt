Two people in a reading group I sit in on — we've been working through Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment — got stuck in an argument we had to cut off, and I was asked to referee. I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move rather than tell me who wins.

The case they were arguing about is real enough in outline: a mid-sized city, recovering from a near-default, amended its charter to include what its drafters called a fiscal Ulysses clause. The city may not issue bonds to cover an operating deficit. There is one exception, a *declared revenue emergency*, which requires all of the following: a two-thirds vote of the nine-member council; a thirty-day wait between first reading and the vote; a written certification of the emergency from the finance director, who is appointed by the mayor; and — this was the part the drafters were proud of — a maturity date falling before the end of the council term that authorizes the borrowing, so that whoever borrows is still in office when the bill comes due.

A decade later the city's largest employer relocated mid-year, revenue fell sharply, and the council used the exception to borrow for payroll in sanitation and water treatment, on the argument that the alternative was service collapse. The vote was six to three, exactly the threshold. Two of the six were term-limited and left office before the bonds matured. The finance director who signed the certificate had been appointed by the mayor who requested the borrowing.

The exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Renata: The clause did what it was designed to do. Read it properly — it doesn't forbid deficit borrowing, it makes it slow, costly, and public, and it makes the people who borrow the people who have to repay. That is tying yourself to the mast, not swearing off the sea. That it was used once in ten years, for a shock nobody drafting it could have specified, is evidence the calibration was right, not that the clause is decoration.

(2) Theo: A precommitment released at the moment of temptation hasn't obviously done anything. Ulysses's ropes held. If the crew had untied him because he was shouting a very persuasive argument about how this particular stretch of coast was different, we wouldn't say the arrangement worked; we'd say he was never really tied. *Merely expensive* is the load-bearing phrase. A wealthy enough agent can always buy its way out, and here the price of the option is set by the same body that wants to exercise it. You have built a speed bump with a plaque on it.

(3) Renata: Then you've set the bar at *impossible*, and that can't be right. A rule no later majority can ever lift isn't self-binding, it's something else entirely; the point of the sirens is that Ulysses gets to hear the song and survive it. What the clause buys is translation: a fiscal panic has to be converted into a supermajority, a month of delay, and a signed certification before it becomes policy. That conversion is the work. The option isn't removed; the route to it is changed.

(4) Theo: Translation into whose judgment? Here is the dilemma. If you trust the later council's delayed, supermajoritarian, certified judgment, the earlier council had no reason to distrust it — the panic is exactly what the delay and the threshold are supposed to neutralize, so the precommitment is redundant. If you don't trust it, you shouldn't have let that same council decide whether the emergency was real, and you shouldn't have had the certificate signed by the mayor's own appointee. So the clause is redundant or porous, and which one it is gets decided case by case, after the fact, by whoever wants to borrow.

(5) Renata: False dilemma. A Ulysses contract never required the earlier self to be wiser about everything — only about one thing: that in the heat of the moment the costs get pushed onto somebody else. The clause targets that one bias and targets it structurally, through the maturity rule. And your porousness point cuts my way: the override is expensive enough that six of nine had to go on record by name, and two of them were gone before the bonds came due, which is a real political price, not a free pass.

(6) Theo: That last detail is the one that should worry you. The two who were leaving were the most willing to vote yes, precisely because they wouldn't be there. So the clause's central mechanism — make the borrowers eat the cost — was defeated by ordinary turnover, which is the most predictable feature of a ten-year-old rule. And that brings me to what I actually care about: there is no *self* here. Ulysses is one man with a bounded, temporary impairment and a known standing desire to hear the song; the city in year ten is a different council, a different mayor, partly different voters, facing a shock nobody wrote the clause about. Calling that self-binding flatters one group's coercion of a later group with an individual-level analogy it hasn't earned.

(7) Renata: That's the dead-hand objection in a new coat, and it proves too much. Every law binds people who didn't enact it. If continuity of identity were required for legitimate precommitment, no contract, treaty, or constitution could bind successors, and your objection would have to swallow all of law.

(8) Theo: Then drop the Ulysses story. If the justification is going to be *this is ordinary legal authority and ordinary legal authority binds successors*, then the whole apparatus of impaired selves, temporary passions, and self-binding is decoration — it does no argumentative work that *it's the law* doesn't already do. You can have the mast or you can have the general authority of law. I don't think you can have both, and I suspect the analogy is popular because it lets the second pass itself off as the first.

What I'd like from you:

Go through moves (1) through (8) in order, saying for each what is right, what is too strong or mistaken, and where the disagreement is merely verbal. Please keep separate the claims about this clause in this city and the claims about Ulysses contracts generally, since I think both of them slide between the two. Then give me the strongest single point on each side and the weakest; say whether (7) actually answers (6) or changes the subject; and say what both of them are missing — in particular whether there is a description of what a clause like this does that neither of them has on the table. My own sense, which may just be my confusion, is that both keep treating the clause as something that works on the will of whoever is bound by it, and I couldn't tell whether that's a feature of the case or a joint blind spot. One more thing I'd like addressed: whether a dispute like this is even decidable from a single case, given that a precommitment which works may be invisible and one that fails is conspicuous. Finally, if you think one of them is more right overall, say so, say by how much, and say why — but make the move-by-move part the bulk of your answer. Please don't restate their positions back to me, and don't give me readings or a bibliography; I want the adjudication and the reasoning behind it.
