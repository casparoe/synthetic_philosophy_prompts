Hi — I need help with something that came up at work, and I have to write a paragraph about it by Friday.

I work at a small research institute, mostly historical reconstruction — we piece together what happened from partial records. When we moved buildings six years ago we painted a sentence in the entrance hall, and it ended up in our grant boilerplate and the annual report: "Nothing that is true is beyond finding out." It was meant as anti-mysticism, a dig at the people who say "we'll never know, let's move on." The director loves it. Honestly it's mostly decorative.

Last month a new data analyst, who did a philosophy degree before he retrained, told us over lunch that taken literally our sentence isn't just false but definately catastrophic. The argument as I recieved it: take any truth nobody happens to know — say, whether a certain ship's log was burned or thrown overboard. Then there's a second truth, namely that the log was burned and nobody knows it was burned. And that second truth can't be known by anyone, because to know it you'd have to know the first thing, and the moment you knew the first thing the second one wouldn't be true anymore. So if nothing true is beyond finding out, there can't be any truth that nobody knows — which means everything true is already known and we should shut the institute down. He said it's a standard result with a name and offered to email me the paper; I have not read it and am not going to. Please don't send me links or reading lists either — no journal access and no patience.

Two complications. First: a senior colleague says the fix is easy, just restrict the slogan to claims that aren't about anybody's knowing anything. But that doesn't seem to help us, because the things we actually work on are plain facts about the world that nobody will ever establish, since the evidence rotted or was burned: exactly how many people were aboard a given vessel, what a particular letter said, whether a sample was contaminated before it was logged. Those aren't about knowledge, and they still look impossible to establish. Second: someone else proposed rewording it to "nothing that is true is beyond finding out by someone, at some time, in some possible situation," which feels like a cheat to me but I can't say why.

A third person — the one I half think is right — says the whole dispute is a category mistake and that the sentence was never a claim about truths at all, just a rule about how we behave: never treat a question as closed. But I can't tell whether that's a real answer or just a way of wriggling out of it.

So: is the analyst right that the sentence is in trouble? Is the restricted version any better, or does it cost us the thing we meant? Is the "possible situation" version a cheat? And what do I actually tell the board — keep it, drop it, or change it, and if we change it, to what? Write it like an email please: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just continuous prose I can crib from.
