im a junior and I got nerd-sniped by this after our school polled all 900 students on whether to switch the lunch schedule and the poll somehow beat the three teachers who had actually looked at the numbers. I started reading about Condorcet's jury theorem and now ive built an argument that I'm pretty sure is broken somewhere but I can't see where.

(1) Jury theorem: if everyone independently has, say, a 60% chance of picking the better of two options, the majority does way better than any one person does, and as the group grows the majority's accuracy climbs toward 100%.
(2) Independence is the fake part though. Real people read the same posts, copy each other, get spooked by the same rumor. So part of each person's error is shared — one common lean that pushes everybody the same direction.
(3) The independent part of the error cancels out as you add people. That's the whole engine of the theorem.
(4) The shared part doesn't cancel. It's the same for everybody, so averaging over more and more people can't touch it.
(5) So in a huge group the shared lean is all that's left over, and it decides the outcome by itself: the majority is right exactly when the lean happens to point the right way, meaning accuracy heads to 50%, not 100%.
(6) 50% is worse than the 60% you'd get by just asking one person. And if the lean points the wrong way at all, big groups are worse than useless.
(7) So any real group decision is worth less than asking one random person, and the bigger the group the worse it does. Which is nuts given what happened with our lunch poll.

I really want to keep (2), (3) and (4), they feel like the honest description of how people actually work, and I want to keep the theorem itself. So I guess I'm rejecting (5) or (6), but I can't say what's wrong with them without it sounding like I'm just refusing the conclusion because I don't like it.
