Writing a long weekend-magazine piece on the science of well-being — whether governments should really be measuring it, and what on earth they think they're measuring when they do — and before I start ringing up happiness researchers I want my own profile on pleasure written down, so I can see where it's wobbly instead of finding out mid-interview. Background that might matter: I did a psychology degree before journalism, so I'm at ease with the wanting/liking distinction from the addiction literature and I've read a fair bit of the self-report methodology; the philosophy of welfare is new to me and I'm defintely out of my depth. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I want to know if it hangs together: (1) I'm a hedonist about welfare — the only thing that makes a life go better or worse for the person living it is how it feels from the inside, pleasure and pain, and anything else matters only by causing those. (2) But I think the heterogeneity objection is basically right: there's no single qualitative feel running through the cold beer, the proof finally clicking, the joke landing, the warm bath, sex, the moment in a piece of music where the resolution arrives. There is nothing they all feel like. (3) So I've settled on the attitudinal reading as the fix: what unites the pleasures isn't a sensation but the subject being, in some sense, for the experience continuing — welcoming it, being pleased that this is happening. That's what stops "pleasure" being a gerrymandered list. (4) I want to keep a hard experience requirement though: I don't want this to quietly slide into a desire-satisfaction or preference theory of welfare. Getting what you want is worth nothing to you if you never experience it, and I want my account to say that cleanly rather than fudge it. (5) I think pleasures come in intensities and durations, that equal intensity and duration means equal benefit whatever the source, and I reject any Mill-style hierarchy of higher and lower pleasures — the old sneer about doctrines fit for pigs has never moved me — partly because I want one single scale, since the whole argument of my piece is that this stuff can in principle be compared across people and used for policy. (6) On evidence I don't want to have to choose: I think momentary affect reports (experience sampling, the buzzing-phone studies) and global life-satisfaction surveys are both decent windows onto well-being, and I'm inclined to defer to people's own overall verdict about their own lives. (7) I want the account symmetric: the heterogeneity problem is just as bad at the nasty end — itch, grief, nausea, boredom and a crushing headache share nothing — so whatever does the unifying work for pleasure has to work for the unpleasant cases too.

Some case verdicts I'm attached to and would hate to lose: the addict's rush genuinely is good for them in the moment even when they desperately wish they weren't enjoying it; the absorbed state athletes and musicians call flow is one of the best things in human life even though while you're in it, it can feel like strain and even mild discomfort; and there's something genuinely bad about the plug-in experience machine life even if the person wired in is having a wonderful time. I've also got a vague conviction that some pleasures are tainted or cheapened by what they're pleasure in — the bully's laugh, the schadenfreude — without me being able to say whether that's a remark about welfare or just about morality.

So here's the ask: go through the set above and tell me which pairs actually collide and which only look like they do, and be willing to say that some of my worries are fake. Rank the real collisions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what specifically I'd have to give up and what that would cost me — including what it would cost for the article's argument about measurement. Then tell me which single revision buys back the most consistency for the least damage. I've been turning this over for a week and every time I think I've found the weak spot it dissolves on me, so don't assume I know where the problem is. Please write it in British English, my editor is a stickler about it.
