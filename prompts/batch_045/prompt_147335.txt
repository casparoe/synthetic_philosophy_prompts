i've been asked to draft the admissions-and-membership section for a position paper that a network of european cities is putting together, and before i present it i want to know whether the bundle of things i've been arguing for actually hangs together or whether i'm quietly leaning on two incompatible ideas. here it is, roughly in the order i arrived at each bit:

(a) the reason a border needs any justification at all is coercion. border control is a standing, law-backed threat, and the people it turns away are still, in a sense, subject to it — so the justification has to be addressed to them, not just handed to the citizens who benefit.

(b) the freedom-of-association defence of exclusion doesn't work. letting someone into the country doesn't make you associate with them, and if you really can't stand the new composition you can leave; an association you can walk out of isn't one whose membership you get to veto.

(c) but a political community that has built and sustained a distinct public culture over generations is allowed to weigh that in admissions, and to run schools, language requirements and housing policy with an eye to not losing it.

(d) nobody should end up admissible nowhere. call it a right to somewhere — not to any particular country.

(e) within that, states may select on contribution: skills, language, age, ability to pay in. i don't find selection insulting in itself. i do reject selecting on descent, ethnicity or religion.

(f) the bit i'm pushing in our draft, which may be where it falls apart: membership that travels. someone who has lived and paid in here for four years and then moves abroad keeps the vote and stays on the local tax roll — an 'affiliate' status — and it passes to their children born abroad. meanwhile people who are physically here on multi-year work permits don't get the local vote for years. i like it because it ties the franchise to contribution rather than to being in the room.

(g) family reunification isn't selection at all and isn't up for negotiation: if you're lawfully present, your spouse and minor kids can't be excluded.

(h) i've set aside the 'open borders would wreck the welfare state' objection, because as far as i can tell it's an empirical claim and mostly false.

i don't know which of these is load-bearing and i don't have a favourite i'd happily give up. what i want is an audit: which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, ordered by how badly — and for each real one, exactly what would have to give and what it would cost me elsewhere in the set. i have a vague feeling that something breaks when you zoom out from one country to the whole system of them, but i can't locate it, so if that's where the real problem is, say so. and tell me whether any of it changes if the 'we' doing the admitting and excluding is a city rather than a state.
