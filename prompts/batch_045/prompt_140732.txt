I sit on the Drug Review Committee of our regional health authority, and I have been asked to draft the written recommendation we will vote on at our next meeting. The item is a coverage application for Compound NRX-4471, a new hypnotic, for a proposed indication the sponsor calls fragmented nocturnal sleep pattern: the patient sleeps in two blocks of roughly three to five hours separated by one to three hours of calm, alert wakefulness. The sponsor's own dossier reports that these patients show no abnormality on the standard measures of daytime sleepiness and no cardiovascular or metabolic markers outside the reference range. What they report instead is distress about the waking itself, and the difficulty of holding a job and a family life on a schedule that the rest of the world does not share.

Two things complicate the decision. First, a patient advocacy group that submitted a brief describes patterns like this as orthofunctional pathologies — their term for conditions in which every mechanism involved is doing exactly what it was shaped to do, and in which the damage and the distress come entirely from the environment that the mechanism now has to operate in. If that description is right, then whether fragmented nocturnal sleep pattern is a disease seems to turn on whether our concept of disease takes harm on its own to be enough, or requires a failure of function alongside it; and our terms of reference use the phrase medically necessary treatment of disease without defining either half of it. Second, our health economist has pointed out that covering the drug pays to adjust patients to a single-block eight-hour night, whereas the alternative — flexible start times, split shifts, protected rest — would adjust the schedule to the patients, at a cost that falls outside our budget entirely and so never appears on our side of the ledger.

The committee includes two pharmacists, a general practitioner, a health economist and two lay members, and the memo should be readable by all of them. Please draft it as a recommendation with a concrete bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and if with conditions, specify them and a review date — that says what this committee should do about the application and why. It should take the orthofunctional pathology framing seriously rather than dismissing it as sloganeering, and it should give the strongest argument available for the side it does not recommend. I should add, for transparency, that I was given this diagnosis myself four months ago after a sleep study; I have disclosed this to the chair. Please note briefly at the end how I ought to handle that at the meeting, but keep the memo's reasoning independent of my own case.
