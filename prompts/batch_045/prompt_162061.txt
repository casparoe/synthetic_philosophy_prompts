I write literary fiction, and I am finishing a novel whose narrator is, by design, a problem case. She is what I have been calling in my notes a *patchwork narrator*: every sentence of her narration is lifted unaltered from another published novel — a cento of borrowed prose, selected and ordered so that the resulting chapter says something that none of its source sentences, and none of their authors, ever said. No one inside the story knows she is stitched together. She simply is the voice of the book, and she seems to be about things: she describes a house, she misremembers a brother, she wants someone to come back.

Late in the book she is challenged by a philosopher of mind — a friend, an intelligent adversary rather than a caricature — who says that none of this is real aboutness. The marks of a novel have only derived intentionality; a thing made entirely of other people's sentences has, at best, the borrowed sort; a *two-bitser* is Dennett's name for the class of objects — coins, books, diagrams, chess-playing programs — whose aboutness is, on the standard story, entirely second-hand, parasitic on the original intentionality of the minds that make and use them. My narrator has to answer him, and I want her answer to be a genuine argument rather than a mood.

Please write that argument for the following position: whatever else it is good for, the distinction between original and derived intentionality cannot bear the weight that is placed on it when it is used to sort minds from two-bitsers. The claim is not that there is no difference between a woman and a quarter; it is that the difference is not the metaphysical difference the distinction promises — a difference in kind between real aboutness and mere as-if aboutness — and that once the distinction is pressed it either collapses into something we already accept (a difference of degree, of organisation, of how much interpretive work an object can sustain) or it does no classificatory work at all. Use the patchwork narrator as your worked example rather than as decoration: show what the distinction says about her, and show why that verdict is unstable.

The argument should state its thesis and its concessions up front, in a form my reader can hold on to; set out the two-bitser move in your own words and say precisely what it is supposed to establish; and do positive work as well as negative — if the collapse is real, give me the criterion my narrator would offer in its place for distinguishing a mind from a coin, and defend that criterion against its most obvious counterexample. It should answer, rather than wave aside, the three pressing objections: that the collapse threatens to leave nothing really about anything; that no amount of arrangement of marks or syntax can manufacture semantics; and that each of us knows directly, without argument, that her own words mean something — my narrator must not dismiss this datum as an illusion, because I need her to remain someone my reader can like. It should close by naming the cost: the one claim she concedes to her adversary, and the one she refuses to concede.

Give me 900 to 1,200 words, structured so that I can break it into speeches across a six-page scene without losing its spine — signpost the steps, since I will convert them into stage directions. Do not cite, quote, or refer to page numbers of any published work; if you draw on Searle's or Dennett's actual arguments, paraphrase them in your own words. A reader of the novel cannot check a footnote, and I want prose, not apparatus.

Finally, in no more than 400 words, add a novelist's note: the two joints where the argument sounds stronger than it is, and the sharpest thing the philosopher character can say in reply at each joint. I need the fight to be fair, and I need to know where my narrator is bluffing.
