I spent thirty-eight years as an accelerator physicist at a national laboratory, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a rational-choice seminar in the philosophy department here. Last week the seminar did the dollar auction — the sequential game in which a single banknote goes to the highest bidder while the runner-up forfeits his own bid as well, so that two perfectly sensible agents can end up paying far more than the prize is worth. I could not stop thinking about the Superconducting Super Collider, which I watched being built and then killed from a building two hundred metres away. I would like a careful analysis of the SSC as a dollar auction, and, more valuable to me, an honest account of where that model stops fitting.

A few ground rules first. You have no access to the internet, so please work only from the facts I give below; they are as I remember them, the figures are approximate, and nothing in your analysis should depend on their exact values. I do not want a literature review or a bibliography: name the positions and arguments you rely on by description, and I will supply references myself. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words, in prose, addressed to a scientifically literate reader who is new to the decision-theory side.

The salient facts, to save you the trouble of reconstructing them:

- The SSC was recommended by a US high-energy physics advisory subpanel in the early 1980s and endorsed by the Department of Energy; it was to be a proton–proton collider of roughly 40 TeV collision energy in a new tunnel about 87 km in circumference at a greenfield site in Texas.
- Congress was told in 1987 that the project could be completed for about $4.4 billion. Construction began in 1991. By 1993 roughly $2 billion had been spent and some twenty-odd kilometres of tunnel bored.
- Cost growth was continuous and public: a federal audit reported a large overrun against the construction line item in early 1993, and press reports that spring put the total project cost near $8.4 billion. There were also management and documentation failures that gave critics easy targets.
- The decision was relitigated annually through appropriations. In 1992 the House voted against continuing by a wide margin and the Senate voted for it by roughly two to one, and the project survived conference. It was cancelled by Congress in the autumn of 1993.
- President Clinton's public argument for continuing was that "abandoning the SSC at this point would signal that the United States is compromising its position of leadership in basic science" — note that this is a forward-looking argument about reputation and future standing, not an argument from what had already been spent.
- CERN was already developing the Large Hadron Collider, designed for a substantially lower collision energy and built in an existing tunnel previously used for its electron–positron collider. European money stayed at CERN; India pledged a modest sum; negotiations with Japan foundered on unrelated trade disputes. One documented obstacle to foreign participation was that the project had been sold domestically as a demonstration of American superiority.
- There had been a long internal argument about siting the machine to reuse existing infrastructure and staff at an established laboratory; estimates of the extra cost of not doing so ran from half a billion to well over three billion dollars.
- The machine was never finished. The LHC was built, and the United States later contributed to it and to its detectors, with American groups central to the Higgs announcement in 2012.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, and then a critique of the mapping in both directions.

First, the discipline. Say who the bidders actually were — the "United States" is not an agent, and the coalition behind each annual increment included the Department of Energy, the laboratory and its contractors, the Texas delegation, and the domestic particle-physics community, and its membership shifted over time. Say what the option set really was at each stage: continue, continue at reduced scope, pause and internationalise, let Europe build and join it later, or stop. Say what the prize consisted of, and notice that it is a strange prize for an auction: partly rival (priority, prestige, Nobel-scale credit, industrial contracts, national standing) and partly not, since the knowledge itself is something the loser can still acquire. And identify the auctioneer, if there is one: in the textbook game someone profits from the escalation, and if no such party exists here the analogy has already lost something important.

Second, check whether the trap condition holds at all. The dollar auction bites when a bidder's remaining outlay exceeds what the prize is worth to them, yet dropping out is worse still. But the SSC at 40 TeV would have been a considerably more powerful instrument than the LHC, and the physics case for it was, by the standards of the field, strong. If the remaining expenditure was proportionate to a genuinely larger prize, then this is not an auction anyone was trapped in and the retrospective verdict of "boondoggle" may be hindsight rather than analysis. I want you to take that possibility seriously rather than assume the escalation framing.

Third, three complications that I have not seen handled, and that seem to me to matter more than the sunk-cost psychology the seminar concentrated on.

(a) The buy-in option. In the canonical game the loser gets nothing at all. Here the loser of the race to build could buy a share of the result afterwards, and did. If a cheap buy-in exists, the correct first move is not to bid: let the other side build and purchase access. Does that mean the tragedy was not escalation but a failure to notice an available route, and that the escalation literature's concentration on the decision to continue blinds it to the option of not entering on better terms?

(b) The signalling problem. To obtain good terms from a consortium you may need a credible threat to build alone, and credibility costs money — a bored tunnel is a very convincing thing. On that reading the expenditure was not waste but a bargaining investment, and cancelling it was not an escape from a trap but the destruction of the only asset that was buying a seat at the table. I find this plausible and also suspicious, because it is exactly the argument a vested interest would invent afterwards.

(c) The rhetorical lock. The project was justified at home in terms of national pre-eminence, and that justification appears to have discouraged precisely the foreign contributions that would have converted the auction into a joint venture. If so, the trap was not in the payoffs but in the story the coalition had to tell to hold itself together: the framing that made the project fundable made it unsurvivable.

Fourth, tell me what any of this does to the theory. The escalation literature I have met is largely intrapersonal — self-justification, loss aversion, the desire not to admit error — plus organisational inertia, and its remedies are institutional: fix limits in advance, separate the prospective from the retrospective question, rotate the decision-makers. Test those remedies against this case. Note in particular that Congress relitigated the decision every single year, which is close to the institutional ideal of repeated fresh evaluation, and yet the project continued for years; and note also that annual appropriations mean each decision was small compared with the whole, which is itself a known engine of creeping commitment. Tell me whether the annual structure cut for or against escalation. And be clear about what the theory cannot say: I would especially like to know what observable evidence would distinguish a genuine costly signal from a retrospective rationalisation of money already spent, given that both produce the same behaviour, and whether the theory has any resources for that distinction or is simply silent there.

Finish with a last line in this exact form, so that I can extract it mechanically:

Verdict: LABEL

where LABEL is exactly one of STRUCTURAL-TRAP, SUNK-COST-ERROR, STRATEGIC-COMMITMENT, NOT-A-TRAP — according to whether you conclude that the SSC was a dollar auction in which no one needed to be irrational, that it was escalation in the psychological and organisational sense with the additional spending buying nothing, that the spending was best understood as costly commitment in a bargaining game with a rival, or that it was not an escalation case at all and the cancellation was the mistake.
