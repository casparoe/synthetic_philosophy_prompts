I teach an upper-division epistemology course and I am grading this week's problem set on higher-order evidence. Below is the question I set, followed by one student's answer in full. I would like an independent assessment of the answer before I finalize marks, so please evaluate it on its own terms rather than against how you would have written it.

The question:

Some philosophers distinguish evidence of error from evidence of unreliability. Call a higher-order defeater *pure* if it bears on the reliability of the process that produced one of your judgments but is not itself evidence for or against the proposition judged, and *impure* if it does both at once.

(a) Explain why pure defeaters are the hard case for the view that rationality consists in proportioning your attitudes to your evidence: why they appear to pull belief apart from credence, and how they threaten to leave you in epistemic akrasia.

(b) Dana is a radiologist. She reads a scan and concludes, with confidence around 0.9, that there is no tumor. Variant 1 (pure): she then learns that she was given, without her knowledge, a drug that impairs radiological judgment in roughly one case in five, produces no symptom she could have noticed, and is uncorrelated with whether a patient has a tumor. Variant 2 (impure): a second radiologist of equal skill, looking at the same scan, says there is a tumor. Say what the pure/impure distinction predicts about each variant, and what Dana should end up believing and how confident she should be.

(c) In variant 1, may Dana go on believing that there is no tumor? If she does so while judging that she should not believe it, is she thereby irrational? Answer the question about what she should believe separately from the question about what she should do, and say whether the second answer dissolves the first.

The student's answer:

(a) A pure defeater tells me that the process behind one of my beliefs is untrustworthy while telling me nothing new about whether the belief is true; an impure one does both jobs at once — it is evidence about my reliability and evidence for or against the proposition.

The difficulty with pure defeaters is that they set two demands of rationality against each other. Evidentialism says to proportion confidence to the evidence for and against the proposition. The enkratic principle says that if I believe I ought to believe p, I ought to believe p — and, in its negative direction, that I cannot rationally go on believing p while judging that I ought not to. A pure defeater leaves the first-order evidence alone, so the first demand says to leave credence where it was. But it also says that the judgment came from a process I should not trust, and that seems to make it rational to judge that I ought not to hold the belief. So belief and credence come apart: if belief is credence above a threshold, then nothing has moved the credence, yet the belief has become the sort of thing I must give up. Dana can be 0.9 confident that there is no tumor, and so count as believing it, while at the same time thinking that she should not believe it. That is the akratic combination, and it is why some conclude that one cannot keep evidentialism, enkrasia, and the claim that higher-order evidence ever defeats.

(b) Variant 1 is the pure case: the drug is uncorrelated with whether there is a tumor, so it gives Dana no new reason to think there is one, but it tells her that her reading belongs to a class of judgments that go wrong one time in five. She should therefore lower her confidence that there is no tumor from 0.9 to about 0.72, since there is a one-in-five chance that this reading is the product of an impaired process; and she should in addition treat her original justification as undercut, so that what remains is a credence no longer supported in the way it was. The two effects are distinct and they compound, which is what makes pure defeaters so damaging. Variant 2 is impure, but only in a thin sense: Dana's colleague has seen the same scan and has no evidence Dana lacks, so the disagreement is really evidence about Dana's reliability rather than evidence about the patient. She should lower her confidence in her own reading without becoming more confident that there is a tumor.

(c) She may go on believing. Belief is not under voluntary control, so Dana cannot simply drop it, and it would be wrong to call her irrational for a state she cannot change by an act of will. What she can control is what she does: order the follow-up, send the scan to a second reader, and in general act as if there might be a tumor. Once the practical question is separated from the epistemic one, the puzzle of part (a) largely dissolves. What is left — a belief she would not endorse on reflection — is psychological residue rather than a failure of rationality, since rationality is a matter of responding to the evidence, and the first-order evidence has not changed.
