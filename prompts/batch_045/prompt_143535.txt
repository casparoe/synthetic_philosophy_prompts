I'm a third-year philosophy major taking a cross-listed seminar on philosophy of biology this term, and we've just been assigned the 1979 Gould–Lewontin "Spandrels" paper ahead of the midterm. Before reading it properly I borrowed a popular book on evolutionary psychology from my housemate — one of those general-audience trade paperbacks — because somebody in our study group said its opening chapter on "the adaptationist wars" was a good orientation. That chapter summarizes the argument roughly like this:

"Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper showed that adaptationism is false. Adaptationism, as they defined it, is the view that every trait of every organism is an adaptation, shaped by natural selection for the job it now performs — the Panglossian faith that whatever exists is the best design available, so that for any feature you look at there must be a story about what it is for. Against this they argued that most features of an organism are spandrels: unavoidable by-products of building a body one way rather than another, like the tapering triangles of masonry you inevitably get when you set a dome on rounded arches in St Mark's, which were never designed for anything at all even though the mosaicists later covered them in sacred images. It follows that a trait's present usefulness is no evidence whatsoever about why it evolved, so asking 'what is it for?' is a waste of a biologist's time; what you should study instead is growth rules, allometry and developmental constraint. And since human behaviour is part of the phenotype like anything else, the paper effectively demolished the adaptationist research programmes — sociobiology first, and later evolutionary psychology — that had assumed our behaviour is a set of adaptations."

I need to know how much of that to carry into my notes and how much to discard. The last two sentences feel overstated to me: in lecture our professor said Gould and Lewontin both insisted they were not denying the power of natural selection or that organisms are well fitted to their environments, and she mentioned in passing that Mayr and Maynard Smith conceded a good deal of the methodological criticism while still treating adaptationism as the core of evolutionary thinking. She also mentioned, only in passing, that a critic later argued the architectural structures in question aren't really spandrels at all and that the term should have been something like "pendentive", and I can't tell whether that is a substantive point about the argument or a pedantic one about vocabulary. So please go through the paragraph claim by claim and separate what Gould and Lewontin would themselves have accepted, what they would have winced at as a caricature of their position, and what an adaptationist critic would call simply wrong — in particular: is the "adaptationist programme" they attacked really the thesis that every trait is an adaptation, or has the book substituted an indefensible version for the real target; does their notion of a spandrel cover any feature that wasn't directly selected, or only a narrower class, and does that difference change how strong their conclusion is; is the "no evidence whatsoever" line theirs or an overstatement of a more careful claim about the gap between current utility and historical origin; and does the sentence about sociobiology and evolutionary psychology follow from anything the paper establishes, or is it the sort of conclusion the paper is routinely recruited for without actually delivering?

The reason this matters beyond the midterm is a problem-set case we were given, which I'd like you to re-run under your corrected reading. It's invented: a species of burrowing frog in which males have greatly enlarged forearms; the enlargement is a by-product of selection for larger overall body size under an allometric growth rule and was not itself selected; but males now use those forearms to wrestle rivals in burrow disputes, and the winners get more matings. Our handout says that under Gould and Lewontin this is a spandrel and its present function is irrelevant to its evolutionary explanation. Is that a faithful application or the same misunderstanding again, and does the verdict change if the forearm has since been further modified by selection for grappling? If the corrected version of the argument and the book's version would classify some case differently, I'd love that case too, since I'd rather be able to state the distinction crisply than just say the popular book is wrong.
