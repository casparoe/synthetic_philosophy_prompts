I write novels — three of them out, all about people who run when they'd be better off staying put — so I come at this sideways. I've got no decision theory beyond what I've picked up from blogs, but I know my craft vocabulary cold, and I want a puzzle translated into it. As I understand the setup: Death is a near-perfect predictor of where you'll be tomorrow, so staying in Damascus is near-conclusive evidence he'll be there and taking the coach to Aleppo is near-conclusive evidence he'll be in Aleppo — except nothing you do drags him anywhere, his bags are packed either way. One camp says weigh your action as evidence of where Death is; the other says weigh it as a cause, and since it causes nothing, decide on other grounds. Restate that disagreement in the vocabulary I actually use — foreshadowing, dramatic irony, Chekhov's gun, what's in character for a person, an omniscient narrator who already knows how it ends, the way a plot gets built backwards from its last scene — as faithfull as you can, and then give me the residue: what carried over cleanly, what you had to bend to make it fit, and what my vocabulary just can't say. Two things I care about most. Whether the causalists counterfactual — what would happen if I did otherwise — has any counterpart in a story that's already written. And whether the gap is a hole in how novelists think about action, or a sign that the philosophers' causal influence is thinner than it sounds; there's a third thing I keep circling, the old Appointment in Samarra bit where the servant flees Baghdad and walks straight into the death he was running from, and it feels like neither cause nor evidence. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question, and keep it in American English.
