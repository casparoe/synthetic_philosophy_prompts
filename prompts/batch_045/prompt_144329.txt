I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy. I am putting together a short statement of my position on mind uploading and gradual replacement, to be handed around in advance to a reading group of colleagues — all engineers, none of them philosophers — who have been asked to attack it before we meet. I would like an audit of the position before I hand it over. I do not know where the weaknesses are; if I did I would already have revised, so please do not treat any item below as the one I am most or least attached to.

Here is the set, in my own vocabulary.

(1) Substrate independence. What makes a state of mine the state it is, is the role it plays in the causal-functional organisation of the whole system, not the material that realises it. Neurons, silicon, or something nobody has built yet: if the organisation is preserved, the mental facts are preserved.

(2) In-place incremental replacement preserves the person, and the granularity does not matter. If each unit is replaced by a functionally equivalent unit wired into the running system, taking over the causal role of the part it replaces, then I survive the entire procedure. I do not think there is a threshold fraction, and I do not think the rate matters: one unit per hour, a thousand per second, or the whole system in a single operation, provided each replacement is switched in while the system is running and nothing is ever taken offline, all come out the same way.

(3) Copy-and-delete is death. A destructive scan followed by reconstruction of a functionally identical system produces a replica, and I am gone. The same, in my view, goes for the non-destructive variant: scan me, instantiate an upload, kill the biological original an hour later. The upload was never me, and its continued existence is not my survival.

(4) Survival is all-or-nothing. I reject the line I associate with Parfit (I may be misattributing it) that identity is not what matters and that what matters is psychological continuity, which comes in degrees. Ninety-five per cent continuity is not ninety-five per cent survival. Either the candidate is me or it is not, and if it is not, then its continuity with me is no consolation at all.

(5) No residue. Whether a candidate future system is me is settled entirely by the physical and functional facts. There is no non-physical ingredient, and no brute primitive further fact of survival over and above the causal and functional story, however fine-grained you want to make that story.

(6) Implementation matters in exactly one way. A system that merely matches my input-output behaviour — a vast lookup table, or a roomful of clerks relaying signals to each other by radio in a pattern that reproduces all my dispositions — would not be conscious and would not be me, even though it realises the same input-output function. I am not sure whether this contradicts (1) or is a separate point, which is part of what I want you to tell me.

The case verdicts I have committed to, beyond (2) and (3) above, are these. (a) A hot-swap protocol in which the replacement for each unit is instantiated alongside it, tested in situ for a minute against the live traffic, and only then given the role while the original is destroyed: I survive, and this holds however fast the protocol runs. (b) A full in-place switchover to a duplicate that was built and verified in parallel beforehand, executed as one operation with the system never offline: I survive. (c) Snapshot my complete state, kill me, instantiate the last snapshot in fresh substrate: death, even if the gap is a second. (d) Instantiate two copies from one snapshot, let them run and diverge for a week, then merge them by averaging their parameters into a single system: I have no verdict, and I cannot tell whether my other commitments are supposed to deliver one. (e) I also find that I want the replica in (c), and the upload in the non-destructive variant of (3), to inherit my projects, my obligations, my savings and my unspoken apologies — and I cannot tell whether that is an extra commitment of mine, a consequence of something above, or just sentiment.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, ordered by severity rather than listed as equally bad, and for each say precisely what would have to be given up and what that would cost me. Tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and what the dissolving distinction is. Say whether the package in fact delivers a verdict on the merge case in (d), and if it does not, what the smallest addition would be that makes it deliver one. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what I lose by making it.

Two constraints. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, citations, or pointers to papers: I have no easy access to them and I want the reasoning rather than the references. And please argue in my vocabulary rather than in shorthand — if a position I have described has a standard name, name it once for my benefit, but do not let anything rest on my recognising it.
