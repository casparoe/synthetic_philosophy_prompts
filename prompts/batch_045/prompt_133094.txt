I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly AI and climate — and I keep running into a slogan I can never pin down. Latest case: last week at a conference on AI risk, a political economist on a panel got bored with a discussion of whether the extinction-risk arguments are any good, and shut it down with the old base/superstructure line: the base determines the superstructure, the whole risk debate is just what ideology looks like when a handful of firms own the compute, there's no point evaluating the arguments on their merits because the arguments aren't what's driving anything. He said it flat out, as a way of ending the conversation rather than entering it.

That's the part I want to get at, because a milder version of the claim seems obviously fine to me (you can't have a debate about AI without GPUs, capital, property law) and the version he was using seems to make the slogan unfalsifiable. Somebody in the audience also muttered a distinction I couldn't follow: something like the base explaining which ideas get selected and stick around — funding, platforms, attention, careers — versus the base explaining the content of the ideas. I couldn't get him after the panel.

Can you lay out, as a list, the different precise things the base determines the superstructure could be claiming, from the near-trivial to the wild, with a verdict on each — platitude, defensible-but-needs-an-argument, false, or self-undermining? Three places I keep getting stuck and want handled explicitly:

1. "Determines." Does it mean causes, constrains, explains, or selects for? Those seem to be different claims with different evidence required.
2. The reverse direction. Property rights, contract law, regulation — aren't those exactly the kind of thing that makes production relations what they are in the first place? So how is the causation supposed to run one way only?
3. The escape hatch. A friend says the standard move is that the base determines things only in the last instance, and that this is a joke because the last instance never arrives. Is that fair, or is there a reading of it that isn't just a way of dodging cases?

Then the bit I actually need: which of the readings would license what my panelist did — telling me the arguments in the risk debate aren't worth engaging — and which would leave him having to read the papers after all? And does that selection-vs-content distinction do any work for him, or does it quietly cut against him, since "we only explain why an idea spread, not whether it's true" isn't much of a dismissal?

Give me something I can argue with in print, not a verdict on whether Marx was right. Also: if the strongest reading of the slogan is one nobody who repeats it actually means, say so.
