I help run a small reading group that began as an offshoot of a local rationalist meetup, and we're closing our stretch on minds and machines with the Lucas–Penrose argument. Most of the group has congealed around a one-line dismissal — the Gödel sentence is relative to the system, you can't name your own program, done — and I think that dismissal is too fast: it answers one version of the argument and then quietly stops. So instead of another paper I want to close with a poem.

Write me a sestina: six six-line stanzas in loose iambic pentameter, plus a three-line envoi carrying all six end-words, the end-words rotating through the standard sestina permutation so that the same six words keep coming back in a new order forever.

The six end-words should be TRUE, PROOF, SYSTEM, MIRROR, NAME, and MACHINE. By MIRROR I mean a device we invented in the group: a program that, handed the specification of any consistent formal system, prints out that system's Gödel sentence. Such a program can be written; the diagonal step is mechanical; and the mirror can always go one better than whatever you hand it.

Let the rotation itself be the argument. A fixed rule endlessly generating apparently new sentences is the mechanist's answer to Lucas, so each stanza should be one turn of the dialectic, and the recurrence of the words should feel like the recurrence of the same six moves everyone makes:

1. The naive Lucasian picture — for any system there is a sentence it cannot prove, and I can see that it is true.
2. The mirror's reply: going one better is mechanizable, since for every system there is some machine that outputs its undecidable sentence.
3. Penrose's move that it is the seeing, not the printing, that matters — I can see my own soundness — and the price of that move, which is that I must know which system I am.
4. The standing challenge: name it. Without a name there is no sentence to outrun, and a machine could be as opaque to itself as I am to myself.
5. The scope error: from for every system I can survey there is a truth I can see, nothing follows about whether I am a system.
6. The soundness worry: real people contradict ourselves, an unsound machine proves everything, and the idealization that makes the argument valid is also what makes it not about us.

The envoi should not settle anything. I want the two claims to end up next to each other, both of them true, neither of them a reply to the other — Lucas's insight and the mechanist's mirror passing each other the way they do in every paper on this.

Someone who has actually worked through the argument should be able to tell which stanza is which move and why the order matters; someone who has never heard of Gödel should still get a poem that works. Please begin with the poem itself — no preface, no gloss on the form, no commentary afterward.
