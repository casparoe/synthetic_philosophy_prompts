I convene a working group that drafts consensus guidance for a health-data research consortium. Its members are clinicians, epidemiologists, data scientists, lawyers and two philosophers, and roughly every second meeting deadlocks over an existence question: whether a proposed diagnostic category is a real disease or a construct, whether a deployed risk model makes decisions, whether a data-sharing population is the sort of thing that can have interests. Half the room wants to settle these by stipulating a definition and moving on; the other half thinks the stipulation quietly buries the real disagreement. I have read enough of the philosophical literature on verbal disputes, quantifier variance, conceptual ethics and metalinguistic negotiation to believe there are decent tests for which side is right in a given case, but my facilitators are not philosophers, the room is not patient, and I get ninety minutes per deadlock. So I need something operational.

Please write a decision procedure my facilitators can run: the steps in order, each phrased as a question that can be asked out loud to non-philosophers, what counts as a sufficient answer at that step, and where the procedure stops with a verdict or hands the question back to the group as a values call rather than a diagnosis. Three verdicts matter to me, not two: MERELY VERBAL (dissolved once the contested expression is disambiguated or regimented), SUBSTANTIVE (a first-order or meta-level disagreement that survives regimentation), and what I will call CONCEPTUAL (the parties are disagreeing about which concept we ought to use, which is a normative dispute and must not be closed by fiat even though it is, in a sense, about language). At the core I want: a regimentation step, in which the contested term is replaced by a freshly coined one with stipulated application conditions, to see whether the disagreement survives; a check for whether either party is using the term to track a distinction it takes the things themselves to make rather than one we project, since my understanding is that agreement on all the first-order facts does not by itself establish that a dispute is merely verbal; a downstream-licensing step, asking what each party takes the existence claim to entitle them to — an explanation, a legal or moral consequence, a line in our guidance — on the ground that if the licenses differ, agreeing on usage does not settle the dispute; and, for disputes that do dissolve, a cost-of-relabeling check, because a settlement that puts the whole burden of re-description on one party's vocabulary is not one the group will honour.

Then run the procedure on two deadlocks from our last meeting, showing the answer at each step: (A) clinicians and philosophers disagreeing about whether a symptom cluster in the new dataset is a distinct disease entity or an artifact of how we code it; (B) engineers and clinicians disagreeing about whether a deployed risk model makes a decision for which someone must take responsibility. I expect (A) to come out MERELY VERBAL and could live with that. I would be annoyed if (B) came out MERELY VERBAL as well, because our consent and accountability framework is built on there being a decision-maker; if the procedure returns that, I want to see precisely which step produces it and what we would have to give up in order to keep it. Finally, tell me which kinds of deadlock the procedure cannot settle at all, and at which points the facilitator's own judgment is doing the work while appearing not to.
