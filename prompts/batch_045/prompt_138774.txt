So I read scripts for a very small production company that does mostly historical dramas, and I'm also on the committee for a modest grant that's meant to reward "responsible" storytelling about real people, and the two things together mean I spend a lot of time arguing about what we owe the actual dead when we dramatize them. We never get anywhere, so I thought I'd write down my own gut verdicts and see whether there's a real principle hiding in them. I know philosophers argue about whether the dead can be harmed at all — the old line that death is nothing to us — and I don't want to get stuck there; assume for the sake of argument that something can be owed to them, and give me the principle for the cases. My verdicts: (1) A biopic of a 1930s labour organizer (call her M.) invents a scene where she sleeps with a strikebreaker during a bad patch. It never happened, there's no record of anything like it, nobody who knew her is alive. I think it's not fine. (2) The same film invents her assistant, a fictional young woman who stands in for the dozens of unnamed women in the local. Totally fine, maybe better than picking a real one and pinning a made-up life on her. (3) A novel about a nineteenth-century naturalist where every conversation is taken from his published letters and every event is documented, but reordered so it reads as a slow loss of faith ending in a deathbed crisis — when his letters say repeatedly and explicitly that he never doubted. Nothing is false; the shape is. This feels worse to me than (1), which is strange since (1) has a fabricated event and this has none. A colleague calls things like this "frankenscenes," but it isn't a scene, it's the whole arc. (4) A real humanitarian who forged her war record and invented a rescue that never happened, and whose public life was built on it. A project wants to depict the forgery. Fine — I'd say required. (5) A documented incident where a lifelong pacifist, at nineteen, badly beat a man in a bar; everyone who knew him later says it was the thing he was most ashamed of and tells you nothing about who he was. I don't like using it. Is that consistent with (1)? Both are "here's the moment that makes them look bad," and I'm much more bothered by the invented one than the true one, which feels backwards. (6) A drama set during a real famine, every character invented, no one identifiable, but it ends with an invented friendship and a small act of mercy that makes the whole thing feel endurable. Not fine — and this seems like a different wrong from (1), not misrepresenting anybody so much as lying about the shape of the event, though I can't say why it's different. (7) A real person of whom three facts survive — born here, wrote one letter, died in a fire — so that any story is ninety-nine percent invention. Weirdly fine, or at least much less bad than (1). (8) The subject signed a note before dying: tell the whole truth about me, whatever you find, I don't care how it looks. I don't think that licenses everything, but I can't say what the limit is.

The rule I keep falling back on is something like: you may compress, you may invent minor people, you may even leave ugliness out by omission; what you may not do is add a positive detail — an event, a line, a relationship — that the real person would have refused as a description of themselves, and you may not arrange true details so that they add up to such a description either. But (4) breaks that immediately: the humanitarian would have refused the truth, and showing it is not just permitted but right. So the rule needs repairing or replacing, and I'd like to know which clause is doing the damage and what the repaired version predicts for cases I haven't listed — say, a musical-comedy version of someone's life, or casting an actor whose body is nothing like the real person's, or the thing we're now getting asked about constantly, generating a voice to say lines the real person never said. And then there's the one I actually care about and go back and forth on: my grandmother, dead twelve years, and the stretch of her life she never spoke about and my mother says I shouldn't touch.
