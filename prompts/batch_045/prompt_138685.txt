I teach a final-year undergraduate module on catastrophic and existential risk, and I am considering screening Kubrick's *Dr. Strangelove* as a set text for a session on the case for and against taking AI existential risk seriously. Before I commit to it I want a critical reading of the film through that debate, because I suspect parts of the film illuminate the argument and parts of it quietly mislead.

Please write that reading as a piece of criticism rather than a survey or a review, and take both sides of the exchange seriously: the film, with its particular scenes, tone and causal machinery, and the philosophical case, with its actual structure rather than a parody of it. I would like the reading to work through the following, roughly in this order.

First, the central device. The doomsday machine is dangerous because it works exactly as designed and cannot be recalled once triggered. In my teaching I have been using a distinction between two failure modes: the *committed* one, where a mechanism threatens catastrophe because it is too reliable, faithfully executing a commitment its builders cannot later withdraw; and the *refractory* one, where a mechanism threatens catastrophe because it is insufficiently reliable, doing something nobody specified and nobody can predict. Which of these does the film actually dramatise, and which does the case for AI existential risk need? If the film only gives me the first, is it an exhibit for the wrong argument — and is there nonetheless a reading on which its absurd chain of human error stands in for the second?

Second, where the loss of control really sits. Trace, scene by scene, the points at which the catastrophe could still have been averted and was not: the order being given, the attempts to recall the bombers, the struggle to recover the recall code, the final conversations with the Soviet premier, the last bomber. Does the film locate the loss of control at the moment of design, at the moment of delegation, or at the moment of execution? I ask because it bears directly on where an argument about AI risk should locate the intervention, and I would like the film pressed on that question rather than merely allegorised.

Third, what I think of as the villain-shape of the fable. The film's causation runs through named individuals — a mad general, a sinister scientist, a hawkish general, a strutting ambassador, a petulant premier — and every step in the chain is somebody's decision. My worry is that a parable in this shape teaches students that catastrophe requires villains or madness, whereas the arguments I most want them to engage with locate the risk in competitive dynamics among agents who are each individually reasonable, in the pressure to move first, and in the ordinary incentives of institutions. Does the film corroborate that worry, or does the bureaucratic texture of the war room, and the sheer ordinariness of the machinery, undercut it?

Fourth, what kind of catastrophe the ending depicts. Is it an existential catastrophe in the sense the literature uses — the permanent foreclosure of humanity's future — or merely a civilisational one, with the survival of a selected remnant below ground treated as a policy option? Say whether the difference matters for the analogy, given how much of the force of the existential-risk case comes from the size of what is at stake rather than from the probability.

Fifth, the comic register. Does the satire flatter the audience into a sense of superior sanity, and so disarm precisely the ordinary-incentives version of the argument? Or does absurdity do philosophical work here that scrupulous analytic prose cannot — making vivid the disproportion between the machinery and what is at stake, the gap between a decision and its consequences, the felt ridiculousness of extinction arriving through a chain of administrative steps?

Close by telling me plainly whether I should screen it, and with what framing or caveats, and by marking where the reading fails: where the analogy simply does not hold — for instance, where the danger of a nuclear arsenal depends on its being made of scarce physical stuff and on its doing exactly what its designers intended, while the central worry about advanced AI concerns agency that no one put there and artefacts that cost nothing to copy — and where the film therefore adds nothing that the argument does not already say better.

Please write in British English, in continuous prose of roughly 1,500 to 1,800 words, and do not quote the film from memory: paraphrase any line you need to refer to.
