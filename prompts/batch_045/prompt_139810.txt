I'm in an upper-level decision theory course, and we're currently doing a module on policy under "deep uncertainty" — climate adaptation planning, pandemic stockpiles, that sort of thing. Both the lecturer and the TA keep leaning on the same contrast to domesticate Keynes: risk is a roulette wheel, where you know the odds and can price the bet; uncertainty is an urn whose composition you don't know, or a wheel whose odds are printed too small to read. The moral they draw is deflationary in a specific way — they say Keynesian (or Knightian) uncertainty is nothing more than risk with a parameter missing, that Keynes was doing his best in the 1920s before hierarchical Bayesian models and robust-Bayes decision rules were properly available, and that the correct modern treatment is to put a prior over the possible compositions, widen it until it's honest, and then go on maximizing expected utility (or maximin over a set of priors, if you want to be cautious). On that reading nothing about Keynes forces us to abandon expected utility or to talk about conventions, animal spirits, and liquidity as anything other than folk psychology for a modelling failure.

My study group tried to repair the analogy, because it seemed to us to misdescribe what Keynes was actually worried about. Our proposal was to replace the urn with what one of us called a "blank-faced wheel": you know it's a wheel, you know it will stop somewhere, but you don't know how many slots it has, whether the slots are equal in size, or whether the croupier rebuilds it between spins — so that no amount of watching other people's spins tells you the chances, and there is no stable thing for a prior over compositions to be a prior *over*. We also tried to map Keynes's distinction between the probability of an argument and its weight onto the mean and the precision (inverse variance) of your estimate, so that "weight" becomes a number you can update like any other. The TA replied that a blank-faced wheel is just a hierarchical model with an uninformative prior on top and so changes nothing formally, and that if weight is really precision then weight is just another number — in which case Keynes's claim that some probabilities can't be compared, or even measured, must be an artefact of his not having the machinery, not a feature of the world.

I want to write a short essay auditing the analogy rather than just picking a side, so I'd find it useful to see the correspondence laid out explicitly: what in the wheel case plays the role of Keynes's premise-evidence relation, of the "true" probability, of a repeatable trial, of the agent who knows the odds (the croupier? Laplace's demon? the model?), of the prior over compositions, and of a convention — and then where the mapping fails, with the failures sorted into ones that matter for the deflationary conclusion and ones that don't. In particular: does the wheel picture quietly import the assumption that there is a stable generative mechanism and an ensemble of like cases, when the Keynesian cases (a single investment decision, a war, a technological future that our own forecasts help create) are precisely the ones without one? Does it import the idea that someone knows the composition, so that uncertainty is merely ignorance rather than indeterminacy? Does the "weight as precision" mapping actually fit, given that acquiring more evidence can, for Keynes, leave probability unchanged or even lower it while increasing weight — and given that he thinks probabilities are sometimes not even ordinally comparable? And most importantly: does the "just use a wide prior, or a set of priors" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the urn carrying the whole argument? If the wheel is the wrong source, say what a better analogy would be and why — and if the answer is that the deflationary reading is right for the urn cases but not the ones the policy module cares about, I'd like that boundary drawn explicitly, since that's really what my essay is about.
