I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a teaching hospital, and at our last meeting we spent most of an hour going in circles over a screening proposal that I'd like someone else to referee, because I couldn't see which of my two colleagues was making the deeper mistake. The proposal was to add a one-time genotype panel for what the protocol calls "constitutive immune priming" (CIP) to the adult primary-care intake: roughly 40% of people in our catchment area carry a variant that keeps baseline pro-inflammatory signalling turned up. I should say that I've renamed the condition and invented the trial numbers below, so please treat the whole thing as a hypothetical rather than as something to look up — and please don't spend your answer on citations or further reading; I've read plenty of the literature, and what I want is the argument itself adjudicated. The facts the committee was given were these: bearers historically had substantially lower mortality from intracellular infections, which is why the variant is as common as it is; in our present environment they carry a moderately raised lifetime risk of atherosclerosis and late-onset joint damage; and a trial of a drug that dials the inflammatory set-point down found a reduction in a composite cardiovascular endpoint but no mortality benefit, with the cardiovascular benefit appearing only in bearers who also carry a second, unrelated variant and doing nothing measurable in bearers without it. The detail that actually split the committee was a subgroup finding: a minority of treated patients reported a persistent flattening of everyday motivation and drive. Our population geneticist, Naomi, argued against the panel; our general internist, Idris, argued for it, and their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Naomi: This isn't a disease. A disease is a part's failing to do what it was shaped to do, and here the inflammatory system is doing exactly what it was shaped to do, and doing it better than average. The atherogenesis is just what you get when a system calibrated for one environment runs in another — the same reason salt conservation becomes a liability on a salt-rich diet. Screening people for a trait that is working as designed is disease-mongering dressed up as prevention.
(2) Idris: That makes disease-status hostage to which environments our ancestors happened to live in, which is an arbitrary benchmark for how a person ought to be now. Whatever fixes "the function it was shaped to perform," selection history is a contingent fact about the past, not a standard. And notice that you're leaning on the missing mortality benefit, which can't be the test either: medicine treats infertility, chronic pain and severe migraine, none of which shortens life.
(3) Naomi: The harm test has worse problems than mine. It makes disease indexical to our pharmacopoeia — if we had a perfect anti-inflammatory, everyone with any inflammatory tone would have a disease, and if we had none, nobody would — and on the statistical version of it, a trait stops being a disease purely by becoming universal, which is why your 40% figure should embarrass you. It also has no currency: the trial didn't show these patients doing better, it showed a trade, somatic risk down and experiential drive down, and "whatever the patient prefers" is circular when we are the ones shaping the preference by offering the drug.
(4) Idris: Your malfunction claim depends on how finely we carve the system. Is the elevated inflammatory tone the system succeeding at a protective response or failing at a regulatory one? At the grain of description you favour, any symptom can be redescribed as some subsystem working properly, so "malfunction" is never as neutral as you make it sound.
(5) Naomi: The grain problem is real and I'll grant it, but the fix is to admit that organisms can be decomposed into functional parts in more than one legitimate way, so that contested disease attributions are contested descriptions rather than covert value judgements. What I won't grant is that this helps you: if harm is your test, then a drug that removes the cardiovascular risk and removes motivation leaves you with no way to say whether the patient is better off without first picking a currency, and picking one is exactly the normative act you accused me of hiding.
(6) Idris: I accept the currency point as a reason for caution about screening, but it isn't a reason to say the trait isn't pathological. Being a disease and being worth screening an asymptomatic population for are different questions. It is a disease in the co-variant bearers and not in the others, and that conditionality is a feature of taking harm seriously rather than a bug.
(7) Naomi: But then pathology isn't a property of the person or of any part of them — it's a property of a genotype pair, and pairs can be regrouped indefinitely. Worse, on your account someone acquires a disease purely by learning a fact about their genome while nothing about them changes, which shows that "disease" is doing classificatory work for you, not descriptive work.
(8) Idris: I'll grant interest-relativity at the level of what we decide to do. But the harm itself — the raised risk of atherosclerosis and joint damage — is real and measurable, and your account has no way of registering it as a medical problem at all. "Working as designed" is quietly doing the work of "nothing to be done about it," and that inference, not mine, is the one that deserves to be called a normative leap.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the eight moves, rate it on a scale running from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, and in two or three sentences say what is right in it and where exactly it goes wrong. Then tell me who is more right overall and why, and — this is the part I couldn't do myself — say what both of them are missing: is there a distinction neither party deploys, for instance between a condition's being pathological, its being a legitimate target of clinical treatment, and its being worth screening an asymptomatic population for, or a way of reframing the dispute that dissolves the parts that look intractable? Finally, please be explicit about your own confidence: indicate how confident you are in each rating, and close by naming the single claim in your adjudication that you would be least surprised to have to withdraw if someone pressed you on it.
