I have recently had reason to care about the philosophy of language in a way I never did when I was only teaching it. Until last year, Wittgenstein’s later philosophy was for me a set of seminar-room moves: I taught an undergraduate unit on meaning and used the slogan that meaning is use, together with the idea that concepts like game are held together by family resemblance rather than by a single defining essence. Six months ago my mother had a stroke and was left with severe expressive aphasia. Since then I have been her primary caregiver. We can no longer rely on her producing the right word or on my guessing a private mental picture from her few sounds; we get by through routine, gesture, shared activities, and the small language games we have rebuilt together—asking for tea, recognizing a photograph, following the order of the day. Because communication has become fragile and practical rather than academic, I have found myself rewatching Denis Villeneuve’s Arrival (2016) and wondering whether the film is, despite its science-fiction trappings, a kind of extended meditation on exactly these later-Wittgensteinian themes.

I would like you to read Arrival through the lens of Wittgenstein’s later philosophy of language: language games, meaning as use, family resemblance, and the notion of a form of life, together with his resistance to the idea that a word gets its meaning from a private inner object or experience. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the film to a convenient illustration of a theory, and do not distort Wittgenstein to make him fit the movie; if a piece of the reading is a stretch, say so plainly. Since I have seen the film but cannot consult the screenplay, please paraphrase scenes and dialogue rather than quoting them, and do not give me a reading list or citations.

Please focus on three or four scenes that any attentive viewer will remember and that a reader who has not seen the film recently can follow. First, the early attempts to establish a first shared vocabulary inside the alien shell: the pointing and repetition by which the humans and the heptapods come to attach signs to persons and simple actions, and Louise’s decision to remove her protective suit in order to make contact. Treat this as a case of entering a new language game and, possibly, a new form of life. Second, the later stage at which the human researchers and the heptapods have enough of a shared practice to ask and understand non-trivial things, and the contrasting sequence in which different human communities receive the same logogram and split over whether it means gift, or weapon, or tool. I especially want you to use the idea of family resemblance here: human viewers seem to assume that the right translation must recover one essence shared by all these English words, whereas a Wittgensteinian reading will ask how the sign is actually used in the heptapods’ practice and in the human practices that receive it. Does the film vindicate the family-resemblance approach, or does the plot depend on there being a single correct hidden meaning after all? Third, the climax in which Louise uses what she has learned from the heptapod language to prevent a war, and the closing sequence in which she embraces the life she will have with her daughter even though she knows how it ends. Here I am interested in what the film does with the ethical and temporal dimensions of acquiring a new language, and in whether Wittgenstein’s distinction between what can be said and what can only be shown helps to explain Louise’s final choice—or whether the film is committed to something he would reject, namely the idea that a language can encode a private, non-linear experience of time.

Please also be explicit about the film’s official framing. Arrival openly discusses the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis and suggests that learning the heptapod language changes Louise’s experience of time, so that she seems to remember the future. A Wittgensteinian reader should be suspicious of treating that non-linear time as a private mental content that language merely expresses; part of the point of the private language remarks is to unsettle that picture. Tell me where the film invites this Wittgensteinian suspicion and where, if at all, it nevertheless relies on the very picture of inner experience that Wittgenstein wanted to dissolve. If the honest critic has to conclude that Arrival is more Sapir–Whorf than Wittgenstein, say that.

In terms of epistemic stance, I want you to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually know Wittgenstein, and not to present a contested view as the consensus. For example, it is relatively settled that Wittgenstein rejected the picture of meaning as fundamentally a matter of naming objects, that in the Philosophical Investigations he used family resemblance to discuss concepts such as game, and that meaning is tied to use within a practice. It is much more contested whether his appeal to forms of life commits him to a naturalistic or anthropological grounding for language, whether the private language remarks are best read as a substantive argument or as a therapeutic dissolution of a philosophical temptation, and whether there is any single Wittgensteinian theory of translation or of radical interpretation. Please flag those disagreements rather than smoothing them over.
