I can never tell if that's a real claim or just a way of never being wrong. The recurring case is Malthus — 1798, population pressing against subsistence, the gap closed by famine/disease/war unless people restrain themselves, that whole setup. Obviously the mass starvation didn't happen in Britain when he said it would, obviously the world now feeds way more people at much higher living standards, and yet the "right but early" line will not die. Here's my rough attempt at a distinction, which I suspect is half wrong and I want you to clean up. It feels like there are three different things people lump together when they say a prediction "didn't come true":

1. falsified: the outcome didn't happen and the mechanism isn't running at all;
2. pre-empted: the outcome didn't happen *because* the mechanism was running — the pressure itself called forth the thing that relieved it, so the prediction was blocked by its own approach;
3. not yet: the outcome didn't happen because some precondition the theory needed was never in place, and the clock hasn't run out.

The thing I actually want explained is the conceptual difference between (2) and (3), and between (2) and straight-up falsification. Specifically:

- What would have to be true for "pre-empted" to count as a partial vindication of the theory rather than as an excuse bolted on afterwards? What distinguishes a genuine pre-emption story from an ad hoc immunization?
- Is pre-emption structurally more available to some kinds of prediction than others? A forecast of an eclipse can't be pre-empted by people hearing about it, but a forecast of famine or of a bank run can, and in one of those the belief in the forecast *causes* it. Explain what does the work there, and what it means for a theory that its own truth is what stops it from being observed.
- The nasty bit: a theory that predicts doom *by a date* can be pre-empted, but a theory that predicts doom *eventually* can never be, because there's always more future. So is "eventually" doing any work at all, or does it make the claim unfalsifiable? And does adding a ceteris paribus style "tendency" qualifier make it worse — is a claim about a tendency that is also self-negating falsifiable in any sense, even in principle?
- Last one: what happens to a theory's credit when the thing that blocked the outcome isn't the escape hatch the theory predicted? Malthus's own escape was moral restraint — later marriage, fewer births out of deliberate restraint. What actually happened looks different: societies got richer and birth rates fell anyway, plus there's the reverse causal arrow that gets brought up, where more mouths also mean more hands and more minds, and scarcity itself pulls innovation forward, so population pressure raises the ceiling rather than just banging into it. Is that "the mechanism working via an unforeseen route," or is it a different mechanism that happens to block the prediction? Is there any principled line between "the theory was right and the escape was foreseen in outline" and "the theory was wrong and it only survives because something unrelated saved us"? Can two mechanisms — pressure lowers the ceiling via fixed land, pressure raises the ceiling via induced innovation — both be true, and what does that do to the net prediction?
