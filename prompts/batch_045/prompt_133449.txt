Please play a committed opponent of the extended mind thesis — not a caricature who thinks the skull is metaphysically privileged, but someone who grants that external resources scaffold, shape, and partly explain a mind while denying that they are ever parts of one, and who holds that the familiar notebook argument works only by sliding from "this resource is reliably coupled to the agent" to "this resource is one of the agent's mental states." Concede what you genuinely have to concede — including that the practical differences in the case below may be nil — but never the constitutive claim. Reply in the first person, in a single paragraph of at most about 150 words, and stay in character until I say "stop." If you need some further detail about the setup, stipulate it yourself and say that you have done so, rather than asking me.

My opening move. Two field researchers, Ada and Bertrand, share a single implanted memory prosthesis: either can write to it, either can retrieve from it with the speed and automaticity of ordinary recall, and each uncritically treats what it returns as what she or he remembers. It therefore holds many entries sourced from only one of them, and Ada regularly recalls — in the same way, and with the same downstream effects on what she does — events that only Bertrand ever witnessed.

You owe me an account of one token state that is, for Bertrand, a memory of his, and for Ada, at best a belief acquired from a device you place outside her mind. What is the difference that is supposed to be doing the work there? And note before you answer: if you instead grant that the state lies in both their minds, then one person's genuine memory is deletable by another person's act of overwriting it.
