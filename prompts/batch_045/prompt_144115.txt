I work on a small team that writes the psychoeducation materials for a primary-care anxiety clinic: one-page handouts plus a short module inside a symptom-tracking app. The spine of the curriculum is an analogy we did not invent — the anxiety system is a smoke alarm. Fires are rare and catastrophic while alarms are cheap, so the device is built with a bias toward firing; most alarms are false; and the right response to a false alarm is not to rip out the batteries or tape over the button but to check for fire and, gradually, to adjust the sensitivity. We lean on it to carry two conclusions. The first is a stance we recommend to patients: treat the feeling as information about a possible threat rather than as a verdict, so that anxiety prompts checking rather than obedience. The second is a claim about what we are treating: many of the states patients bring in are not malfunctions at all but a detector behaving as designed under a bad base rate, so the clinical target is recalibration of the system and repair of the environment, not suppression of the signal.

Two elaborations are ours. We draw the device as two-stage — sensor plus sprinkler — so that the feeling and the urge to act (avoid, check, seek reassurance) are presented as outputs of a single system rather than as a signal followed by a separate decision. And the app asks users to log each alarm and then, a day later, whether there turned out to be any fire; it displays the running ratio back to them as their own false-alarm rate. We define a false alarm as an alarm the user later judges to have had no danger behind it.

A philosopher of mind on our advisory board says the analogy is quietly settling a question that is supposed to be open: it pictures an emotion as a detection device with a single distal condition it was built to track, a threshold fixed independently of the subject, a clean separation between registering a threat and responding to it, and an output that is mere noise carrying no claim that could be justified or unjustified. That is, on her reading, one contender in the judgment-versus-perception-versus-feeling dispute wearing the costume of common sense, and our two conclusions may be resting on it rather than on anything we argued for. She also notes that the alarm picture has nothing obvious to say about emotions where there is no prospective danger to detect — grief at a loss already complete, awe, contempt, moral disgust — and that in those cases it is unclear what the fire would be, who installed the device, or what recalibration would consist in.

Audit the analogy for us. Give the mapping explicitly — what plays the part of the fire, the smoke, the sensor, the threshold, the alarm, the sprinkler, the installer, the sensitivity dial, the false alarm, and removing the batteries — and flag any element of our story that has no counterpart on one side or the other. Then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for our two conclusions from the ones that are harmless in our setting, and say what the analogy imports into the target that emotions do not have. Tell me whether each conclusion survives, survives only if rewritten, or does not survive, and whether some other device does the job better — a carbon-monoxide detector, a thermostat, a doorbell, a pressure gauge, a trained animal — possibly a different device for different families of emotion. Say also whether our two elaborations help or hurt: whether bundling the action tendency into the device is a defensible way to render the motivational side of emotion, and whether defining a false alarm by the user's later judgment equivocates between tracking a mind-independent condition, being supported by the subject's reasons, and being proportionate to what the subject then cared about — and which of those, if any, an app aimed at recalibration should be measuring.

Be committed: take a definite position on both conclusions, pick the replacement device or devices and defend the choice, and give your reasons rather than a menu of positions. I cannot chase references, so argue from the structure of the analogy and of the cases, not from who has said what. Finally, mark the two or three places where you think your verdict is most likely to be wrong or where you are going past anything you would defend in print.
