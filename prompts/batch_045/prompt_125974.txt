Nineteen months ago I donated a kidney to my younger sister. The surgery and recovery were unremarkable and I have never regretted it, but the experience left me with what I took to be a settled conviction: that organs should be given and not sold, and that putting a price on donation would damage something I care about. Since the spring I have been sitting on the eight-member lay advisory panel of our provincial organ-donation agency. We do not make decisions; we write short opinions, and the agency usually follows them. Next month we are being asked to opine on a proposal that has made me unsure what my own conviction amounts to, and I want to work out, before the meeting, what consistency with our own record actually requires of us.

Our opinions to date, with the one-line note recorded beside each vote:

1. Cash payments of roughly a day's wage per donation to plasma donors at collection centres — approved. Note: renewable tissue, no lasting cost to the donor.
2. A fixed, non-negotiable, government-set fee for living kidney donation — rejected, 6–2. Note: does not record a reason.
3. Reimbursement of gestational carriers for documented expenses and lost wages — approved. A fee for the work of carrying a child — rejected.
4. Reimbursement of living organ donors for travel, childcare and lost wages, capped and documented — approved.
5. Payments to healthy volunteers in early-phase drug trials, scaled to time and discomfort but deliberately not to the risk assumed — approved.
6. A proposal to let patients make a charitable contribution to the transplant foundation in exchange for additional points on the deceased-donor waiting list — rejected unanimously. Note: allocation should answer to need.
7. Modest cash incentives offered by the health authority to dialysis patients who attend their prescribed sessions — rejected, 5–4. Note from the majority: it treats adherence as something to be bought. I was in the minority.
8. A manufacturer raised the price of an off-patent, single-source, life-saving drug roughly forty-fold overnight — statement issued condemning this and recommending a windfall-profit cap. Slightly outside our remit, but we were asked.

The proposal now in front of us is a Family Recognition Benefit: three thousand dollars, paid by the agency to the family of every deceased person from whom organs are actually recovered. The amount is the same for everyone, regardless of income, region or how many organs are taken; it is not negotiable; it is framed explicitly as recognition of the family's contribution and assistance with funeral costs rather than as a price; and its authors point to consent rates in jurisdictions with comparable schemes as their main argument. We also have a second file, twice deferred: whether hospitals may sell surplus umbilical-cord-blood units that would otherwise be discarded to private biobanks, at a fixed price, with nothing going to the mother.

I do not want a survey of the literature on repugnant markets, and I do not want a list of the standard objections with their labels attached. What I want is applied to our record:

1. Set out the two or three principles that a panel with this exact voting record could be running, stated sharply enough that each one generates a definite prediction, and say for each which of the eight votes above it fits only by straining, and how.
2. Say what each principle predicts about the Family Recognition Benefit, and what the operative consideration is in each case.
3. Give your single best guess about what we should say, with the reason for preferring it — a reason that does not simply restate my conviction that donation should be a gift.
4. Name one further case, which we have not voted on and which our agency could actually put to us in the next year, that would best separate the two leading principles, and give each principle's prediction about it.
5. Say whether the cord-blood file is settled by whichever principle we adopt for the Family Recognition Benefit, or whether it raises a genuinely separate question. And if our precedents underdetermine the Benefit, state plainly what we would be committing ourselves to by voting either way, and which precedent is the closest analogue.

Please keep the whole answer under a thousand words.
