I am putting together the final session of a graduate reading group on non-classical treatments of inconsistency, and I want to close with a diagnostic instrument rather than another survey: a decision tree that takes a participant from what they are willing to say about concrete cases to a named position, together with a taxonomy of the space the tree carves up. I would be grateful if you would build it. The audience is students who have first-order logic and truth tables and nothing beyond that: they can answer any question about a case, but they cannot answer "which logic do you accept?", and no node may presuppose a label.

What I want, concretely.

1. A tree of ten to twelve branch nodes. Every node is a question about a concrete case, phrased so that someone with no exposure to the literature can answer it and so that the answers genuinely divide the positions rather than the vocabulary. Twelve to sixteen leaves. The root should itself be a case — I would suggest whichever case you think most cleanly separates "this is only a defect in our information" from "something in the semantics or the consequence relation has to give" — and you should say in one sentence why you chose it.

2. These cases must appear, though you may add others: (a) the sentence that says of itself that it is false; (b) a borderline instance of a vague predicate, e.g. a man at the boundary of baldness, and then the disjunction saying that he either is or is not bald; (c) a revenge variant, e.g. a sentence saying of itself that it is not true; (d) a legal code in which one valid statute requires something and another valid statute forbids the very same thing; (e) two sources of equal reliability, one asserting a proposition and the other its negation, merged into a single database with no further evidence either way; (f) an inconsistent but predictively successful scientific theory, such as the old quantum theory or the early infinitesimal calculus; (g) a case where it looks as though you ought to do something and also ought not to do it, all things considered; (h) an inference from a disjunction and the negation of one disjunct, where there is also independent but questionable evidence for that disjunct; (i) a set-theoretic or Curry-style paradox where the culprit might be the conditional rather than negation.

3. Each leaf carries three tags. First, which classical principle it surrenders, if any: explosion, excluded middle, non-contradiction in one of its formulations, disjunctive syllogism, a structural rule such as transitivity or contraction, or none. Second, whether its central claim is about truth, about consequence or validity, or about what we should accept, assert, or believe. Third, whether classical logic still holds in consistent domains on that view — wholesale revision, local restriction, several correct consequence relations at once, or no fact of the matter because the choice is stipulative.

4. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what would separate them if anything could. I am fairly sure one such pair will be the glut logic that tolerates no gaps and the logic that tolerates both gaps and gluts; if the only thing that distinguishes them is behaviour on revenge sentences, say so rather than manufacturing a node.

5. Mark the combinations of answers that no actual position occupies, and diagnose each: incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. I would rather have empty cells with a reason than a padded tree. In particular, tell me where a leaf is really an engineering stance about managing inconsistent information rather than a claim about truth — whether reasoning paraconsistently over a contradictory corpus carries any commitment about true contradictions is the question my group actually cares about, and I want that made explicit in the notes. If a "chunk and permeate" style answer for case (f) deserves its own leaf rather than collapsing into the instrumental stance, argue for it; if not, say why not.

6. Two of my working assumptions to check rather than accommodate. First: my draft treats the gap side and the explosion-blocking side as a clean fork, because if nothing is ever both true and false then a conjunction of a sentence with its own negation is never satisfied and ex contradictione quodlibet follows for free; on that assumption no gap theory is paraconsistent in any interesting sense and the two families sit on separate branches. Someone has told me this depends on the definition of validity — preservation of truth versus preservation of non-falsity — and that on the second reading a Kleene-style gap theory blocks explosion after all. Settle this, tell me whether it changes the shape of the tree, and if the honest answer is that it depends on which notion of validity one adopts, tell me which notion my handout should fix and why. Second: my draft has the glut-theoretic position and the paraconsistent one as a single leaf, on the ground that anyone who says some contradictions are true must reject explosion and anyone who rejects explosion must think some contradiction might be true, so no case could pry them apart. If that is wrong, give me the case that does pry them apart, in language a student who has read nothing can answer; if the difference is real but is a difference in what one says about truth versus what one says about inference, or about consistent versus inconsistent domains, say so and tell me how to represent it in the tree.

Format: the tree in a compact schematic I can photocopy onto one page, then at most a page of notes covering the flags, the empty cells, and the two checks. Where you name a position or a system, give the usual name, but make the description do the work — the group should be able to follow the handout without recognising any name.
