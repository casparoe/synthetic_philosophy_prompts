I run a little rationality-adjacent film night — six of us, mostly people who came out of the LessWrong/ACX orbit and who have now spent something like two years intermittently arguing about Gödel, Escher, Bach in our group chat — and next month we're doing Arrival (the 2016 Villeneuve one). About half of us have also read the Chiang novella it's based on, so feel free to draw on either, and I'd actually like to know if you think the film and the novella come apart on the specific point below. Here's the angle I want to try. Hofstadter has that bit in GEB about where meaning lives — frame message, outer message, inner message, illustrated with things like a message in a bottle and the problem of an intelligent civilization decoding a record it has no context for. As I rember it: the frame message is just this is a message, decode me; the outer message is everything you have to already know or figure out in order to decode it (what code, what conventions, what shared context); the inner message is the thing actually being said. I want to read Arrival as essentially a two-hour film about the failure and eventual repair of those three levels, and I have a pet thesis I want stress-tested rather than humored.

My thesis: Heptapod B — the semasiographic written language, as opposed to the spoken one — isn't a message with any content at all. It's a pure outer message, a piece of decoding hardware that installs itself in whoever learns it, and its entire payload is the change in the reader. On that reading the heptapods didn't come to tell us something; they came to upgrade our receiver, and the thirty-three-hundred-year "we'll need your help later" line is, like, an afterthought bolted on so that humans have something inner-message-shaped to argue about. Is that clever or is it just a category error — does Hofstadter's scheme survive a case where the outer message is the whole point, or does it break (and if it breaks, is that interesting or just a pun)? Connected sub-questions I want handled seriously: the offer weapon versus use weapon episode is the obvious set piece, and Wikipedia's plot summary says the Chinese reading is plausibly downstream of them having made contact through mahjong, i.e. through a maximally competitive game — so is that a pure outer-message failure, where everyone in fact decodes the inner message correctly and still dies because they're reading it inside a zero-sum frame? Same question for the Russian team's there is no time, and for the one-twelfth-of-the-message-split-across-twelve-shells business, where the inner message is literally unrecoverable unless the nations acquire the outer message first. Also: Ian's Fermat's-principle scene — causal versus teleological formulation of the same physics — looks to me like the cleanest case in the film of one inner message under two different outer messages, and I want to know if that's right or if it's just a physics fact with a metaphor stapled to it. And the film's own frame (the opening images of Hannah, which turn out to be premonitions) plays a frame-message prank on us: we decode them as memory, which is exactly the mistake the movie is about. One of our group also insists the aliens' motive is basically acausal trade with extra steps; tell me if that's a useful frame or a distraction.

Where I most need you to push back: I don't want to flatten the film into a diagram. So (a) tell me where this lens distorts what Arrival is actually doing — my worry is that the Louise-chooses-to-have-Hannah-anyway material, and the whole free will versus knowing-the-future thing, isn't really a decoding problem at all, and that reading it as one turns the ending into a logic puzzle instead of a grief. If you think the honest critic has to say the lens runs out there, say so plainly. (b) Give me the two or three scenes you'd actually point the group at in the room, with what you'd say about each — I'm leading the discussion, not writing an essay, so I need things that will generate an argument among six people rather than a clean summary they'll all nod at. (c) If there's a place where the film knows something about meaning that Hofstadter's three-level story doesn't have a slot for, that's the thing I'd most like to walk away with. And please skip the bibliography or further-reading list: we're not going to look anything up and I'd rather you spend the words on the reading itself.
