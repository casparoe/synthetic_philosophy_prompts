I'm a journalist working on a feature about methodology in the science of consciousness — specifically about what a researcher is entitled to do with the things a subject says about their own experience. Let me fix the terms so that we're arguing about the same thing. The method Dennett calls heterophenomenology asks the investigator to take up a third-person stance: treat everything a subject says — together with their behavior, their neural measurements, and anything else publicly checkable — as a text to be interpreted, and assemble the subject's heterophenomenological world, the world as their reports depict it, while remaining officially neutral on whether those reports are true. The advertised virtue is that the method neither credulously trusts nor preemptively dismisses introspection, and so yields an objective science of consciousness without first-personal premises.

The familiar complaint — that this is behaviorism with better instrumentation — I take to be largely a misfire, since the method admits anything a subject can say or do rather than confining itself to movement. So please don't spend the piece on it.

The difficulty I want handled is one I'll name myself. Call a candidate phenomenal difference *shy* when it satisfies three conditions: (i) the neural or functional difference that would, on the hypothesis under test, realize it is stable and independently measurable; (ii) it shows up in the subject's reports only as noise — absent spontaneously, absent under prompting, not recovered by discrimination training, and at most faintly visible in the usual second-order probes such as confidence ratings or post-decision wagering; and (iii) consequently, the two hypotheses — this is a difference in experience versus this is a difference with no experiential signature at all — predict identical heterophenomenological records: same protocols, same latencies, same neural data. Note that this is not the philosophical fantasy of a full behavioral duplicate of a human being; it is a local, small-scale underdetermination of exactly the sort that actually arises in work on near-threshold and unattended vision, where reports are known to be noisy and training-sensitive. That locality is what makes it awkward.

The position I want argued for is this: heterophenomenology's neutrality claim is best defended, and quietly reformed, as a calibration norm governing an instrument of finite resolution — the report channel — rather than as a metaphysical thesis about what consciousness is. On this reading the method's output is not a verdict on which differences are phenomenal but a resolution-limited map of reportable differences, published with explicit error bars, in the way an image from a microscope is published together with the diffraction limit that bounds it. Argue that this reconception does genuine work on three fronts:

1. It gives neutrality a formulation that does not beg the question against the realist about experience. The investigator is not denying that there might be facts about experience beyond the reports; she is declining to let an uncalibrated channel bear evidential weight it cannot carry.
2. It answers the shyness problem without collapsing into eliminativism. An instrument of finite resolution is not blind to everything; it is blind below a threshold. The right response to a threshold is to characterize it, report it, and where possible build a second channel — not to announce that the region below the threshold is empty.
3. It answers the worry about the investigator's own case. The experimenter's introspective vocabulary does enter into the interpretation of subjects' reports, and her own reports are never themselves heterophenomenologized by anyone else. On the calibration reading this is not a scandal and not a regress to be terminated by fiat: the experimenter is part of the instrument, calibrated against other experimenters in the way any instrument is cross-checked.
