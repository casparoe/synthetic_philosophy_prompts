Slightly random request. I help run a local time bank — you do an hour of childcare or gardening, you earn one time credit, and you can spend it on an hour of anybody else's labour, whether that's plumbing, benefit form-filling or guitar lessons. Every hour is worth exactly one hour. We've got an ongoing argument about whether that's actually fairer than just paying each other in cash, and one person reckons the scheme is basically lifted from Aristotle: exchange needs a common measure, he thought coin was only a conventional stand-in for that measure, so we're nearer the real thing than the cash economy is. Someone else said that's rubbish, since an hour of one person's work plainly isn't worth an hour of another's.

I've got to talk the volunteers through it next week and I'd like to make the first case as strongly as I can.

Could you argue that a scheme like ours comes closer to meeting Aristotle's conditions on just exchange — the reciprocal proportionality bit in book 5 of the Nicomachean Ethics, with need doing the work he says holds a community together — than ordinary money-priced exchange does? Two objections I'll definitely get, so please handle them: first, that market prices at least track how much people want a thing, whereas pegging the rate at one hour ignores differences in skill, training and how grim the job is; second, that his distinction in the Politics between running a household and the business of getting wealth should make him suspicious of anything that's really about racking up credits for yourself.

No citations or quotes needed — I rememeber the passages roughly, I just want to see the argument written out so I can judge whether it survives being said out loud. Plain English, no jargon I'd have to define. Around 500 words.
