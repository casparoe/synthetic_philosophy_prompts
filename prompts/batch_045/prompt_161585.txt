I TA for an intro ethics course, and this week's section on Glaucon's challenge in Book 2 of the *Republic* went sideways. The section leader introduced the ring of Gyges by saying it is basically Tolkien's One Ring: you put it on, you become invisible, nobody can punish you or even blame you, and you end up doing whatever you want — which shows, as Glaucon wanted it to, that people are just only because they are watched. One student pushed back. In Tolkien, she said, the Ring does not merely hide you; it works on you, feeding on and inflaming whatever the wearer already wants, which is why the characters who refuse it refuse it on the grounds that using it even for good ends would turn the user into a tyrant. On that reading, the One Ring does not test Glaucon's hypothesis at all: it is a different experiment, like testing whether people steal after you have dosed them with something that makes them want to steal. The section leader replied that this is Glaucon's point as much as Tolkien's — real impunity is not a one-off change in your circumstances but a change in the kind of person you become, so a ring that removed the risk of being caught while leaving your character untouched describes a case that never arises, and Glaucon's setup smuggles in the very thing the experiment was supposed to be testing, namely a stable character that does not depend on what enforcement is around it.

A third student offered a version I found more interesting than either: the real analogue is not invisibility but unlogged access — an admin account whose actions leave no audit trail, or a throwaway identity online. He said this is the honest version of Glaucon's experiment, and he used it to draw the opposite conclusion: people with unlogged access mostly do ordinary, mildly self-serving things — bending rules, taking small advantages — but not the seduction and murder and usurpation Glaucon predicts, so Glaucon's claim that the just man and the unjust man would end up behaving identically looks false, and the magical ring was doing the work all along.

I would like an audit rather than a verdict on who is right about justice. Give me the correspondence table for each of the three versions — what plays the role of invisibility, of the removal of sanctions, of the agent's unchanged desires, of the just and the unjust man, of the kingdom Gyges takes — and then sort the disanalogies: which ones matter for the specific job Glaucon's ring has to do (isolating whether justice is valued for its own sake, and whether its origin is a compact among people who cannot wrong each other with impunity) and which are incidental staging. Two questions I especially want addressed. First, is the character-eroding reading of Tolkien's Ring a defect in the analogy or a discovery about Glaucon's setup: can the experiment even be specified without assuming that preferences and dispositions survive the removal of external enforcement, and what happens to the challenge if they do not? Second, does the unlogged-access version actually deliver evidence about Glaucon's claim, given that its users are not fully invisible, that most of what they can do is trivial, and that Glaucon's prediction concerns what you would do when literally nothing is at stake for you? If none of the three is a good model of what Glaucon needs, propose a better one and say what it buys that these do not.

Please flag your own confidence as you go: tell me which claims hold on any reading of Plato or of Tolkien and which turn on contested interpretations, and mark the places where you are extrapolating. And if anything in my framing is confused — if the question of whether two thought experiments test the same hypothesis is messier than I have made it, or if the disagreement above is not really about the analogy — say so plainly rather than answering the question as posed.
