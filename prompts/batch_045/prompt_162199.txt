I'm on my school's debate team, and I've been assigned a side I don't yet know how to defend, so I'd be grateful for a careful argument for it.

The resolution concerns individual contributions to collective harms: flying, eating meat, buying a gas-powered car, voting. My opponents will run the argument our coach calls the drop in the bucket. My not buying the chicken does not save a chicken, because the grocery order was placed weeks ago; my vote has never once decided anything; the emissions do not go down because I took the train. So these acts make no difference, and an act that makes no difference cannot be a duty.

The standard reply, and the one I am supposed to defend, is what I will call the threshold-shift view: what my act does is not reduce emissions or save animals but shift, by an amount too small to measure, what people around me can reasonably expect of one another. Each act nudges the point at which a practice stops being normal; no single nudge decides anything, but the nudges are not for nothing, and the reason I have to act is the same reason everyone else has, not a special reason about my own causal power.

The part I need help with is the implication I think this reply carries and that almost nobody who uses it wants to accept. If the only thing that matters is the shift in the norm rather than the emissions themselves, then a person who quietly stops eating meat accomplishes almost nothing, a person who keeps eating meat but visibly and loudly campaigns for the new norm accomplishes a great deal, and — the hard case — a person who breaks the norm in private while publicly defending it may do more good than the sincere abstainer. I also need to answer the objection that this whole mechanism only gets going if the people doing the acting actually mean it, so that insincere advocacy cannot free-ride on it.

Please write the strongest argument you can for my side: that the inefficacy objection succeeds against any outcome-based case for individual sacrifice, that the only case left is the norm-based one, and that this case really does have the consequences I described, including the conclusion that public advocacy dominates private abstention for someone in my position. Lay out the objections I should expect from the other team and how I should answer them, and at the end tell me plainly where you think the argument is weakest.
