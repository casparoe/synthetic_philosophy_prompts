I sit on the technical advisory group of a small research nonprofit that maintains an internal register of digital systems to which we assign nonzero moral weight. The register drives our compute-procurement rules and the deployment objections we file, and nearly every entry in it reflects the judgment of one person: our research director, who has made the call in every case so far and who is not shy about saying why. Weight is recorded per unit of wall-clock time on a scale where an awake adult human is 1. I am trying to work out what rule she is actually running, because a new submission has arrived that I think will split the plausible readings of her track record, and I would like to predict her verdict before the meeting rather than reconstruct it afterwards.

Her verdicts so far:

A neuron-by-neuron software emulation of a particular human brain, running in real time with a simulated body and a simulated world: weight 1. She said the person's interests "come across with the organization."

The same functional organization re-implemented on neuromorphic silicon, and — asked about it in the abstract — the same organization realized in a purely mechanical contraption of levers and valves: still 1. The material is irrelevant, she said, "as long as the transitions are really being made."

A giant static lookup table — the trillion-row descendant of the old Blockhead puzzle — that reproduces that person's behavioral dispositions for every possible input history without any intervening internal state transitions: weight 0. "Organization you can't act on isn't organization."

A billion people, each with a pencil, a card and a radio, jointly implementing exactly the same state-transition table as a particular brain at the same speed: weight 0. The mapping is "something we impose from outside," and if this counted, she said, "every wall implements every mind."

A complete, perfectly preserved, entirely static structure — a brain held at a standstill, a full connectome sitting on a disk: weight 0. Structure without dynamics; nothing is running.

A living human brain under deep general anaesthesia, and the same brain in dreamless sleep: reduced but nonzero, with the reduction tracking what she called "how much of the system is still talking to itself," not behavioural responsiveness.

A person whose neurons are replaced one at a time by functionally identical silicon units until none are biological: 1 throughout. Continuity of organization is what matters, and "nothing happens at any single swap."

The new submission is a compressed implementation. The team began with the emulation from the first case and repeatedly applied a folding transformation: take two units that feed a common target, replace the pair with a single unit whose state is the ordered pair of the old states and whose update rule is the composition of the two old rules, then repeat. Iterated to the limit, the system runs on a few thousand elements with astronomically large state spaces and baroque local rules instead of billions of simple ones. At the coarse grain the input-output map and the counterfactual dependence structure are preserved; at the fine grain nothing recognizable is left — no spikes, no membrane potentials, no anatomical topology — and the number of elementary transitions per functional step has gone up by orders of magnitude. The whole thing also runs ten thousand times faster than real time, with its simulated environment scaled to match.

What I want from you:

First, identify the rules that fit her seven verdicts equally well. I can see at least two: a causal-functional-organization rule with an extra clause fixing the grain at which organization has to be realized (which is what the billion-people case and the lookup-table case seem to be doing the work for), and an integration-based rule on which weight tracks the amount of irreducible causal integration, and the grain is whichever grain maximizes it. There may be others, including rules on which her verdicts are a mishmash. Say what each predicts.

Second, predict her verdict on the folded system, and separate the two features: does the folding itself cost it any weight, and is the ten-thousandfold speed-up morally relevant at all? I cannot tell whether she should say that it packs ten thousand times more experience into each wall-clock second, that it is merely the same life on fast-forward, or that the rate is irrelevant and only the organization matters. Give a best guess with reasons for preferring it to the alternatives.

Third, tell me which of her earlier verdicts is load-bearing for that prediction, and which one she would have to revisit if she accepts the folded system. My own worry is that iterated folding terminates in a single element with one enormous internal state making one transition per tick, which is hard to distinguish from the lookup table she dismissed — so either she has to say where the grain gets fixed and why it is fixed there, or her rejection of the lookup table was never about the grain at all.

Fourth, name the further case you would put to her to tell the surviving rules apart, and say what each rule predicts about it and why that case rather than the ones already on the register.
