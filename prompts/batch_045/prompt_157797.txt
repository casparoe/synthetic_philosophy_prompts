I'm giving a paper at a military ethics conference hosted at a service academy in about six weeks, and the room is going to be mostly uniformed: JAG officers, a couple of LOAC instructors, a chaplain or two, and a brigade commander who'll sit in out of politeness. Also two revisionist just war philosophers have been flown in to comment, and everyone in the room knows those two think the moral equality of combatants is basically a superstition that got laundered into legal doctrine. I need Q&A prep, not a rehash of my argument.

Quick background on why I care: my younger brother enlisted last spring, infantry, straight out of high school, and dinner at my family's house has been unbearable since — my granddad was a conscientious objector in Vietnam, my other grandfather flew transport, and they've been at each other about this for forty years. The paper defends combatant equality while conceding the revisionist starting point: I grant that liability to defensive killing is individual and responsibility-sensitive, and that no collective fact about who fought a just war does any work at the level of persons. My move is that the typical combatant in a modern interstate war still isn't liable, for a different reason — what I call the manifest-authority condition. Roughly: what matters isn't whether the war is in fact unjust or whether he believes it is, but whether the political process that sent him gave him adequate reason to treat participation as morally optional — whether the injustice was legible to him through the channels he actually has, given that he's been handed a chain of command, a training pipeline that rewires his reflexes, a legal review he isn't entitled to read, and peers whose lives depend on him showing up. I argue the condition fails on both sides in nearly every real war of the last century, so equality holds as a fact about our institutional circumstances rather than as a deep principle. The case I lean on hardest is a conscripted sniper in a war everyone in his unit knows is a sham: I claim even he isn't liable, because the alternative makes liability depend on how much independent moral self-education a nineteen-year-old owes his own government.

What I want is the eight or so objections I'll actually get from that room, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would really say it — the JAG officers care about whether this gives their soldiers any usable guidance and whether it exposes them to prosecution, the LOAC people care about the gap between legal combatant privilege and the moral question and will probably say the law is what's teachable and I shouldn't destabilize it, the chaplains care about moral injury and what it does to a kid to be told he's been murdering people, the commander cares about discipline and whether my view makes selective refusal respectable, and the two philosophers will press the sniper case and ask why this isn't just the old epistemic argument with new vocabulary. For each: a reply I can give in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and an honest verdict on whether the objection has real force or I can brush it off. Tell me what to concede in the first two minutes before anyone asks — my instinct is to concede up front that the view offers a platoon leader exactly nothing operationally, but tell me if that's a self-own. And I definately want help with the reciprocity objection I'm dreading from the LOAC side, the one where they say that if we teach soldiers the other side's fighters are murderers we destroy the reciprocal restraint that gets our own people taken alive instead of shot; that's the one I think actually bites and I dont have a good answer. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and flag the single reply that has to land or the paper doesn't survive the session.
