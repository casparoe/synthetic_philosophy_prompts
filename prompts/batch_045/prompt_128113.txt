Two people at a department dinner got into an argument about habitat destruction and wild animal suffering, and I've been turning it over ever since. Roughly:

(1) Maya: most wild animals are small, extremely fecund, and die young, so if their lives are net negative then a hectare of intact habitat is running a big ongoing welfare deficit, and preserving it starts to look like a bad idea. Clearing it cuts the number of lives being lived on that land. So on welfare grounds we should at least be uncertain rather than horrified, and conservation money may be doing more harm than good.

(2) Theo: but actual habitat destruction is rarely sterilization — it's conversion to edge, scrub, and early-successional habitat, which is exactly what generalists and high-fecundity species thrive in. So the headcount per hectare can go up while species richness crashes, and per-capita welfare gets worse too: less cover, more starvation, more predation exposure. Worst of both worlds. It's an empirical mess, so you can't make "destruction reduces suffering" your default.

(3) Maya: the empirical mess cuts my way too. If you grant the arithmetic, the prescription isn't "degrade habitat however you like," it's convert it thoroughly or leave it alone — half-degrading is the worst option. And even if per-capita welfare drops, if it stays negative, cutting numbers still wins. Your position needs wild lives to be net positive, or the drop in numbers to be tiny, and neither is something anyones confident about.

(4) Theo: you're treating conversion as permanent, and on the timescales that matter it isn't. Cleared land regrows; abandoned farmland goes to scrub and then forest, and succession is the high-birth-rate, high-death-rate regime par excellence. So a one-off conversion doesn't cancel the flow of suffering, it prepays it: a pulse of extra births and deaths now, roughly the same steady state later. It's a suffering loan, not a cancellation — and if you discount the future at all, a bad trade.

(5) Maya: two things. Cities and actively farmed land don't un-convert themselves, so permanence is fine for the cases that matter. And your "loan" framing hides a population-ethics choice: the animals in the regrowth wouldn't otherwise exist, so if not being born doesn't harm anyone, the pulse of suffering counts and the averted lives don't — which makes conversion look better than your framing, not worse.

Tell me how confident you'd be in each judgment and where you'd want data before committing.
