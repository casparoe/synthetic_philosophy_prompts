Hey — I've been reading about Malthus on my own for a while now (no classes, just wikipedia rabbit holes and a used paperback I got for four bucks) and I keep hitting the same move and I can't tell if it's legit or if it's a scam. Someone predicts population outruns food supply, the famine doesn't show up on schedule, and instead of saying the theory was wrong they say it was *right but early* — the pressure is building, the model just had the timing off, the checks are coming. I sort of see why that's suspicious to people who care about falsification and I sort of see why a long-run prediction deserves some slack. What I want is something that makes me feel where the line is.

So: write me a letter. An annual letter to investors from a hedge fund whose entire strategy is the Malthusian thesis, sent in a year where the thesis obviously has not paid off — global hunger down, crop yields still climbing, fertility collapsing below replacement across the rich world, nothing resembling a Malthusian crisis anywhere on the horizon. The fund has to explain, in writing, to people who can redeem, why it is still right. Around 700 words.

The thing I really care about: the comedy has to come from getting the defensive moves right, not from making the fund sound stupid. I want the actual rescues that real Malthusian-adjacent people make — redefining carrying capacity around whatever resource is newly scarce this decade (phosphorus, topsoil, aquifers, pollinator collapse, pick your poison and make it sound actuarial); re-weighting the model toward a different "check" now that famine didn't show up; pointing out the original prediction was conditional on everything else staying put so it was never falsified, merely not yet triggered; extending the horizon, because the thesis is a long-dated instrument and "early" is not the same as "wrong"; arguing that the Green Revolution doesn't count as a refutation because it was a one-time subsidy from cheap fossil fuel and so is itself a borrow against the future; the move where the twentieth century becomes the anomaly and the nineteenth the rule. Give them a name like "Malthusian Capital, LP — Long-Dated Theses" and let them have invented a metric (a carrying-capacity utilization ratio, a headline misery multiple, whatever sounds like something a fund would actually put in a deck) and a named clause for each escape hatch, e.g. a Fossil-Energy Exclusion Clause. If a footnote about Boserup belongs in there, put it in.

Some constraints:

- Keep it deadpan. No winking, no "little did they know," no punchline where the fund admits it's full of it. A reader should be able to tell what's being made fun of from the shape of the letter alone.
- Nothing in the letter should be something an actual smart Malthusian couldn't say. I want the satire to be fair. Ideally someone who's sympathetic reads it and thinks: yeah, that is the move I would make, and then feels a bit sick about it.
- At least one line where the fund is honest in a way that's worse for them than lying would have been — the kind of candour that's in the letter to build trust and quietly sinks them.
- By the end I should have actually felt the methodological question (when is "right but early" a fair defense of a long-run prediction, and when has the rescue become ad hoc?), but the fund should never break character to editorialize about it. Let the structure do it — say, by rolling the horizon forward another century, or by announcing a new fee for "Malthus Timing Risk" charged to investors who redeem before the thesis matures.
- Don't cite anything or hand me a reading list, and don't assume I know any philosophy-of-science vocabulary, because I don't. If a concept is needed, have the fund define it in its own words, in passing, like it's explaining a position to a client.
- Oh, and if I've got some detail about Malthus wrong (I think he softened the whole thing in later editions by adding moral restraint as a check?) don't worry about it, it isn't load-bearing for the bit.
