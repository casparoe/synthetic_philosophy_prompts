I work as a software engineer on an internal decision-support service, and at a design review last week a colleague on the platform team pushed for what she called a collect-then-decide policy: before the service issues any recommendation it should pull in every signal it can cheaply get, whether that is telemetry, feature-flag metrics, or third-party data, on the grounds that extra information can never make a decision worse. When I pushed back she appealed to something called Good's theorem, which I had not heard of before and which I have since tried to reconstruct from the whiteboard: if you are an expected-utility maximizer with a fixed probability model and a fixed utility function over acts and states, then conditioning on a free observation before you choose cannot lower your expected utility, because for each possible observation the best act given that observation is at least as good as the act you would have chosen without it. Her claim is that this settles our design question. I have no formal philosophy background, I am not confident I have reconstructed the result correctly, and I came away with the sense that the theorem is doing less work in her argument than she thinks it does.

Please argue for my side: that Good's theorem cannot support the practical maxim that a decision-maker, or a decision-making system, should always gather more information before acting. I do not want a general survey of the value of information; I want the argument built out of three situations that seem to me pervasive in the systems we actually build, and for each one I want the specific premise of the theorem that fails named explicitly. First, the guarantee is denominated in the agent's own credences: take a deployment decision where shipping the new configuration pays plus 2 if it is better and minus 1 if it is worse, keeping the old configuration pays 0, and our prior is fifty-fifty, so shipping is best with expected value 0.5; now suppose we additionally get, for free, a dashboard metric that our model treats as 80 percent accurate but which is in fact independent of the state, because it tracks a confounder nobody conditioned on, and we adopt the policy of shipping when the metric looks good and otherwise staying put. My own arithmetic says this drives our real expected payoff down to roughly 0.25, and I would like you to check that arithmetic and then say exactly what the theorem's non-negativity claim does and does not tell us in this case. Second, the observation is not a pure information event: seeing the signal can remove options we valued, because we can no longer honestly say that we did not know or publicly commit to a course of action we would otherwise have stuck to, or it can change what the remaining options are worth, and in either case the step that compares the best post-signal act against the blind act is not comparing like with like. Third, the world talks back: when the counterparty also sees the signal, or sees that we looked, and best-responds to what we do with it, I suspect a case can be built in which the uninformed version of us can guarantee a strictly positive payoff whatever the other side does, while a perfect public signal drives that guarantee to zero; if so, please write out the payoff matrix explicitly and do the arithmetic, and if that particular construction fails, replace it with one that works.

Two more things. The strongest reply available to my colleague is that all three cases are merely scope violations: she never claimed the theorem covers bounded agents, agents with belief-dependent or menu-dependent payoffs, misspecified likelihoods, or strategic settings, and for an idealized Bayesian facing a fixed single-person decision problem the result stands untouched, so my examples show only that real systems are messy. Please meet that reply head on rather than ignoring it, and in doing so address the worry I cannot shake, which is that the theorem starts to look close to vacuous: given its antecedents the conclusion follows in about one line from the fact that the maximum over acts is at least the value of any particular act, and whenever the antecedents fail the theorem says nothing at all, so it looks less like a reason to go out and collect information and more like a bookkeeping constraint that forces your attitude toward learning-then-choosing to line up with your attitude toward choosing-now. Please also say plainly if you think one of my three cases is a bad example, or if I have misstated the result itself. Keep the whole thing to roughly 700 to 900 words, give me no citations or suggestions for further reading, and wherever you use numbers, show the arithmetic.
