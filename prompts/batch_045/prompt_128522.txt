I'm in the final year of my philosophy BA and I run the revision sessions for our seminar on contemporary moral philosophy. The instructor has offered to let me draft one of the questions for the take-home final (ninety minutes, closed book, no readings to hand), together with the answer key, and I want to use it on contractualism and the aggregation problem. The trouble is that everyone in the seminar can already do the standard thing: they can state that a contractualist assesses complaints one at a time, that the strength of one person's reason cannot simply be added to another's, that this is supposed to be a feature rather than a bug, and that it is why contractualism gets the intuitively right answer in the usual case where one person is seriously hurt so that a very large number of people can each avoid a trivial inconvenience. A question that lets them say that and stop is a question that marks everyone the same, so I need something that forces them past it.

The case I have in mind came out of an argument in the library with two people from my study group, both of whom I thought answered too quickly. A research station of a hundred people has to get through a hundred cold nights. On any given night they can run a generator: the person on duty that night loses sleep and endures a painful shift, call it minus ten, while the other ninety-nine are warm and comfortable, call it plus one each. Or they can leave it off, and everyone gets zero that night. Take the one-shot version first: tonight only, run it or not. That is the familiar structure, and the contractualist answer is supposedly not to run it, because the duty person's complaint is stronger than any single beneficiary's complaint about missing out on plus one, and the ninety-nine complaints cannot be stacked. Now take the sequence: the same choice on each of a hundred nights, and we get to decide the rota in advance. If we rotate, each person is on duty exactly once and is a beneficiary ninety-nine times, so each person's net position across the sequence is plus eighty-nine; if we never run it, everyone is at zero. Every single person is better off under the rota, nobody at all is left with a complaint, and yet what happens on any individual night is in no way different in kind from the one-shot case. So the question I want is, in effect: are the one-shot verdict and the sequence verdict both defensible, and if not, which one goes, and what precisely has to give for it to go?

A good answer has to do several things I'd like the marking scheme to name explicitly. It has to say what the restriction on summing complaints actually comes to and then show that the two cases are structurally identical, since the sequence is just the one-shot repeated with the identity of the burdened person permuted, so the difference in verdict cannot be explained by the numbers. It has to take a position on whether aggregation within a single life over time is legitimate, and if it says yes, identify what makes it legitimate when aggregation across lives is not, rather than just asserting that one is fine and the other is not; I'd expect the interesting move here to be something about there being a single subject whose life it is, or about only one person having the whole run of experiences. It has to say what the costs are if the contractualist instead refuses aggregation within a life as well. It has to address whether the right verdict is allowed to depend on how the decision is carved up into occasions of choice, night by night versus as a hundred-night plan, and why that sort of sensitivity to description is or is not objectionable. There is also a trap I want flagged: some of the stronger students will reach for an ex ante or veil-of-ignorance version of contractualism, and the key should say what that move actually accomplishes here, since as far as I can tell it accomplishes nothing, there being no uncertainty in the case, and adding uncertainty about who will be on duty on a given night would just reapply the same restriction to expected complaints. Finally, I'd like one point that is genuinely A-grade only: whether the distinction between episodes and whole lives survives if personal identity is not a deep further fact, and if it does not, whether the contractualist is left with utilitarianism as the only non-arbitrary stopping place or whether there is a third option I haven't thought of.

So what I need from you is: the exam question itself, written so that it is fully self-contained and states the case and both versions of it completely, since students will have nothing in front of them; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words; and a marking scheme out of twenty-five, with five or six must-hit points, the marks each is worth, one line on how candidates typically get each one wrong, a short note on what separates an A answer from a B, and a note on anything that should earn zero. Please don't put in citations, a bibliography, or a reading list: we haven't assigned anything beyond the seminar's central book, and the question has to be answerable from the framework plus the case alone.
