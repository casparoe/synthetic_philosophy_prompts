I'm seventy-one, retired health physicist — thirty-five years of dose assessment and ALARA optimization, mostly on the civilian side — and for the last three years I've been sitting in on a moral philosophy reading group at the university near where I live, which is the first philosophy I've read since a compulsory course as an undergrad. They've asked me to present next month on where I've actually landed, so I'd like a consistency check before I stand up in front of them. Sorry if this runs long; I'd rather you recieved the whole mess than a tidied version of it.

Here's roughly where I seem to have ended up:

1. Wrongness is contractualist in Scanlon's sense — an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject — and what draws me to it is that the justification has to be made to each person one at a time rather than by adding everybody up. That fits something I believed my whole working life: the individual dose limit is there precisely so that no single person gets traded away inside a collective-dose calculation in person-sieverts.

2. But I also think the numbers really do count, and not only as tie-breakers. If the choice is between one technician taking a near-certainly fatal acute dose during a spent-fuel transfer, or doing the transfer in the way that adds an extra lifetime fatal-cancer risk of about one in a hundred thousand to the roughly three million people downwind — thirty-odd expected deaths against his one — then I think you have to take the second option, and it would be wrong not to. I can't get myself to say his complaint wins there.

3. And yet I don't want to say that's because total suffering is the currency; becoming a straight utilitarian at seventy-one would be a bit humiliating. So I need some other account of why the numbers count, and I don't have one.

4. I think complaints have to be assessed ex ante, by expected burden at the moment of decision, not ex post — afterwards you can never tell whose cancer came from whose exposure, and it seems to me mad that the rightness of the decision should depend on that.

5. I think there's a de minimis floor: an individual risk below roughly one in a hundred thousand per episode generates no complaint at all — not a very weak complaint, no complaint. Plus a rider I made up because without it the floor seemed monstrous: you can't keep imposing such risks on the same people indefinitely. Call it the repeat-exposure clause.

6. And I want to keep both of these: that it's wrong for the sheriff to frame an innocent man to stop the mob burning down the town, and that it's plainly required to impose tiny unconsented risks on everyone in the ordinary public-health way — fluoridation, speed limits, permitted discharges from a facility. They feel completely different to me. But every time I try to say why, both come out as "impose on A for the sake of B," which is the same shape.

So what I want is an audit, not a lecture: which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and for each real tension what would have to give and what it would cost me. Rank them by severity rather than handing me six equal-sized problems. I don't have a favourite to sacrifice and I genuinely can't see which one is load-bearing, so please don't assume I'd quietly drop any particular one. The thing I keep going round in circles on: whatever it is that makes (6) come apart feels like it might also hand me (2) for free, and I can't tell whether it does or whether it just looks as if it does.

Practical notes: American English please — I spent a couple of years in Oxford in the eighties and I keep drifting into British spellings, and the group takes the piss. Don't give me a reading list, citations, or names to chase up; I can't easily get to a university library and I want your own reasoning, not directions to somebody else's. And assume I know the physics — explain the philosophy.
