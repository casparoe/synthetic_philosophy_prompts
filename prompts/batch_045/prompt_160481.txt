I'm writing the final paper for a seminar on the ethics of food, and I have got stuck on one step of the argument. I would be grateful for a careful comparison of two proposals for fixing it.

The background step is the least-harm argument as we have been discussing it: if what matters is the total suffering my diet causes, then I should count not only the animals slaughtered for meat but also the field animals — rodents, birds, rabbits, insects — killed by tillage, harvest and pest control on the cropland my diet requires. Since ruminants can convert grass that humans cannot digest, pasture-raised beef needs very little cropland, whereas chicken needs a great deal for feed, and pulses need a fair amount directly. On a naive death-count, the least-harm diet can therefore turn out to include beef and to exclude chicken.

My worry is that the number of deaths is the wrong unit, and I have been given two rival replacements for it.

Proposal A, the occupancy baseline. A hectare is never empty. The counterfactual for pasture is not bare ground but a wild herbivore guild plus the predators and parasites that track it; the counterfactual for cropland is not zero field animals but a population whose members mostly die young and painfully. So I should count netted deaths: deaths caused by agriculture minus deaths that would have occurred on that land anyway. Applied consistently, this seems to shrink both the crop-death bill and the beef bill, and it makes the comparison depend on the ratio of farmed-animal numbers to displaced wild numbers.

Proposal B, the foreclosure metric. What matters is not that an animal died but how much welfare-weighted life it lost: roughly, the welfare range of the animal multiplied by the remaining life-years it would have had under the best feasible alternative available to it, multiplied by the expected net hedonic value of those years. A cow slaughtered at two years forecloses years of a large-brained animal's life; a broiler forecloses weeks; a field mouse killed by a combine forecloses a short wild life that may well be net negative; an insect forecloses days, if it has a welfare range at all.

Please take the following stylized figures as given for the exercise — they are simplified and invented, so do not correct them. Per million kcal delivered to the consumer: pasture-finished beef, 4 cattle slaughtered, negligible cropland, 0 field vertebrates, 60,000 insect deaths; chicken, 400 birds slaughtered, 0.15 cropland-hectare-years, 10 field vertebrates, 2 million insect deaths; pulses, 0 slaughtered, 0.4 cropland-hectare-years, 25 field vertebrates, 5 million insect deaths. Assume a single consumer, a fixed diet for one year, calories and land both held fixed, no offsetting donations, and grazing on marginal land that could not be cropped.

In about 800 words, compare A and B as decision procedures for the practical question of what this consumer should eat, and tell me which one I should adopt in the paper. Please address, among whatever else strikes you as relevant: whether netting should be done in deaths or in suffering; what each metric implies if wild lives are on balance not worth living; the replaceability problem, since the animals killed for meat or by harvest would not otherwise have existed at all; how each handles the deep uncertainty about insect sentience; whether either metric is vulnerable to being gamed by choosing its counterfactual; and whether either one collapses into a conclusion its own defenders would reject, such as that paving habitat is a welfare improvement or that crop deaths are a benefit to the animals killed. I do not need citations or further reading, and please do not spend your word count on the empirical accuracy of the figures — I want the conceptual comparison, and a verdict on which currency is the less misleading one.

Please end with a one-sentence summary line beginning Verdict:, and after it a final line of exactly the form \answer{occupancy}, \answer{foreclosure} or \answer{neither}.
