A long-form magazine article I read recently introduces illusionism about phenomenal consciousness roughly like this: the view, associated with Daniel Dennett and Keith Frankish, is that consciousness is not real — what we call experience is a user-illusion produced by introspection, which represents our perceptual states as having ineffable, intrinsic, qualitative properties that nothing in the brain actually has. On this view there is no hard problem of consciousness, only the puzzle of explaining why we are so convinced that there is one, which is a task for cognitive science rather than philosophy. Nor is the view especially radical: it is simply physicalism taken seriously, since once you grant that everything is physical you have to grant that qualia are nowhere to be found in the physical world. The article then raises what it treats as the decisive difficulty: an illusion is itself something it is like to undergo, so explaining consciousness away as an illusion reinstates the very thing it was meant to remove. It concludes that illusionism implies we are all zombies, creatures with no inner life at all, and that since each of us knows with certainty that we are conscious, this is a price no one should pay — which is why most philosophers have set the view aside.

I had encountered the view before and came away with a different impression, namely that illusionists want to give up phenomenal properties rather than consciousness as such, so I suspect the piece is unfair somewhere, but I cannot locate where it stops being accurate. Please go through it claim by claim and separate what a sympathetic but careful illusionist would accept, what is a tolerable simplification for a popular audience, and what is straightforward misrepresentation. Two points I especially want addressed: whether the closing objection about illusions needing to seem like something is being presented as a refutation when it is really something the view offers an answer to, and whether the framing of illusionism as the default commitment of any physicalist is one that physicalists who reject illusionism would themselves reject. Finally, tell me whether the corrected version of the summary still leaves the view with a genuine burden, or whether the article's difficulties were the only ones worth taking seriously.
