I run a small rationality reading group — people who mostly arrived through the sequences-era blogs and have since drifted into different corners — and we have got ourselves stuck on underdetermination. Not on whether it exists, but on which of several different things is being claimed when someone says that the evidence underdetermines the theory. I keep watching the same word do two incompatible jobs: on the one hand, a debunking move, where underdetermination is supposed to show that scientific consensus is only ever social; on the other, a license to hold a favored hypothesis at low cost, on the grounds that no evidence anyone could gather would separate it from the mainstream alternative, so priors may settle the matter. Before we can argue about whether either move is fair, I want an instrument that sorts a person by what they think about cases rather than by which label they would claim, and I want it as a decision tree rather than as prose.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: give me the branching questions first, the combinations of answers they permit, and only then the named positions located inside that space. These are the distinctions I want to do the work:

(a) Weak versus strong underdetermination — underdetermined by the evidence we happen to have, or by any evidence we could in principle obtain — with a node that cleanly separates "no intervention we can currently run" from "no intervention that is physically possible."

(b) Whole theories versus individual hypotheses, and whether Duhem-style holism about auxiliary assumptions is what generates the slack in the first place.

(c) Exact empirical equivalence versus merely fitting everything currently on hand, including the point associated with Laudan and Leplin that sharing the available evidence is not the same as being empirically equivalent, since novel auxiliaries and novel predictions keep arriving.

(d) Whether the extra-empirical tie-breakers — simplicity, conservatism, scope, unifying power — count as evidence about truth or only as pragmatic reasons to pick one for the lab; and, separately, whether the residual spread in posterior probability across the equivalent class is decision-relevantly large. I have been calling this the half-life of underdetermination: how long the slack lasts and how much credence it actually redistributes.

(e) The source of the slack: holism about auxiliaries, unconceived alternatives in the style of the new induction from the history of science, or incommensurable and paradigm-relative standards of what counts as evidence — including the variant on which interests and values legitimately break the tie and that is no defect.

I want the entry node to be a case I have been using, and I would like it repeated in some form downstream. Two fitted models — two trained nets, or two climate models, if you prefer — agree on every observation either has been checked against and on every intervention anyone has actually run, but they disagree about the outcome of an intervention nobody has run and, for practical purposes, cannot yet run. Is that a case of underdetermination at all, and if so, of which kind?

Leaves I want represented, with the caveat that you should call one "unnamed" and describe it in a line if you are not confident the label is standard: the view that underdetermination is real but local and temporary; the unconceived-alternatives pessimist who thinks the historical record shows we are systematically missing better theories; the holist who takes the slack to be global; the incommensurability position; the constructive-empiricist-flavored position on which the tie is genuine but only belief about observables was ever at stake, so nothing is lost; the Bayesian response on which empirically equivalent theories have identical likelihoods, so priors and simplicity do the work and there is no rationally unresolved slack; and the value-laden pluralist who thinks the tie is broken by interests and that this is fine. Add whichever leaves the logical space supports that I have not listed.

Constraints on the nodes. No node may ask about a label or a school; each must be a concrete case or a plain claim a thoughtful person could answer cold, in two or three options, without knowing where it leads. Keep it to six to eight branch nodes and no more than ten leaves. Identify at least three combinations of answers that the questions permit but that nobody occupies, and say briefly why each is empty — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate rather than inventing a question to pry them apart; I suspect the transient-underdetermination leaf and the unconceived-alternatives leaf are hard to distinguish, and I would like to know whether the difference is real or verbal. For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment and one on what would actually change that leaf's holder's mind.

I do not need citations, reading lists, or page references; name positions only where you are reasonably confident the name is the usual one. If a decision tree is the wrong shape for this material, say so and give me the taxonomy version instead, with each branch labeled by the claim that splits it from its siblings.
