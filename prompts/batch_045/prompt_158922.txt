Two people in my board game group got into it on Sunday and I've been going back and forth on it since. Nadia said lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism is incoherent. Her argument: the view says pleasure and pain are commensurable — one hedonic scale, pluses and minuses you can add up — and also that once pain passes some severity threshold, no amount of pleasure can outweigh it. But if they really sit on one scale with finite magnitudes, then for any pain there's some number of pleasures that adds up to more, so the threshold rule is a stipulation bolted onto a measurement assumption that contradicts it. Commensurability or a threshold, pick one.

Sam said she's conflating two things. Commensurability is about whether the quantities share a unit and can be compared; lexical priority is about the shape of the ordering you put on them. You can measure both in the same units and still refuse to trade at any finite rate — a lexicographic ordering over one dimension is perfectly representable, it just makes the aggregation discontinuous at the threshold.

Nadia conceded that and switched. Her second point was about mixed experiences: the pleasure of scratching an itch, of a cold drink when you're overheating, of a horror movie being scary. Those pleasures seem partly *constituted* by the discomfort they relieve or play against, so you can't first weigh the pain lexically and then add the pleasure, because how big the pleasure is depends on the pain being there. Sam said that's an objection to summing moment-by-moment hedonic values, not to the threshold: apply the rule to whole episodes or whole lives — compare lives worst-suffering-first, then by net — and relief cases never come up as counterexamples, since nothing in them is anywhere near the threshold.

Third, Nadia went for the cliff. Suffering intensity is continuous, so take two experiences differing imperceptibly, one a hair under the threshold and one a hair over. Her view says it's better to impose ten-to-the-thirty cases of agony at 99% of the threshold than to permit one case at 101% — the first pile is commensurable and can be outweighed, the second can't be outweighed at all. An arbitrarily small difference in one experience flips the verdict on an arbitrarily large pile of others. Sam said vagueness at a boundary is the price of any threshold view, that it still gets the central cases right, and that it was only ever meant as a rule for avoiding the worst outcomes, not a complete ranking of worlds.

Then the one I followed least well: Nadia said that if above-threshold suffering can't be traded off at all, expected-value reasoning breaks — you can't weigh a tiny probability of torture against a certainty of enormous happiness, so either the view gives no guidance under uncertainty or it has to switch to minimizing the probability of the worst outcome, which then ignores how bad that outcome is when it does happen, and pushes you toward spending everything on shaving a risk from one-in-a-billion to one-in-a-trillion rather than relieving a million people's actual severe-but-sub-threshold suffering. Sam said that's just maximin, which is a respectable decision rule on its own, and that the view can rank lotteries worst-tier-first and then by probability of that tier.

I couldn't tell who was getting the better of it and I honestly don't know which side I'm on. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say which points are actually good and where someone is equivocating or deflecting — and then, separately, what both of them seem to be assuming without defending?
