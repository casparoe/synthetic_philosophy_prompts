**The question**

A driver sets out along a motorway with two identical, indistinguishable exits. He cannot tell the two intersections apart, he cannot remember whether he has already passed one, and he cannot count. At each intersection he must choose between EXIT and CONTINUE. If he exits at the first intersection his total payoff is 0; if he exits at the second it is 4; if he continues at both he drives on to the end of the motorway and gets 1. These payoffs are the whole of what he cares about: no discounting, no cost of driving.

(a) Suppose that before setting out he settles on the plan: at each intersection, exit with probability *p*. Write the expected payoff of this plan as a function of *p*, find the optimal *p*, and give the resulting expected payoff.

(b) Assume from now on that he has settled on the optimal plan from (a). When he finds himself at an intersection, what probability should he assign to its being the first? Show the reasoning, since the number is what the rest depends on.

(c) Using that belief, compute the expected payoff of EXIT and the expected payoff of CONTINUE. In evaluating CONTINUE, assume that if he reaches a further intersection his behaviour there is again governed by the same plan — he has no way of binding his later self to a different action. Say what your numbers imply about whether the plan is one he will in fact carry out.

(d) His expected payoff evaluated at the intersection exceeds the expected payoff he assigned to the plan before setting out, even though he has learned nothing he did not already know he would learn: he was certain in advance that he would find himself at an intersection, and certain that he would be unable to tell which one. Say in a few sentences what does the explanatory work here — a genuine acquisition of information, the failure of some principle connecting earlier and later expectations, a change in what is being averaged over, something else — and give the reconciliation.

**The answer**

(a) If he exits at the first intersection (probability *p*) he gets 0. Otherwise (probability 1 − *p*) he reaches the second, where he exits with probability *p* and gets 4, or continues with probability 1 − *p* and gets 1. So the expected payoff is

E(*p*) = (1 − *p*)[4*p* + (1 − *p*)] = 1 + 2*p* − 3*p*².

This is a downward-opening parabola, so its stationary point E′(*p*) = 2 − 6*p* = 0 is the unique maximum: *p* = 1/3, with E = 4/3. The plan beats either pure strategy, since always exiting gives 0 and always continuing gives 1.

(b) He always reaches the first intersection and reaches the second only with probability 1 − *p* = 2/3, so the expected numbers of visits stand in the ratio 1 : 2/3, and

P(first | at an intersection) = 1/(1 + 2/3) = 3/5.

Equivalently: of the 5/3 intersections he expects to encounter, one is the first. It is not 1/2, because the two visits are not on a par — reaching the second is contingent on having continued at the first.

(c) EXIT: with probability 3/5 this is the first intersection and the payoff is 0; with probability 2/5 it is the second and the payoff is 4. Hence

E(EXIT) = (3/5)(0) + (2/5)(4) = 8/5.

CONTINUE: if this is the first intersection (probability 3/5) he will now reach the second, and since continuing is only worth doing on the understanding that he takes the second exit, he will exit there and collect 4; if this is the second (probability 2/5) he drives on to the end and collects 1. Hence

E(CONTINUE) = (3/5)(4) + (2/5)(1) = 14/5.

Continuing therefore strictly dominates, and the same reasoning will apply again at the next intersection, so the driver continues at both and collects 1 rather than the 4/3 the plan promised him. That is the paradox: the ex ante optimal plan is not implementable, because at the point of action the agent always prefers to deviate from it.

(d) The explanation is ordinary conditionalisation, and no special apparatus is needed. On arriving at an intersection the driver has learned that he did not exit at the first one, an event whose prior probability was 2/3, and conditioning on it raises his expectation from 4/3 to 8/5 exactly as Bayesian updating on any other piece of good news would. The 4/3 figure is thus not the value of the plan at all once he is in the middle of executing it; it merely averages in the 0-payoff branch that by then he knows has been avoided. The right number to work with is the 8/5 he computes at the intersection, and the discrepancy between the two standpoints is only the familiar difference between evaluating a plan from outside and evaluating it from within — not a failure of any principle about how expectations ought to evolve over time.
