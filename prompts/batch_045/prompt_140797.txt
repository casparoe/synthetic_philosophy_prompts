Our little maths-and-philosophy reading group did twin primes last night and I've been chewing on it ever since. The book we're working through says, more or less: think of the primes as a lottery. Every whole number n buys a ticket, its chance of coming up is 1/log n, and the draws are independent. Then it goes on to say we already know there are infinitely many twin primes — the expected number of pairs below x works out at roughly x/(log x)^2, which runs off to infinity, so all that's missing is a proof, and anyone who says we've no idea is being wilfully obtuse.

I can't tell how much of that conclusion is actually coming from the picture, so: can you audit it? Start with the correspondences — number ↔ ticket, being prime ↔ winning, 1/log n ↔ whatever it corresponds to, independence ↔ whatever it corresponds to — and tell me what plays the role of the lottery machine, i.e. what in this setup is supposed to be doing the randomising, given that whether 561 is composite isn't a chancy matter at all. Then the breaks, and please seperate the ones that matter for the use she's making of it from the ones that don't. The ones I can see: a real lottery is independent by design, that's what the machine is for, whereas here independence is just being presumed; and the picture gets the constant wrong — she doesn't mention this but someone in the group did, the version with the product over odd primes folded in gives about 1.32 times as many pairs. And there was something about short intervals where the model isn't merely off by a constant but wrong in a more interesting way — I've half remembered that bit so don't trust it, tell me if it's nonsense.

What I really want though is the bit about how you'd write the presumption down. My mate's fix: don't presume primality events are independent, presume independence only after you've divided out everything you already know — n is odd, n and n+2 can't both be multiples of 3, and so on up through the small primes — and presume independence of whatever's left over. He calls it residual independence. Is that an actual constraint or just a redescription with extra steps? In particular: is there a principled place to stop dividing out (which primes count as "small"?), does the rule as he's stated it deliver the 1.32, and does it touch the short-interval problem or is that failure of a different kind — missing structure rather than missing a correction factor? And is the presumption a presumption about events or about variables — does it say the same thing if you redescribe the very same facts in terms of gaps rather than individual numbers?

Then the thing I want to take back to the group: does the "we already know" survive? If a heuristic argument resting on a presumption we know to be locally false can still be the best guide we've got to whether a conjecture is true, what exactly is doing the work — and what do we say to the person who reckons it's just dressing up a guess in a probability distribution? If there's a better picture to put in its place, one that gets the local obstructions and the clumping in without smuggling in a randomiser, I'd take that as well.
