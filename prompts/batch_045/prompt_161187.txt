My friend Sam did his PhD in logic and now spends an unreasonable amount of time online arguing with people about Gödel, and he has this running habit of grading pop-science claims as either follows, doesn't follow, or follows but only once you add something — and I've slowly realised his verdicts hang together even though I can't tell what rule he's actually running. From memory: he says it follows that no consistent, computably axiomatised theory strong enough to encode a decent chunk of arithmetic proves every truth about the natural numbers, and that Peano arithmetic can't prove its own consistency (on the assumption it is consistent); he gave a grudging follows-but-you-need-essential-undecidability-as-an-extra to the claim that there's no algorithm for deciding theoremhood in such a theory. He says it doesn't follow that the human mind therefore outruns any machine (he is extremely bored of Lucas–Penrose), that arithmetic might be inconsistent without us ever noticing, that a final theory of physics is impossible, that no language can define its own truth predicate (his line was that this is Tarski's theorem, a cousin rather than a consequence), or that nobody can ever be justified in believing arithmetic is consistent — though he concedes that any actual consistency proof has to lean on something beyond the system itself. And for there are truths no system can establish, full stop, he split it: fine up to no system can establish if you mean relative to a given one, dead on arrival once you say full stop.

The new case is a clause out of a draft standard for AI systems that someone in my reading group brought in last week; call it the self-certification clause. It says a system may execute an action only if, working entirely within its own rule-set and using nothing outside it, it first derives a certificate that the action satisfies the safety spec, where the spec is written in the system's own language and the system's rules are rich enough to encode ordinary arithmetic. The group wants to know whether Gödel shows that no system can satisfy this clause, or at least that any system satisfying it must sometimes refuse to act. I told them Sam would say doesn't follow, but I'm no longer confident, because I can see at least three rules that fit his whole track record and come apart here: one saying that Gödel only ever yields conclusions about computably axiomatised systems that can encode arithmetic and only about unprovability inside such a system, never about minds, machines, worlds or what anyone is justified in doing; one saying the engine is self-reference through a provability predicate, so anything with the shape of a system proving a global fact about its own reliability is blocked just as its own consistency is; and one saying nothing absolute ever follows, since every undecidable sentence gets settled by a slightly stronger theory, so the theorem only ever generates relative limits and uniform-recurrence claims.

So: what would Sam say about the self-certification clause, and which of those rules is he really running?
