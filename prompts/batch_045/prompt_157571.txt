I help run a tiny reading group — four of us, mostly lapsed grad students — and we're crawling through Hume's *Natural History of Religion* (1757, the first of the *Four Dissertations*). We stalled on the argument in the Introduction and I want help with the argument itself, not a summary of the book or a blurb about Hume.

Here's the passage (old spelling, including "it's" for "its", is as in the text):

"The belief of invisible, intelligent power has been very generally diffused over the human race, in all places and in all ages; but it has neither perhaps been so universal as to admit of no exceptions, nor has it been, in any degree, uniform in the ideas, which it has suggested. Some nations have been discovered, who entertained no sentiments of Religion, if travellers and historians may be credited; and no two nations, and scarce any two men, have ever agreed precisely in the same sentiments. It would appear, therefore, that this preconception springs not from an original instinct or primary impression of nature, such as gives rise to self-love, affection betwixt the sexes, love of progeny, gratitude, resentment; since every instinct of this kind has been found absolutely universal in all nations and ages, and has always a precise, determinate object, which it inflexibly pursues. The first religious principles must be secondary; such as may easily be perverted by various accidents and causes, and whose operation too, in some cases, may, by an extraordinary concurrence of circumstances, be altogether prevented. What those principles are, which give rise to the original belief, and what those accidents and causes are, which direct its operation, is the subject of our present enquiry."

Things I'd like you to take apart for me:

1. What exactly is the test for being an "original instinct" here? As far as I can tell it's two conditions — found in all nations and ages, and having a precise determinate object it inflexibly pursues — but I can't tell whether Hume needs religion to fail *both* or whether failing either one is enough. Related: is the hedged clause about nations with no religion ("if travellers and historians may be credited") load-bearing for the argument, or just a cautious aside he doesn't really need?

2. I've been using a rough distinction in my notes between mechanisms that are uniform in *whether they fire* and mechanisms that are uniform in *what they produce*. A mechanism could be reliably triggered in every normal human and still spit out wildly different content depending on what the environment feeds it. It looks to me like Hume's evidence only touches the second kind of uniformity, and I want to know if that's fair or if I'm reading a modern distinction into a text that doesn't have it. Test case: imagine a population where 98% of people form some belief in invisible intelligent power, but no two of them agree about what it is, and the remaining 2% never do. Which of Hume's claims, if any, does that population refute?

3. What does "secondary" mean? Does it mean *causally derived from other principles* (fear, hope, ignorance of causes — the stuff he gets to later), or only *not primitive / not an original deliverance of nature*? Does the difference matter for the book's project, i.e. for treating religion as something with a natural history rather than a revelation?

4. Hume opens by splitting two questions: religion's foundation in reason, and its origin in human nature. He seems to think he can give a fully naturalistic story about the second while leaving the first intact. Can that split actually hold? If the origin story is that gods get invented out of anxiety about future events and ignorance of causes, does that leave the rational foundation untouched, or does the genealogy do quiet debunking work anyway? I'd like you to say which way you think it goes and why, not just lay out both options.

5. Finally: give me the strongest version of the inference in that passage — the version that isn't vulnerable to the obvious modern "an innate mechanism can have variable output" objection — and then tell me what that strong version would *fail* to establish even if it succeeded. If you think one of my readings above is just wrong, say so plainly and tell me what the sentence says instead.
