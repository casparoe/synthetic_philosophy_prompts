Hey — I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this term and we spent two whole weeks on Mary's room, and I came out of it genuinely unsure whether I'd watched a deep debate or a broken one. Quick recap in case it helps: Mary is a colour scientist who has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room, learns every physical fact there is to know about colour vision, and then sees a red tomato for the first time; the intuition is that she learns something new, what it's like to see red, so there are facts the physical facts don't cover, so physicalism is false. The three replies we went over were that she doesn't gain a fact but only abilities (to recognise, imagine and remember), that she doesn't gain a fact but only acquaintance with a property she already knew about in another guise, and that she gains a special sort of concept, a phenomenal one, so an old fact gets represented in a new way and the feeling of new information is a trick of the concept rather than a clue about the world. Also someone in my seminar said the person who invented the case ended up rejecting his own argument, and I get the impression that was supposed to settle things and just... didn't. So: why hasn't this moved since the early eighties? Everyone on both sides knows every argument on the other side by heart, and nobody seems to shift.

My hypothesis, which my TA said was "either sharp or a category mistake", is that the two sides aren't even answering one question. The anti-physicalist side is doing metaphysics — is there a feature of reality the physical story leaves out — while all three replies are about Mary's epistemic situation, her concepts, her abilities, her modes of presentation, and the slide from "Mary couldn't have known this from the physical facts" to "there is something the physical facts don't cover" is the bit nobody actually argues for, it's a modal assumption smuggled in. But then there's a second and separate fight about what "physical" even means — all facts supervene on the physical, all facts are the sort of thing physics describes, no mentality at the fundamental level — and I cant tell whether that one is a verbal dispute or whether it's the real load-bearing one underneath everything else. My flatmate, who does visual neuroscience and has no patience for any of this, says the whole thing reduces to introspection being unreliable and Mary's surprise being a fact about Mary rather than about the world. She might be right, and I don't know how to tell.

I've been trying to build cases that would separate these diagnoses, and I want to know if any of them actually do any work or whether they're all downstream of the same intuition. (1) Incremental Mary: instead of one red tomato she's shown a long series of extremely similar reds. If the news is a new fact per experience, she seems to learn one at every step, which is either an absurd explosion of facts or a sign that the intuition isn't tracking facts at all. (2) One-sentence Mary: could anyone have told her, while she was still in the room, what she learns on release, in a finite number of ordinary non-demonstrative sentences? If not, is that because the fact isn't in the physical information, or just because the fact has to be presented to her in a certain way? (3) Reverse Mary: someone who has had every colour experience there is and knows no physics, who then learns the physical story — she also seems to learn something, and if that's supposed to show anything, the argument seems to generalise into showing that chemistry isn't physics either. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, substantive, two questions under one label, or bedrock intuitions on both ends — which of my three cases, if any, would tell the diagnoses apart, and what would genuinely count as progress? Please dont just hand me a list of the positions and who holds them, I've read those; I want the shape of the stalemate, and an honest answer about whether anything could end it.
