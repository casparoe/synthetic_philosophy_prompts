On Saturday I have to stand up in front of roughly two hundred people and give the eulogy for my godfather, who died last Sunday at eighty-two. He taught French and drama at the same comprehensive school for thirty-eight years, and he read Sartre seriously, and argumentatively, for longer than that — not as a youthful enthusiasm but as a book he checked his life against. Half the room will be former colleagues and ex-pupils who know exactly what he believed and will catch every allusion; the other half are his widow's family, practising Catholics, for whom the ordinary and decent things to say about the dead are that they are at peace, that their suffering is over, and that they were always the man we loved. I cannot say those three things honestly, and I also do not want to spend the funeral quietly signalling that I consider them superstitions. The speech has to comfort the people who believe them, without being a lie by Hugh's standards and without being condescension by mine.

Two further things constrain it. First, he left a typed undated note with his will. Its substance, as I would put it, is this: do not turn me into a character; if you need to talk about me, talk about what I did on particular occasions and leave it visible that I could have done otherwise; and do not tell people I am at peace, since that is a state I never chose and that I now have no way of revising. I would like the note treated as binding on the speech. Second, there is something the speech has to carry rather than smooth over. In 1994 he resigned from that school in the middle of a term. He had written a reference for a seventeen-year-old who had been caught with the exam papers; the head wanted it withdrawn; Hugh refused and walked. Plenty of people in the room still think that was a mistake, and some think it was the best thing he ever did. He never once described it to me as a matter of principle — he described it as a thing he did on a Tuesday, and then had to live with. The easy versions of that story are the ones I want to avoid: the version where he was always the sort of man who would do that, the version where the school was plainly wrong and has since learned better, the version where it all makes sense now that we know how the rest of his life went. Those are precisely the versions his own philosophy calls false, and the ones he would have hated most.

To make this workable I have been using a distinction of my own, and I would like the speech built on it. By trait-talk I mean ascribing someone a character: predicates that sit still, that explain the action by pointing behind it to what the person was like, that survive every particular thing they did. By act-talk I mean describing what someone did on an occasion, in enough concrete detail that the alternatives are still on display — what else was available to him, what it cost, what he was refusing, what he did not yet know. A eulogy is almost pure trait-talk by genre, and Hugh's note is a request for almost pure act-talk. So: build the speech out of act-talk, and where you use a trait-word at all, use it once, deliberately, near the end, and let it be something the speech has earned rather than something it rests on. He had a private name for what he was against — he called it the passport photograph, the habit of fixing a person with one still image taken at the end — and if you can find a way to use that phrase, or something like it, in language a non-philosopher would accept, I would like that.

Two more requirements. In the spoken text, please use no philosophical vocabulary: no authenticity, no bad faith, no existence precedes essence, no absurd, and Sartre's name at most once. Whatever structural work those ideas do, they have to do it in plain words — Hugh's widow, Maureen, is not a Sartrean, she has asked me for something that sounds like him, and some of that vocabulary genuinely wounds her. And the consolation, such as it is, has to be dramatised rather than asserted: the thought that the people in the pews are not finished, that nothing about them is settled by what they have been so far, should land as something they feel about themselves and not as a thesis I hand them about freedom.

Please answer in these sections, in this order:

1. "The eulogy" — about 1,000 to 1,100 words, written to be spoken in seven or eight minutes, with no internal headings and no stage directions beyond a few in brackets where a pause or a name has to land.

2. "What the speech refuses to say" — about 300 to 400 words, listing the consolations and the narrative shapes you deliberately left out, and saying what each one would have implied about Hugh or about the mourners.

3. "Lines to cut" — about 150 words, naming two or three passages that a mixed room might not bear, and what I should substitute if I decide in the moment that they are too much.
