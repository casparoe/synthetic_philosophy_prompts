THE QUESTION (parts are worth 6 / 8 / 6, total 20):

In this course we use the following stipulative definition. Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU):

(1) There is a single hedonic scale. Pains and pleasures both have magnitudes on it, measured in the same unit (call them hedons); pleasures are positive, pains negative; magnitudes can be summed within a life and across lives.

(2) Fix a threshold T on intensity, T > 0. For any painful experience whose intensity exceeds T, the excess above T goes into the outcome's priority tier; the remainder of that experience's magnitude, together with all pleasures and all pains at or below T, goes into the outcome's ordinary tier.

(3) Betterness is lexicographic on the ordered pair (priority tier, ordinary tier): A is better than B iff A has less in the priority tier; if they are equal there, A is better iff A has the higher net ordinary-tier total.

(a) [6] LTNU claims both that pleasure and pain are commensurable and that no amount of pleasure can compensate for a unit of above-threshold pain. Explain how both can be true at once. Be explicit about what "commensurable" commits you to, and what it does not.

(b) [8] Give what you take to be the strongest objection to LTNU, and explain how the threshold is supposed to answer it. Say which reading of T you are using — a threshold on the intensity of a single experience, on a creature's lifetime total, or on the aggregate total in an outcome — and whether the force of the objection depends on that choice.

(c) [6] What does LTNU recommend under uncertainty? Would an expected-value maximiser who puts a very large finite weight on suffering recommend the same thing? Close by saying, in a sentence or two, which objection you think survives.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER:

(a) "Commensurable" here just means there is one scale of hedonic magnitude: pains and pleasures both have a size in hedons, you can say that one pain is twice as intense as another, and you can add them up within a creature and between creatures. What it does not mean is that the two are tradeable at some rate in the betterness relation — that is a separate claim. You can have a single unit and still order things lexicographically; think of comparing pairs of numbers by their first coordinate. Both coordinates are in the same unit, but no gain in the second ever makes up for a loss in the first. So LTNU is not committed to there being some enormous finite N with one unit of priority-tier pain worth N units of pleasure; if there were, it would not be a lexical view at all. That is also why LTNU does not need the incommensurabilist's argument, the one where suffering is not just negative happiness and the two are not on a single scale. It can grant a single scale and still get priority out of the ordering rather than out of the metaphysics of the scale.

(b) The strongest objection is the discontinuity objection, the "one pinprick outweighs a galaxy of bliss" complaint. LTNU says we should always prioritise reducing suffering over increasing happiness, so if one creature has a headache above T, removing it improves the outcome more than adding any finite amount of happiness to everyone, even a billion blissful lives. There is no amount of happiness that closes the gap. The threshold is meant to take the edge off: pains below T never enter the priority tier, so ordinary aches, itches and boredom just get weighed against pleasures at the normal rate, and the view only gets extreme above T. I am using T as a threshold on the intensity of a single experience, and the choice matters — if T were an aggregate threshold instead, then a large population of mild pains would push you back into the fanatical result. But I think the threshold mostly relocates the problem. Two reasons: the boundary (a pain just under T is handled normally, a pain just over T is infinitely more important, so you get a fresh arbitrariness right at T), and the fact that nothing in the view tells you where to put T even though its position is doing all the work. So the threshold answers the objection, at the cost of a new one.

(c) Under uncertainty LTNU is, for practical purposes, just expected-utility maximisation with a huge finite weight on the priority tier: represent the priority as, say, 10^100 hedons per unit of above-threshold suffering and maximise as usual. The recommendations come out the same, because with a weight that big any realistic probability of above-T suffering swamps any realistic happiness gain — you still take the small reduction in risk over the large gain in happiness, and we don't need to worry that lexicographic orderings lack a real-valued utility representation. One last point in the view's favour: the lexical clause also gets LTNU out of the repugnant conclusion. Since above-T suffering trumps any quantity of happiness, no number of lives that are barely worth living can outrank even a small amount of suffering, so LTNU will not tell you that a vast population of drab, barely-worth-living lives is better than a smaller population of ecstatic ones.
