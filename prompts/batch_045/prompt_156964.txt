I've drifted into thinking that the sunk cost fallacy gets diagnosed way too often — that there are plenty of real situations where letting what you've already spent actually count is the rational move, and that the standard advice (ignore everything behind you, pick whatever has the best prospects from here) is incomplete rather than basically right. Its not an abstract worry for me: I've put four years and most of my savings into a company that isn't working, the honest forward-looking numbers say walk away, and I keep not walking away, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's weakness or something I could defend in a seminar.

The props I'm leaning on, so you can knock them down one at a time: (a) a policy-level argument — at each individual decision point quitting looks best, but the policy of quitting whenever quitting looks best does worse in the long run then a policy that sometimes eats the cost, because almost anything worth doing has its payoff piled up at the end and because your estimates at every node are noisy; (b) an evidence argument — my earlier self's willingness to sink four years is a signal about the project's prospects that I can't regenerate from where I sit now, and discarding it throws away real data; (c) an adversarial argument — when someone else's best move is to wait me out, being the sort of person who eats sunk costs is a credible threat, and credibility is worth something; (d) a constitutive argument — with some projects (a marriage, a craft, a political campaign) the good you were after partly consists in having stuck with it, so quitting doesn't spare the value, it unmakes it.

What would count against the position? I want defeaters ranked fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to keep separate what a defender of my view would concede as a genuine hit from what they'd shrug off as a misunderstanding. In particular:

- Is (a)–(d) just relabeling? Each one looks restatable in purely forward-looking terms — option value, information, strategic commitment, ends that persistence partly constitutes — in which case the bare historical fact of the expenditure does no work anywhere, and what I'm defending is either trivially true or empty. I don't know whether that redescription is a refutation or just a clarification of what I meant. And relatedly: have I insulated the view into unfalsifiability by hedging with "sometimes" and "it depends on the case"? If so, say whether that's actually a defect for a claim of this shape or just what an honest "sometimes" claim inevitably looks like.
- Which of (a)–(d) is load-bearing, and what survives if you delete it? My guess is everything collapses without (a), and that (a) is doing the same work as the case for taking one box in Newcomb's problem — which makes me suspicious of it in a way I can't quite articulate, so please articulate it or tell me the analogy is wrong.
- The consequence I like least: if staying is rational for me, why isn't it rational for a government to keep a war going because of the people already dead? That's the example everyone reaches for when they want to show sunk cost reasoning is poison. I'd rather keep my position than that application, but I want to know what blocking the inference costs me and whether the block is principled or ad hoc. Say clearly if the view can't distinguish the Concorde from a cult.
- What would settle it empirically? I'm inclined to treat it as close to decisive against me if debiasing turns out to work — if firms or people who force themselves to ignore past investment systematically do better in ordinary, non-adversarial settings — because my view seems to predict they should do worse. Tell me if I'm weighting that wrong, tell me what I should predict instead, and be explicit about how much the standard lab findings on escalation of commitment really show given that they're mostly about artificial, one-shot, non-adversarial tasks.
- And the thing I'd find most persuasive either way, personally: whether any version of this defense can even be addressed to a reflective person. A commitment device that only works if you don't notice it's a device can't be handed to someone who has already noticed, which might mean the argument can't be given to me at all — only about me.
