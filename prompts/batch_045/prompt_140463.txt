I am putting together a handout for a first-year seminar on Dennett's fourfold taxonomy of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures — and I want to stop my students reading it as a simple ladder on which every rung just does more of the work inside the creature and is therefore better at staying alive. The exercise I have in mind scores each of the four on one dimension only: how much of the testing in generate-and-test happens outside the individual creature's own head, that is, how much of the weeding out of bad moves is done by the world rather than by the creature's own preselection. Give me a number from 0 to 100 for each kind — 0 meaning the creature can screen its options internally and nothing outside it has to pay for a mistake, 100 meaning that virtually all the filtering is done by the environment at the cost of real organisms — plus a one-sentence reason for each score. Then sort the four into tiers, and tell me which two are close enough that you would happily swap them.

The answers I get when I try this on colleagues are always monotonic, and I think that is exactly the mistake, which is the point of the exercise. A Popperian creature internalises the test, but a Gregorian creature seems to export it again — into artefacts, notation, diagrams, habits of wording and other minds — so that the world, or somebody else's world, is doing the screening once more, just on a different timescale and with a different kind of casualty. So please say explicitly where Gregorian creatures land relative to Popperian ones on this scale, and whether that makes the taxonomy a ladder at all or something more like a zig-zag. A second wrinkle: the same question for a Gregorian creature reared without any of the accumulated equipment of its kind — no language, no artefacts, no one to copy, but otherwise neurologically intact. Does its score move, and if so does that show the number belongs to the kind in its normal setting rather than to the kind itself?

Finally, I would like you to keep apart two things that I suspect my students will run together: where the test physically happens, and who bears the cost when a test fails. The two can come apart badly — a creature may die for a hypothesis it never entertained, or may learn from a disaster that happened to somebody else entirely. If you rescored the four on the second dimension instead of the first, would the ordering change? Where, and why? Please keep the whole thing to a page at most; it is going on a handout, so I would rather have a sharp ordering with reasons than a tour of the taxonomy.
