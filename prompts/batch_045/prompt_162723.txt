I'm writing a short position paper for a political theory reading group on social choice and individual rights, and before I commit it to paper I'd like an audit of whether the pieces actually fit together. Here is where I have landed.

(1) I work in the standard social choice framework: there is a fixed set of social states, each individual has an ordering over them, and a procedure assigns a social ranking to each admissible profile of individual orderings. I take the social ranking to be a full weak ordering, complete and transitive, and I take unrestricted domain as the default.

(2) Weak Pareto is one of two things I am not willing to drop: if everyone strictly prefers x to y, then society ranks x above y. It strikes me as about as uncontroversial as anything in this area.

(3) The other is a minimal liberal condition: for each person there is at least one pair of alternatives differing only in some feature of that person's own life, over which the social ranking follows that person. The example I have been using has three alternatives: nobody reads the novel (N), Prude reads it (P), Lewd reads it (L). Prude ranks N above P above L, on the ground that she would rather read it herself than have Lewd corrupted by it. Lewd ranks P above L above N, since what he would most like is for Prude to read it. Prude is decisive over the pair consisting of P and N, Lewd is decisive over the pair consisting of L and N, Pareto then applies to the pair consisting of P and L, and the result is the familiar cycle with no best element.

(4) My response is not to abandon (2) or (3) but to restrict which preferences count. Call it the no-meddling filter: a person's ranking of a pair is admissible only if it responds solely to differences in those coordinates of the social state that belong to her own sphere, where the spheres are given by an assignment of each coordinate of the state space to exactly one person. Lewd's preference for P over L turns on what Prude reads, which is not his coordinate, so it drops out, and with it the Pareto step that closes the cycle.

(5) I also want rights to be trumps rather than weights: what the liberal condition protects is not one more consideration to be aggregated against how strongly people want things, but a constraint that excludes options outright. On that reading the two-reader case has a clean answer: Prude's right excludes P, Lewd's right to read excludes N, and L is what remains.

(6) Finally, I resist a diagnosis that keeps coming up in the group, namely that the lesson of the whole exercise is that the framework is informationally too thin. Adding intensities or interpersonal comparisons of utility would not, as far as I can see, change anything in the argument, and I therefore do not count informational poverty as the moral to draw.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and how badly. If I had to guess I would say the weak point is (3), since the pair over which each person is decisive seems to be doing a great deal of work for such a thin condition, but I have been wrong about this before, so please do not read that as a hint about where to look or about which commitment I would be willing to surrender. Be specific about what each real conflict would force me to give up, and rank the conflicts by severity rather than flagging them all as equally serious. Where a pair only looks inconsistent, say so and explain why the appearance misleads; I would rather not discard a commitment I can keep.

Two further constraints. First, do not simply report that the set is inconsistent if it is; give me the cheapest revision and say what it costs me substantively. Second, separate what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who know it: I want to know which of my claims are close to unanimous, which are live disputes, and where you are yourself uncertain or extrapolating. Please argue it out rather than pointing me to anything to read.
