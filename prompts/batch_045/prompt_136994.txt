I had a long argument with a friend last night about the vessel I help look after — a nineteenth-century wooden boat kept afloat by a small conservation trust, and therefore replaced in pieces, more or less continuously, for as long as anyone has been keeping records. The argument started as a quarrel about whether the trust may honestly describe what visitors walk around as the original vessel, and I came away convinced that our disagreement was not about boats, and not even really about the old puzzle of the ship whose timbers are replaced one at a time. It was about something I don't have a word for. Here is the pattern I kept noticing. If our boat's timbers are replaced one by one and the discarded ones are simply burned, nobody in the room hesitates: it is the same boat, and my friend agreed. But if the discarded timbers are stacked in a shed and a shipwright later reassembles them into a second boat, a surprising number of people — my friend among them — start saying that the reassembled one is the original, or at least that the boat we have been sailing has had its claim weakened. Nothing about the boat we sailed is different in the two stories; the entire difference lies in what happened, later, to stuff that had already left it. And yet the effect is oddly specific. If the old timbers are fed one at a time into the upkeep of some other working boat, so that after thirty years that other boat contains most of the original timber and ours contains none of it while both have stayed in continuous service, nobody at all — not even my friend — wants to say the two boats have traded identities. And if the timbers in the shed are reassembled into a barge, or into the roof of a boathouse, the pull largely disappears, though it partly survives if the reassembly is only finished after our boat has finally been lost.

So my question: is there an established concept for the fact that whether a thing persists seems to depend on what becomes of the matter that has left it, and specifically on whether that matter is later collected back into something presenting itself as a candidate of the same kind, and on when that happens? My friend offered three deflations and none of them satisfied me. He said it is just the old ship-replacement puzzle, about which there are two standard philosophical answers — one on which a thing is stretched out through time the way a road is stretched out through space, and one on which the timber assembly and the boat are two different things sitting in the same place — and that I have merely found a version neither answer was designed for. He said it is just vagueness, there being no exact number of replaced planks at which the boat stops being the same boat. And he said it shows only that our talk of sameness is really talk of something that comes in degrees. The vagueness diagnosis is the one I most want tested, because the judgments I described are not tracking quantities or borderline quantities at all: they flip while the quantity is held fixed, and they flip non-monotonically, coming back when the shed is emptied into a barge.

A bare label would not help me, so please do the carving rather than the naming. Is what I have described one phenomenon or two — a competition effect, where a thing's hold on its identity is settled by comparison with rival candidates rather than by its own history, and a separate recollection effect, where the mere re-gathering of shed matter into a thing of the right kind does the work? How would each be distinguished from the standard two answers my friend gave, from ordinary vagueness, and from the plain claim that sameness is a matter of degree? And where are the edges? Does the effect survive if the reassembly is staged so that every discarded timber becomes part of some boat the instant it is removed, with no interval in which it is merely stacked? Does it survive if the timbers are copied rather than reused, so that the material is new but the arrangement is inherited? Does a later reassembly make it have been false, earlier, that our boat was the same boat, or does it only make us revise a claim that was true when we made it? If the answer is that nothing established fits, say so, give the thing a name I can use, define it tightly enough that the boundary cases above come out on one side or the other, and say what would fall outside it.
