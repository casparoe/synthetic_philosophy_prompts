Two historians of the global economy, who have spent a decade talking past each other about whether the question "Why did the Industrial Revolution happen in Britain and not in China?" is even well posed, have agreed to close out their dispute as an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint document they could both sign.

Party A — the contrastivist realist — holds that, once the contrast class is fixed, the question is well posed and admits of a substantive answer: there is a relevant contrast class, there were difference-making factors distinguishing the members of it, and the fact that the threshold was crossed in one place and not another is a fact about the world to be explained rather than an artifact of the question. Party B — the question-skeptic — holds that the question as ordinarily posed is at best badly underspecified and at worst an artifact of framing: the units are mismatched in scale, the "not China" treats a continent-sized empire as a single agent with a single opportunity, the relevant counterfactual is indeterminate, and the formulation quietly encodes a teleology in which industrialization is the default path that one region "failed" to take.

Before drafting, stipulate the terms both sides have already accepted in writing, and hold yourself to them:

1. The outcome to be explained is a threshold crossing, not a named event: output per head rising sustainably for at least two generations, with inanimate sources supplying a growing share of mechanical energy, first crossed somewhere in the window 1750–1850.
2. The comparison is run under a matched-pairs protocol: the comparator for Britain (or England) is the lower Yangzi Delta — Jiangnan — not the Qing empire as a whole, on the grounds that these units are comparable in area, population density, commercialization, and market integration. Both parties must state whether matching at this scale dissolves the puzzle, relocates it, or leaves it exactly as it was, and why.
3. The question is to be decomposed into (Q1) why the Malthusian ceiling was breached anywhere at all, and (Q2) why it was breached there first. Both parties must say whether a full answer to Q1 plus a full answer to Q2 exhausts the original question, which of the two carries most of the explanatory weight, and whether the decomposition is even clean — a candidate "leakage" worry being that some factors invoked for Q2 (access to coal, access to New World land, the shape of long-distance trade) are themselves downstream of earlier European positions, so that they belong partly to Q1.

Structure the document in four parts.

I. Agreed claims, numbered, eight to twelve of them. These must be claims neither side would water down: about what counts as a well-posed contrastive question in general, about the economic and demographic facts of the comparison around 1750 that are not in dispute, about what kind of factor could in principle do the explanatory work, and about what neither side is claiming (for instance, that no party is relying on civilizational or cultural essentialism). Nothing vacuous, nothing one side would only sign under protest.

II. A residual-disagreements table, one row per crux. Columns: the crux, stated as a question whose answer would move at least one party; Party A's position in wording A would choose for itself; Party B's position in wording B would choose for itself; and the kind of disagreement it is — empirical, conceptual, verbal, or pragmatic. Separate genuine disagreements from ones that turn out to be about words, and where you find a verbal component, say exactly which sentence is ambiguous and what substantive residue survives disambiguation. Candidate cruxes to consider, in addition to any you find better: whether "China did not industrialize" means that no part of the Qing empire crossed the threshold or merely that it did not cross first; whether a contrastive why-question requires a determinate counterfactual or only a relevant contrast class; whether a difference-maker that is itself unexplained counts as an answer; and whether the verdict on well-posedness depends on what the question is for — prediction, guidance for later developing economies, or the assignment of responsibility.

III. Pre-registered predictions. For each crux, name the one cheapest observation, comparison, or argument both sides agree would bear on it, record what A predicts it will show and what B predicts, and flag any crux that is in fact unsettled by anything available without new archival work. Include both parties' answers to the "1700 submersion test": had Britain gone under the sea in 1700, when and where would the threshold have been crossed, and with what confidence?

IV. Concessions and movers: what each party grants the other, and the single development each would count as a reason to abandon their own position.

Write it as a symposium document — formal, precise, free of rhetoric, roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words. Refer to views by their content only; do not name, date, cite, or attribute anything to any author, school, or work, and do not supply references or further reading: the point is the structure of the disagreement, not who holds it.
