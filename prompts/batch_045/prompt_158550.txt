Here is the disagreement I witnessed. Two senior colleagues in my department's ethics workshop — call them Nadia and Marcus — got into an argument about whether karma can be understood as a genuine theory of moral desert. Neither would budge and they asked me to referee; I genuinely couldn't tell. Here is the exchange, as close to verbatim as I can manage.

(1) Nadia opened by saying that karma is best read not as a causal mechanism — not as a story about how the world transmits a deed into a consequence — but as a claim about desert: the world's distribution of well-being and suffering tracks moral merit, so that agents ultimately get what they deserve. She stressed what she called karma's institutional advantage: because desert is implemented impersonally, no state, judge, or crowd has to inflict anything. Punishment by human hands is always corruptible, disproportionate, and contaminated by the punisher's own blameworthiness; an impersonal moral order sidesteps all of that. So karma, on her reading, is the only version of retributivism that survives the standard critique of punishment.

(2) Marcus replied with what he called a dilemma. Desert, he said, is a normative relation: to deserve suffering is for suffering to be a fitting response to you and your deed. But an impersonal causal order can no more "respond" than an earthquake can. So a purely natural version of karma gives you causation without normativity, which is not desert at all; and the moment you reintroduce normativity, you seem to need someone who is doing the responding — a judge, a lawgiver, a punishing agency — which puts you straight back into the human (or divine) institutions Nadia wanted to avoid. There is no third thing in the middle, he said: the doctrine is unstable, and every attempt to stabilize it collapses it into one horn or the other.

(3) Nadia answered that Marcus was assuming desert must be inflicted by an agent, and that this is exactly the assumption a karmic theory should reject. She distinguished what she called "expressive" desert: a structure can register appraisal without any appraiser, the way the scoring rules of a well-designed game track skill, or the way a well-written novel's ending can be fitting without anyone deciding it. She then added a second, more specific point: on the versions she finds defensible, the karmic link is not a transaction — one bad act, one unit of suffering — but a claim about character. Actions deepen dispositions, dispositions reshape what experiences are open to an agent, and so the "ledger" is really the shape of a life. Retribution, she said, is an immature reading of the doctrine; the mature reading is about formation.

(4) Marcus conceded the character point but said it was a bait-and-switch. A claim about how character shapes a life is a causal-psychological claim, and it is perfectly compatible with nobody ever getting what they deserve: the person who cultivates patience and clarity may nonetheless die young of a painful illness in a famine, and nothing in the dispositional story says her suffering was fitting. If the psychological reading is all karma amounts to, he said, it predicts nothing about the moral appropriateness of outcomes, and the doctrine's distinctive content — that suffering answers to wrongdoing — quietly evaporates. You start with "the world is morally structured," you are challenged, and you retreat to "character has consequences," which nobody denies.

(5) Nadia then brought in the跨-life point: classical accounts, she said, do not require the ledger to close within a single lifetime, and they also allow karmic fruit to ripen in a community or a lineage rather than in an isolated individual. So the famine case is not a counterexample, because the relevant accounting period and the relevant bearer are both wider than Marcus is assuming. She added that this is a virtue rather than an evasion: any theory that demands a visible match between deed and outcome within one biography is committed to an absurdly compressed moral timetable, and ordinary retributivists have the same problem with late-onset harms and with wrongs whose consequences outlive the wrongdoer.

(6) Marcus said this move was the one that actually sank the theory. Desert, he argued, is non-transferable in a very strict sense: it attaches to the agent who acted, in virtue of that action. If the being who later suffers shares no memory, no character, and no psychological continuity with the agent who acted, then making them suffer is not giving the wrongdoer his deserts — it is visiting the consequences of one person's deed on another, which is the paradigm of what desert theory exists to rule out. And Nadia cannot escape by strengthening the continuity: if the future bearer remembers the deed, owns it, and is psychologically continuous with the doer, then it just is the same agent, and karma reduces to ordinary personal identity plus ordinary consequences of action, with the exotic cross-life machinery doing no work at all. So, he concluded, karma is either too weak to be desert or too strong to be coherent, and the width of the accounting period is irrelevant.

(7) Nadia's last move was to reformulate. She proposed dropping both the private ledger and the requirement that the fruit land on the agent, and instead treating karmic residue as an outstanding claim in the shared world: what a wrong act leaves behind is not a quantum of pain owed to anyone, but an unsetled relation — a harm unacknowledged, a repair unperformed, a distortion in the practices and expectations people have to live inside — and that residue persists and presses on whoever is positioned to deal with it, not only on the one who caused it. On this reading, she said, the "fruit" of an act is the unfinished business it creates, and the appropriate response to it is repair rather than suffering. She claimed this escapes the transfer objection, because what travels is the claim generated by the harm, not a punishment looking for a body to land on.

(8) Marcus answered that this concedes the whole dispute. A theory whose central relation is an outstanding claim demanding repair is not a theory of desert but of corrective justice or liability, and those come apart in familiar ways: liability can be discharged by anyone, including people who did nothing wrong, and it is insensitive to the agent's moral quality in a way that desert is not. He also raised two cases he said the reformulation cannot handle: wrongs with no identifiable claim-holder, and victims who refuse repair or who would be made worse off by it. If karma has to be rewritten until it is a duty-of-repair view, he said, the rewrite has removed the very idea that anyone gets what they deserve.
