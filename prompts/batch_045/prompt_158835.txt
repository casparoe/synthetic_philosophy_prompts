My thesis is that it isn't about deduction at all — it's about the act of drawing a conclusion from something, whatever kind of "from" that is. But every time I write down the argument for that thesis it ends somewhere I don't want to be. Here's the version I'm least unhappy with:

(1) Carroll's point: a thinker can grant every proposition on your premise-list, and grant the associated conditional as well, and still not draw the conclusion — because each new conditional you write down is just one more thing they can grant without moving an inch. So no premise-list ever logically forces a conclusion on a thinker who doesn't already bring a rule along with them.

(2) But that demand doesn't look like it has anything to do with deduction specifically. Take the zero-step argument: "A. Therefore A." The Tortoise can grant A as a premise and still decline to draw A until he's also granted "if A then A," and then "if A and (if A then A) then A," and on and on. So what the Tortoise is demanding isn't a bridge to a new conclusion; it attaches to the act of drawing at all.

(3) And here's a meaner variant I invented, call him the Super-Tortoise: he grants every conditional you write down, instantly, no complaints. Then he asks for one more premise — that the sentence-tokens on your list mean the same thing at the end of the argument as they did at the beginning, and mean the same to him as to you. That's a premise, so by (1) it needs its own conditional, and it also needs its own stability-premise, which needs its own conditional, and so the regress restarts inside the list itself.

(4) So: if Carroll's regress is a genuine problem, it isn't a problem about the justification of deduction (modus ponens, conditional proof, whichever rule you like). It's a problem about whether inference as such — treating one thing as a reason for another, at any strength — can ever be ratified. And via (3) it's also a problem about whether anybody has ever understood an argument, since every argument needs its terms to stay put while it runs.
