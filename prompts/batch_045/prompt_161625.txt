I'm drafting a short position paper for a research meeting on the ethical implications of panpsychism, and I want my commitments audited before I circulate them. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) Constitutive (Russellian) panpsychism is the best available answer to the hard problem: experience is the categorical ground of physical dispositions, macro-consciousness is constituted out of micro-experiences, and the combination problem is serious but not decisive.
(2) The disvalue of suffering does not diminish with the simplicity of the subject: there is no discount for cognitive unsophistication.
(3) Given (1) and (2), the expected amount of welfare-relevant experience in the world is many orders of magnitude larger than what commonsense morality counts, so getting this right should dominate ordinary cause prioritization.
(4) I nevertheless do not want to move any resources toward investigating or preventing "fundamental" suffering, and I don't think the astronomical-stakes expected-value reasoning I accept for existential risk carries over here.
(5) I suspect micro-experiences are non-valenced — a bare feel of, say, mass or charge rather than of pleasure or pain — so the suffering conclusion may not follow.
(6) I aggregate total rather than average welfare, and I take it seriously that this requires a determinate number of subjects.

Please audit the set: which pairs genuinely pull against each other, ranked by severity, and for each what would have to give and what that costs; which apparent tensions dissolve once you separate things I've run together; and which single revision would clean up the most. Be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict and where you are extrapolating beyond anything actually argued for in the literature. And if the framing as a whole rests on a confusion, or if one of the six is doing no real work in the package, say so plainly rather than working politely through the list.
