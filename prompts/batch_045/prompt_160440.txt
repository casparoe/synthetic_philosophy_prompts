I am a novelist, and for the past two years I have been at work on a book that alternates between the British sack of Benin City and the present day, where the objects carried out of it sit in European and American collections. The research has left me with an argumentative shape I keep encountering and cannot name, and I would like either the name or, failing that, a clean carving of the thing itself.

Here are the instances. First: a curator argues that a looted altar head should remain where it is because everything that has happened to it since 1897 has given it a second life — it has been catalogued, photographed, published, compared, seen by millions, drawn into a general history of human making; the removal, on this telling, is the beginning of a wider career, not the end of a narrower one. The same curator, in the same conversation, argues that the head could not be returned to any purpose, because the cult that made it is no longer practised, the kingdom's institutions have been transformed, and the object's original function is gone beyond recall. So the object's post-removal history is invoked as continuity when continuity helps the holder, and as rupture when rupture helps the holder. Second, older and cleaner: the case for keeping the sculptures taken from the Parthenon has rested at various points on the claim that they now belong to a collection of the whole world, and on the claim that their original setting is irrecoverable, the temple a ruin, the frieze dispersed beyond repair. The more thoroughly an object was torn out of the world that made it, the more available the argument becomes that there is no such world for it to go back to.

That last sentence is the part I most want identified. The destruction and the justification are not two separate facts here; the wrong's own consequences are what is offered as the reason the wrong need not be undone, and the worse the original injury, the better the argument gets. This is not, I think, ordinary hypocrisy, because both halves can be true at once of different aspects of the same object: its physical custody can have been broken while its social meaning persisted, or the reverse. It is not motte-and-bailey, because neither claim is a modest fallback from the other; they are two full-sized claims pointing in opposite directions. And a lawyer I put this to mentioned title ripening through possession and claims going stale with time, which does not fit either, since those depend on mere lapse, whereas here the lapse is something the taking itself manufactured and the taker's successors now profit from. A philosopher I mentioned it to said there is a view that very old wrongs can cease to generate present duties once circumstances have changed; but that seems to be about changes that would have happened anyway, and this is precisely the opposite case, the one where we would least want the duty to lapse.

What I need is not a label but the shape. Is this one phenomenon or two — the opportunistic switching between continuity and rupture, and the injury that cures itself — and if two, how do they connect? Does the established vocabulary already contain it under some description, or is it genuinely unnamed, in which case name it and define it for me? I need the boundary cases most of all. What happens to the argument when the object's original context would have vanished regardless — through iconoclasm, decay, conversion, changes internal to the community that made it? What when the holder is not the institution that destroyed the context, but a later purchaser? What when the object has in fact acquired a genuine second community, including among descendants of its makers who disagree among themselves about whether it should travel? And what when the object's present value consists almost entirely in its being a record of its own theft — when the second life just is the evidence of the crime? Does that strengthen the holder's case or collapse it?

I should say why I want this at such length. The novel has a scene in which a curator and a claimant argue, and I have found that I cannot write the curator well until I can state his case in its strongest and most internally consistent form rather than as a caricature, which means I need to know exactly which move he is making, where it holds, and where it fails. So please do not give me a reading list or citations — I could not evaluate them, and they would only defer the answer. Give me the concept: what it is, what it is not, which of my instances are central and which peripheral, and where its edges lie.
