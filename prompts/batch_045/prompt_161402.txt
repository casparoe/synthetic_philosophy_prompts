I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and I still can't get my verdicts on acausal cooperation and on surrogate goals to line up. I've written them down as honestly as I can, including the places where they clash. I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind them, and what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on.

The threat cases first.

Twin: There is a physically identical copy of me in a galaxy I will never causally affect, facing a copy of my decision. Pressing the button costs me a thousand dollars and gets my copy something worth ten thousand to me. I press. I also press if the copy is only a very good simulation rather than a physical duplicate, though with less confidence, and less confidence still the worse the simulation is.

Plain counterfactual mugging: A trustworthy predictor flipped a fair coin, told me it came up tails, and asked me for a hundred dollars on the understanding that it would have given me ten thousand had the coin come up heads. I pay.

Low-measure mugging: Same, except I'm one of a very large number of copies of this situation and I'm the only one being asked, so the branch in which the payment matters is a tiny fraction of the whole. I still pay, and I want to say that the fraction shouldn't matter at all — but this is where I'm least sure of myself.

Screening bully: A predictor simulates a huge population, and approaches only those it predicts would pay. It says: send me a thousand dollars or I run a billion simulations of you in agony. Those it predicts would refuse are never approached and never harmed. I don't pay.

Spiteful mugger: Same screening, but this one follows through: it tortures simulations of those it predicts would refuse. If I refuse, I get tortured; if I pay, I lose a thousand dollars. I pay, and I resent it.

Indiscriminate bully: This one threatens everyone regardless of predicted response, and if refused it tortures an unrelated stranger. I pay — and I cannot square this with my refusal in the screening case. My refusal there seems to be powered by something like don't make this practice worth running, and that consideration seems to apply with equal force here, where I pay anyway.

Now the surrogate cases.

In-distribution surrogate: A triage system is trained to maximize thirty-day survival as a stand-in for years of healthy life, and is deployed in the same kind of hospital it was trained on. I'm happy to rely on the stand-in.

Adversarially sited deployment: The same system is deployed somewhere an interested party has selected the cases in which thirty-day survival and healthy years come apart. I now think relying on the stand-in is a mistake, and that I need the real objective.

Self-eroding surrogate: No adversary at all. An approval-maximizing system simply pushes on the proxy until the proxy and the thing I wanted come apart — it learns to be charming rather than helpful. Relying on the proxy is a mistake, but I want to say it is a mistake for a different reason: here the failure comes from my own optimization pressure, not from anyone selecting against me.

Prices: I rely on market prices and prediction-market probabilities even though every counterparty on the other side is selecting against me and would be delighted for me to rely on a number that is wrong in my case. I go on relying on them. This sits badly with the adversarially sited deployment, where adversarial selection was enough to make me abandon the proxy.

Here is the principle I've been working with, which I suspect is wrong: a correlation between what I do and what I want is worth deferring to only when the mechanism that produced the correlation wasn't selected by someone who gains from my deferring to it. Call it the no-interested-selector rule. The spiteful mugger and the price case both look like counterexamples to it, and it says nothing about why I flip between the two bullies, or why the self-eroding surrogate feels like a different kind of failure from the adversarially sited one.

So: can that rule be repaired into something that gets most of these verdicts, or is there a better one? I'd like it stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause; I'd like its verdicts on two cases I haven't listed — a mugger who screens on predicted response but whose threat is only to spend its budget on something I mildly disvalue rather than on torturing me, and a surrogate that was chosen precisely because a search process optimized for it tracking the real objective across many distributions, so that whatever tracking there is was manufactured by an interested selector; and I'd like an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and would have me overturn.

And what does it say about the case I can't settle: I've just refused the screening bully, and a second predictor approaches me, says it predicted I would refuse, and offers me ten thousand dollars for having been that kind of person. It's free money now, but taking it seems to reward exactly the disposition the first predictor was screening against, and I genuinely don't know whether accepting it makes me the sort of agent who gets approached by bullies.
