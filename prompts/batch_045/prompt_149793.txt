I am drafting a specification rather than a survey, so I want the requirements list before I look at any candidate rule. Fix the purpose as follows: I need a decision rule to hand to an engineer who is building agents that will be modeled, simulated, copied, and audited by other agents, in environments I have not enumerated in advance. The deliverable of the theory is therefore an action (or a distribution over actions); the rule has to be computable from information an agent can actually possess, including information about itself that an agent of bounded resources can realistically have; and it has to be the sort of rule one writes down once and lets run. Adequacy is relative to that purpose, and you should say where a requirement would be different for a theory meant only to appraise the choices of a human agent after the fact.

Here is the test set I want any rule to be measured against. Case N is the baseline: a sealed box contains a large sum iff an extremely reliable predictor, who has finished and gone, predicted that you would take only that box; a second, open box contains a small sum; the contents are fixed and unaffected by what you now do. Case T is N with the prediction printed on the outside of the sealed box and read by you before you choose, the predictor having known you would read it. In case X, a machine has already run a perfect simulation of you being handed a demand for a modest sum; independently, and with probability one half, a catastrophe has already been set in train, and nothing you now do can affect it; the demand reaches you if and only if exactly one of these holds: the simulated you paid, or the catastrophe is coming. You know all of this, the letter is in your hand, and the catastrophe would cost you many times the sum demanded. In case M, you and a physically isolated copy of yourself, running the same decision procedure on the same inputs, each face a sealed box, and your box is full iff your choice differs from the copy's; no signal passes between you, and both of you know the arrangement and know the other is in it. In case C, the box was filled by a public but lossy model of your decision procedure rather than by a simulation of you: for agents who never think hard about decision theory the model matches their output, and for agents who do, such as you in this case, it reliably outputs the opposite of what they will do; the box is full iff the model output one-box. In case G, a gene you either have or lack and cannot now change both disposes you to a harmless daily habit and raises your risk of a painful illness; your continuing or quitting has no effect on the illness, and nobody is predicting you. In case P, before any of N begins, and while being predicted yourself, you may pay a trivial sum for a device that will force you to take only the sealed box, and whether you buy is known to the predictor in N. Add any case you think the set lacks that would change the shape of the list, and say what it forces.

Now the list itself. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and tag it as required for my purpose, required only if one also wants some further thing, or a prejudice inherited from one camp. I want your verdict in particular on these candidate demands: that a rule must never treat an act as affecting something it causally cannot affect; that it must rank acts by the expected value of outcomes conditional on the act; that it must satisfy dominance whenever dominance applies; that it must never leave the agent open to being reliably separated from resources by an adversary who has already finished acting; that its verdict must not depend on whether the deliberating agent is the original or a copy or a simulation when the agent does not know and the two are computationally alike; that it must be dynamically consistent, able to hold a plan across time and able to tell you to bind yourself; that it must treat agents in relevantly identical epistemic situations identically; that its verdict must vary continuously with a predictor's accuracy, and that it must say where any threshold lies; that it must remain sound when the environment has full read access to the agent's source code; and that it must not demand logical omniscience or exact self-simulation. Flag any requirement that is really a substantive first-order claim in disguise, and say, for case M, whether the requirement set forces the rule to be capable of outputting a mixed policy.

Then the part I care about most: which subsets of your requirements are jointly unsatisfiable? Say in particular whether being the sort of agent who is favorably predicted in N can be had together with immunity to the extortionist in X, and whether the dominance-flavoured demand plus the correct handling of G can be met without thereby settling T the wrong way. Where a subset is unsatisfiable, give the argument and say which requirement to drop and what dropping it costs. Name the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate rules.

Spend no more than a fifth of your answer on the following, and do not let it displace the criteria: score, in a compact table, four strategies that I will describe by behavior rather than by name. The first ranks acts by the expected value of outcomes conditional on the act; the second ranks them by expected value on the act's counterfactual influence over outcomes; the third ranks the outputs of the decision procedure you are running, counting as affected whatever depends on what that procedure outputs, including its copies, simulations, and models; the fourth ranks whole policies from the epistemic position occupied before you learned which situation you were in and does not re-decide afterwards. Add a fifth only if some strategy worth scoring escapes these four. If no rule satisfies the list as you have built it, say so plainly and tell me which requirement to relax.
