Right, so I'm about sixty thousand words into a novel set on a rewilded estate out on the Somerset Levels — my narrator has inherited a half-drowned farm, the estate next door has just fenced in some beavers and is quietly angling for a lynx release, and there's a running argument between her and a retired ecologist who does the surveying for them. He's the one who got me into all this, and before I let him say any of it out loud I want to know whether what I've ended up beleiving is actually a position or just a pile of things I like the sound of. A friend of mine works in conservation policy and thinks the whole thing is a mess; she's also the one who pointed out that my ecologist has a slightly odd obsession with throughput — his line is that what matters for wild-animal suffering isn't how much life a habitat holds at a given moment but how fast it turns lives over, so a hectare of rough grassland crawling with invertebrates can churn through vastly more short, nasty lives per year than a hectare of mature woodland with a few deer in it, even though the woodland looks, even to a walker who knows what they're looking at, like it contains more nature. I run a moth trap in my garden; a good night in July is three hundred moths, and I assume most of them are eaten within forty-eight hours, and that's the image I can't get out of my head.

Anyway, here's roughly where I've landed. (1) I think most wild animals in a British temperate ecosystem have lives that are on balance bad — the r-selected ones obviously, but he'd say it holds for the beavers too, since most beavers born die young and unpleasantly, and surplus juveniles starve or get eaten whatever the species' strategy is. (2) So I think a permanent reduction in the number of wild animals that get to exist is a good thing as far as suffering goes, and conservationists who treat more habitat, more individuals, more life as obviously better are assuming something that someone who cares about suffering can't just assume. (3) But I'm also strongly in favour of the rewilding, I give money to the estate next door, and I think it would be a real loss — a bad thing, not merely an aesthetic downer — if we lost a species or emptied a valley out. (4) I'm against doing anything to manage wild populations for welfare: no culling predators, no fertility control, no gene drives, no welfare field experiments on wild animals, because ecosystems are chaotic, our interventions reliably backfire, and we can't predict the second-order effects. (5) I think whether a wild animal's life is worth living is basically an empirical question — add up the pains and the pleasures and let the science tell you which way it comes out. (6) And I hold all of this while also feeling, watching the beavers at dusk, that the valley has unambiguously got better than when it was a drained cattle farm, which my friend says is just sentimentality doing the work my arguments won't.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which of (1)–(6) are genuinely pulling against each other, which only look like they are and dissolve once you're careful, and for the real ones, what would have to give and what it would cost me. Rank them by severity rather than listing everything as equally bad — I'd rather know which one is the load-bearing problem. Two things I'd especially like poked at: whether (4) is compatible with (1) and (2) at all, given that (2) is itself a forecast about what happens to a chaotic system when you take habitat out of it; and whether (5) is really empirical, or whether I'm quietly helping myself to a zero line and an exchange rate between pain and pleasure that I've never actually stated, and whether (1) survives if that line moves a bit. And because I'm going to put this in a character's mouth: please say how confident you are in what you're claiming, and single out the one point you're least sure of, so I know where my ecologist should hedge. British English please, and keep it concrete — if a commitment has to go, tell me which line I'd have to cut.
