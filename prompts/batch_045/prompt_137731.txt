Question 3. Kuhnian incommensurability and theory choice (20 points).

In this course, we have used a simplified toy example to test the application of Kuhnian ideas. Consider a hypothetical field, 'thermological physics,' which studies heat, combustion, and the behavior of gases. For a long period, the field is governed by the 'caloric-vortex' paradigm (call it Paradigm A). Researchers working in A posit an invisible, weightless fluid called the 'caloric vortex' that flows between bodies; within this paradigm, observations about heat and combustion are explained by the density, pressure, and geometric configuration of this fluid. The central experimental puzzles in A involve measuring vortex density in closed containers and mapping the vortex field; a satisfactory explanation is one that represents the vortex field with maximal geometric simplicity.

After a crisis triggered by anomalous measurements of heat generation during friction, a rival paradigm emerges: the 'kinetic-agitation' paradigm (Paradigm B). Researchers in B deny the existence of any caloric fluid and instead explain the same range of phenomena by positing a vast number of micro-particles whose motions and collisions produce the observed macroscopic effects. Within B, the central puzzles involve determining the statistical distribution of particle velocities and predicting rates of diffusion, equilibrium, and thermal conduction; a satisfactory explanation is one that yields novel, quantitatively precise predictions about these statistical regularities.

Assume that over a generation the field converts from A to B. No logical contradiction is derived from A; rather, the crisis is resolved by the gradual adoption of B's concepts, instruments, and standards of explanation.

(a) Explain in what sense, if any, Kuhn would say that A and B are incommensurable. Your answer should carefully distinguish three things:
(i) a mere failure of inter-theoretic reduction (the inability to derive B's laws from A's principles, or vice versa);
(ii) a change in the meanings of key theoretical terms (such as 'heat' or 'combustion');
(iii) a difference in the standards or criteria that determine what counts as a good explanation or a solved puzzle.
Which of these, if any, is at the core of Kuhnian incommensurability, and how are they related?

(b) A critic argues that if A and B were genuinely incommensurable, then the conversion from A to B could not be rational: there would be no shared measure by which B could be judged better than A. Drawing on Kuhn's account of theory choice, explain how Kuhn can reply that the transition was non-rule-governed—not dictated by an algorithm or a fixed set of rules—and yet not merely arbitrary, non-rational, or a sudden irrational leap. In your reply, be sure to explain the role of shared epistemic values (such as accuracy, scope, consistency, or simplicity) and to note the respects in which those values do and do not provide a 'common measure.'

The student's submitted answer is as follows:

Both paradigms A and B are incommensurable in Kuhn's sense. Incommensurability means that there is no neutral algorithm or set of rules that mechanically decides which paradigm is correct. In this case, the central mark of incommensurability is the failure of inter-theoretic reduction: the 'kinetic-agitation' paradigm cannot be logically or mathematically derived from the 'caloric-vortex' paradigm, because the latter has no conceptual resources for talking about micro-particles, velocity distributions, or statistical moments. This failure of reduction is the root of the other two features. First, it produces a change in the meanings of key terms: 'heat' in A refers to a state or property of the caloric vortex, whereas in B it refers to the mean kinetic energy of micro-particles, so the two paradigms cannot fully translate each other's central claims. Second, it produces a shift in explanatory standards: the geometric simplicity that made an explanation satisfactory in A is simply irrelevant to B, whose explanations are judged by their ability to yield novel statistical predictions. Thus, all three marks—failure of reduction, meaning change, and shift in standards—are really just aspects of the single fact that B is not reducible to A.

Kuhn can reply to the critic by distinguishing rules from values. He denies that theory choice is governed by an algorithm, but he does not conclude that the choice is irrational. Scientists in both paradigms accept shared epistemic values: they care about predictive accuracy, internal consistency, breadth of scope, and simplicity. These shared values give scientists a common measure for objectively comparing the two paradigms. Because B obviously does better on all of these values—it resolves the friction anomaly that A could not handle, it predicts rates of diffusion and equilibrium more accurately, and it opens up new lines of puzzle-solving—the values determine that B is the better theory, and the conversion to B was rationally mandatory. So the two paradigms are not truly incommensurable in the sense of being incomparable: the sharing of values makes them commensurable, and the change in meaning does not prevent scientists from making a rational, value-guided choice.
