I'm on the Community Benefit Committee of a nonprofit regional health system. We control one discretionary fund of about $3.1M for the fiscal year, and we have to vote on how to spend it by the 28th, so I need something I can circulate before then.

Here's the case. There's a four-year-old in our catchment — I'll call her Maya — with a rare pediatric neurodegenerative condition. Her team's prognosis without treatment is roughly two years; with a one-time gene therapy priced at $2.65M she'd likely survive into adulthood with significant but manageable disability. If we fund it, we zero out two things: a home-visiting nurse program for pregnant patients in three rural counties, and a school-based asthma program covering about 600 kids. Our own analysts say the visiting program averts somewhere around nine infant deaths over five years, but the interval they gave us runs from 2 to 19, and one of our members points out (correctly, I think) that the underlying evidence for home visiting is mixed. The asthma program is mostly morbidity and quality of life, not deaths.

Two things make this a real tradeoff rather than a shell game. First, most of those two programs sits on a restricted prevention grant that legally cannot be moved into treatment, so we can't just reorder things. Second, Maya's family has raised about $410K on GoFundMe, and our policy says money raised for a specific patient can't be applied to a therapy we declined — half the committee thinks that policy is heartless, half thinks it's the only thing keeping us honest.

The committee is split in a way that feels philosophical rather than factual. One side says: we are being asked to let a child we know by name die so that children we will never meet might not, and that is using her as currency. The other side says the symmetry argument — nine statistical deaths are nine deaths, our reaction to the named child is the identifiable-victim effect dressed up as principle, and if the anti-aggregation point forbids trading her for them it forbids trading them for her too, which means it can't be the whole story. I honestly don't know which of those is stronger, and I want the memo to say which one it's voting against and why that one is genuinely hard.

Three procedural devices have been floating around and I want you to rule on them — adopt the ones you think are right, reject the ones you think are confused, and don't feel obliged to use all three:

- the naming test: we run the decision twice, once with Maya's name and file in front of us and once with her replaced by 'one statistical four-year-old with the same prognosis drawn from our catchment,' and if the two votes come out differently, that gap gets recorded as a finding we have to explain — either a bias to be corrected or a fact about what we owe people we can identify;
- the discharge condition: before we count any net gain from taking something from one group to give it to another, we have to name an actual in-kind transfer back to the losers, funded in the same motion, or state on the record why no such transfer is possible and why that doesn't defeat the argument;
- two ledgers: losses that destroy a life or the capacities a life plan needs go in a different ledger from setbacks that happen within a life, and offsetting across the ledgers needs its own justification rather than just bigger numbers on one side.
