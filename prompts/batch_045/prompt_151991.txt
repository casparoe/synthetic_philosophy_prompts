I'm about 60k words into a novel and I've painted myself into a corner, so I want to borrow a brain that isnt mine.

The setup: a small city art museum, leaky roof, one overworked conservator — that's my protagonist, Marla. For forty years a gloomy little panel painting of a frozen canal has hung in the stairwell, labeled "attributed to Willem Schaft," who is a nobody, and nobody has looked at it twice. Then during a routine clean Marla finds, under later overpaint, a fragment of a signature and some underdrawing that she's fairly sure means the thing is actually by Hendrik Oosterling, who is a very big deal. Don't worry about whether the conservation science is right; I'll fix that later. Keep the technical stuff vague enough that a real conservator wouldn't snort.

Here's the knot, and it's the part I can't get right. An old picture framer in town — Ezra — has spent twenty years forging paperwork, never pictures. Letters between dealers, an inventory list, a bill of sale, a penciled note in the margin of an exhibition catalogue. He has never touched a painting in his life. He is also, it turns out, right: he worked the attribution out decades ago, and the documents he invented happen to describe what is actually true. He wants the painting recognized because the museum let him go in 1998 and this is his revenge, or his vindication, and he couldn't tell you which. So Marla ends up holding a true conclusion that rests on evidence she now knows is manufactured, and she can't separate the true part from the invented part without throwing away both.

And the other half of it: the moment the painting is an Oosterling, it stops being theirs. Roped off, climate case, insurance, a loan to a bigger museum, and then the trustees quietly sell it. The people who loved it as a shabby stairwell picture — the guard who has walked past it for thirty years, Marla's father — lose it at exactly the moment it becomes valuable. So the truth about the original destroys whatever was good about having it, while the lie would have preserved that, at the cost of the record.

What I want from you is a short story, 1500–2500 words, that works through this rather than just illustrating it. Marla has to decide something concrete near the end and live with it — no shrugging, no closing line about how she never really knew for sure. Give the case for the record real teeth: have someone (a registrar, a curator, a trustee) argue that a claim backed by forged evidence is worse than no claim at all, and that a painting whose paper trail has been faked has been damaged even though nobody laid a finger on it. And give the other side a real voice too — someone who thinks the painting never cared who painted it, and that what Ezra did was testify, badly.

Don't let anyone state the philosophy out loud. No character gives a small lecture about authenticity or about why originals matter. The argument should be in what people do and what they lose. And please skip the easy outs: no fire, no theft, no deathbed confession, no auction-room reveal, no long-lost descendant turning up.

Its fine if you change names, genders, the era, the country — I'll take the story apart and use the bones, so it doesn't need to match my characters voices. Just don't make it a puzzle with a solution; make it a thing that happens to people.
