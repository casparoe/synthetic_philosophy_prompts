I've got a revise-and-resubmit deadline hanging over me and a graduate seminar on reasons and rationality at the same time, and a student asked me something in office hours that I answered really badly, so now I'm obsessing over it. The setup: the fitting-attitude account of value says, roughly, that for something to be valuable is for there to be reason to favor it, and the famous objection is the money case — a stranger offers you a million dollars to admire him, and now you have a reason to admire him, but it isn't the sort of reason the analysis was after, since it tracks nothing about him. Everyone then goes hunting for a principled way to pick out the reasons of the right kind, and I've never found any of those proposals satisfying; they all feel reverse-engineered from the handful of cases we already have intuitions about.

Here's where I want to be pushed. I'm starting to think this isn't a problem about value at all, but one instance of a mismatch that shows up anywhere a state has correctness conditions that don't line up with the reasons there are for being in it. Belief is the obvious parallel: the million dollars isn't evidence, so it also isn't a reason to believe you're a poached egg, and nobody treats that as a crisis for the theory of epistemic reasons. You can run the same thing for intention, and probably for assertion too.

Please argue that the wrong kind of reasons problem is not really about value, but is a special case of that general mismatch, and that, seen this way, the standard fix (restrict the analysis to reasons of the right kind) is definately less ad hoc than it looks, because the very same move is independently available and independently motivated for belief, intention, and assertion, where nobody thinks they need a special clause. Then say what the general account of right-kind-ness would have to be, and what it implies about a case I don't think anybody has discussed: a scientist is offered a large prize if she comes to genuinely believe some hypothesis that is in fact true but very badly supported by her evidence, and then publishes in favor of it. Wrong-kind reason for the belief, or not, and why?

Lead with your conclusion in one sentence, then give me the argument in no more than 400 words. No headings, no bullets, and please dont spend words recapping the literature or naming papers — I've read them, I want the argument.
