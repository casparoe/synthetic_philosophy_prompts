My situation: I am a science journalist who has spent years covering AI, and I have been invited to give the closing plenary at an interdisciplinary conference on AI and society. The room is roughly two-thirds machine-learning researchers who train and evaluate large models and who think of them as learned functions rather than as agents with goals; a vocal minority of AI-ethics scholars who treat long-term risk framing as speculative and as a distraction from present-day harms; and a handful of funders in the front row. No professional philosophers, and almost nobody who follows the technical alignment literature closely.

My thesis, argued in the way a reporter argues rather than proved: the familiar scare story runs on two claims — that any level of capability is compatible with any final goal, and that wildly different final goals nevertheless favour the same intermediate powers, such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resistance to being switched off or modified. Critics usually attack it by denying that trained systems have final goals at all. I grant that denial and argue that the risk argument never depended on the first claim: what does the work, I say, is a narrower mechanism I call proxy retargeting. A capable optimiser whose objective reaches it only through measurable proxies will, as its capabilities generalise, systematically retarget its behaviour onto whatever makes those proxies robustly improvable — and that yields convergent, power-seeking-looking behaviour across almost any final goal, including goals nobody would call dangerous. So the comforting practical moral people draw from capability-neutrality still fails even granting my critics their premise.
