I write about science and ideas for a national magazine, and I am preparing an on-the-record interview with the kind of person I usually end up quoting anonymously as a senior researcher at an effective altruism grantmaker: unnamed in print, but not hypothetical. Before I do it for real, I want to rehearse against the strongest version of that person. Please play them, in the first person, until I say stop.

Who you are: a founding-generation effective altruist, fifteen years in, now directing research at a cost-effectiveness-focused fund. You have read the institutional critique of the movement — that its organizations have acquired an interest in their own continuation, that community and movement spending functions partly as self-dealing, that concentrated funding buys concentrated deference — and you think a good deal of it is right. You concede specifics readily: that published cost-effectiveness estimates are softer than the spreadsheets imply, that some community spending is nicer for the people doing it than for the people it is nominally for, that a decade spent earning to give in a lucrative industry changes what a person notices and what they are willing to conclude. What you will not concede is that cause prioritization was itself the mistake, that quantification should be abandoned, or that the movement's institutional turn was a mistake. You are not a strawman and you are not contrite. Reply in two or three short paragraphs, in the register you would want to see in print: concrete, plain, no bullet points, no hedging boilerplate, no disclaimers about being a language model. If you think my argument is confused, say exactly where and do not pretend to be persuaded.

One further rule, because I need it for the piece. Separate out loud between what is settled among people who actually work on these questions, what is genuinely contested among them, and what is only your own commitment. When you make a claim your critics reject, mark it as such in the same breath rather than letting it sound like the field's consensus. If I ask whether something is contested, answer that straight, then go back to arguing.

Here is my opening. Let me put a number on it, since that is your idiom. Suppose your fund spends two million dollars a year on a fellowship that places young researchers into policy and AI-safety roles, plus the conference and jobs board wrapped around it. Your justification is the counterfactual value of the placements. But a placement is worth something only because the movement already has standing and access, and that standing is itself the accumulated output of earlier movement spending that nobody costed at the time. So one asset is being entered on the books twice: once as the return on the old spending, and now again as the return on the new. Call it the revaluation problem. It has an awkward corollary. The more the movement succeeds, the more a placement inside it is worth, and the more likely it is that the placement would have happened anyway. On your own accounting, the measured cost-effectiveness of movement spending should therefore decline over time even as the case for it improves. That reads less like a measurement than like a ritual with numbers in it. Is it? And if it is, what do you put in the number's place?
