I am revising a paper on personal identity in which I want to press into service the center-of-narrative-gravity picture of the self: the proposal that a self stands to the narratives a human being produces, is guided by, and is made to figure in, roughly as a center of gravity stands to the mass distribution of an extended physical body. I need the comparison to carry two conclusions. The first is deflationary: the self is not an additional ingredient of the world over and above the stream of behavior, speech, and storytelling, so there is no inner witness, no substantial ego, nothing that could be discovered by turning away from the narratives to something behind them. The second does the real work in my argument: a self can remain strictly the same through complete turnover of its material and psychological base, in the way a body's center of gravity can stay exactly where it is while every particle in the body is replaced or redistributed, provided the resultant remains stationary. A referee has objected that the second claim is the load-bearing one and that the analogy is carrying all of it: a center of gravity is the stock example of something abstract, non-substantial and causally idle, so modeling the self on it settles in advance the very question my paper is supposed to answer.

What I want is an audit of the comparison rather than a verdict on the view. Please proceed in the following order.

First, set out the mapping explicitly, in a table if that is clearest: what plays the role of the extended body, of mass, of spatial position, of the weighting or integration rule by which the center is computed, and of the center itself. Be explicit about the fact that the elements on the target side are representations rather than bits of matter, and say what, for a narrative, plays the role that mass plays for a particle: frequency of telling, causal efficacy in guiding action, emotional centrality, uptake by other people, or something else. Say also what plays the role of spatial position, given that narratives are ordered in time and in narrative role rather than in space.

Second, work through the candidate disanalogies and tell me, for each, whether it damages my two conclusions or is merely cosmetic. The ones I can see are these: a center of gravity is causally inert, whereas the self is supposed to be the author of the narratives and the locus of agency and responsibility; a body has exactly one center of mass, whereas a person can sustain several internally coherent but mutually inconsistent narrative streams; mass is a conserved, intrinsically measurable quantity combined by a fixed rule, whereas narrative weight has no unit, no conservation law and no privileged weighting; the mass distribution belongs to the body intrinsically, whereas many of the narratives that make up a self are authored by other parties; the center of gravity is defined over elements that are not about it, whereas autobiographical narratives are already about a self, which threatens circularity; the center of gravity is a determinate point that any competent observer locates in the same place, whereas any narrative resultant seems to shift with the interpreter's weighting; and a center of gravity can fall outside the material body altogether, in empty space.

Third, apply the mapping to two cases I use in the paper and tell me what the analogy commits me to in each. In the Fission Case, after a certain event a subject sustains two autobiographical streams, each as internally integrated as the other, mutually inconsistent on matters of importance, each endorsed by half of her community, with nothing further available to break the tie. How many centers of narrative gravity are there? If the analogy is taken strictly and the streams are weighted equally, does the self turn out to be a single point lying between them, in a region occupied by neither, as the center of mass of a binary system lies in the empty space between the two bodies? Is that a welcome result or a reductio? If instead the honest answer is two selves, what becomes of the claim that there is no further fact beyond the resultant? In the Ghostwritten Subject, nearly the whole autobiographical record is produced by other parties, a biographer, a family, an institution, and the subject subsequently acts on those stories, which gradually reshape her conduct. Where is the center of narrative gravity here, and does the analogy force the conclusion that the self is the resultant of stories the subject did not author?

Finally, tell me whether my two conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the gravitational comparison was doing the arguing for me. If it was, propose a better analogue for the specific work I need done, whether a legal person, a corporate agent, a focal point in a coordination game, a dominant attractor in a dynamical system, the implied author of a text, or something else you think fits, and say what that analogue would license that the gravitational one cannot, and what accepting it would cost me.
