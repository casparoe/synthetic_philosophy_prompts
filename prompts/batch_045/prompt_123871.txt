You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive science who has published on the extended mind thesis and on the metaphysics of cognitive artefacts. Please think this through carefully: before you answer, spend a paragraph or two brainstorming what a practice like the one below could be for, and what the least charitable reading of it would be, so that you don't simply rationalise the status quo. Then give me the reconstruction.

I sit on my university's assessment committee, and we are rewriting the rules on which external resources students may use in exams. The existing patchwork is what I want you to analyse. Closed-book exams are the default, but many courses permit one handwritten page of notes or an instructor-supplied formula sheet. Printed or photographed solutions to similar problems are banned even when the student made them herself. Calculators are permitted in some courses and not others, apparently depending on whether the course is meant to certify arithmetic fluency. Spell-check and error-flagging grammar tools are fine; autocomplete that proposes sentence continuations is not. Dictation software and human scribes are granted as accommodations to students with diagnosed dysgraphia, while the very same software used by a student without a diagnosis counts as a violation. And our honour code defines academic dishonesty as presenting as one's own work, or knowledge, that is not one's own.

The last clause is what keeps bothering me, because it looks like the whole system rests on an ownership relation between a person and a piece of knowledge that I can't spell out — and because the thing we permit (a formula sheet, i.e. an external store consulted at need) is structurally the closest to the paradigm case in the extended mind literature, while the thing we forbid (a tool that transforms input into output) is the case people usually treat as merely causal scaffolding. So: what theory of cognition, and of the boundary between a person and her tools, does this patchwork presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view in the vocabulary of the extended mind debate, including whether it has to commit itself to anything like a parity principle or to the familiar checklist about an external resource being reliably available, automatically endorsed and easily accessible (I'm paraphrasing that from memory, so correct me if I have it wrong). Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: does any part of it actually require the falsity of the extended mind thesis, or is every rule compatible with it, and does the storage-versus-processing distinction our rules rely on survive scrutiny? Tell me too whether the practice is better explained without any metaphysics at all — as a view about what a grade certifies, about credit, fairness, or portability across contexts — and, if so, which rival theories of mind would license exactly the same rules, so that the rules are philosophically underdetermined.

Finally, be concrete about the counterfactual: if the committee took the extended mind thesis seriously, what would have to change? Is a closed-book exam then morally akin to testing a student with part of her memory temporarily disabled, and if the analogy fails, where exactly does it fail? What becomes of the honour code's ownership language — what could it mean for a belief or a piece of knowledge to be someone's own — and what becomes of the line between an accommodation and a cheat, given that both work by coupling a student to an external system? Please also flag where you think we are simply inconsistent and where the apparent inconsistency dissolves under a better description, and be explicit about which of your claims are contested in the literature and which are ones you'd defend without hedging. Argue from the structure of the cases rather than from sources: I need something I can circulate to a committee of non-philosophers, so no citations, no footnotes and no suggested reading. Put the reconstruction first, the diagnosis of incoherence second, and any recommendations last, and mark clearly when you are speaking as a philosopher and when you are speaking as someone with a view about how a university should be run.
