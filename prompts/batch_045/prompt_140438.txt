Had a long and slightly annoying argument with a friend last night about how you're supposed to study consciousness without cheating. Shes coming from the phenomenology side, I'm the one who actually has to run the studies, and we ended up on heterophenomenology -- Dennett's version of it, where you take what people say about their experience seriously as data but treat it as text to be interpreted from the third-person standpoint, neither granting it the authority of a special access nor throwing it out as mere behaviour. I use it because it gets me out of arguments I have no way to settle; she says that's exactly the problem, that the method looks fine when a subject says "I saw a red patch" and falls apart the moment they report an absence, a contradiction, or something that couldn't have happened, and that my "neutral" interview is really just my own categories with the microphone pointed at someone else. Neither of us moved, and I realised afterwards that I couldn't have written down what I'd actually do in the hard cases if she'd made me.

So I want it as a procedure rather than a slogan. Concretely: I run the subjective-effects side of a small drug study -- a sedative arm where we're interested in reports of "nothing at all," plus a pilot with a psychedelic -- and the write-up has to go to a regulator, so whatever we do has to be auditable: someone else should be able to take our raw transcripts and land on our categories. The people doing the interviews are undergrad RAs with maybe three hours of training, sessions are 12-15 minutes the next morning, and I can't ask for anything that needs a philosopher standing behind the interviewer's chair. Give me the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure stops and says no, where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one, and what I'm supposed to do about the fact that the order I ask things in visibly changes what people report -- the interviewer is co-authoring the text, and I don't know what the method says to do about that.

Three cases I genuinely don't know how to handle, so run the procedure on them: (1) a participant says flatly "there was nothing, I wasn't there, no time passed," then scores clearly above chance on a recognition test for words we played during that exact window -- do I record "no experience," "experience the subject did not report," or something else, and what am I entitled to conclude? (2) Two participants both tick the same item on our ego-dissolution scale, but one glosses it as "I was the whole room and also nowhere" and the other as "I stopped caring what happened to me" -- can the procedure tell me whether these are the same reported phenomenon, and if it can't, what does it tell me to do with the tick? (3) someone in the psychedelic pilot reports watching the session from the ceiling and describes, accurately, what a nurse was doing at the far end of the room; my PI wants it in the paper, I want it in an appendix, my friend says we're both already doing bad metaphysics -- what does the method actually license me to say? If the honest answer is that this procedure can't deliver something I want out of it -- that free reports can't be used to argue between theories, say -- tell me that plainly, I'd rather find out before I write the protocol. And please keep clear what's actually settled about this method and what's contested among people who work on it: I don't want to write "standard practice" in a document that goes to a regulator for something a good chunk of the field thinks is behaviourism with extra steps. Don't send me off to papers, I already have a reading pile.
