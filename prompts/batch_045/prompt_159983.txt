I am assembling a handout for a final-year seminar in political ethics, and I want its centrepiece to be a decision tree that walks a student from a verdict on one concrete case to a named position on the problem of dirty hands. I do not want a survey of the literature, and I do not want a bibliography or any reading suggestions: what I want is a working classificatory instrument, built from the logical space of the question rather than from a list of familiar labels.

The tree should start every student at the same place: they have read Case A below and have said only that there was nothing else she could have done. From there, each branch node must be a question about a case or about a claim concerning a case, phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which position it leads to. No node may ask the student which theorist or school they agree with, and no node may use the vocabulary of the positions themselves: no tragic, realist, absolutist, consequentialist, deontological, pluralist, or remainder. Where a node question invites the answer it depends, say what it depends on and make that dependency a node of its own.

Six distinctions must each appear somewhere as a branch, though you may rephrase and reorder them:

1. Whether what conflicts with the moral prohibition is itself a moral requirement, or a requirement generated by the office alone.
2. Whether an act can be at once what the agent ought to do and morally wrong, or whether ought and wrong cannot both apply and the appearance of conflict must be dissolved.
3. Whether wrongful action by an office-holder must be concealed or publicly disavowed in order to count as a case of the relevant kind: Case D is built to test this.
4. Whether the wrongness attaches to the person, to the office, or to the act, and whether it survives being passed to a subordinate, an external contractor, or a committee: Case C is built to test this.
5. Whether the agent's reasons for acting can make the difference between the relevant kind of case, ordinary corruption, and mere misfortune: Case E is built to test this.
6. Whether the availability of resignation or refusal defeats the claim of necessity where the replacement would act worse: Case B is built to test this.

The cases, which I want used as the objects of the node questions and each used at least once:

A. A recently elected head of government is told that a detained suspect has information about a device that will kill thousands. She authorises coercive interrogation she regards as a grave violation of the suspect's rights, tells almost no one, and publicly denies it afterwards.
B. A senior civil servant signs off a payment scheme whose design he knows will, through an error the minister refuses to correct, cause a small number of foreseeable deaths. He could refuse; his deputy would sign without hesitation and would quietly widen the scheme.
C. A committee of nine votes, each for different reasons, for a policy that will foreseeably result in drownings at sea. No member intended that outcome, each would have voted against had the others done so, and the vote is procedurally regular and publicly recorded.
D. A government openly legislates, with full publicity and majority support, for a measure a court has ruled violates a constitutional right, and defends it in parliament as necessary. No one is deceived about anything.
E. The same action as in Case A, but she authorises it chiefly because failure would end her administration and she is attached to holding office.

I want seven to nine branch nodes and ten to fourteen leaves, small enough for a class to walk through in a quarter of an hour. Each leaf should carry the name of the position or the word unnamed, followed by one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Among the leaves there must be: an absolutist position; a monist consequentialist position; a position on which the necessity of state is a distinct morality rather than a licence to do wrong; a position on which the act is not really wrong under the right description; a position on which the conflict is real but unresolved and what matters is what the agent can remain faithful to; and a position on which the wrongness belongs to the office rather than the person. In addition, include at least two leaves you take to be defensible but that are rarely named, and mark them unnamed: I am interested in what the space allows, not only in what has been occupied.

Two further things. First, identify at least one combination of answers that my six distinctions make available but that nobody, so far as you can tell, occupies, and say whether it is empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or merely because unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and state what further question would separate them, if any exists. Close with a single paragraph on which two of the six distinctions are load-bearing, in the sense that flipping a student's answer there moves them furthest across the tree, and on what that suggests about where the real disagreement lies.
