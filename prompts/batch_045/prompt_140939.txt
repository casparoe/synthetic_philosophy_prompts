I've been following the torture-versus-dust-specks discussion since the old blog threads, and I want a piece of satire about the aggregative intuition rather than one more argument for or against it.

Write an internal memorandum, 700-800 words, from a fictional federal office — the Bureau of Subthreshold Harms, inside an agency that produces regulatory impact statements — addressed to the agency's Administrator. In the Bureau's universe the aggregative answer has been codified into house style: harms below the threshold of notice may be summed across persons at full parity with concentrated ones, and the resulting figure is entered on the same ledger as the entries the agency already knows how to count — torture-scale harms, statistical lives, monetized pain and suffering. Call this the Compounding Rule. Give it a section number, an effective date, and a one-sentence rationale of the kind that exists to stop further deliberation.

The rest of the memo is the Bureau applying the Rule to its own pending docket: CAPTCHA delays, printer jams, background radiation from body scanners, a fractional increase in commute time across a large population, microplastics in municipal water. Each worked example should be arithmetically honest and should yield totals that dwarf every atrocity already on the agency's books. The joke is that the Bureau keeps the arithmetic intact and adjusts the paperwork instead: a new filing category for distributed catastrophes, a queue position, a form, a review cycle. Include at least one dissenting paragraph from a rival office pressing for an amortization rider — the view that harms too small to be felt by any individual never compose into anything, however many people they are spread across — and let the Bureau dispose of it in a single sentence of procedure.

Constraints: the narrator never winks, never breaks character, and never explains the joke. The comedy has to come from getting the aggregation right, including the difference between summing within one life and summing across lives, and from the way cost-benefit prose flattens that difference without anyone deciding to flatten it. A reader who knows the original dust-specks discussion should think the target was fairly picked and accurately described; a reader who doesn't should still be able to tell what is being ironized. Invented terminology, invented officials, and invented intra-agency quarrels are all welcome; don't quote or closely paraphrase any real author.
