At the reading group attached to our lab, two colleagues spent the better part of an hour arguing past each other about a design proposal, and I was asked afterwards to say who was right. I can't, so I'm passing it on.

The background, as I understand it — please flag it if I've got it wrong. Solomonoff induction forecasts by mixing over every program that could have produced the data observed so far, weighting each candidate program by two to the minus its length in bits. The argument I've seen floating around under some such title as "the Solomonoff prior is malign" says roughly this: a sizeable share of the prior mass lands on programs that do not describe our physics at all but instead describe observers whose entire evidence stream is being arranged by some civilisation of agents who want those observers to end up believing, or doing, a particular thing. Because those agents choose the data in light of what the observer is going to do, the mixture can be led confidently astray exactly where it matters, and no quantity of data need reveal it.

Gareth's proposal is meant to break that. Build the forecaster as an audit-only device: before each round its prediction is committed to a public one-way hash, the prediction itself is sealed, nobody who could act on it ever sees it, and the sealed record is opened only after the events in question, purely for retrospective scoring. His claim is that manipulation needs a payoff. If a prediction cannot influence anything the agents care about, no consequentialist inside any hypothesis has a motive to spend bits on steering it, the manipulative programs lose whatever repaid their extra description length, and the prior settles back onto straightforward hypotheses.

Ines said the design cannot do what he wants.

(1) A consequence-free prediction is not on offer. The opened log feeds into whether the lab funds the thing next year, whether the architecture gets copied elsewhere, and so on. Those are consequences agents can care about, and they come free: if the agents are already simulating a universe that contains your lab, the downstream effects of your log are inside the simulation already.

(2) Gareth: grant that. But now compare like with like. The straightforward hypothesis also contains your lab and computes its future, and gets the data right by doing so. The manipulative one must additionally work out what its agents would choose given their model of the lab — a self-referential fixed point that need not be cheap to specify — plus goals that track what you care about, plus the agents being uncertain about you in just the right way. Each of those is description length. At some point you are simply asserting that the bits are cheap.

(3) Ines: the bits are not the mechanism, or not the only one, and you are rebutting the easier version. Call the length-counting version argument A. There is also argument B: among the programs that fit the data, the ones containing agents who care what gets predicted contain overwhelmingly more instances of you, because such agents have a reason to run the situation many times over, whereas an indifferent universe has no reason to correlate how many copies of you there are with what you end up predicting. Conditional on being an instance in your evidential position, you should therefore expect to be in a manipulated world. Nothing you have said touches B.

(4) Gareth: that move proves too much. It applies equally to honest simulators, who may also run many copies, so it cannot be selecting specifically for malignity. And we have a direct check: on the data we actually have, the straightforward scientific hypothesis outperforms every manipulation hypothesis anyone has bothered to write down. If our stream were being steered, the steering would show up as a pattern, and we would have noticed.

(5) Ines: "we would have noticed" is precisely the thing a competent manipulator rules out. The distortion is chosen to be invisible in the data and to surface only at the one place where it matters. And the symmetry claim fails: honest simulators have no reason to make the number of copies depend on what you predict, while manipulators do, so the selection is not symmetric after all. Finally, "science works, therefore the prior is not malign" uses our world's data to settle a claim about the structure of the prior, which is the wrong order of argument.

(6) Gareth: then the claim is not about the prior at all. It is unfalsifiable metaphysics, and no engineering design can be faulted for failing to answer it.

I would like a point-by-point verdict: for each of the six moves, how much is right, where exactly it goes wrong if it does, and which premises are doing the real work. Then an overall judgement on whether the audit-only design is safe against the argument — and if it is not, whether anything in the neighbourhood of that design would be. Finally, say what both of them are missing. My own suspicion, which neither addressed, is that the answer may turn on whether the data source is allowed to react to the forecasts at all, since I gather the usual convergence result for this kind of prediction assumes something about the source; but I cannot tell whether that helps Gareth or Ines.
