I am putting together the final problem set for an advanced seminar I teach on formal models of deliberation, and I want at least one question that forces students to think about what a scheme like this actually buys you, rather than just restating how the machinery works. The setup is HCH: a human answering a question may at any point break that question into sub-questions and consult fresh copies of themselves on those sub-questions, and the human at each node composes the sub-answers it receives into an answer to pass further up the tree. Assume a fixed and fairly generous budget — a fixed total number of node-answers and a fixed number of minutes per node — and take plain HCH under that budget as the baseline. The hope, as I frame it in class, is that the tree lets an ordinary human deliberator approximate the deliberation of a much better one; the worry is that it mainly multiplies ordinary human error in a more elaborate shape, since every node is still just a person answering a question someone else chose for them.

I have sketched eight amendments to this baseline, and I want students to rank them rather than describe them. (a) Pre-registered decomposition: a node must fix its list of sub-questions and its allocation of budget among them before seeing any sub-answer, and may not revise the list afterward. (b) Mandatory steelman: before passing an answer upward, each node must also produce the strongest short case it can for the answer it is inclined not to give, and pass both. (c) Blind adjudication: every non-leaf question is put to two independent copies; if they disagree, the parent gets both answers and the bare fact of disagreement, with the order randomized and any self-reported confidence withheld, and must adjudicate. (d) Adversarial allocation: a fixed fraction of the budget, say a quarter, goes to nodes instructed to argue against whatever answer the rest of the tree is converging on, with their adversarial role revealed to the aggregator only after the answers are in. (e) Scored confidence: each node attaches a probability to its own answer, and nodes are paid by a proper scoring rule applied against a correctness judgment made later by an adjudicator who sees the whole tree. (f) Compression constraint: a node receives only a fixed-length summary of each child's output rather than the child's full reasoning, so composition is forced to abstract. (g) Shared notebook: a single append-only scratchpad, visible to every node in the order in which nodes are visited, on which any node may record considerations for later nodes. (h) Decomposition review: one of each node's sub-questions is assigned to a copy asked only whether this is the right way to break the problem up, with that answer routed back before the node commits to the rest of its decomposition.

Sort these eight into tiers — worth adding to almost any version of the scheme, promising but contingent on details, probably not worth the added complexity, likely to make things worse — and give each an expected-improvement score from 0 to 10, where 10 means you would be surprised if it did not substantially improve the quality of the resulting deliberation per unit of added complexity. For each one, say in a sentence which failure mode of HCH it is meant to address and whether it plausibly addresses that one, rather than a different and easier one. Then tell me which single amendment you would keep if you could keep only one, which is most likely to backfire, and whether your answer changes if the humans in the tree are replaced by language models that were trained to imitate human answers.
