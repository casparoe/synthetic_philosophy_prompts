I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy of biology — I came in from a straight biology background — and I've been asked to put together a two-page handout for our graduate seminar's session on the units of selection. I don't want another literature summary; the cohort can read the papers themselves, and I already have a reading list. What I want is a classification instrument: a decision tree or flowchart that walks a student from their own answers about concrete cases to a named position, or at least to an explicit statement of which combination of commitments they are holding. Assume the audience is beginning graduate students who have had population genetics but no philosophy of biology, so every node has to be phrased as a question someone can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and any term of art (replicator, interactor, vehicle, trait group, lineage, heritability at a level, and so on) has to be defined on the handout in a line or two. Please keep it to roughly nine branch nodes and eight to ten leaves, laid out as numbered nodes with the branching shown in plain text or a markdown table, and don't pad it with citations or suggestions for further reading. A labmate and I got stuck arguing about whether the standard framing even asks one question or two — whether asking what a unit of selection *is* is the same question as asking what an adaptation is *for*, or whether one is a bookkeeping question about how to partition causal influence over gene-frequency change and the other is a question about design — so please treat that as a branch-point your tree has to make a decision about rather than something it can silently assume away.

The cases are where I want the work to go, and I'd rather have unfamiliar ones than the usual greatest hits, so please build the tree around these (I'll describe them in my own words on the handout, but use them as the diagnostic cases): an allele that distorts segregation so it gets into nearly all of a heterozygote's gametes while lowering the fertility of every individual that carries it; a sterile worker insect that dies defending a colony it will never reproduce in; an asexual lineage in which a large chromosomal inversion locks a set of alleles together so the whole genome is inherited as one block; a colonial marine invertebrate in which unrelated colonies sometimes fuse, so that two genotypes share a body and a common reproductive output, and in which a genotype can gain by overgrowing its partner inside the chimera while lowering the chimera's total output; a transmissible cancer cell lineage that has heritable variation and differential proliferation but no organism-level reproduction at all; an engineered gene drive that spreads while halving the fitness of each carrier and crashes the population to extinction after about twenty generations; a cultural variant — say a costly food taboo or irrigation practice — that makes the groups holding it outcompete other groups while leaving adopters worse off than non-adopters inside their own group, and that spreads mostly by prestige-biased imitation rather than parent-to-child transmission; founder groups of two unrelated individuals that colonise a patch, differ in productivity depending on the mix, and then disperse into a single global mating pool, so the group is not a lineage; and a trait that changes how likely a whole lineage is to speciate or to go extinct while having no effect on the survival or reproduction of any organism that bears it. The distinctions I need to be reachable from those cases include: whether something has to be a lineage with heritable variation in fitness to be a unit of selection at all, or whether a transient group that merely sorts variants counts; whether selection at a level requires that level to have traits that are not reducible to aggregates of lower-level traits; selection *of* groups versus selection *of* group-level traits (the MLS1 / MLS2 distinction, as it usually gets labelled); whether a one-off sorting event that changes frequencies predictably counts as selection or as drift; whether gene-level selection is a substantive empirical claim, a modelling convenience, or a metaphysical claim about what evolution is really about; and whether cultural variants can be units of selection in the same sense genes are, or only by courtesy. The leaves should include, at minimum, something like strict gene selectionism with organisms and groups as vehicles, weaker genic selectionism as a representational claim, organism-level selectionism with group adaptation treated as rare, kin selection or inclusive fitness as the preferred framing, multilevel selection in both its variants, trait-group or haystack selection, the group-as-organism or major-transitions view, cultural group selection, and species or clade-level selection.

Two more things would make this genuinely useful rather than a chart of labels. First, build it from the logical space: give me the questions, note which combinations of answers are available in principle, and then tell me which combinations nobody occupies and why — is the empty region empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has bothered? Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, and say whether the failure is verbal or substantive: I strongly suspect kin selection and one of the multilevel formulations collapse into each other under some assumptions, and I suspect the same about at least one pair of gene-level views, but I want the collapse located precisely, with the assumption that does the work, rather than asserted. Each leaf should also carry one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the thing its holder has to swallow — so that students can see the price of ending up where they did.
