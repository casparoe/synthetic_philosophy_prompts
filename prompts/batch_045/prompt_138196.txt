I'm in a small reading group on value theory — three philosophy grad students and an economist, meeting weekly — and we've spent the last several sessions on hard choices: the cases where you must pick between two options and it seems false to say that one is better, false to say they're equally good, and false to say you're merely ignorant or confused. I came in wanting to defend three claims and have ended up unable to hold all three at once. They are: (i) some hard choices are genuine, in the strong sense that the options really stand in some fourth relation — something other than better, worse, or equally good — and it isn't just vagueness or uncertainty; (ii) at least some goods are not tradeable against money-like goods at any finite rate, so that no finite amount of the latter compensates for the loss of the former; (iii) the comparative facts are perfectly sharp — for any two options there is a determinate fact about which relations hold between them, even if we can't discover it.

Here is the argument I built for last session. Take A to be a life spent as a professional cellist in a decent regional orchestra and B a life spent as an immigration lawyer, and suppose, per (i), that A and B are incomparable. (1) Small improvements exist: for any option X and any good g there is an option X+g that is better than X by an arbitrarily small margin, where the margin is measured scale-relatively — small compared with the option it is added to, not merely small in absolute units. (2) Insensitivity: if X is incomparable with Y, then X+g is not better than Y. Otherwise the hardness of the choice would be hostage to penny-level adjustments and could be dissolved by tossing in a dollar, and the point at which the extra dollar made the difference would be arbitrary. (3) Composition: (2) holds at each step of a chain, so if A is incomparable with B, then A improved by any finite sequence of small improvements is still not better than B. (4) Cross-currency cycling: the sequence in (3) may draw on different goods at different steps — a little money, a little health, a little recognition, a little money again — and cycle among them indefinitely, so no single good's threshold can be held responsible for any change in the relation, while every step stays small relative to the option it modifies. (5) But then no finite amount of money, health, recognition or combination of them, however large, makes the cellist life better than the lawyer life — and that is absurd; the cellist life with all of that plus ten additional healthy years plainly is better. (6) So A was never incomparable with B; nothing is incomparable with anything; there are no hard choices in my sense.

I reject (6). But blocking the argument earlier seems to cost me something else, and this is where I'm stuck. My economist friend says the culprit is (1): scale-relative smallness isn't smallness, because after enough multiplicative steps the gap is enormous even though each step was small. I don't see why that matters, given that each step *was* small relative to the pair being compared at that step. If I reject (2) instead, I owe an account of where in the chain A stops being incomparable with B and starts being better — and since every step is tiny, that cliff looks both sharp, as (iii) demands, and completely arbitrary, which makes me suspect (iii) is the real casualty. And if I reject (5), I seem to be committed to a version of (ii) for every pair of incomparable options and every good, which is far more lexical priority than I wanted and, in the cellist case, plainly false.

So: name the step you would reject, and explain why it looked inoffensive — what makes it plausible enough to have survived my writing it down. Then price the alternatives: for each of the two or three most attractive exits, what does the view that takes it have to give up, including what it has to say about the cliff, about cycling across goods, and about cases where the chain runs downward through small worsenings instead. If part of my framing is confused — if "incomparable," or "small improvement," or "option" is doing two jobs that I've conflated between steps — say which, and where the equivocation sits. If it helps, assume I'd rather keep (i) and (ii) than (iii), but tell me what holding (i) actually commits me to about the structure of the comparative relation, and whether anything I've said distinguishes genuine incomparability from a merely indeterminate betterness verdict dressed up in more flattering language.
