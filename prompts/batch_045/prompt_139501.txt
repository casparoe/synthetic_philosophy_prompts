I was at a dinner party last weekend where two colleagues — call them Marta and Jonas — got into an argument about whether a neutral country with conscription (Finland was the working example) should replace it with an all-volunteer professional force. I've been reading a bit about deterrence by denial versus deterrence by punishment, and about how shrinking mobilization windows are supposed to change the value of mass reserves, so I had some vocabulary for it, but I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, and neither could anyone else at the table. They went back and forth roughly like this. (1) Marta opened by saying conscription is forced labor: roughly a year of a young person's life taken under threat of criminal sanction, and levied on an arbitrarily narrow slice of the population — in practice young men — which makes it both coercive and discriminatory. A volunteer force lets people sort by comparative advantage: the state buys military labor from whoever values it least relative to their alternatives, and everyone else pays in money. Coercion is replaced by a price, which is how we handle every other kind of work. (2) Jonas replied that conscription is not only a way of raising an army but a political technology: when the people who decide on war must send their own children, and the officer corps is drawn from the whole society, the domestic political cost of using force goes up. A professional army removes that check — it is an instrument a government can employ with almost nobody outside the military having skin in the game — so the volunteer model isn't just a different funding mechanism, it deletes a constraint on the state. (3) Marta said that in a neutral country the constraint has nothing to constrain: there is no expeditionary war for such a government to launch, the army exists purely to make invasion unprofitable, so the civic-republican benefit Jonas described is worth about zero in the case they were actually arguing about, and all that remains is the coercion. (4) Jonas answered that even for pure territorial defense the two models purchase different things, and proposed that conscription be understood as storing military capacity as latency: the state pays in the cheap time of nineteen-year-olds and receives a large trained reserve it can call up later; money budgets are small and contested, whereas young adults' time is abundant and, to the state, nearly free. He added that such a stored capacity is distributed through the population, so no future parliament can quietly disband it or hand it over the way it could a standing force — Sweden's own reversal on conscription being, to him, evidence that a professional founding is reversible in a way a militia society is not. (5) Marta countered that "stored capacity" is too flattering: skills decay, mass without readiness is not deterrence but a target set, mobilization takes weeks, and precision strike and cyber attack have compressed the window in which reserves can be converted into combat power — so at equal budgets you may not be buying capability at all, just a training liability and a mobilization plan that may not survive contact. (6) Jonas said deterrence isn't about the mean of combat power but about the attacker's uncertainty: a large citizen reserve widens the variance of outcomes for an invader, and a state contemplating a land grab is typically risk-averse about the tail in which the campaign goes very badly; a small professional force has a tighter, more predictable distribution, which makes the gamble easier to price. (7) Marta closed by saying variance cuts both ways — a force of partly trained reservists also has a tail in which it collapses, and collapse is precisely what invites intervention — and that Jonas keeps walking past the deeper tension: armed neutrality is a bargain, not a shield. You must be costly enough to attack to deter, and small enough in appearance not to threaten; conscription improves the first horn and erodes the second, because a nation-in-arms next door reads as latent offense, whereas a professional force is easier to size, cap, declare, and therefore easier to reassure with.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the seven moves, say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, half right, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, and why — and be explicit where a move is dialectically effective but doesn't actually answer the previous one. Beyond the blow-by-blow, I'd like you to take up four things I couldn't settle. First, whether Marta's coercion objection is better modeled as an objection to forced labor or as an objection to a tax in kind, and whether the verdict shifts if the obligation is gender-neutral, if it can be discharged through alternative civilian service, or if it can be bought out with money. Second, whether Jonas's "raises the political cost of war" argument really has no purchase for a state that only ever fights defensively, or whether it reappears in some other form once you ask who sets the thresholds for calling up the reserve or for resisting at all. Third, whether deterrence by denial is best modeled as a function of expected combat power, of the variance of outcomes, or of the attacker's uncertainty about its own estimates — and what each of those three models separately implies about conscript versus professional force structure, since Jonas's variance point struck me as clever but possibly orthogonal. Fourth, whether the neutrality-versus-reassurance tradeoff in move 7 is a genuine dilemma, and if so what shape the optimum has. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing: I have a suspicion that the real choice isn't the binary they were fighting over (short universal service, a professional core plus a part-time territorial guard, reserve-only obligations, civilian national service), and that neither of them asked who ends up bearing the physical risk under each arrangement, or what conscription does as a socialization and state-capacity institution rather than as a way of producing soldiers. I don't need a policy recommendation for any actual country — I want to know who was right about what in the argument as it was actually made.
