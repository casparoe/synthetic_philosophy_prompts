I sit on the Committee on Educational Policy at my college, and in three weeks we have to send a recommendation to the faculty senate about a degree requirement that nobody currently on the committee can justify. The rule dates from a 1973 curricular overhaul: every student must take two courses outside their own academic division, and—this is the part that always trips people up—both of those courses must be in the same department, so a student can't satisfy breadth with four unrelated one-off electives. The registrar's file contains a single paragraph from 1973 saying that breadth without sequence is tourism, and nothing else. The committee reauthorizes the rule every five years, mostly by not thinking about it, and our assessment office confirms it has no outcome data in either direction. This time the politics are sharper: two colleagues want it struck as unnecessary friction on transfer students and double majors; the chairs of Classics and Geology have each written me long, careful, oddly emotional letters defending it without quite saying what it does; a colleague in political theory argued at our last meeting that removing it would change something about the college that no mission statement captures; and two members have privately pointed out that the small departments enroll a noticeable share of their seats through it, which is true, which is the most powerful explanation anyone has offered, and which nobody will say out loud in open session because it sounds like defending a requirement for budgetary reasons.

I've been reading around in Burkean conservatism and in the Chestertonian version of the same instinct—the one about not tearing down a fence until you know why it was put up—and I don't want the memo to be a piece of applied Burke, but I do want it to take the underlying problem seriously: that an inherited practice can carry information no individual reasoner has, that the demand to state a rule's purpose may be unmeetable even when the rule is doing real work, and that the people who benefit from a rule are often the least able to articulate what it's for. To make this tractable I've sketched a rough taxonomy that I'd like you to use or to replace: load-bearing (still doing the original job), converted (original rationale dead but now serving some unacknowledged function), vestigial (pure cost), and expressive (its value isn't causal at all—it's part of what the institution means, and repealing it changes the self-description rather than the outcomes). I've also sketched two tests I think matter more than the standard question "do we know what it's for?": a reversibility-and-legibility test, on the view that the Burkean presumption should weaken sharply when a repeal is cheap to reverse and when we have some mechanism for noticing what we lost before the loss becomes permanent; and a counterfactual-replacement test, on the view that the real choice is almost never rule-versus-no-rule but rule-versus-whatever the senate will invent to fill the vacuum—in this case almost certainly a mandatory interdisciplinary capstone that would be far more burdensome than what we have. What I need is the memo itself: a bottom line at the top, choosing among retaining the rule unchanged, repealing it, sunsetting it in two years pending a study, suspending it for one entering cohort with a pre-registered plan for observing the effects, or converting it—naming the function we actually think it serves and forcing an open vote on that function. It should give reasons my colleagues can reply to rather than reasons that merely win, and it should give the losing side its best day in court: I take the strongest anti-Burkean point here to be that the fence principle is nearly free to invoke, unfalsifiable in practice, and ratchets toward inertia, since any rule whatsoever can be defended by gesturing at purposes nobody has discovered yet. I'd also like a short appendix saying how the memo thinks we should handle the enrollment hypothesis—including whether the honest move is to route this to a body that can weigh money out loud.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1,100 words, with headings and a one-paragraph summary I can read aloud at the start of the meeting. One more thing, which matters to me more than the recommendation: as you write, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material—for instance, whether the Burkean presumption in favour of inherited arrangements is best read as an epistemic claim about distributed experience or merely as a precautionary rule under uncertainty, and whether Chesterton's principle is really a demand for understanding or a demand for standing—and don't let the memo quietly present the contested reading as the consensus one. Mark explicitly every empirical claim that our actual record doesn't support, including any claim about what the rule is currently doing. Give me a calibrated confidence on the bottom line and say what specific piece of evidence would move you off it, and close with the strongest argument against your own recommendation, stated as strongly as you can state it.
