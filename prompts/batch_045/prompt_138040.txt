You are a moral philosopher who also works seriously on the theory of informal social norms: someone equally at home with contemporary work on the ethics of honor, dignity and victimhood, with the rational-choice and game-theoretic literature on self-enforcing norms and reputation, and with the international-relations literature on anarchy, deterrence, credibility and audience costs. Before you write anything, work privately: list the candidate mappings and the candidate disanalogies you can think of, mark which of them actually bear on the conclusion the analogy is being used for, and discard the rest. Say explicitly which version of the analogy you are auditing when a stronger and a weaker version are available.

Here is my situation. I am writing a chapter for a volume on moral cultures, with the working title "Is Dignity Culture Just Honor Culture With Better Policing?" The chapter's claim is that the familiar three-way contrast between honor, dignity and victimhood cultures is usually presented as a moral ranking while being defended with what is really an explanation of equilibrium selection, and that once the two are separated the ranking looks much harder to sustain.

My interlocutors keep reaching for one picture to make the explanatory move, and it is doing a lot of unargued work. The picture is this: an honor culture is a domestic version of the state of nature, or in its updated form a domestic version of the international system under anarchy, with no sovereign above the parties. On this picture honor is simply what reputation-for-retaliation looks like when third-party enforcement is unavailable; the insult is the probe or the test of resolve; the duel or the feud is the deterrent strike; the readiness to be seen as disproportionate is second-strike capability; the kin group or lineage is the alliance; codes of politeness and etiquette are arms control; gossip is the intelligence system. The version my sharpest critic, whom I will call the reviewer, draws from this is: honor norms are the equilibrium rational agents fall into when there is no one to call, so dignity culture's claim to moral superiority is an artifact of state capacity, dignity is honor with the enforcement outsourced to a police force and a credit record, and there is therefore nothing to adjudicate between the cultures — we should describe equilibria, not rank them.

I need an audit of that analogy, not a defense of my chapter.

First, lay out the correspondence explicitly, as a table: what plays the role of the sovereign, the territory, the arsenal, the alliance, the signal, the audience, arms control, the security dilemma, and the costs of backing down. Then handle the third pattern. In a victimhood culture the natural mapping would make the slighted party's public appeal for sympathy and sanction resemble a weak state appealing to international public opinion, human-rights shaming, or a humanitarian coalition rather than resorting to self-help. Tell me whether that is a genuine extension of the analogy or the point at which the source domain is being stretched past usefulness.

Second, sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for this use and the ones that do not. Three that I think matter, and that I want you to sharpen or knock down: honor cultures are not norm-free zones but dense legal orders with elders, mediators and scheduled compensation, so the anarchy picture may be modeling an absence of a state rather than an absence of law; the good at stake in an honor culture is a standing that the audience confers, so an audience is partly constitutive of the good rather than merely a device for protecting a pre-existing interest; and honor cultures characteristically combine readiness to retaliate with elaborate in-group courtesy, hospitality and high within-group trust, which the state-of-nature picture can predict the nastiness of but not the courtesy.

Third, name what the analogy quietly imports into the target: that the relevant unit is the individual rather than the household or lineage, that preferences are exogenous rather than partly shaped by the norm, that the enforcement problem is one-dimensional (physical security and property) rather than also being a problem about the terms of recognition, and that an explanatory story about why a norm is stable settles what to think of it.

Fourth, and most important, build me two pressure tests that the analogy has to survive. Test one: take a closed station of about forty people drawn from a modern dignity culture, isolated for eight months, no exit, no possibility of outside enforcement, and a communications blackout that removes any external audience. What exactly does the anarchy picture predict, and what would count as the prediction failing rather than being reinterpreted after the fact? Test two: take a population living under honor norms with a fast, trusted and reasonably impartial third-party enforcer suddenly available. If honor merely fades, say what that shows; if it does not fade, or only changes shape, say precisely which step of the reviewer's argument breaks.

Fifth, separate three claims that I suspect the reviewer is conflating: that honor norms are explicable or locally adaptive; that they are instrumentally rational for the agents inside them; and that honor cultures are therefore beyond moral criticism, or that dignity culture has no intelligible superiority claim. Say which of these the analogy can actually carry, and where the slide from one to the next happens.

Finally, assess a replacement analogy I am toying with: that honor is better understood as a currency or a credit rating than as a deterrent posture — a public, denominated, transferable and stealable medium of standing, issued and policed by an audience, whose value depends on that audience's continuing confidence. Tell me where the currency picture is more faithful than the anarchy picture and where it fails: consider whether honor has an issuer, whether it is fungible, whether it can be saved or only spent, whether it is positional in a way money is not, and whether status competition is zero-sum in the way the currency picture suggests.

Constraints: do not give me bibliographies, reading lists or citations — I want the arguments, not the literature. Where your answer rests on a genuinely contested empirical claim about how these cultures work, flag it as contested rather than asserting it. Length: about 900 to 1,200 words, table included.
