I am setting the final paper for a final-year undergraduate seminar on consequentialism and its critics. The paper is two hours, closed book, and candidates write a single essay of about 1,200 to 1,500 words, chosen from four questions. I need one question on the demandingness objection, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The failure mode I want to design out is the essay that runs: consequentialism requires maximising; maximising requires large sacrifices; therefore consequentialism is too demanding; therefore adopt satisficing consequentialism, or a scalar view, or a threshold view, or acknowledge agent-centred prerogatives. That is a taxonomy of positions rather than an argument, and it is exactly what a well-prepared but unoriginal candidate will produce if the question permits it.

The crux I want the question to isolate is this. The demandingness objection admits of two readings. On the quantitative reading, the defect is the amount of sacrifice consequentialism requires, and the remedy is a theory that requires less. On the structural reading, the defect is that consequentialism recognises no agent-relative reasons, no standing on the agent's part to organise her life around her own commitments, and the sheer volume of what is demanded is only the most visible symptom. To force candidates to choose between the readings, I want the question to contain a case in which they come apart: a case in which complying with consequentialist demands costs the agent nothing that consequentialism itself counts as a cost, so that the complaint cannot be a complaint about lost welfare and must, if it survives at all, be a complaint about something like authorship of one's own life. My sketch is an agent whose well-being is by stipulation unaffected, who adapts fully to whatever she does, takes as much satisfaction in any work she undertakes, and sustains equally good relationships either way, while what she gives up is only that the shape and direction of her life is set by the requirement to produce the best available outcome rather than by her own projects.

Please draft the following.

1. The question as it will appear on the paper: self-contained, with the case stated in full and the sub-questions made explicit. It should require candidates to say whether the objection applies in the case; explain what the answer to that shows about which reading the objection needs; explain why the familiar non-maximising revisions, satisficing and scalar consequentialism and threshold views, are responsive to only one of the two readings; address the worry that the quantitative reading must appeal to an independent account of how demanding morality may permissibly be, which is precisely what is in dispute between consequentialists and their critics; and take a position on whether, if the structural reading is the right one, the demandingness objection has any force of its own or is merely a vivid symptom of the deeper complaint about agent-relative reasons.

2. A model answer of about 900 words, in a register appropriate to a strong final-year essay, reaching a definite verdict on the last of those points rather than balancing considerations and stopping.

3. A marking scheme organised around five must-hit moves, with marks for each totalling 20, and, for each move, a one-line note on how candidates characteristically fail to make it.

4. Three or four sentences on what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer, naming the specific thing the A answer does that the B answer only gestures at.

Two constraints. First, no named philosophers, titles, or citations anywhere in the question, the model answer, or the scheme: this seminar's reading list changes from year to year, and the items have to stand on their own. Use only generic labels, maximising consequentialism, satisficing and scalar consequentialism as the family of views that reject the requirement to maximise rather than reinterpret what maximising demands, threshold views, agent-centred prerogatives. Second, do not let the model answer resolve the crux by stipulation or by appeal to intuition alone: it should argue, and it should say what would have to be true for the verdict to go the other way.
