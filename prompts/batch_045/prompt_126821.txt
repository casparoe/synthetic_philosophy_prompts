I'm a senior at my high school and I run our philosophy club. Next week I have to lead a forty-minute session on the surprise exam paradox for about a dozen tenth- and eleventh-graders, with a whiteboard and nothing else. A few of them have taken our logic elective and will want to write things down symbolically; most haven't and will be meeting the puzzle for the first time. I don't need the paradox explained to me — I've worked through the standard five-school-day version, the backward induction that seems to show the teacher's announcement cannot be true, and the awkward fact that an exam on the Wednesday nevertheless surprises everybody. What I need is a plan for how I should explain it.

First, the order: what has to come before what, and what does the later material depend on? My own instinct is that the puzzle can't even be stated until the room has a grip on the difference between the announcement's being false and the announcement's being something its intended audience cannot use as a premise in their own reasoning about it. But I suspect that distinction is the hardest thing in the session and also the thing half the room has no vocabulary for, so I don't know how early I can press it, or what has to be in place before it will mean anything to them.

Second, the case that carries the session. I'm tempted not to use the five-day version. Suppose instead the teacher says the exam will fall on some school day in the last two weeks of term, while the number of school days remaining isn't fixed, because any day lost to a snow closure gets tacked onto the end. My thought is that the backward induction then has no final day to get started on, so the argument never gets off the ground and the announcement is plainly satisfiable — which would seem to show that the fixed, publicly known endpoint is doing more of the work than the self-referential character of the announcement is. But I'm not confident that's the right moral, and I don't know where that variant belongs: at the beginning, as the thing that makes the puzzle feel like a real argument rather than a trick, or at the end, as a test of whether anyone has actually understood the diagnosis of the original.

Third, the misconceptions, and the point in the sequence at which I should head each one off. I can guess at three: that the upshot is simply that the teacher was lying or that the announcement was false; that surprise here is a matter of probability, so that a randomly chosen date is somehow more surprising than a predictable one; and that the induction and the plain fact of surprise are two arguments of equal standing, from which it follows that logic can prove anything you like. I'd rather be told about the ones I haven't thought of than run into them live.

Finally, two things. Give me one question I can put to the group at the very end that would show me the diagnosis has actually landed, as opposed to showing me that they enjoyed being puzzled. And tell me what I should decline to go into at this level, so that I can say out loud, rather than awkwardly around, that I'm not going into it.

Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and in British English.
