Two people on my team got into it at lunch last week about something called moral trade, and they asked me to referee. I said I would but I genuinely cant tell who won — I'm a backend dev, never took a philosophy class in my life, and both of them had me nodding at different points. Here's roughly how it went.

The setup: Nadia cares intensely about animal suffering. Raj cares about malaria bednets. Nadia offers Raj a deal — she'll donate $200 to his bednet charity if he goes vegetarian for three months. Raj reckons three months without meat would cost him maybe $30 worth of hassle and complaining. Nadia thinks his three months of vegetarianism is worth considerably more than $200 in animal terms. So Raj gets $200 of what he cares about for a $30 cost, and Nadia pays $200 and gets something she values more than $200. Nobody's coerced, and each of them comes out ahead measured on their own scoreboard. That much they both agreed on.

Then:

(1) Raj: This can't really be creating value. There's one world, and the trade just moves stuff around in it; what makes both of you "richer" is that you're scoring the same slice of reality at two different exchange rates. If you agreed about what's valuable, at most one of you could gain. So the gains are an artifact of your disagreement, and each of you thinks the other's scoreboard is partly mistaken. I'm not convinced that profiting off someone's (as you see it) erroneous values is a moral improvement rather than just arbitrage on error.

(2) Nadia: Two replies. First, disagreement about value is the resource being exploited, and that's not parasitic — that's just what trade is, everywhere. Second, and more importantly: if you think I'm wrong about animals, that's a reason for you to like this deal even more. Whatever I'd do with that $200 otherwise, you think it's worth less than $200 going to bednets. So conditional on me spending my money on my cause instead of yours, buying your vegetarianism is the best available use of it from your point of view too. The trade doesn't require you to respect my values, only to prefer this to what I'd otherwise do.

(3) Raj: Sure, but look at what that argument rests on — a counterfactual that only I can see. The trade is worth it to you only if I wouldn't have gone vegetarian anyway. I know whether that's true and you don't, and the mechanism pays me more for reporting a bigger cost. So I should exaggerate how much I'd hate it, or quietly plan to do it regardless and bank the $200 for my own cause. Any scheme whose gains depend on the other side's private counterfactual, and which rewards you for reporting a worse one, is a badly built mechanism. You're not buying a change in the world, you're buying a story, and you can't audit it.

(4) Nadia: That's true but it's an implementation bug, not a refutation of the principle. You fix mechanism-design problems with mechanism design — make him name his price first, tie payment to something verifiable like grocery reciepts, only trade with people you've known for years. Every market runs on asymmetric information and markets still mostly work. "Some trades are scams" isn't "trading is bad."

(5) Raj: OK, grant that. Here's the one that actually gets me. If paying people to stop doing the bad thing is legitimate, it becomes profitable to start doing it. I don't eat much meat now — but if the market rate for quitting is $200, why not take up steak for a month and then let Nadia pay me to stop? Or announce I'm buying a big SUV unless someone pays me not to? Once there's a market in behavior change, people will manufacture the behavior in order to sell the change, and the "improvement" is now funded by a threat the seller invented. And there's no price signal that tells you which case you're in, because the counterfactual is inside the other person's head and they can edit it at will.

(6) Nadia: Real problem, wrong conclusion. It tells you what to screen for, not that the whole thing is broken: only trade over conduct that predates the offer, or that the person has some independent reason to maintain. Also this shape of thing exists in ordinary markets — you can only sell what you own, and people do acquire things in order to sell them, that's not corruption. And note the scam isn't free: to sell quitting, I have to actually live the vice for a while, which costs me something. That limits it.

(7) Raj: Two last things. One: the framing treats morality like a taste. I take money to stop eating meat — but if I thought eating meat was seriously wrong, being paid to stop would be corrupt, and paying me would be a kind of indulgence-buying. So moral trade seems to only make sense between people who already treat each other's moral views as basically preferences. It quietly assumes the thing most people supposedly deny, that moral claims aren't binding on everyone. You can't both think "this is wrong, full stop" and think it's fine to auction off your compliance. Two: bilateral trades like this settle things between the two of us and dump any difference on people who never got a bid. Markets handle that with prices; there's no common currency here, so there's nothing like an invisible-hand result guaranteeing the whole thing is fine.

(8) Nadia: On the first: I've got three levers for changing your behavior — argue, force, pay — and paying is the one that leaves you freest. I care more about the actual animals than about your moral hygiene, and the indulgence charge proves too much anyway: paying a farm to install better equipment is the same shape as paying you and nobody calls it corrupt. On the second: fine, add a filter — don't trade when there are significant costs to people who aren't at the table. That's a constraint on which trades to make, not a reason to make none. And notice what your position costs: if we both refuse to trade, we're both worse off by our own lights, which is a pretty odd thing to insist on out of principle.

I'm paraphrasing and they went round a few more times, but that's the shape of it.

Then tell me what both of them are missing, if anything. If some step really does bottom out in a metaethical disagreement, say so and say exactly what turns on it, rather than just gesturing at "it depends on your metaethics" and stopping.
