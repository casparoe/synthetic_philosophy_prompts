Here's the question I set: Explain Molinism's doctrine of middle knowledge and how it is supposed to reconcile divine providence with libertarian human freedom. Your answer should (a) distinguish middle knowledge from God's natural knowledge and free knowledge and say where it sits in the logical order, (b) explain what counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are, what it is for a possible world to be feasible, and how God is supposed to exercise providence by actualizing one, (c) contrast Molinism briefly with theological determinism and with open theism, and (d) state the grounding objection and give one Molinist response to it.

And here's the answer I'm grading (I've transcribed it as written, typos included):

Molinism is named after the sixteenth-century Jesuit Luis de Molina, who introduced middle knowledge (scientia media) as a solution to the problem of evil. The doctrine holds that God's knowledge falls into three logical moments. First, natural knowledge: the necessary truths and the whole range of logical possibilities, which are independent of God's will. Second, middle knowledge: counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, i.e. truths of the form 'if agent S were placed in circumstances C, S would freely do A'. These are contingent, but they are not brought about by God. Third, free knowledge: everything that depends on God's decree, including the whole actual future. The crucial point is the order. Because middle knowledge is logically prior to the divine decree, God can survey the possible worlds and see which ones are within his reach. A world is feasible for God just in case he can weakly actualize it: he creates the circumstances and the creatures, the true counterfactuals of freedom do the rest, and their free choices fill in the remainder. So God keeps exhaustive providential control without determing anyone's choices — he doesn't cause the choices, he arranges which situations people find themselves in. This is what separates Molinism both from theological determinism, on which the divine decree settles what creatures do, and from open theism, on which God simply doesn't know future free actions.

The main objection is the grounding objection. What makes a counterfactual of creaturely freedom true? It can't be God's decree, since middle knowledge precedes the decree; it can't be the creature's own choice, since in most cases the creature never faces those circumstances at all. So it looks as if the counterfactuals are brute, unexplained facts. But the Molinist has a reply: because God's middle knowledge precedes his creative decree and belongs to his natural cognitive perfection, the counterfactuals are true in virtue of the divine understanding — God sees them in his own ideas, so to speak — and so nothing is left brute.

Middle knowledge also helps with the problem of evil, via transworld depravity. A person suffers from transworld depravity when there is no possible world in which she is significantly free and never sins. If, as seems plausible, this is true of everyone God could have created, then God could not have made a world of free creatures without moral evil, and so the existence of moral evil is no objection to his goodness; being perfectly good, he would actualise the best of the feasible worlds, assuming there is a best.
