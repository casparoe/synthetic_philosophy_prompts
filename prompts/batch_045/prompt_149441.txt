Please act as a professional philosopher who also has a working second competence in archaeological and anthropological method, and who has followed the rationalist and effective-altruism arguments about population ethics, non-identity cases, and average-versus-total views of welfare. Take your time: before drafting anything, brainstorm which of the disputes below are real and which are merely verbal, and only then compose the document. I want formulations that a hostile reader on the other side would still accept as a fair statement of their own position — neither vacuous enough that nobody could object nor slanted enough that only one side would sign.

I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about a decade, and the question of whether the Neolithic transition was a mistake keeps resurfacing in my circles, usually in the form of the old Jared Diamond line about the worst mistake in human history, the Sahlins-derived affluent-forager thesis, and the more recent popular backlash against the declension narrative. My reading group has split into two camps and after a year they are largely talking past each other, so I want the document an adversarial collaboration would produce. Camp one, call it the Declension side, holds that the adoption of agriculture was bad for the people who lived through it: more labor, worse and narrower diet, crowding and infectious disease, new inequality, worse prospects for women, and a one-way ratchet that no household could exit once the surrounding population had switched. Camp two, call it the No-Mistake side, holds that this is not the kind of thing that can be a mistake at all: no agent chose it, there was no feasible alternative in which everyone remained foragers, the transition is better described as an evolutionary and demographic process driven by lineage-level advantage plus demographic externalities, and judging it by the average welfare of a much larger population is the wrong measure. Please draft the joint statement both could sign: a numbered section of agreed claims, non-vacuous and including what each side would count as evidence against its own view, followed by a disagreements table.

For the table I want the cruxes to be stated as sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, with each side's position given in wording that side would accept for itself, and for each crux a concrete case, dataset comparison, or natural experiment that both sides agree would bear on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Several cruxes strike me as worth including, but use your judgment about which are genuine and add better ones if you see them. First, the population-and-identity crux: if agriculture raised carrying capacity, then most of the people in the later population would not have existed under the forager counterfactual, so on a total-welfare view the transition looks better and on an average-welfare view worse, and it is unclear whether it is worse *for anyone* in a way that has a determinate subject — please say whether the identity problem makes the mistake claim ill-formed or merely hard to evaluate, and whether either side should take a stand on average versus total. Second, decomposition of the package: sedentism, plant domestication, animal domestication, storage, heritable property, surplus, crowding, and eventually states are usually bundled, and I would like the disagreement located as precisely as possible — for instance whether sedentism without farming, or cereal-grain dependence specifically, or the pastoral route rather than the grain route, carries most of whatever harm there was, and whether rich aquatic environments where people were sedentary and numerous without full domestication function as a natural experiment both sides accept. Third, the ratchet: how reversible the transition was in practice, whether the lock-in ran through population size, technology loss, or dependence on stored grain, and whether a multi-equilibrium or collective-action structure means the transition was individually rational and collectively bad rather than mistaken. Fourth, the comparator problem: whether the baseline should be the immediately preceding forager populations of the same regions, ethnographically documented twentieth-century foragers who mostly lived in marginal habitats, or time-allocation and nutritional data from modern foragers, and how much each side's conclusion depends on that choice. Fifth, the definition of affluence: leisure, consumption, subjective satisfaction, and low variance or insurance against famine are different things, and I suspect part of the dispute is about which one is being measured. Please end with a short section stating plainly which parts of this dispute you judge to be verbal rather than substantive and why, what both sides agree would actually move the debate, and what neither side should claim given the evidence they can currently point to.
