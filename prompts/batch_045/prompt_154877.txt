I'm a few years into a PhD on interpretability — philosophy of ML side, sort of straddling the ML group — and I'm finally writing the chapter where I have to actually engage the realism-about-representations literature instead of just bracketing it, and I want to make sure I'm not setting up a strawman.

The view I want to defend is something I've been calling convergence realism. I train sparse autoencoders on a bunch of independently trained models — different seeds, a few architecture tweaks — then match the learned dictionary features across runs by cosine similarity of their decoder vectors plus an overlap score over which datapoints they fire on. A big fraction of them recur: same qualitative description when I read off their top activating examples, and roughly the same behavioural effects when I clamp them during generation. To me that kills the worry that SAE features are just an arbitrary basis we imposed on activation space, because if the decomposition were arbitrary it wouldnt keep reappearing in networks that never saw each other's weights. And once a feature is decodable, steerable, and recurrent, it feels like the anti-realist has nothing left to deny.

So: what is the strongest remaining version of the claim that the recurrent features my pipeline hands me are real-but-not-representational — real in the way a gear in a gearbox is real, causally load-bearing but not about anything, not something the network is thereby keeping track of? Every reconstruction I manage of that move, the intentional-stance it's-all-just-useful-ascription line my supervisor keeps pushing, collapses into flatly denying that anything internal matters once the features are causally efficacious, which can't be right. I clearly am getting the position wrong somewhere. Give me the best version of it, and where I've gone wrong, in one paragraph, 300 words max.
