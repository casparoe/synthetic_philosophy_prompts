So I'm about 60k into a historical novel — late Roman north Africa — and my POV character is a bishop I made up, Honoratus, who's a rigorist about lying (roughly the absolutist camp people associate with Augustine, though I'm not writing the man himself and Honoratus isn't supposed to be a mouthpiece for him). Problem is I've already written him ruling on a bunch of lying cases in earlier chapters, a beta reader says he's inconsistent, and now I have to write the scene I've been putting off and I can't decide whether he does the thing or not.

His track record as written so far:

- Soldiers ask a deacon's wife where a runaway is. She says "I don't know." She does know. Honoratus: that's a sin, and she should have said nothing at all or told them to go to hell instead.
- A grain merchant tells a ridiculous tall story in a tavern, everybody laughs, nobody believes a word of it. Honoratus: still a lie, but a small one; he lets it go with a prayer.
- A physician tells a dying man he'll recover. Honoratus: wrong. He makes the physician go back in and tell him the truth.
- A widow is hiding two fugitives and, asked if they're in her courtyard, says "they are not here" — she means something like "not here in the sense you mean," a mental-reservation sort of move. Honoratus: no. That's a lie. Stop.
- A man carrying a letter is stopped by a gang looking to kill the person it's addressed to. He refuses to answer, they beat him, he says nothing. Honoratus: this is the right way. You're allowed to not answer.
- Two chapters back he's facing a mob hunting a tax collector. He doesn't say a word, but he points down the wrong road. I wrote him doing it and then hating himself for a week. I genuinely can't tell if that's consistant with the rest of it, since pointing isn't asserting anything false.

Here's the scene I owe my editor. The fugitive is a twelve-year-old hiding in the bell tower. The person asking is not a mob and not a murderer: she's the town magistrate, a decent woman doing her job, and she questions him under oath, in the church. He answers in one sentence — something strictly true that she will definately take to mean the boy has gone north. And separately, a few hours before he's questioned, he leaves the tower door unlatched and a lamp burning so it will look like someone bolted out the back.

So: does he say the line? But more usefully for me, what rule is he actually running? Because I can see at least three that fit all six of those verdicts — (a) never assert what you believe to be false, and everything else is fair game; (b) never give any sign in order to plant a false belief in someone, words or otherwise; (c) never let your words mean something other than what you believe, though what your hands do is a different matter. And maybe a fourth: something about an oath, or about being asked a direct question by someone who has a right to ask, where answering the question as asked matters and not just the literal truth of the sentence.

Which one does the track record point to, what does each of them predict about the sentence and about the door-and-lamp business, which should I write him as running, and — the part that would be most helpfull — if I want a later chapter to make the rule unmistakable to a reader, what case should I put him in next? One more wrinkle: the novel has a small heretical sect, the Simplices, who hold that every sign is covenantal, so even locking a door is a kind of speech. I'd like to use them but don't want them to be a device that just exists so I can agree with them.

Sorry, long one. I care less about the verdict than about the shape of the rule — I can write either version of the scene, I just can't write it if I don't know which consistency I'd be breaking.
