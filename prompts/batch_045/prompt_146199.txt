I have no formal training in philosophy — I've been reading about this on my own, in the evenings, for a couple of years — so please be patient with the jargon if I get it wrong, and tell me when I've misused a term. I don't want sources, citations, or a reading list: I can't follow them up where I am, and what I'd get from you is the analysis itself.

The episode I want analyzed is the endgame of the campaign to eradicate smallpox. The facts as I understand them — correct me only if I have something badly wrong: by the early 1970s mass vaccination had pushed the disease back to a handful of mostly poor, mostly rural regions; the campaign then switched to a strategy of surveillance and containment, hunting down each outbreak, vaccinating the ring of contacts around it, and moving on, rather than trying to vaccinate whole populations; the last naturally acquired case was in Somalia in the late 1970s; eradication was certified a couple of years after that; and virus stocks were deliberately kept rather than destroyed. I've also read that containment sometimes involved inducements and some compulsion, but I don't want the answer to depend on whether that's right.

What I'm asking for is an analysis of that endgame through the lens of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — as I hold the view, suffering below a certain threshold cannot be compensated by any quantity of mild happiness above it, so the prevention of the one is lexically prior to the production of the other, with no exchange rate between them — and then the same analysis run in the other direction, from the case back to the theory.

Four things I've been chewing on and can't settle. The labels are my own.

1. Tail discipline. In the final years, the cost per remaining case prevented rose enormously: money spent chasing the last outbreaks would have bought a great deal of ordinary medical benefit if it had been spent elsewhere. On a single scale, where pleasure and pain are commensurable, the endgame looks close to indefensible; on a lexical view it looks mandatory, provided the suffering prevented really is below the threshold. So is the endgame the cleanest real-world case where the lexical view is right and the balancing view is wrong, or is it a reductio — the point at which the view spends without limit? And notice that the campaign did stop, and stopped while live virus was still being stored. Under a lexical reading that looks like either incoherence or a place where the theory simply has nothing to say. Which is it?

2. The bearer question. Where, exactly, does the threshold sit? On a moment of experience, on a bout of illness, on a whole life, or on a population's rate of incidence? Smallpox is a good testing case because it produced, in the same person, an acute illness that most survivors got through and lifelong damage — blindness, disfigurement — that we would normally count as terrible while the lives containing it were plainly worth living. Whatever you say about the bearer decides whether the campaign averted threshold-level suffering at all. So tell me which bearer the theory needs, and what happens to the case if we move the threshold somewhere else.

3. The risk version. What was actually bought was mostly the removal of a small risk of catastrophic suffering borne by enormous numbers of people, not the cancellation of a fixed pile of it. So the question sharpens into this: is a one-in-a-million chance of below-threshold suffering still lexically prior to any finite amount of ordinary happiness? If yes, the view appears to give lexical priority to nearly every risk-reduction project in public health, which I take to be too much. If no, then there is some exchange rate between probability of threshold suffering and ordinary goods after all — and I want to know whether that smuggles commensurability back in through the probability door, and whether the lexical view survives it.

4. Whether any of this needed a common currency. My suspicion is that the practitioners' real justification was quietly lexical: they treated the elimination of the disease as non-negotiable, and then did perfectly ordinary cost-effectiveness reasoning about how to achieve it. If that's right, the case is not a vindication of commensurability in practice, and the philosophical fight about whether pleasure and pain can be measured on one scale does less work here than people suppose. Tell me whether that reading holds up, and whether the episode therefore leaves the commensurability question untouched rather than settling it.

Where the theory is silent about this episode, say that it is silent rather than forcing it to speak. And in the end, tell me whether you read the case as evidence for the view, as an anomaly it has to absorb, or as a kind of case it never anticipated.

Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single claim you would actually defend — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of what I've asked, and no references.
