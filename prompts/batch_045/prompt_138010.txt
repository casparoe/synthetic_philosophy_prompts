I work as the sole analyst on a small event-driven account — roughly nine million dollars of a family office's money — and three or four times a week I have to size a position on a discrete, more-or-less binary outcome: a central-bank decision, a merger-arbitrage spread, the resolution of a piece of litigation. My probability estimates come from an internal forecasting panel of four people whose track record I have audited: they are well calibrated in the middle of the range and badly overconfident in the tails, so that outcomes they call 90% confident land perhaps 60% of the time, and their long-shot enthusiasm is worth almost nothing. The account has three constraints that I cannot design around. Any single position above one and a half percent of the bankroll has to be signed off by an investment committee that meets weekly, is comfortable with fractions and percentages, and is not going to follow an argument about ergodicity. The evaluation horizon is twelve months with quarterly reviews, and several of the contracts I care about resolve after that window closes. And if the account ever falls thirty percent below its high-water mark, the mandate is withdrawn automatically — an absorbing barrier, not a soft stop.

What I want from you is a written sizing procedure my committee and I could actually run in fifteen minutes, in a fixed order, on a proposal described by four inputs: the contract price, the panel's probability estimate, the time to resolution, and whatever is already on the book. For each step say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates with a zero stake, where the stake is capped by the mandate rather than by the criterion, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values — for instance how much weight to give a small probability of breaching the drawdown barrier, or whether to take a position that is right on the merits but that I cannot explain in two sentences to the people who sign it. I would like the procedure to be explicit about the following, because these are the places I keep losing the thread: that the criterion is about time-average growth rather than expected value, and how to put that to a committee that thinks in expected returns; that the textbook stake is derived in the absence of a barrier and is therefore strictly wrong for me, and what a defensible approximate correction looks like rather than an ignored problem; when it is legitimate to size each bet on its own rather than jointly with the rest of the book, and what to do when it is not, including bets that resolve after the evaluation window and bets correlated with positions I already hold; how to shrink stakes for estimation error in a way that is tied to the panel's recorded miscalibration rather than to a rule of thumb like halving; and what to do on the days when nobody will commit to a number at all. I have a friend at a larger shop who insists that maximizing expected log wealth and maximizing long-run growth are the same claim and that risk aversion has nothing to do with either of them, and I am never quite sure whether he is right or whether it matters for what I do on a Tuesday.

Then run the procedure on three live cases. First, a contract offered at 22 cents where the panel says 35%, resolving in about six weeks, uncorrelated with anything else I hold. Second, a merger spread at 94 cents that the panel puts at 97%: a small return, a 3% chance of a break, and a break large enough to put the account within a few percent of the barrier. Third, a long-shot at 4 cents where the panel says 9% — the highest expected-return line on my sheet, the one I am most tempted by, and the one I suspect your procedure will tell me to take small or not at all; if so, I want to see that worked through rather than skipped. Finally, tell me in a short closing paragraph what the procedure cannot do, and specifically whether any of it transfers to a quantity that is not money: I am also asked to decide how many analyst-hours to put into a speculative research project, and a committee member has started arguing that the same geometric logic applies there too, which I doubt but cannot quite say why.
