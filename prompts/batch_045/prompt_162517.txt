The thing I keep coming back to is the argument that children couldn't possibly learn their language from what they hear, so the grammar must be partly built in (innateness, poverty of the stimulus, that whole cluster). I want you to translate that argument into my vocabulary as honestly as you can, and then tell me what didn't survive.

Here's why I'm stuck. Every time I read someone lay out the argument, the conclusion comes out as "therefore the learner must have an inductive bias". But that's just no-free-lunch: it's true of any learner whatsoever, including a lookup table, and it cannot be what people have been fighting about for sixty years. And when I try to squeeze out what the *extra* claim is supposed to be, it seems to evaporate back into "you need some bias" again. So part of what I'm asking: is there real content there, or is the argument weaker than its fans think?

I invented two tests to try to pin it down; maybe they're garbage, tell me if so:
- The bit-budget test. Split the kid into (wiring plus starting state) and (stuff acquired from the input). Call the first part the built-in part. Then the interesting question isn't *whether* there's built-in stuff — obviously there is, the kid isn't a blank slate in any sense — but how many bits of it there are and how specific: is it more like "this learner prefers hierarchical rules over counting words", which feels like a couple of architectural choices, or more like "this learner already comes equipped knowing that you can't move a word out of a certain kind of clause", which feels like a big pile of very specific furniture?
- The compression test. If a fact about the language could be recovered from a big enough corpus by a general-purpose compressor that knows nothing about language, then it's in the data, not built in, whatever anyone says. If it couldn't be, it's genuinely built in.

Then three cases I want run through the translation one at a time — what maps cleanly onto architecture-and-training-data talk, what you had to bend to make it fit, and what my vocabulary has no way of saying at all:

1. Auxiliary fronting. Kid hears "the man who is tall is happy" and also hears questions, and somehow learns that the "is" you move to the front is the one belonging to the main clause, not the one tucked inside the relative clause. Nobody ever hands them the wrong version with a red X on it. In my terms that's generalization from positive examples with no negative labels, which is... just ordinary learning? I feel like I'm either dissolving the case or missing the point, and I can't tell which.

2. The wanna case. As far as I can tell you can contract "who do you want to beat" into "wanna", but not "who do you want to win" where the who is the one doing the winning — the version with the gap in subject position resists it. (Correct me if I've got that backwards.) Supposedly nobody teaches this and you can't dig it out of the input. My vocabulary wants to say "constraint on the hypothesis space", but then I can't tell whether it's a language-specific constraint or just a general preference for simplicity that happens to rule it out.
