A friend in my philosophy of maths reading group talked me into an argument I really dont want to accept, and I want to know which step to break.

(1) Benacerraf's dilemma: you can't have all three of — mathematical sentences getting the same kind of semantics as ordinary ones, so that 'there are two primes between 5 and 11' really quantifies over numbers; knowledge requiring an appropriate causal-informational link between the knower and the things known; and our actually having mathematical knowledge.

(2) The dilemma only bites because of the causal-link clause: numbers, if there are any, are abstract and don't enter into causal relations, so if knowledge needs a causal-informational link then our arithmetical beliefs aren't sensitive to the numbers themselves, and one of the other two has to give.

(3) But that clause isn't a principle about mathematics. It's a general theory of knowledge, so it should govern everything we take ourselves to know.

(4) And most of what we take ourselves to know isn't something we're in causal contact with: electrons (we see tracks, not electrons), other minds (we see behaviour), the future (nothing there yet to be in contact with), what would have happened if I'd turned left instead of right, maybe moral facts too.

(5) So the same three-way squeeze runs in each of those cases: either the sentences don't really quantify over what they appear to, or we don't have the knowledge.

(6) But we do know there are electrons, that my neighbour is in pain, and that the sun will rise tomorrow — or at least we know those no worse than we know arithmetic.

(7) So the causal-link clause proves way to much: it generalises into a scepticism about unobservables, other minds, the future and modality that nobody, least of all the people pressing the dilemma, is willing to accept.

(8) And if the clause goes, the dilemma has no bite: face-value semantics for mathematical language is compatible with mathematical knowledge, and the whole challenge dissolves.

That last step is the one I don't want. The dilemma feels like a real problem and I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than swallow (8), and I'd happily give up the generalisation at (3) if I could say what makes numbers special. But I cant see it: my guess is the leak is around (4) or (6), and every patch I try either lets electrons in and keeps numbers out by fiat, or else defuses the dilemma entirely.

Which step do you reject, why does it look fine on first reading, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular: if the fix is to weaken or drop the causal clause, does that reopen some other problem about what mathematical terms refer to, and can that other problem be solved without putting causation back?
