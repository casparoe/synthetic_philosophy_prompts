I spent my career in atmospheric chemistry and have, since retiring, been reading my way into moral philosophy, mostly on questions about what we owe to people who come after us. I have written the notes below and would be grateful for your comments on them. Let me say at the outset what I am not asking for: I do not want a bibliography or a reading list, and I would rather you did not cite anything. I want to know whether the central argument has real content, where it breaks if it breaks, and whether it is worth expanding into a paper or quietly filing away.

**The pure rate of time preference is not a moral parameter — notes toward an argument**

**1. The dispute I want to dissolve.** Policy for the climate is assessed by discounting future costs and benefits at a social discount rate, standardly decomposed as r = ρ + ηg, where ρ is the pure rate of time preference, η the elasticity of the marginal utility of consumption, and g the rate of growth of consumption per head. Nearly all the philosophical argument has been about ρ. One side holds that a benefit's falling to a person merely because she lives later is no reason to count it for less; the other holds that the bare passage of time, or the opportunity cost of capital, or the contingency of who will exist, licenses some positive rate. My impression is that this dispute is at a standstill, and that both sides have conceded the important point prematurely: they agree that ρ is a moral parameter whose value must be settled by substantive ethical argument. I want to argue that it is not a moral parameter at all, and cannot be settled by the considerations either side invokes, because in the circumstances in which the question actually arises a nonzero ρ cannot be stated without helping oneself to something the theory must disallow.

**2. Why I am not satisfied with the familiar argument.** The usual case for ρ = 0 is that date of birth is morally arbitrary, like race or birthplace, and that counting a stranger's welfare for less because she is born two centuries hence is not impartiality but discrimination. I find this persuasive but inconclusive. It invites several replies — that temporal position is not a property of a person in the way that race is, that we are not required to be impartial across time in the way we are across space, that a small positive rate is a modest insurance against our own uncertainty, and so on — and each reply preserves the assumption that the question is a substantive one to be argued about. I have no new argument at that level. What follows is meant to work at a different level: not on the ethics of birth date but on the conditions under which a weighting over generations can be formulated at all.

**3. The no-seam argument.** By a *generation* I mean any partition of all persons who ever live into temporally ordered, collectively exhaustive classes G₁, G₂, …, assigned weights w₁, w₂, …. Two premises about the object being partitioned, and one about theories of it.

(P1) The human population is a continuous overlapping chain. There is no instant at which the living are wholly replaced, and no interval during which their composition is constant. It follows that there is no non-arbitrary way to locate the boundaries between generations: a boundary every twenty-five years, or at the mean age of childbearing, or at cohort lines fixed by decade of birth, yields different partitions, and the boundaries can be displaced continuously without any demographic event marking the displacement. Any individual can be moved from one class to the next by a small shift of the grid.

(P2) A normative theory may not make its prescriptions depend on arbitrary features of the representation in which those prescriptions are stated. This is the analogue, in ethics, of the requirement that a law be stated in a coordinate-independent form; the requirement is what licenses us to say that a result is about the world rather than about the grid we happened to impose on it.

(P3) Hence a weighting schedule may not encode any fact about where the boundaries fall. But the form in which intergenerational discounting is ordinarily written — a weight constant within each generation, stepping down at each boundary — does exactly that. Move every boundary forward by a decade and the same persons, with the same welfare, fall into different classes and are counted differently. The prescription has changed while nothing about the world has.

(P4) The only schedule insensitive to the location of the boundaries is a constant one.

(P5) A constant schedule over generations just is the refusal to discount welfare as such — that is, ρ = 0.

(C) Therefore ρ = 0 is not a moral verdict on the arbitrariness of birth date; it is a condition of the theory's being well posed. And a positive ρ, on this reading, is not an ethical position that has been refuted but an empirical error in disguise: the assertion, false as a matter of demography, that humanity arrives in discrete and non-overlapping installments.

**4. What the argument does and does not touch.** It leaves ηg untouched: how much a unit of consumption is worth to a person who is much richer than we are is a separate question, and it is where I suspect the real moral weight of climate policy lies. It leaves extinction risk untouched, since the chance that there is no one to benefit is a probability and not a rate. But it has two consequences I cannot tell whether to treat as implications or as reductions of the argument.

(i) Nothing in §3 privileges the forward direction. If the chain has no seams in either direction, then the weight owed to a person's welfare does not decay with distance into the past either, and the moral significance of a wrong done seven generations ago is in principle undiminished. I would rather not be committed to strong claims about historical injustice, and I am unsure whether my argument commits me to them or whether some asymmetry between fixed past and open future can be recovered.

(ii) If nothing is discounted and the future is very long and possibly very populous, then the expected value of any candidate policy is dominated by its effects on remote people, and the difference between policies collapses into the difference between their effects on how many people there will be, and which ones, in ten thousand years. This looks like the objection that such views license fanaticism, and I would rather not be committed to that either.

**5. The two objections I can raise against myself, and why I do not trust my answers to them.** The first is that §3 shows only that the *discretised* form of discounting is ill defined, and that a smooth exponential weight in continuous time has no boundaries to be displaced and so satisfies (P2) as comfortably as a constant does; on this reading my argument begs the question by writing the schedule in step form. My reply is that the smooth exponential is only the limit of the step schedules, and that if every member of a family of schedules is inadmissible because it encodes a fact about the grid, I do not see how the limit acquires a privilege that none of its instances has. The second objection is that even a smooth rate requires a distinguished origin — the moment of evaluation — and that I cannot distinguish arbitrariness in the grid from the mere situatedness of the evaluator without assuming what is at issue. My reply is that the present is given to the evaluator while the grid is chosen by the theorist, and that coordinate invariance was never required to be invariance under moving the measuring apparatus. I do not find either objection fatal, which makes me suspect that I have not fully understood them.

**What I would like from you:**

1. Take the chain at P3 to P4 and tell me whether it holds, and if not, what exactly is doing the illicit work. Is my reply to the first objection in §5 correct? I would particularly value a diagnosis of why the reply looks right if it is not.
2. Is the claim in (C) — that a positive ρ is a demographic error rather than an ethical position — defensible, or is it rhetoric that collapses as soon as one asks what the smooth continuous formulation looks like?
3. Of the two consequences in §4, which is a genuine cost of the view and which rests on a confusion? If one could have the argument while avoiding only one of them, is there a formulation that achieves this, and what would it have to give up?
4. Does the argument engage the dispute described in §1, or does it change the subject? Is there a reading on which it is consistent with everything both sides of that dispute believe, in which case I have written a verbal point rather than an argument?
5. Structural advice. Is §2 needed, or does it advertise a debt I do not actually owe? Is the analogy to coordinate independence illuminating or does it mislead me about the kind of invariance I need? And, bluntly: is there a three-thousand-word paper here, or is there a footnote?

I would prefer comments that take the argument seriously enough to try to repair it over comments that simply reject P1 or P2; but if P2 is simply false, or false in the form in which I have stated it, please say so plainly.
