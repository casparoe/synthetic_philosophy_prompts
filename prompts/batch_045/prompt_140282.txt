Hey — posting this here because my advisor is out of town and I've been going in circles on it for about a week. I'm a formal epistemology PhD student, working mostly on imprecise credences (set-of-representors stuff, the permissivism-adjacent end of it), and a friend from the econ department backed me into the following over coffee. It's basically the Dutch book argument for probabilism, run a bit harder than it usually gets run, and as far as I can tell it either refutes itself or refutes everyone, and I can't find the seam. Setup: an agent, a bookie, and finite packages of bets; a bet on a proposition pays $1 if it's true, $0 otherwise. Say a bet is settleable only if some procedure the bookie can actually run — with the hardware and the lifetime she has — determines whether it was won; call that the signing condition, I'll need it in step (7).

(1) Exploitable ⇒ defective: if there's some finite package of bets, each of which the agent is rationally permitted to accept, whose total payoff is a net loss however the world turns out, then there's something rationally defective about her credal state.

(2) Bridge: her fair prices just are her credences. Precise credence in A → one price she'll pay for a $1 bet on A; imprecise credence → a range of permissible prices; no opinion about A → no price at all.

(3) Dutch book theorem plus its converse: a package of bets she'd accept at her fair prices can be arranged to lose money for sure iff those prices violate the probability axioms. (Synchronic case, forget about diachronic stuff for now.)

(4) Now suppose she has a credal gap about some A — she has never considered whether the number of leaves on the tree outside my window is even — or her credence in A is imprecise across a wide interval. Then there's no price for a bet on A that she's rationally required to refuse: she has no sharp standard to measure an offer against, so every offer, however lousy, is one she isn't required to turn down.

(5) So the bookie hands her a package on A — buy high, sell low, the usual sandwich — every single bet of which is permitted by (4), and which loses money for sure. By (1) she's defective. And the standard escape hatch, that she'll spot the package and refuse it, is closed: by hypothesis she can't tell a sandwich from a free lunch, since telling would require exactly the opinion she lacks.

(6) So (1)+(3)+(4) entail that rationality requires a precise, sharp credence in every proposition whatsoever. No gaps, no imprecision, no permissivism about which doxastic states are allowed. That's way stronger than probabilism and I dont believe it.

(7) It gets worse. The axioms are standardly stated over an algebra, so close the set of propositions she has sharp credences in under the Boolean operations, and the axioms hand out 1 to every tautology and 0 to every contradiction. Rationality now requires certainty about every logical and mathematical truth, including ones whose shortest proof is astronomically long, and about every empirical proposition too. Either every finite agent is defective at every moment of her life, or defective has stopped doing any work.

(8) And here's the bit that smells. The bookie in (7) can only run the exploit if she can settle the bets she's offering, i.e. figure out the truth values involved. For the astronomically-long-proof tautology she can't, any more than the agent can. So the bookie is being credited with exactly the kind of access whose absence in the agent does all the work in (4) — the signing condition gets enforced against one party and not the other. That asymmetry is where I suspect the cheating is, but I can't tell whether it's a separate flaw or just (4) again in a different hat.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (1) and (3) — I like the exploitation framing and I really dont want to have to rebuild my second chapter on gradational-accuracy grounds — so I'm hoping the damage is in (4)-(5) or in the local-to-global move between (6) and (7). But I want the price list. If I reject (4), do I owe a positive story about what it is to have no opinion about A that doesn't collapse into having a maximally imprecise one, and if it does collapse, does (5) just come roaring back? If I reject the step from permitted to accept each bet to permitted to accept the package, what am I signing up for about holism in rational choice, and does that holism make the converse in (3) hard to even state? If I instead restrict the whole argument to an algebra of propositions she actually has opinions about, doesn't it go quiet exactly where I need it — it then says nothing about which propositions she must have opinions about, though maybe I should just accept that and say the argument was never in the business of telling me? And if I bite the bullet somewhere — coherence as a requirement on an ideal agent with a complete, logically omniscient opinion set — what's left of the argument for agents like us, and is there anything in it that could underwrite the ordinary claim that my undergrads are being irrational when their credences across a partition sum to 1.4? My friend also says the whole thing shows Dutch book arguments can't tell the difference between being probabilistically coherent and simply having no opinions at all, and that this is a different and worse problem than the omniscience one; I can't work out whether that's a third problem or the same objection in other clothes.
