I'm screening Arrival for my philosophy of language seminar next week and I want to get past the usual Sapir-Whorf hand-waving. Read it through Quine's indeterminacy of translation instead.

Two things I keep going back and forth on. First: the nations nearly go to war over whether the visitors are saying weapon, tool, or gift — that's basically a gavagai, and the film seems to resolve it as a fixable mistake, the Chinese general just lacked context, more data settles it. But that is precisely the picture Quine is attacking; equally good manuals can fit all the evidence and still disagree about what the term picks out. Does the film cheat here, and if so, is the cheat load-bearing for the plot?

Second, the wrinkle I actually care about. As shown, Heptapod B is a written, non-linear system not tied to any spoken form: no pointing, no queries, no assent and dissent to observation sentences, no way Louise can elicit a yes or a no. Quine's argument leans on that stimulus-and-response scaffolding. So does the film's setup make indeterminacy worse — even less to anchor reference to — or does it show that Quine's scepticism was really an artifact of how thin the radical translation scenario is, and that real translators have more to go on than his field linguist does?

Then Louise's new non-linear experience of time is the film's proof that she has genuinely got their language. Is that a defeat of the indeterminacy thesis, or is the film sneaking in evidence a Quinean has to disallow — altered inner experience and downstream success in action, neither of which can discriminate between manuals?

Give me something fair in both directions: where the film genuinely dramatizes the thesis, and where it just wants the comfort of determinate meaning and I should say so to my students. No citations, just argue it, 600 words-ish, I'll read it out loud.
