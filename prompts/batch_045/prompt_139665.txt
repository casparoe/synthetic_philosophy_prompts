I'm convening a graduate reading group next term on whether cultural change is Darwinian in any interesting sense, and I've realised we keep arguing past each other because nobody has written down what we're asking a theory to do before we start scoring the contenders. So I want the specification first: the list of requirements an adequate selectionist (or otherwise Darwinian) theory of cultural change has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each — for the purpose of explaining, and in principle predicting, why traditions accumulate, persist, and disappear in a population, at a grain that could be checked against a corpus of material culture plus ethnographic observation. No citations and no reading suggestions; I can assemble those myself.

To save you from having to look anything up, here are the cases I want each requirement anchored to, and you should feel free to add better ones of the same shape. Two island populations of equal size, same environment, same starting toolkit: population A's toolkit is stable across four centuries; population B loses three techniques — a type of bone needle, a fish-hook design, a waterproofing method — over the same period, after the two people who made each best die within a few years of each other without having trained apprentices. Nothing else changed; no famine, no migration. I also want you to use: a transmission-fidelity budget (a technique needing many steps to acquire cannot survive copying fidelity below some threshold, yet the same population transmits melodies sloppily and net knots almost perfectly, so the theory has to say what sets fidelity and why it differs by domain); my overnight-extinction test (if every bearer died but all artefacts and descriptions survived, versus if one competent adult survived with nothing to hand, which traditions recover, and what that tells you about where the item was stored); items that spread while harming their bearers (costly or dangerous practices), which forces the question whether for-the-good-of-the-item talk is doing explanatory work or is decoration that can be dropped without loss; and multiple independent invention of the same technique in unconnected populations, which should force you to say what a unit's identity conditions are over time, how we would count units in a corpus, and why similarity must not be read as descent. On top of the cases, I care about these structural requirements: that the theory not build between-group advantage into the definition of a cultural item, since selection can act within groups, between groups, and on individuals; that it be compatible with what we know about how people actually learn — reconstructively, with prestige and content biases, often off artefacts and scaffolding rather than by imitation; that it tell us which pattern would falsify it, given the old adaptationist-programme worry that one can always tell a story after the fact; and that it declare its scope across domains like technology, language, science, religion, law, and fashion.

For each requirement, say why it is a requirement, and sort them into the ones you take to be non-negotiable against the purpose I've fixed and the ones that are really preferences of a camp dressed up as criteria — I suspect the demand for particulate, countable units with population-genetic tractability is the biggest offender here, and I want your view on that. Then the part I most want: flag any subset of the desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied. My own suspicion is that there is a real tension between (i) needing units with stable identity and copying fidelity high enough to explain the ratchet and (ii) taking seriously that transmission is largely reconstructive and pulled toward cognitive attractors, and a second tension between wanting quantitative, predictive population-genetic structure and wanting the content-dependent biases that make the theory psychologically realistic, since the latter seems to buy fit at the price of unfalsifiability. Tell me whether those are genuine impossibilities or artefacts of asking for the wrong kind of theory, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Only after that, in a sentence or two each and with no citations, how the rough families on the table do: the meme-as-replicator picture, population-genetic dual-inheritance models, the cultural-attractor or epidemiological picture, and cultural group selection. Keep the weight on the criteria — the scoring is the part I'll redo with the group anyway.
