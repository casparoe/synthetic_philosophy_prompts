I chair the behavior-support committee at a nonprofit that runs eleven group homes for adults with profound intellectual and multiple disabilities. My younger sister moved into one of them two years ago, so I now also see the output of our process from the family side, which is part of why I'm asking. Every resident has a written preferences-and-goals plan, rewritten annually and reviewed quarterly, signed off by a state regulator; in practice that document determines how a person spends their days — what they are offered, what they are taken to, what staff will wake them for, what gets removed from their environment as a safety risk. None of the residents use speech, so every one of these plans is inferred from behavior. Our current method is essentially a frequency count: over a two-week structured trial a support worker offers a pair of options ten times, and whichever one the resident approaches, reaches toward, or looks at longer gets written into the plan as a preference. The workers who run the trials have high-school educations, no statistics, about fifteen minutes per trial, and usually two or three other residents to supervise at the same time; anything I hand them has to fit on one laminated sheet, use no equipment the unit doesn't already have, and hold up when a reviewer asks how we know.

I recently read about inverse reinforcement learning — inferring an agent's reward function from its observed actions — in a machine-learning context, and it made me anxious about our own practice in a way I can't fully articulate to my committee. The parts I keep circling are these. First, behavior underdetermines the reward function: many different orderings of how much someone cares generate exactly the same choices, and, counterintuitively, a person who always picks the same thing tells you less about the strength of the preference than someone who mostly picks it but occasionally doesn't. Second, choices are made against an option set that we constructed, and a long history of being offered almost nothing can itself shrink what a person reaches for, so a frequency count can quietly launder staff convenience and institutional routine into the resident's own preferences. Third, motor effort, positioning, staff cueing, satiation, and fear of the alternative all move behavior in the same direction that liking does, and our trials don't separate them. Fourth — and this is the one I'm least sure I've got right — a reward function inferred from someone's behavior is not automatically the thing we ought to be serving; there may be cases where the plan should say something about what we owe the person rather than what they'd pick. I'd like the procedure to preserve those four worries while being usable by someone who will never read the theory, and to be honest about which of them it has quietly dropped.

So: please write me a decision procedure my committee can adopt for turning observed behavior into a sentence in a plan. In order, the questions to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which tests a support worker can actually run in fifteen minutes on the unit; where the procedure stops and returns no inference is licensed from this behavior; and where it hands the question to a named human being — the resident where possible, otherwise a family member or an advocate — because it is a judgment about values and not a matter of evidence at all. The output should be a plan sentence a regulator can audit, not a preference ordering, and I'd like it to allow sentences that are instructions to staff about the environment rather than claims about what the resident wants. Then run it on three cases: a resident who picks the quiet room over the music room nine times out of ten (I want to know whether that means she seeks quiet or is avoiding noise, and the plan sentences differ); a resident who reaches for a cookie more often than an apple when the apple is placed slightly farther away and reaching is effortful for her; and a resident who has refused every offered activity for two years and now spends most of the day in bed, where I suspect the honest answer is that the behavior is underdetermined and the right move is something other than writing down a preference. At least one of these should come out somewhere my current frequency count would not have gone, and at least one should be marked as a case the procedure cannot settle. Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire — which residents, which kinds of option, which staff behaviors — and what a reviewer should look for to notice that it has.
