I'm a first-year PhD student rotating into a lab that works on social learning in animals, and I've been asked to circulate a one-page position statement before our reading group on Dennett's four kinds of minds — the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian levels. I care about this more than usual because the taxonomy has become the frame I use for my own material, and I'd rather the statement not fall apart five minutes into the discussion. Below is the set, more or less as I plan to write it. I've included a couple of case verdicts because that's where the reading group will press me.

(1) The four kinds are real levels and they are cumulative: anything Popperian is also Skinnerian and Darwinian, and nothing is Gregorian without being Popperian first. The taxonomy is a taxonomy of learners, and I want it to be sharp — every learner has a determinate level, not a mixture of levels with a bit of this and a bit of that.
(2) What marks the Skinnerian-to-Popperian transition is where the cost of a bad trial gets booked. A Skinnerian creature has to emit the behaviour and absorb the consequence in its own body; a Popperian one runs the trial on an internal surrogate first and lets the surrogate take the hit instead. So the level is fixed by who or what pays, and not by whether the creature is conscious and not by whether it is usefully described as representing anything.
(3) Being Gregorian is not a fourth mechanism sitting alongside the other three. It is Popperian machinery retrofitted with imported tools — words, numerals, diagrams, maps — that make the internal surrogates cheaper, better and more numerous. Gregorian names an upgrade in the quality of what gets tested, not a new way of learning.
(4) Those tools are physical artifacts, and once one is woven tightly enough into a creature's routine — reliably available, consulted automatically, trusted without re-checking — it is literally part of that creature's mind rather than an input to it. The boundary of the mind can run outside the skull.
(5) Rich, reportable, self-reflective consciousness requires Gregorian equipment: until something word-like has been installed inside you, you can be an excellent perceiver and learner while there is nothing it is like to be you in the distinctively human sense.
(6) A verdict I need for my own work. Consider a lineage that learns only by observation: individuals watch the exploratory actions of others, register which of them ended badly, and copy the survivors. Nothing is ever tried out inside the learner, but the learner never pays for a failure either — the cost is borne by other bodies entirely. I have been calling these second-hand Popperians in my notes, and I classify them as Skinnerian creatures with an extra input channel: not Popperian, and not a fifth kind.
(7) A verdict everyone in my department actually wants to argue about. A system trained purely to predict the next token in the deposited output of human minds, with no simulator it runs trials on and no consequences of its own to absorb, is — I want to say — a Gregorian creature, since its entire cognitive endowment consists of imported cultural tools. At the same time (8) I want to say there is nothing it is like to be such a system, and I don't think calling it Gregorian commits me to denying that.

What I'd like is an audit rather than a lecture. Which pairs are actually in tension, and which only look like they are? I have a feeling a couple of my worries are bogus — my guess would be that (1) and (3) are compatible after all, and likewise (4) and (5) — but I would rather you check that guess than confirm it. For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each one tell me exactly what I would have to give up, what the cheapest available revision is, and what that revision would cost me downstream in the rest of the set. Finally, if there is a single revision that cleans up the most, name it — and tell me whether it forces me to give up the idea that there are four levels at all.
