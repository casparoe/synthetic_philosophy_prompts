ok so I need a thing for my school's "speech corner" on friday (its not a real debate, everyone just talks for like three minutes and then people in the room can push back) and I want to argue that white lies are actually ruder than saying what you really think. But I want it to be actually good, not just "honesty is a virtue lol".

Here's what got me thinking about it. My friend Mira says "omg this is amazing" about literally everything — a burnt batch of cookies, a drawing that was honestly bad, my little brother's finger painting. Last month I showed her a poem I'd spent a week on and she said the exact same thing in the exact same voice, and I realised I had no idea whether she meant it, and it didn't feel good, it felt like nothing. So the first part of the argument is about that: praise is only worth anything because it isn't automatic, and its value is sort of a shared thing that we all draw on. When people hand it out for free all the time it stops carrying information, and the people who pay for that are the ones who most need to be told when something is genuinely good — people who aren't already confident, people who don't have anyone at home telling them. Mira loses nothing by doing it, so she keeps doing it. I want that part argued properly: the idea that being "nice" this way costs you nothing and quietly costs somebody else something.

Then there's the obvious comeback and I need the argument to survive it: half the time nobody ever finds out and nothing bad happens. My grandma knits me a sweater every year and they're always kind of horrible and I always say I love it, and she'll never know, and it makes her happy for a whole afternoon. So I don't want the argument to be "you'll get caught eventually," because in that case you won't. What's the actual reason it's still bad? My guess is something like: when you decide for someone that they can't handle the truth, or that the thing they made isn't worth being honest about, you're treating them a bit like a child, and wrapping it in a compliment doesn't undo that. But I'm not sure that's the best version and I want it said better than I can say it.

Also — this is where I keep getting stuck — Mira's family is Japanese and she says that in her family you just wouldn't say the thing that hurts someone's feelings, and that that isn't "ruder," it's just a different set of manners, and who am I to say my way is the better one. I really don't want to sound like I'm standing up there saying some countries are rude and mine is correct. But I also don't want to collapse into "well, it's all relative," because then I have no speech. So can you work out what the honest version of my position is: am I actually committed to something insulting about a whole culture, or can I hold my position without that, and how?

One more thing it has to handle. I don't think bluntness is automatically good either. There's a guy in my class who says every mean thought out loud and then goes "what, im just being honest," and he's just a jerk with an excuse. So please seperate what I'm defending from that. Maybe there's a real difference between saying the true thing carefully and just blurting, and also a difference between both of those and saying nothing at all or changing the subject — which maybe is actually what people should do more of, instead of lying. If the strongest version of my argument turns out to be "usually say the true thing kindly, and when you can't do that kindly, say nothing rather than lie," then give me that version instead of the one I walked in with, and tell me straight if that's what happened.
