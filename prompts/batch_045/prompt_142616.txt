I'd like you to work as two things at once: a philosopher who has taught the rule-following literature at graduate level, and someone who has actually stood up at a dinner and given a toast that landed. Please take your time with this one — the failure mode here is not being wrong, it's being wrong by two degrees, which is worse, because it will sound right to me and to half the room.

**The situation.** I need a toast, and I need it to be honest, which is turning out to be hard.

For forty-one years a man named Milo ran a small fiction magazine, *The Common Room*, out of a walk-up. He had no editorial guidelines except one line he repeated: "Print what someone could read out loud to another person." He never wrote the line down himself; it's attributed to him because he said it constantly. He is seventy-eight. Fourteen months ago he had a stroke; he understands everything and has maybe seven words left. He will be in the front row on Friday night. I was his apprentice at nineteen.

The magazine is being handed over, and the handover has split. One group — call them the Archive people — has digitized roughly forty years of Milo's accept/reject decisions with the manuscripts attached, and a friend of the board has trained a system that now reproduces his verdicts on held-out past submissions at an accuracy the Archive people find decisive. It goes live this month as a pre-screener for the slush pile. The other group — the Room people — says the thing itself was never the decisions but the Fridays: Milo read the shortlist aloud to whoever was in the office and decided afterwards, out loud, in front of us, and they say that a machine trained on his *no*'s has learned nothing about *that*. Both sides quote Milo. He said, often, "anyone can learn this." He also said, often, "you either have it or you don't." There is a manuscript currently circulating that is made of text-message screenshots and audio clips, nothing in forty-one years is remotely like it, and both camps are waiting to see what the other does with it.

**The philosophy I'm leaning on — please correct it if it's off.** My thumbnail, from a night class years ago: Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein argues that for any rule and any finite run of past cases, endlessly many rules fit that run and diverge later, and that nothing about a speaker — no inner state, no intention, no disposition — singles out one of them as *the* rule she meant. So there is no fact of the matter about which rule she was following; "she meant addition rather than the deviant rule that agrees with addition until some large number and then returns five" isn't true and isn't false. But — and this is the part I care about — Kripke's own way out is *not* "so any continuation is as good as any other," and it is not "meaning is just whatever the community says, so majority wins." It's that the shared practice of going on together is all the ground there is, and that our meaning-talk does a different job than stating facts: it certifies someone as one of us and licenses expectations. People call the whole package "Kripkenstein" because most scholars think it isn't Wittgenstein's own view; I don't care about that here.

**The occasion.** About ninety people in the back room of a restaurant: Milo's family, forty years of interns and contributors, local press, and two philosophy professors on the board who will hear a footnote in anything I say. Seven minutes. It is a handover toast, not a eulogy — he is alive and in the room.

**Lines I don't want crossed.**
- No claim that there is a fact about what Milo's standard really was that we might now get right: no "what he meant was…", no "he would have wanted…" used to report a fact.
- No drift into "so any reading is as good as any other," and no drift into "it's just whatever the people in the room happen to do" — I think both are misreadings, and either would make the evening worse.
- No verdict on the screening system. If it appears at all, it appears in passing. The toast is not about machines; I don't want to smuggle in a decision on it under cover of a warm story.
- No words put in Milo's mouth — no "and Milo would tell us tonight…". He can't answer.
- No proper names from philosophy and no vocabulary from it: nobody in the room should need to know what "normativity," "regress," or "disposition" means, and the two professors should be able to hear the structure without being told it.
- It has to be warm and particular, not a lecture with jokes bolted on. Material you can use: the Friday read-alouds; the story he rejected in silence one week and reversed after hearing it out loud, and when I asked him why, he said "it doesn't stand up straight" and couldn't get any further than that; and what he said to me the week I started, "there's no formula, kid — you just keep showing up on Fridays," which I have never been able to hear as just a throwaway line.

**What I want back.** First, before drafting, four or five paragraphs of working notes: what the honest options actually are for the toast's central claim about *carrying on*, given that the two obvious ones are closed to me; which of my six prohibitions you think is right and which, if any, is philosophically misguided or would make the speech impossible to give (push back if you think I've misread Kripke somewhere that matters); and which two or three of the particulars above could carry the weight that a rule can't. Then the toast itself, 850–1,000 words, first person, ending in an actual toast line I can say while looking at him. Then a short afterword — 200 words — noting any place where you had to fudge something, and any place where you think my thumbnail is wrong.
