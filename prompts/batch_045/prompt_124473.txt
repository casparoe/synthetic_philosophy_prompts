I'm a high school senior, and a few of us have been running an informal philosophy reading group; we spent a couple of months on Newcomb's problem and the smoking-lesion case, and then someone brought up counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, which is where I got properly stuck. Because the two standard versions are the only ones anyone ever discusses, I started inventing variants to see whether my reactions track anything real or just track the framing. Below are my gut verdicts, written down before I tried to theorize about them. (Omega, throughout, is a predictor I take to be honest as well as accurate, and it never lies about its own setup.)

1. Omega explains the setup in advance, flips a fair coin, the coin lands tails, and Omega asks me for $100; had it landed heads, Omega would have transferred $10,000 to my account last Tuesday if and only if it predicted I would pay if asked. Verdict: I pay, and it feels less like generosity than like settling a debt.
2. Same numbers, but Omega never explains anything: in the heads branch it silently deposited $10,000 into an account I stopped checking, and in the tails branch it simply appears out of nowhere and asks for $100, having made its deposit decision by predicting how I would respond to being asked cold. Verdict: my gut says no, and I can't tell whether that's cowardice or a real difference from case 1.
3. Omega flips the coin only *after* I decide, promising $10,000 on heads if I chose to pay and taking my $100 either way on tails. Verdict: obviously pay — but I want a principle that reaches this verdict for a *different* reason than in case 1; if it gives the same verdict for the same reason in both, I think it's cheating.
4. Omega predicted me by simulating me for about a minute, and is right 60% of the time about people like me. Verdict: pay — but I'd stop paying if its accuracy dropped below a few percent, which suggests my verdict is really arithmetic.
5. Omega decides on the heads-branch deposit using only a coarse statistical model built from facts I already know (my age, my school, the fact that I own a certain T-shirt); it never simulates me at all. Verdict: I don't pay — being asked tells me nothing I didn't already know about what the model said — though I suspect I would pay if I were unsure which bucket the model had put me in.
6. Omega tells me it may be running me as a simulation rather than speaking to the original, and that in the heads branch the $10,000 went to the original regardless of which of us is now being asked. Verdict: I pay either way; whether I'm the copy seems irrelevant, and I can't say why.
7. Omega formed its prediction by simulating me as I was two years ago, before a long stretch of arguments changed how I think about all this. Verdict: I'd pay, since the old simulation and I probably agree — but I'd be much less sure if it had simulated a stranger who merely shares my demographics, which is case 5, where I don't pay.
8. XOR blackmail: a letter arrives saying that it was sent if and only if exactly one of the following holds — my house will burn down, or I will pay the sender $1,000. Verdict: I don't pay; paying just moves $1,000 to the sender in exactly the worlds where the house would have been fine anyway.
9. Before any coin is flipped, I'd pay $1 for a device that forces me to pay in case 1, and I'd also pay $1 for a device that forces me to refuse in case 8. Both feel right, and I don't know whether that convicts my verdict in case 2.

The principle I keep trying out on myself is this: hand over the money exactly when a different output from my decision procedure would have produced a different, payoff-relevant result somewhere, treating the predictor's prediction as a function of the policy I am running rather than as a fixed fact about the world — and never when the correlation between my paying and the good outcome is explained entirely by things I already know. Please test that against the nine verdicts, repair it where it breaks, and state the repaired version clause by clause with the case that motivates each clause. I especially want to know which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I ought to give up, and whether the ambush case (2) is genuinely different from case 1 or whether my reaction there is just the standard case with extra confusion. It would also help to have your principle's predictions on four cases I haven't made my mind up about: the mirror image of XOR blackmail in which the letter is sent iff exactly one of (a surprise $10,000 windfall that is already settled, my paying $100) holds; a counterfactual mugging where the heads-branch $10,000 goes to a famine-relief fund instead of to me; two hundred identical requests with my memory wiped between each one and only a single coin flip at the start; and a counterfactual mugging addressed to me after I have taken something that makes it physically impossible for me to hand over the money. Please argue from the cases themselves rather than pointing me at papers or reading lists — I have no library access over the summer.
