My flatmate Nino and I have been stuck on this since a pub quiz three days ago and it's getting out of hand — we both did a bit of philosophy at sixth form years ago, so you can assume I know the basic shape of the question about why knowledge is supposed to beat mere true belief, and I don't need to be told to go read Plato's Meno. What I want is a diagnosis of the argument I built, because the conclusion is ridiculous but every step looks fine to me and I can't find the seam.

Set it up like this: imagine two people who both truly believe that the last train leaves at 00:47 — Mina, who checked the timetable app, and Tomas, who was three pints in and just guessed, and happened to guess right. Both catch the train. Now the argument. (1) Hold a belief's content fixed, hold its truth fixed, hold how confident and how stable it is fixed. If two people's beliefs match on all of those, the beliefs are equally useful for getting around the world, so there's no difference in their practical value. (2) So the only place a difference between knowledge and mere true belief could live is in how the belief was arrived at — its causal history, the reliability of the process, whether it was safe, whatever truth-conducive condition you like. (3) Call this the inheritance principle: a feature of how a belief was arrived at can matter only by making it more likely that the belief has some valuable feature it might otherwise have lacked, and the valuable feature in question is basically always truth. (4) But when we compare knowledge with true belief we've already stipulated that the belief is true, so there is no extra truth left for the history to deliver; by (3) it therefore contributes nothing to the value of that belief. It gets swamped. (5) So knowledge is no more valuable than true belief. (6) That can't be right: we credit the person who checked, we pay for expertise rather than for lucky guesses, and we'd be embarrassed to say that a true belief arrived at by flipping a coin is just as good as one arrived at by careful reasoning. (7) And here's the bit that makes me think I've built something broken rather than discovered something: nothing in (3) or (4) depends on the history being specifically causal. Run it with having good reasons instead of a reliable process, or with coherence, or with intellectual virtue, or with understanding — as long as the thing's worth is supposed to come from its link to truth, steps (3) and (4) swallow it too. My argument doesn't just beat reliabilism, it beats the idea that anything besides truth itself is epistemically valuable. It also seems to entail that a true justified belief in a Gettier-style setup is every bit as good as knowledge, which nobody believes.

Nino's contribution, for what it's worth, is that we should just deny (6) and say knowledge isn't actually more valuable, we only feel like it is — which feels like giving up, but he says it's the cheapest exit. I'd much rather keep (6) and keep the inheritance principle in (3), because (3) is what makes epistemic stuff non-magical. So: which step do you actually reject, what exactly is wrong with it (is it an equivocation, a shift in scope, a modal slide, a bad assumption about how values add up?), and what does each of the exits cost me? If (4) is the bad step I want to know what the right way to compare the value of a true belief with the value of a truly-believed-in-a-good-way is, and if (3) has to go I want to know what's supposed to replace it so that I'm not just saying reasons and reliability are valuable for free.
