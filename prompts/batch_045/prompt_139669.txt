The essays are anonymous to me, but I know the writer is someone else in the seminar.

Two pieces of background from the course handout that the question relies on. First, the handout's criterion for when a physical system implements a computation — the counterfactual-mapping criterion: a system S implements a computation C only if (i) some mapping takes states of S onto formal states of C; (ii) the causal relations among S's states mirror the transition rules of C, and not merely C's input-output pairs; and (iii) that mirroring is counterfactually robust, in the sense that it would still have held for inputs S never actually receives, at least within the range in which S's parts keep behaving the way they in fact do. Second, a running example the class was asked to apply it to: the tidal adder — a tidal basin fed by two sluice channels from separate reservoirs, where within a fixed range the equilibrium water level in the basin tracks the sum of the water heights in the two channels, and beyond that range the basin overflows and the output level stops tracking the sum. The rubric is out of 20: 4 points for distinguishing the different things someone might mean by the brain is a computer and identifying which one a computationalist about the mind is actually committed to; 5 points for explaining how the criterion answers the emptiness charge and applying it to the tidal adder; 6 points for facing up to the follow-up worry that the criterion may be satisfied by far too much, and saying what the computationalist should reply; and 5 points for handling the second half of the critic's argument, about neural signalling. Partial credit is expected, and the margins are supposed to say what would have earned the missing points.

The question we set, and the answer I am grading, are below.

Question: A critic of computationalism argues as follows. Saying that the brain is a computer is either empty or false. It is empty, because for any physical system whatsoever one can find some mapping from its states onto the states of some formalism, so the claim that the brain computes comes out true no matter what the brain is actually like and tells us nothing about it. It is false, if it means that the brain is a digital computer in the engineering sense, because neural signalling is graded, continuous and noisy rather than discrete and exact. Write about 600 words in which you (a) distinguish at least two things someone might mean by the brain is a computer, and say which of them a computationalist about the mind must be committed to; (b) explain how the counterfactual-mapping criterion is supposed to answer the emptiness charge, and show what it delivers when applied to the tidal adder; (c) consider whether the fact that very many systems satisfy the criterion — perhaps everything with a stable counterfactual structure does — leaves any substantive content in the computationalist's claim, and say what the computationalist should reply; and (d) assess the second half of the critic's argument about neural signalling.

Student's answer: There are three claims that get run together in the slogan that the brain is a computer. The first is that the brain implements some computation or other. The second is that it implements one particular computation rather than another. The third is the one that matters to a computationalist about the mind: that having the mental states we have just consists in implementing that computation, so that anything else implementing it, in the right way and with the right causal role in producing behaviour, would have those mental states too. Only the third is a theory of mind; the first is nearly vacuous and the second is a claim for neuroscientists. So the critic's dilemma has to be aimed at the third claim, and it is not obvious that either horn reaches it.

The emptiness horn is meant to work like this: for any system you like you can cook up some mapping from its physical states onto the states of some formalism, so no system fails to implement some computation, and calling the brain a computer imposes no constraint on anything. The counterfactual-mapping clause blocks the move. A gerrymandered mapping that merely relabels a system's actual sequence of states will typically break as soon as you ask what the system would have done with an input it never in fact received, and clause (ii) rules out mappings that match input-output pairs while ignoring what goes on in between.

Applied to the tidal adder the criterion does real work. The basin's water level can be mapped onto numbers, and the way that level depends on the two input heights mirrors addition, so clauses (i) and (ii) are satisfied. Clause (iii) is satisfied too, within limits: push either reservoir past the range in which the channels and gates behave as they do and the basin overflows, so the adder adds only over a bounded domain — which is no worse than a pocket calculator, which will not multiply a trillion by a trillion either. So the adder computes.

But that is precisely the trouble, and I think it shows the criterion buys the computationalist less than it appears to. Almost anything with a stable counterfactual structure can be paired with some computation it implements: a rock's microphysical states change in complicated but stable ways, a hurricane has counterfactually robust dispositions about what it would do given conditions it never meets. If the rock and the hurricane implement computations in the same sense in which the adder implements addition, then everything is a computer, and we are back where the critic started. Saying that the brain is a computer is then as empty as saying that it is a physical object, and the third claim inherits the emptiness, because it says only that our mental life consists in implementing some computation drawn from the enormous class of computations that any object whatsoever implements.

The second horn does not work either. Computation need not be digital: analogue computers were built and used for decades, and their whole point is that a continuous quantity in the machine stands for a quantity in the problem being solved. So from the fact that neural signalling is graded and noisy it does not follow that the brain performs no computation at all. There is a stronger reply available as well. By the Church-Turing thesis, any function that can be computed at all can be computed by a Turing machine, and a Turing machine is a digital device manipulating discrete symbols on a tape. Whatever computation the brain performs can therefore be carried out by a digital machine, and the brain is, computationally speaking, a digital computer; the contrast the critic is relying on disappears.

Finally, the Chinese Room — Searle's argument about a person who follows rules for manipulating symbols they do not understand — is often brought in here, but it is aimed at a different target. It grants, for the sake of argument, that the system implements the computation, and then denies that implementing it suffices for understanding. That is an objection to the third claim, not to the first two, and it needs to be answered on its own terms.
