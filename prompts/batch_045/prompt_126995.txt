You are a metaphysician who has spent real time on Zeno-style supertask cases: Thomson's original lamp argument, the best-known reply to it in the literature, and the more recent arguments about whether supertasks are physically possible. I don't want citations, a reading list, or a survey of who said what — assume I've read the standard back-and-forth and that I'm asking you offline. What I want is a principle. Please work before you write: sketch two or three candidate principles, run all seven of my cases against each one, and only then commit to the one you think wins.

In every case there is an ordinary lamp and a switch. The question is always the same: is there a fact of the matter about whether the lamp is on at the limit time, and if so, which? Here are my verdicts, going by gut.

1. The standard lamp. Off at t = 0; the switch is thrown at t = 1 − 2⁻ⁿ for n = 1, 2, 3, …; nothing else ever happens to the lamp. Verdict: no fact of the matter at t = 1. And I'm also confident that the classic conclusion — the lamp can be neither on nor off, so the supertask is impossible — overshoots: the right thing to say is that the story is incomplete, not that the task can't be done.

2. The found lamp. Exactly as in 1, except that at t = 2 someone walks in and finds the lamp on, and I know that nothing touched it after t = 1. Verdict: determinately on at t = 1. This is the pair I can't get comfortable with. Cases 1 and 2 have identical histories up to and including the limit time, yet I want to say "no fact" in one and "on" in the other. Your principle has to either earn that difference or tell me to drop one of the verdicts.

3. The eternal alternator. The switch is thrown at every integer time, forever in both directions: infinitely many tasks, no first one, no last one, no accumulation point anywhere. Verdict: perfectly determinate at every time, and not even faintly puzzling. So sheer infinity of tasks is not what's doing the work.

4. The laggy lamp. As in 1, but the lamp has a fixed relaxation time ε > 0: it cannot complete two changes of state within ε of each other, and scheduled throws that arrive too early simply don't register. Verdict: determinate — for ε = 0.1 I make it off, and please check my counting — because for any ε > 0 only finitely many throws ever land, so the schedule stops mattering. Same schedule as 1, different mechanism, opposite verdict.

5. The wobble switch. The lamp is on iff a rod's displacement x(t) is positive, where x(t) = (1 − t)·sin(1/(1 − t)) for t < 1. The continuous quantity has a limit — the rod coasts to 0 — but the lamp's binary state oscillates forever. Verdict: still no fact of the matter. A student points out that if we set x(1) = 0, the continuous extension, and keep the rule "on iff x > 0", the answer is trivially off. That strikes me as cheating, but I can't say why. Settle it: is he right, and what turns on it?

6. The lopsided lamp. Thrown so that over (0, 1) it is on for a total of 2/3 of the time and off for 1/3: on for 1/2, off for 1/4, on for 1/8, off for 1/16, and so on. Verdict: I still say no fact of the matter, but this is where my nerve fails. In the approach to t = 1 the lamp is on about twice as much as it is off, and I feel the pull of "on". If your principle says "on" here, does it also say "on" in case 1? If it says indeterminate, what exactly kills the majority-of-the-time reasoning?

7. The ganged pair. Two lamps wired to one switch so that exactly one of them is on at any moment; thrown as in 1. Verdict: no fact of the matter which one is on at t = 1, but it is determinately true that exactly one of them is. So "there is no fact of the matter" can't just mean "every claim about the state at t = 1 lacks a truth value." Tell me which idiom I should be using instead, or overturn my verdict.

My own tentative principle, for you to test, break and repair: the state at the limit time is determinate iff either (a) only finitely many throws land in some neighbourhood of the limit time — equivalently, the state function is eventually constant as you approach it — or (b) something not settled by the switch schedule pins it down: a boundary condition, a later observation, a stipulation about the mechanism. Otherwise the story admits several completions and none of them is privileged. I like that this gets 1, 3 and 4. But I worry that clause (b) is a cheat: the real world always contains some further fact, so doesn't (b) make every case determinate after all, which would mean "no fact of the matter" was only ever a claim about my description and never about the lamp? And I can't see what it says about 7.
