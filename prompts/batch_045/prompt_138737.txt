I am the registrar and collections manager of a mid-sized municipal museum: roughly nine thousand objects, mostly European paintings, prints and decorative arts, plus a small contemporary collection that includes several works acquired as certificates and instructions rather than as unique objects. Three or four times a year something turns up that makes us unsure what we actually have — a technical report, a new attribution in the literature, a dealer's opinion, an heir's photograph, an unsolicited email from someone who claims a forgery ring. Each time this happens the same argument starts, and it never resolves: the curator says the object in front of us has not changed and still does everything a visitor needs it to do; the director worries about donors and about the reproduction of one image on half the city's promotional material; the finance office points out that our insurance schedule is keyed to attribution, so a change of wording has a money consequence; someone on the board asks why we are wasting money on a question that makes no difference to how the thing looks. I want to stop having that argument from scratch.

What I need from you is a written decision procedure my office can run on any case where what is in question is what an object is: whether it is by the hand claimed, whether it is of the period claimed, whether it is an original, a version, a studio product, a later copy, an imitation, or a fake. It should issue in one of a small number of dispositions — keep as catalogued; keep on display with a changed credit line or label; keep with a changed label and a changed valuation; withdraw from display; decline or return a gift; refer elsewhere — and it should say what has to be established to reach each.

Please ground the steps in whatever the philosophy of art actually establishes about what is lost when an original turns out to be a copy, and about why that loss is not, or not only, a perceptual one. I do not want the literature summarised for its own sake; I want its distinctions doing work inside the steps. In particular, I have found that the arguments that blow up in committee are always one of four, and I would like the procedure built around them unless you think the decomposition is wrong:

One, the perceptual question — whether anything available to a competent viewer, looking at the object as it hangs, changes if it is a copy. My experience is that it does not, and I want the procedure to say exactly what follows from that, so that people who stop at this step can be told where else their objection has to go.

Two, the question of whose doing this is and under what constraints it was done. The difference between working towards an achievement and working backwards from an effect that already exists seems to me to be doing more real work here than the question of how the object looks, but I have never been able to say it in a sentence the board will accept.

Three, the question of what the object now tells us. Even an untouched picture becomes a different witness once we know it was made in 1920 rather than 1660, and my sense is that this, rather than beauty, is where most of the damage of a forgery actually lies — including the damage to everyone who has written about it in good faith.

Four, the question of what we have promised: to the public buying a ticket, to the donor or lender, to the field that cites our catalogue.

Three further constraints. First, the procedure has to be runnable by me and one assistant with no conservation training, in about a working day per case, because the external conservation scientist takes four months and every report has to be justified against the acquisitions budget. Say explicitly what to do when the evidence will not settle the matter, who carries the burden, what the interim disposition is, and how long an interim label may stand. Second, it must handle the contemporary works that arrive as certificates: I have been told there is a technical term for works whose identity conditions allow correct instances to be produced later, under supervision, after the artist's death, and I want to know whether those go through the same procedure, a branch of it, or none of it. Third, it must give me graded label wording for weakened attributions — the trade's formulae along the lines of studio of, circle of, follower of, manner of, after — together with the rule for choosing between them, and it must say what each does to the insurance figure.

Run the finished procedure on these, with the reasoning shown:

A. A seventeenth-century cabinet picture bought in 1974 as an autograph work. Technical imaging now shows it to be a contemporary workshop version, probably painted partly by the master and partly by an assistant, and probably sold at the time without any claim one way or the other. Nothing about it as an object has changed. What does the label say, what does it do to the valuation, and what happens to the seventy-four file?

B. A light work acquired in 2011 as a certificate with the artist's fabrication instructions, fabricated under studio supervision and accessioned as the work itself. The artist has since publicly disavowed our instance on political grounds, while continuing to license identical instances elsewhere, and has asked us to label it as unauthorised. Is this the same kind of question as A at all?

C. A painting that has hung here for forty years, is the single most reproduced image we own, and was shown by technical evidence to be the work of a known imitator of the master we catalogued it under. Our curators are privately of the view that it is a better picture than any genuine work by that master that we could ever afford. A dealer has offered a serious sum for it on the understanding that it will be resold, at a markup, as a celebrated forgery.

Also give the procedure a stated position on a standing practice of ours: for four months each year a light-sensitive painting comes off the wall and a high-grade reproduction goes into its frame, and the only disclosure is a line in small print at the bottom of the wall text. Tell me whether that is anyone's business but the conservator's.

Finally, two things. Mark, at each step, the places where judgment genuinely cannot be eliminated and has to be handed to me, to the director, to the board, or to our lawyers — including anything that is really a question of rightful ownership rather than of what the object is. And tell me plainly which of the cases above comes out against us. I would rather have a procedure that returns an answer my director will hate than one quietly rigged so that everything we already do turns out to be defensible.
