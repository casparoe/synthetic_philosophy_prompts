I spent forty years in physiology, most of them on feedback control in endocrine systems, and in retirement I have been reading in the theory of well-being. The position I keep coming back to is what I have seen called tranquilism: the view that, as far as a person's welfare is concerned, the only things that matter are the absence of suffering and the absence of craving, and that the felt goodness of getting what you wanted is not a separate positive contribution at all but only the quieting of the wanting that came before it. It strikes me as far more plausible than any view that counts pleasure as a positive good. But I cannot get it to line up with the verdicts I actually have about particular lives, and I would like to know what principle those verdicts support.

Let me state the version I have been working with, so that it can be tested rather than guessed at. Call it T. A person's welfare at a time is lowered by suffering and by craving, and by nothing else; it is never raised by anything. Craving is to be read dispositionally rather than phenomenologically: it is any state with the functional profile of reaching — a forward-directed dissatisfaction with an object, which is quieted by the arrival of that object and which natively motivates — whether or not the subject would report an urge. On T the felt goodness of relief, satisfaction and enjoyment is exhaustible by the removal of such a state, and so never adds. I have also been toying with a distinction borrowed from my old field: between phasic craving, the episode of reaching, and what I would call tonic pressure, a standing background readiness that can be high or low without any episode occurring, rather as a thermostat's gain can be set high without the furnace ever switching on. I do not know whether that distinction earns its place.

Here are my verdicts.

1. The absorbed afternoon. Six hours on a derivation: no frustration, no wish to stop, no sense of striving that I can recall, time simply gone; at the end a few minutes of satisfaction and then nothing. My verdict is that the afternoon was good for me, and not merely in the thin sense of containing little suffering. I want to say the engagement itself counted. Yet at every step I was reaching for the next one, which makes me unsure whether I am reporting a value or a memory of one.

2. The peach. The same fruit eaten when I am genuinely hungry, and eaten an hour after a large lunch with undiminished gustatory pleasure, purely for company. The first is better. But the difference is much smaller than I expected, and I cannot bring myself to say the second contributed nothing. T says it contributed nothing.

3. The glow. Two years on a project; a week of elation when it appeared; six weeks later I was exactly where I had been before. My verdict is that the elation contributed almost nothing to my welfare over that period, and this is the place where the tranquilist reading seems to me simply correct. I list it because it sits badly beside the first case: if the glow after achievement is worth so little, why is the absorbed work itself worth so much, and is that really a difference in the work?

4. Relief farming. I walk up steep hills in cold rain, I eat food hot enough to hurt, I watch films that frighten me. My verdict is that these are fine, and more than fine: I am inclined to say such an afternoon goes better than an equivalent one without the discomfort, and I do not think of the discomfort as a cost that must be outweighed. On T an afternoon with added suffering and subsequent relief can at best be neutral. This is my hardest case.

5. R. After a neurological injury he reports no desires, no boredom, no distress; he initiates nothing and sits contentedly. My verdict is that something is missing for him and that he is not better off than I was in case 1, though on almost any reading of T he is doing better than anyone. This is my strongest verdict against the view.

6. S. A man whose regulatory physiology was a shambles — a flattened and elevated cortisol profile, blood pressure, broken sleep architecture — who reported no distress at all and meant it. My verdict is that S. was worse off than his reports implied, and I want to count something as bad for him. Read phenomenologically, T cannot; read dispositionally, it can only if a non-conscious regulatory strain counts as craving, and I do not see how it can, since strain has no object and nothing quietens it. If S. is worse off, then suffering too may have to be read non-phenomenologically, and I would like to know where that reading stops.

7. Grief. Months of acute distress after a friend's death, which I would not trade away even if I could, and which I do not think I am wrong not to trade away. My verdict is that it was bad for me — it was suffering — while also holding that I am not thereby required to judge that stretch of my life worse overall. I cannot tell whether that is two claims or one confused claim, and I would like to be told.

8. Wanting without liking. A man cued into craving by the sight of a bottle he no longer enjoys. Bad, clearly and without qualification. This is the case that keeps me attached to T.

What I would like from you: one principle, or at most two, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with each clause paired to the case that forces it and with an explicit note of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would rather surrender verdicts than accept a principle that saves each one by adding a clause for it, so please say plainly which verdicts have to go and which one you would give up first. Then apply the result to four cases I have not settled: a life in which desires are many and strong but almost always and almost immediately met, so that frustration is rare; a person who has come to want tranquility and now craves the absence of craving; a creature whose states I doubt have propositional content at all, a dog or an infant, where I want to know whether the dispositional reading covers it, excludes it, and whether the exclusion is tolerable; and my own death, how bad for me that is and why. If the principle that survives is no longer recognizably tranquilism, say so, and say what in my verdicts pushed it out.
