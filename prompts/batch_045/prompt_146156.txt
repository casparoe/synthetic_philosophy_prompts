I am finishing a paper on the reliability of introspection, and before I send the last section to my supervisor I want to know what she will say about the final case in it. She has given me a verdict on every case I have put to her so far, and the verdicts are these:

1. Participants rate the intensity of the pain from a calibrated pressure stimulus that varies randomly from trial to trial; the ratings track the stimulus almost perfectly. She says these reports are reliable, and calls this the boring case.

2. Participants report whether they currently have visual mental imagery and how vivid it is; a minority report none at all, and there is no external check on any of the reports. She says they are reliable, including the negative ones, while conceding that there is nothing to check them against.

3. Participants choose between two very similar items; the experimenter arranges which item ends up in front of them; they then explain why they chose the item they are now holding, sometimes endorsing the one they never picked. She says these reports are unreliable, about the preference and about the reasons alike.

4. Participants report that their evaluation of a candidate was unaffected by an incidental factor such as an ambient odour or the weight of the clipboard they were handed. She says the denials are unreliable even when the participants are confident, and that here we know about ourselves only what we could know about anyone else.

5. At unpredictable moments during a stressful task, participants report whether they feel anxious as opposed to merely keyed up or excited, in a setting where the situational cues are deliberately left ambiguous. She says these reports are reliable, and that introspection earns its keep here precisely because the situation gives nothing away.

6. At the end of a session, participants report which of two earlier episodes involved the more intense experience: which headache was worse, which interval felt longer. She says these are unreliable, because we reconstruct from memory and from a theory of how we should have felt.

7. Just before answering a difficult question, participants report whether they know the answer or are only guessing; the reports are scored against accuracy. She says they are reliable, and that metacognitive feelings of this kind track something real even though they are not sensory.

8. In a detection study she particularly admires, participants get thousands of trials of feedback while learning to report whether a faint stimulus was present, and end up reporting very accurately, in signal-detection terms, whether they consciously saw it. She says these reports are reliable, and she stresses the training rather than the subject matter.

The case I want to add is this. Participants are shown a briefly flashed array of twelve letters, immediately followed by a pattern mask. The array itself is identical on every trial; only the presentation duration varies. Immediately after each trial they answer two questions: whether they consciously saw any letters at all, and, on a seven-point scale, how much of the array they experienced in detail — how full or rich their visual experience was. The instructions never define detail or richness, no feedback of any kind is given, and in a between-subjects condition the identical question is put on an inverted scale, framed as a question about how sparse or gappy the experience was. Participants are not asked to name the letters.

What will she say about the two questions, and how confident should I be in that prediction? More usefully: what rule is she actually running? I can think of several that fit her eight verdicts equally well — that introspection is reliable only for currently conscious states and only about their phenomenal character rather than their causes; that it is reliable only where the report comes from a dedicated inner monitoring rather than an inference from one's own behaviour, one's situation, or a folk theory of oneself; that it is reliable only where the reporter has a discriminative concept of the state in question, sharpened by practice or feedback rather than supplied by the experimenter's question. Say which of these you think she is running, which of them fit the eight verdicts about as well and where they come apart on this one, and whether any of them is knocked out by a verdict she has already given. Then tell me which single further manipulation of this design would best separate the surviving rules, and what each of them predicts about it.
