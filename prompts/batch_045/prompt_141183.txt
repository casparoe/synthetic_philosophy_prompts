So my brother and I are trying to decide whether to sell our dad's house, and its been three months of going in circles. We've swapped every document — the inspection, the agent's emails, the financing paperwork — and I still think there's maybe a 70% chance the buyer is stringing us along, while he puts it closer to 30%. A friend who did a PhD in econ heard about this and sent me the following:

"Aumann's agreement theorem says this: if two people have a common prior — meaning they started out with the same beliefs before either of them looked at any of the evidence — and they're both rational Bayesians, then the moment they tell each other their honest probability estimates, those estimates have to be identical. Rational people can't 'agree to disagree.' So if two people disagree after fully disclosing everything, someone is either reasoning badly or isn't being straight about what they actually think."

He's since doubled down a few times. He says that since we've both read all the same documents, we obviously have a common prior, so one of us is either irrational or hiding something. He also says the practical upshot is that we should just average our numbers and work with 50%, because the truth has to lie somewhere in between and that's more or less what the theorem tells you to do. And he says the people who wriggle out of it by saying "oh, we just have different priors" are doing something unfalsifiable — you can always explain away any disagreement that way — so nobody serious pays attention to that objection. Oh, and he threw in that this is why a rational person would never take a bet from someone who disagrees with them.

I don't know how much of this to swallow, and two things keep nagging at me. First: until I raised it last week, my brother genuinely hadn't considered the possibility that the buyer's financing falls through. He had no number for it at all. I don't see how a result about two people updating over the same space of possibilities handles that. Second, I suspect his 30% isn't a clean factual guess — it's partly "how much would I kick myself if this blew up," which feels like it's smuggling in something other than belief.

Can you go through what he said piece by piece and tell me what's basically right, what's a simplification, and what's subtly wrong in a way that changes the conclusion? Specifically: does his conclusion that one of us is irrational or dishonest actually follow even granting the corrected version, is the averaging advice something the result supports, is he being fair to the "it's just different priors" objection, and do my two worries actually break the argument or are they irrelevant?
