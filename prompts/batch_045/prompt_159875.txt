In a reading group I have been defending a version of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: below some threshold of severity, pleasures and pains are tradeable against one another on a common scale, but once suffering passes that threshold of intensity, no finite amount of mild happiness compensates for it — above the threshold, suffering lexically dominates happiness rather than merely outweighing it. One member keeps dismissing this with an analogy: pleasure and pain are, he says, simply pluses and minuses in a single ledger — one currency, one running balance, the exchange rate fixed by how much of one you would give up for a unit of the other — so of course any suffering has some price in happiness, and a lexical threshold is just a refusal to do arithmetic.

Please audit that ledger. Give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the currency, the exchange rate, the balance, the zero point, the account holder, and whatever makes two entries denominated in the same unit. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for this particular use from the ones that are merely cosmetic, and say what the ledger quietly imports that the target lacks — path-independence, unbounded substitutability, a single subject of the balance, and anything else you find. Does the no-threshold conclusion survive once those are on the table, or was the ledger carrying it the whole time?

I would also like a verdict on a rival picture I have been toying with: not one ledger but two instruments, a thermostat that continuously trades degrees of warmth against degrees of cold, and a smoke alarm that fires at a threshold no matter how pleasant the room otherwise is. Is that genuinely better, or does it just restate my conclusion as an analogy? Finally, say where your audit is most likely to fail, and what would move you to grant that the single-ledger picture is right after all.
