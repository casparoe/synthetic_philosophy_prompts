I'm a novelist and I've been stuck in an email argument with a formal semanticist I met at a residency two summers ago, and we have definately not moved an inch in two years, which is starting to feel like data.

My angle: I write about people changing gradually. A marriage going sour one small slight at a time, a character becoming the kind of person who'd do the thing that would've horrified him in chapter one, a draft I revise sentence by sentence until one day it isn't the same book. So I've got skin in the game on the sorites, and when I finally went reading around in it a few years back I found a literature that seems to be doing exactly what my inbox is doing. The people who say there's a sharp cut-off somewhere and we just can't ever know where; the ones who say there's no fact of the matter at the borderline and the sentence just goes truth-valueless; the degree-of-truth people; the ones who say the cut-off slides around with context and what's at stake. Everyone's arguments are on the table, everyone knows everyone else's arguments, and as far as I can tell nobody has conceded anything structural in decades.

My own hypothesis, which I only half believe: it isn't one disagreement. Part of its stalled because of a verbal tangle about what words like "determinate" and "meaning" are supposed to be doing, part because there's a hidden empirical question about what speakers actually do when they're *forced* to rule rather than musing about one grain at a time, and part because two different questions are sharing a name — one about what a semantics for a language like English should look like, one about whether the world itself can be fuzzy independent of us. And here's the bit I'd push hardest, which I don't see discussed much: almost every case used in this literature is a synchronic series — piles of sand, lines of men, colour chips — while the cases I actually care about are diachronic, one object changing across time. My readers behave really differently about those. Forced to say yes or no — is he a bad person yet? is this still the same novel? — they don't hedge, they just pick, and they get annoyed at me for asking. Which makes me think the "tolerance" intuition everyone leans on might be an artefact of the tidy synchronic cases.

So diagnose it rather than adjudicate it. Which parts are verbal, which substantive, which are different questions under one heading, which are bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for, and where is an empirical question quietly doing the work? Tear my hypothesis apart where its wrong. Then tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis — some experiment, some formal result, a distinction, a change of question — or tell me a permanent stalemate is the right prediction and why.

Please don't give me a tour of the theories or a reading list; I want a diagnosis with reasons, and keep it under 600 words.
