I run a graduate seminar on problems in the philosophy of perception, and I built the following argument as a way of showing that Molyneux's question is harder than it looks. I have since come to think it proves too much, and I cannot find the move that lets the air out.

(1) Molyneux's question, in the form that comes down to us in Locke's Essay, asks whether a man born blind and newly made to see could tell by sight alone which of two objects set before him is the one he formerly knew only by touch — the cube, say, and the globe. Call this the matching judgment.

(2) The matching judgment relates two states: a present visual one and a remembered tactile one. To make it, the subject must take the two states to concern the same object, or the same shape.

(3) Taking two states to concern the same shape requires a representation of shape available to both: something held in common between the visual and the tactile case that is not itself either visual or tactile. Call the thesis that we have such representations amodalism.

(4) So immediate success entails amodalism. That is the unsurprising half.

(5) Suppose instead that he fails. For him to learn the judgment later, some pairing must get the learning started: at some point a visual state and a tactile state must both be present to him as presenting the same shape, and he must be able to tell that they do. Otherwise there is no error signal, nothing to reinforce, and the association between the two never gets off the ground.

(6) But being able to tell that the two states present the same shape just is the matching judgment, which he was supposed to be unable to make. So failure-then-success requires amodalism exactly as much as success does; and permanent failure is not a finding but a silence.

(7) Therefore the experiment has no possible outcome that tells against amodalism: yes confirms it, no is either temporary or evidentially inert. Amodalism has been made unfalsifiable by construction, and Molyneux's question has been converted from an empirical question into a theorem.

(8) That is absurd. Molyneux's question is the sort of thing a newly sighted person could in principle settle, and the no answer is perfectly coherent.

What worries me more is that I can run a mirror of the argument the other way. Suppose he succeeds immediately. Success is equally consistent with a natively amodal shape representation and with extremely fast calibration during the first moments of sight, before the test is administered; and since any test must be administered after sight begins, no yes-outcome distinguishes the two. So the yes answer is unfalsifiable as well, and the question is empirically empty — a conclusion I reject even harder than (7).

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (8) than (3) or (5), and I would rather give up amodalism than give up the claim that the question is answerable by finding things out. But I would like to know what I am paying, and I suspect something cheaper is available.

I also want the diagnosis tested against three cases I invented for the seminar, because an account that only works on the original pair of objects is not worth much.

(i) The twelve solids. Before the operation the subject is trained by touch alone on twelve wooden solids and on their names, and is trained to sort them blindfolded into pairs of like shape. Afterward he is shown two at a time and asked to name them. Suppose he fails at first and succeeds within some weeks. On your diagnosis, what exactly did he acquire, and from what signal? State explicitly whether the account you give needs an amodal representational format, or whether it can do without one.

(ii) The new channel. A fully sighted adult is fitted with a device that encodes a distal property she has never sensed — the direction of the ambient magnetic field, say — as the location and intensity of a vibration across her back. She already knows how to point north, by landmarks and by compass. The Molyneux-style question: on first wearing the device, could she tell from the vibration alone which way is north? If the argument above is sound it should generalize here unchanged. Does your diagnosis give the same verdict here as in the original case, and should it?

(iii) The reverse case. A man born without tactile sensation in his hands acquires it as an adult; he is asked whether he can tell by touch which of two objects before him is the one he is looking at. Is this structurally the same question? If your diagnosis treats it differently, say what the difference is, and say whether that asymmetry shows the original argument was quietly leaning on something special about vision.

Please name the step; explain why it looked fine; price the alternatives among the positions a seminar would take seriously — the associationist or empiricist one, the nativist or amodal-format one, the sensorimotor or enactive one, and the view that separates bare discrimination from recognition or concept application; and tell me plainly if some part of the difficulty is verbal, a shift in what can tell which is which is being read as, rather than a disagreement about how the mind is put together. If instead you think the argument is valid and the only question is which premise is least costly to abandon, say that, and say which premise and why. Finally, if one of my three cases is a bad test of your diagnosis, say so rather than making it fit.
