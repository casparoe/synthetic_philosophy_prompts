I built a little ranking tool for my team (sprint planning mostly, and yes, lunch) and someone on a forum told me the set of properties I want is provably impossible and pointed me at something called Arrow's theorem. I dont have any philosophy background — I write backend services — and I'd rather work out what my own gut verdicts commit me to than just be told its impossible. So here are my verdicts on actual cases from the last few months. Twelve people voting; the options are M = mobile app redesign, B = billing rewrite, O = onboarding bug bash.

0. The tool has to output something for every possible set of ballots, including the cycle case below. I can't return an error or refuse to answer when the input looks weird.

1. All 12 put O above M. Output has to put O above M. Obviously.
2. Only two options on the table, 7 of 12 put M above B. M wins. Also obviously.
3. 7 people vote M > B > O, 5 vote B > O > M. So M is first choice for 7 and dead last for 5, and M also beats both other options head to head. My gut: M wins.
4. Same profile, but now look at B: nobody ranks B last, it's first or second for all 12. My gut: B wins. That's the whole reason we rank things instead of just each picking one.
5. Everyone ranks a joke option R (rewrite it all in Rust) dead last. Adding R shouldn't change the relative order of M, B, O, and removing it shouldn't either.
6. This is the one I care most about: I want to take the top 3 of the output and treat that as the ranking of just those 3, without re-running. I reuse outputs for shorter meetings and I don't want the answer to depend on which options happened to be on the ballot that week.
7. Nobody's ballot should just get printed back as the result, not even the tech lead's.
8. Shuffling the order the ballots arrive in must change nothing. Same set of ballots, same output, every time.
9. Split us into the backend 6 and the frontend 6. If both halves, run seperately, put O above M, then the whole team has to put O above M.
10. If someone moves O up one slot on their own ballot, O must not come out lower. That would just be a bug.
11. I'd rather the tool never emits a tie, but I'll take a tie over something arbitrary.

My current rule: for every pair of options, count who more people prefer; score each option as pairs won minus pairs lost; sort; break ties by average rank. Two things went wrong with it. First: we had 11 ballots, a 12th came in late ranking O first, and O fell from 1st to 3rd. I assumed my sort was broken. It wasn't — two other options' scores went up more than O's did. Second: we hit a cycle (5 vote M > O > B, 4 vote O > B > M, 3 vote B > M > O), the rule emitted a three-way tie, and I broke it by hand, which is dumb.

Cases I haven't made up my mind about, where I want your rule to tell me what to think: adding a near-duplicate option (rewrite it in Rust / rewrite it in Rust with Axum) — my instinct is that cloning an option shouldn't help it or hurt it, but I don't know whether my other verdicts force that. Partial ballots: 4 people have never touched the billing code and write "no opinion" for B. I do not want that treated as ranking B last; what should it be treated as? Whether the tool should emit a full ordering or just a single winner — I assumed a full ordering, but if winner-only makes the impossibility go away, say so. And whether any of my verdicts say anything about one guy who has started voting strategically, favourite first and everyone else last.

So: is there one principle, or a short list of conditions, that gets all of this? State each clause precisely and say which case motivates it. Be honest about which of my verdicts can't all survive — I suspect 3 and 4 are the pair that can't both hold and that 6 is the expensive one, but I want to be told which single verdict is cheapest to give up and what I get in exchange. Then give me the surviving rule in one sentence I can paste into the README, because I have to explain it to 12 engineers who will not read a paper.
