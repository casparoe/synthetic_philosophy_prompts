hey — I'm in a course on Christian origins this term (history dept, not divinity, so it's all "what will the sources bear" and basically no theology) and I've gotten stuck on something that seems to be stuck for everyone else too. We spent two weeks on the criteria of authenticity — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, Semitisms and Palestinian-environment stuff, contextual credibility — and the pattern in the readings was always the same: one scholar announces a criterion, applies it to a saying or an episode, gets a result, and then the next scholar applies the *same* criterion to the *same* saying and gets the opposite result, or points out that the criterion only fires if you already know the answer, or that it rests on claims about first-century Palestine we just don't have the data for. My seminar leader said this has been the state of play since the 80s and that the last fifteen years have mostly been one camp arguing the criteria are methodologically bankrupt against another camp saying we have literally nothing better and should keep using them carefully. Nobody seems to have moved, and my flatmate (classics) says it's the same shape as the quest for the historical Socrates, which I'm not sure helps but it stuck with me.

What I can't work out is what *kind* of stuck this is. My hypothesis is that it isn't really a fight about the criteria at all: it's that different people want the criteria to produce different products — a biography of an individual, a causal explanation of how a movement got started, or a licensed reading for a community that already cares — and each side then declares the criteria broken because they don't deliver its product, while using the same vocabulary of "authentic". But I can also see a boring empirical question hiding inside (how often does material that passes multiple attestation actually go back to the figure it's attributed to, in cases where we have some independent check?), or a straight disagreement about whether the gospels are evidence or are the thing to be explained, or maybe just the authentic/inauthentic binary itself being the problem — the ill-posed option. I don't know how to tell these apart from the inside.

So: diagnose it, don't survey it. Please don't give me a reading list or a bibliography or a tour of what everyone has said about each criterion — I've got those, and I'd rather have your reasoning than your citations. Take the two sides as you understand them and tell me which of these diagnoses (or something else entirely) is actually doing the work, with reasons, and then what would count as progress given the diagnosis you land on. Two things I want you to address specifically: (1) I've been toying with the idea of treating it like a calibration problem — instead of arguing about the criteria, run each one over material about some first-century figure we have decent independent knowledge of and see what its hit rate actually is, then discount accordingly. Would that settle anything, or does the analogy break, and why? (2) A guy in my seminar keeps insisting that once you admit the texts are testimony rather than data, the criteria stop being a discovery procedure and become a discipline on your reading — a way of making your interpretation accountable rather than a way of finding facts. That sounds to me like definately moving the goalposts, but maybe it's the actual insight and I'm missing it. Give me the honest version, including if your answer is that no progress is possible here and here's why.
