I write plotted fiction of an unfashionably determinate kind: I fix the final chapter before I write the middle, and a large part of the craft lies in deciding what, earlier in the book, the ending can honestly be said to have hinged on. I have agreed to contribute a short essay on causation in narrative to a collection, and before I commit my working assumptions to print I would like them audited. Several of them I hold for quite different reasons — some because they strike me as true of the world, some because they are forced on me by the practice of writing — and I have never checked whether they compose.

My working commitments are these.

(1) Causation is counterfactual dependence: a species of difference-making, not the production or transmission of anything. For the same reason I resist interventionist accounts. It seems to me wrong to build into the metaphysics of what causes what either an agent's capacity to manipulate or a model somebody has chosen to write down. The counterfactuals are there in the world, fixed, as I understand the standard view, by an objective ordering of similarity between possible courses of events — the Lewis–Stalnaker apparatus, as I have met it in secondary discussion — and that ordering is not a matter of convention or of what we happen to care about.

(2) Causal claims are contrastive and interest-relative. There is no such thing as the cause of an event full stop; there is only: this rather than that brought about this rather than that. Which contrasts are in play is settled by what is at issue, and managing which contrasts are salient to a reader is a substantial part of what a novelist does.

(3) Absences are causes in the fullest sense. The letter that never arrived, the sentence left unsaid, the call not made: these are not second-class causal citizens, not mere allowings that lapse into something weaker than causation, but causes on all fours with the door slamming.

(4) Reasons are causes of actions, and the reasons that explain what a character does are facts — that the letter had arrived, that the train had already gone — rather than merely the character's internal states.

(5) My story-worlds are deterministic: everything that happens in them follows from earlier happenings together with how the world runs. I do not take this to threaten the claim that only some of the earlier happenings are causes of the ending and most are mere background conditions.

(6) In judging what caused what in a plot, I hold the ending fixed. The question is: given that the book ends as it does, what earlier did it depend on? I do not allow the ending itself to vary in the nearest alternative course of events, and I set aside backtracking counterfactuals — that had she not missed the train she would have been killed in the crash — as irrelevant to what the missed train did.

Two of these worry me in advance, though I am not confident either worry is real: I suspect (2) sits badly with the closing clause of (1), and I suspect (5) quietly makes every earlier event a cause of the ending.

What I want is an audit, not a tutorial. Please tell me which pairs of these commitments actually pull against each other, ordered by severity rather than listed as if equally bad; for each genuine tension, what exactly would have to be given up; what is the least costly revision that restores consistency; and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set — in particular, whether it drives me toward the interventionism I resist in (1), or toward demoting absences in (3), or toward relocating reasons from facts to events in (4). Tell me also whether there are tensions I have not noticed, including between items I have treated as comfortable neighbours. Say plainly which of my two suspicions is the one that dissolves on inspection and why it dissolves, and whether any other apparent conflict in the set is merely apparent. Finally, if I can make only one change, which single revision does the most work, and what does it leave me unable to say?
