Write a decision tree for an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar whose students can recite the standard labels but freeze when handed an unfamiliar biological case. The instrument should take a user from a case description to a named position on what the unit or level of selection is in that case, without ever asking them which label they like.

Start from a fixed root: the user has observed that some trait has increased in frequency across generations in a population and must say what the selection was on. Every branch node must be a question answerable from a well-specified description of a case by someone who does not know which leaf it leads to. Draw your nodes from the conceptual space rather than from the textbook list of names — questions in the vicinity of: whether there is variation in the trait among candidate units at the level in question; whether the candidate units reproduce, in the sense of producing further units of that same kind on a comparable timescale; whether the benefit of the trait accrues to the same token entity that pays the cost; whether the fitness differences at the higher level leave any residual once the lower-level changes have been fully accounted for, or whether the higher-level story is merely a re-averaging of the same changes; whether the trait in question is a property of a single organism or only of the collection (an aggregate of member traits versus something that only the collection has); whether the entities at the lower level have aligned or conflicting transmission routes. Where you think a node belongs that is not in this list, add it.

Aim for roughly fourteen branch nodes and eight or nine leaves, presented as an ASCII tree or a cleanly indented numbered list. The leaves must include strict replicator-level selection, organism-level selection, kin selection or inclusive-fitness accounting, group selection in the interdemic sense, multilevel selection in both its variants (the one where collective fitness is the average of member fitness and the one where it is the collective's own output of new collectives — gloss both when you first use them), species- or clade-level selection, a pluralist leaf for those who hold that several of these are alternative bookkeepings of one causal process, and at least one leaf for a view that resists the whole framing (a developmental-systems or niche-construction style position, if you think that is where the resistance genuinely belongs). Each leaf should carry a one-sentence characterization, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and a tag saying which of the three questions below it is principally an answer to.

The three questions are a teaching distinction of my own that I want the tree to enforce. Distinguish the unit of causation — the entities whose actual properties produce the fitness differences; the unit of bookkeeping — the level at which one chooses to sum or partition the change, where the alternatives may be formally intertranslatable; and the unit of explanation — the level at which the story is most perspicuous, and the level that supports the counterfactuals a working biologist actually wants to draw. Tag each leaf with which of the three it mainly answers, and flag each branch node where the three answers come apart, since those nodes are where I expect the arguments in class to happen.

Build the tree so that it works on all six of the following cases, which you should use as your test set and refer to by these names:

(i) The sentinel rodent. A burrowing rodent lives in colonies of about forty, all descended from one breeding pair. Roughly a third of juveniles become permanently sterile adults that never disperse, give alarm calls, and maintain the tunnels; colonies with more such adults produce more dispersing breeders. A genetic marker shows no allele associated with the sterile caste: whether a juvenile becomes one depends on how much food it was fed as a pup.

(ii) The driving allele. An allele transmitted through males disables the sperm carrying its alternative, so it reaches ninety percent of the offspring of heterozygous males. Individuals homozygous or hemizygous for it are forty percent less fertile. It also causes carriers to scent-mark food caches that relatives feed from.

(iii) The monastic aphid. A maternally transmitted bacterium causes its insect host to secrete a waxy public good that feeds the whole gall, and also to sterilise uninfected hosts in the same gall. Infected hosts are somewhat shorter-lived than uninfected ones in galls where no one is infected.

(iv) The productive colony. A trait raises the number of daughter colonies a colony founds while lowering the lifetime reproductive output of every bearer relative to non-bearers in its own colony; local colonies bearing it at high frequency tend to collapse.

(v) The flightless clade. In a clade of island beetles, reduced dispersal is an individual-level trait; at the level of lineages, wingless species speciate faster and go extinct faster, and there is no variation among species in anything relevant beyond what is already fixed by the dispersal trait.

(vi) The inherited burrow. A species modifies its local environment in ways that reliably alter the development of its own offspring, and the modification persists across many generations.

After the tree, include a short diagnostics section with three parts. First, list every pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and say whether the indistinguishability is formal, causal, or merely practical. Second, identify the regions of the logical space your nodes allow but that no actual position occupies, and for each say whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has bothered with it. Third, give a trace running two cases from root to leaf — the sentinel rodent and the flightless clade — and note the node at which a careful user would reasonably stall, and why. Use no citations, no author names as authority, and no bibliography; if a distinction needs a label to be usable, define it inline.
