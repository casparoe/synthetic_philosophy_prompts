I am a staff engineer at a studio that operates a persistent online world with roughly two million monthly players, and I own the service that triages player-harm reports: everything a player submits as "someone took something from me" or "someone ruined this for me" flows through it, and it decides whether a report is closed automatically, sent to a human reviewer, or escalated to a council that includes elected player representatives. My current task is the reviewer runbook, which is in effect our operational answer to a question I am not trained to answer: which losses inside our world are real losses, and which are only losses in a game.

The constraints. Reviewers are contractors with about three minutes per ticket and no background in any of this. They can see server-side facts — logs, inventories, patch history, who was present and when — and the player's own written statement; they cannot verify anything about the player's life, health, or mental state. Every decision needs a code from a fixed menu plus two sentences of justification, and a sample is re-reviewed each quarter, so the runbook has to make similar cases come out the same way and has to be defensible to people who will ask why one player's loss was reversed and another's was not. Restoration itself comes in three technical flavours, which I suspect matters more than it appears to: we can roll back to a prior state, we can recreate an object from a template, or we can do nothing because the loss is not representable in our data model at all. I also want restoration and sanction kept as separate outputs — I can accept that a real loss occurred and still think the right response is not to reverse it.

I do not want an explanation of the underlying metaphysics, and I do not want citations or a reading list. I need a procedure. Please write me the decision procedure my reviewers should run, in order, where for each step you give the question, what counts as a sufficient answer given the evidence a reviewer actually has, and the exit condition: close as not a harm, restore, sanction the other player, or hand to a human because this is a question of values rather than of fact. Name the points where the procedure must stop and say that it is stopping, rather than forcing an answer. And tell me which steps are doing real work and which are only there to make the output auditable.

The cases the runbook has to handle — please make sure each lands somewhere definite, or is explicitly refused:

1. A full-loot server where taking another player's entire inventory is a stated rule; the taker followed the rule, and the loser opted into the server knowing it.
2. An item we can restore perfectly, bit for bit, by duplicating it; the player insists that what came back is not the thing that was taken.
3. A rival who deliberately crashed the instance during a progress attempt, so that a guild's completion was never recorded and the recognition for it went to someone else. Nothing was destroyed; an irreversible opportunity was.
4. A memorial that players built for a dead friend, demolished by the player who lawfully bought the land and did not know what it was; the players who built it have not logged in since and may never see the demolition.
5. A silent model update to a companion NPC that a player had interacted with daily for two years: nothing deleted, no rule broken, and the player reports the loss of a relationship.
6. A character deleted near the end of a final season, where re-earning everything is possible in principle but not in the circumstances the player is now in.
7. An item whose value came almost entirely from a real-money market on another platform that our own terms prohibit.

Then run the finished procedure on three of these, chosen by you, including at least one where the answer is one my team will not want to hear. My guess is the NPC update, since honouring it would mean versioning and pinning persona state indefinitely, but pick whichever case actually puts the most pressure on the procedure.

Three further things, marked explicitly. First, where the procedure must accept a player's own account of their experience as sufficient even though nothing server-side corroborates it, and where it must not. Second, where my engineering instincts are likely to be leading me astray: I keep reasoning that a rollback is idempotent and therefore undoes the harm, and I keep reasoning that if we can copy an object then nothing scarce was taken, and I am not confident about either. Third, the question the procedure is not allowed to beg: whether the fact that we can switch the world off, and some day will, is supposed to change what we owe in cases 1 through 7. I would rather have that stated as an open decision with the two options laid out than quietly assumed in one direction.
