so I help run a small reading group on Darwin and Turing and what people call the strange inversion of reasoning — competence first, comprehension later, maybe never. Last session we got stuck on something adjacent to the text rather than in it: how often that idea now gets wheeled out as a conversation-stopper. Someone mentions a big model, a market, a hospital triage protocol, a supply chain, and someone else says "well, nobody understands how it works, that's basically Turing's point," and then the question of who is answerable just evaporates. I want to make fun of that move without making fun of the idea itself — the inversion is real, the slide from "comprehension isn't required for competence" to "comprehension is a defect" is what I'm after.

Write me a leaked internal standards document, 900–1100 words, formatted as numbered clauses with a short definitions section, in the deadpan house voice of an ISO-style certification body. Invent the body (something like an Institute for Competence Assurance) and call the standard something like "Comprehension Risk Standard CRS-4: Requirements for Certified Incomprehensible Operation." The client is just "the Firm." Don't name any real company, product, or living person; the target is the discursive habit, not anyone.

What has to be in it:

1. A definitions section that does the real philosophical work. Define "competence," "comprehension," "post-hoc rationalization," and two things I'm inventing: explanatory capture (the observed effect where once a team's stated rationale starts steering the process, performance measurably drops — verified by double-blind A/B) and the Comprehension Lag Ratio (the gap between what a system can do and what anyone can say about it; a shrinking ratio is a reportable defect).

2. A tier structure, Tier 1 to Tier 4, where each tier requires less comprehension than the last. Top tier, call it Presumptive Opacity, requires that no employee including the original builders be able to state any true generalization about the system, and that attempts to do so degrade it. Make the tier criteria sound like ordinary quality management.

3. A clause mandating scheduled rationalization purges — the destruction of documents that explain how things work — on the grounds that retained explanations attract liability and mislead new staff into thinking comprehension is the goal.

4. Two worked examples in corporate form, one clearly a riff on Darwin (an ops process nobody designed, just inherited by copying whatever last quarter's winning team did) and one on Turing (a forecasting engine that outruns anything its engineers can say about it).

5. A clause on "accountability interfaces": there must be a named human who takes the blame, and that person must be selected precisely for not understanding the system, since understanding would make them an accomplice rather than a conduit. Let one honest objection surface in a sub-clause and be dismissed in jargon.

6. The peak: the standard applied to itself. The auditors are themselves certified under CRS-4, nobody at the Institute can state what the certificate means, and this is presented as the highest grade of compliance. Client organizations are forbidden from asking.

Please don't let it tip into Luddism or hand-wringing — the document should be cheerfully, bureaucratically pro-black-box throughout. A reader who knows the Darwin and Turing material should be able to tell that you're exaggerating a real feature of the inversion rather than inventing a fake one; a reader who doesn't should still be able to tell that something has gone badly wrong. No quotations from anything, no citations, nothing that needs the web. Couple of typos is fine, I'll clean them up.

Then add a 150-word afterword, out of character, saying where the satire leans on a contested reading — I'm thinking specifically that "competence" in the Darwinian case is metaphorical because selection isn't an agent with a standard of success, and that the Turing-side claim is arguably about a research strategy (build first, understand later) rather than a recommendation about finished systems — which of those readings the piece needs in order to be fair, and one sentence on what a sympathetic defender of the inversion would say makes the whole thing a cheap shot.
