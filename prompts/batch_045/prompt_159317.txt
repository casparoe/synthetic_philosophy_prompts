I am a novelist, and for the better part of two years I have been writing a book whose turning point is not an event. A woman who is the only person in a position to make one phone call does not make it; two days later someone dies; and I want the reader to close the book convinced that the un-made call stood among the causes of the death, and not merely as its occasion, its backdrop, or the thing that makes the death feel worse. The technical difficulty is that a novelist cannot stage an absence directly. The page has to show something happening, and everything I know about how readers allocate causal attention says that a paragraph in which nothing occurs will be read as filler rather than as causation.

Reading around the philosophy has helped less than I hoped. I can sketch the rough shape of the dispute: whether omissions are not events at all and so cannot enter causal relations; whether they cause only where some norm, duty, or expectation has been violated; whether bare counterfactual dependence is enough, given that for any outcome there are indefinitely many things nobody happened to do, almost none of which we would think of calling causes; and whether talk of causation by omission is really talk about explanation, about responsibility, or about the pragmatics of blame. A friend who teaches tort law insists that without an antecedent duty there is simply no omission to speak of, which I suspect is a lawyer's convenience rather than a metaphysician's truth. In any case the material I have reached keeps treating omissions as one kind, and my material will not sit still for that. I have begun sorting them into four: absent agency, where no one was in a position to act; withheld agency, where someone was, could have, and did nothing; blocked agency, where someone was positioned to act and would have, except that the capacity to act had been quietly removed by an institution that never issued any order one could point to; and unformed agency, where the failure is not one of action at all but of noticing, of forming a belief, or of putting a name to what one already plainly sees. I would like your view on which of these can genuinely be causes rather than mere conditions, and what makes the difference.

As a discipline on myself I keep rereading Kazuo Ishiguro's novel about the butler Stevens, because it is made almost entirely of such things: the failure to question an employer's political judgement while arranging his house and his meetings; the failure to be at a dying father's bedside because one is downstairs serving dinner; the dismissal of two housemaids, carried out without demur and later describable only in the vocabulary of professional standards; the entry into a room where a colleague is visibly in distress, and the elaborate pretence of having noticed nothing. Read the book for me through my fourfold scheme, naming and describing the scenes you would point me to, and tell me how much of our willingness to call these omissions causes rests on their violating a role or a norm rather than on their making a difference. Is the novel's own causal picture closer to a duty-based account or to a counterfactual one? Do its omissions compose, so that one omission causes the next and the chain, rather than any link in it, is the real explanation of the life? Is the narrating voice itself an instance of the fourth kind, a failure of attention rather than a refusal to act? And tell me where the book resists the reading: where treating this man as a cause of what happened is precisely the distortion the novel was written to expose, and what the novel knows about causation by omission that the philosophical papers do not.
