I am trying to work out what I actually think about precommitment — the personal case, in which someone binds their later self, and the constitutional case, in which one generation's settlement binds later ones — because my verdicts about particular arrangements are confident and yet refuse to line up behind any rule I can state. Below are the verdicts as I hold them, followed by the rule I have been half-using to justify them. Please test that rule against the cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me which of my verdicts cannot survive. I am not asking for a survey of the literature: I want the analysis rather than the bibliography, and I want you to argue from the cases rather than from authorities.

My cases and verdicts.

1. A recovering addict, lucid, asks a companion to hold his money and to refuse him cash for three weeks. He begs, then threatens, then offers to pay double. The companion refuses. I think the companion should refuse, and that this is respect rather than paternalism.

2. The same arrangement enforced instead by a time-locked container that nobody can open for the full period, at any price, for any reason. I now hesitate: the device works better and yet strikes me as less respectful than the companion.

3. As in 2, except the container can be opened early on payment of a large but finite sum, which he cannot comfortably afford. I approve of this more than of either 1 or 2 — an ordering that I cannot justify, since a weaker precommitment is being rated above a stronger one.

4. A woman with bipolar disorder, while well, authorizes involuntary hospitalization and medication during any future manic episode, the authorization expiring after a fixed period and subject to independent review. Honoring it during mania is, I think, clearly right.

5. The same woman, while well, stipulates that if she is ever calmly and stably depressed for a year and asks to stop treatment, her request is to be overridden. I reject this, and I cannot explain the difference from 4 without using the bare word irrational, which is doing more work than it can bear.

6. A writer promises to deliver a manuscript in six months under a penalty clause, no withdrawal permitted, and then receives a better offer. The promise binds. Note that there is no asymmetry of judgment here at all: the later self is, if anything, better informed.

7. Someone signs, lucidly and for money paid up front, a permanent and irrevocable contract of personal servitude with no exit. Not binding, and not merely because of fraud or duress; I would void it even if the signature were spotless.

8. A founding generation entrenches freedom of expression and fair trial against repeal by an ordinary majority. Legitimate.

9. A founding generation entrenches a detailed property regime and a religious establishment, under an amendment procedure so demanding that it has never once been used. Not legitimate. My objection here cannot simply be that entrenchment is undemocratic, since I accept 8; I need to know what separates them.

10. A rights provision repealable only by identical votes in two consecutive parliaments with a general election in between — a lock that a transient majority cannot defeat but a persistent one can. I approve of this more than of any other entrenchment device, including 8.

11. A state ratifies a human rights convention with no withdrawal clause: I approve. The same state signs a hundred-year agreement locking tariff policy with no exit: I reject it. I keep wanting to say that the difference is subject matter, and that the difference is procedure, and I cannot hold both thoughts at once.

12. A city bans new highway construction by referendum, permanently. Twenty years later a majority, citing new traffic data, wants one. I want the ban to be revisitable but costly to revise: delay plus supermajority, friction without a lock. Yet in 8 I accept something very close to a lock, and in 12 there is more new information than in 8.

13. A father's will funds a trust that pays his adult daughter only if she does not marry outside her faith and does not enter a specified profession. Not binding; offensive. But 8 and 9 are precisely one generation binding the next, so I need to know why the founders may and the father may not — and whether the answer is about who is bound, or about what is being bound, or about whether the binding generation and the bound one belong to the same continuing agent.

14. Demographic drift. A constitution is adopted by 52 percent of a small electorate and entrenched under the two-parliament lock of 10. Over thirty years the electorate triples through immigration and naturalization; the original ratifiers are a small minority of the living; most voters never consented and cannot realistically amend. My approval weakens but does not collapse. Is there a principled decay rate here, and what is it a function of — elapsed time, turnover of persons, change in the reasons, or something else?

15. Reverse Ulysses. At twenty, convinced by a moral argument, she makes a public pledge never to eat meat, backed by a forfeitable deposit held by an association. At forty, having read and thought far more, calm and lucid, she wants out and is willing to lose the deposit. I say: the deposit is forfeited, the pledge is not enforced. But the addict in case 1 also has new experience — he knows how the craving feels, and I want him held. I cannot separate these by calling one of them irrational, which is the move I rejected in case 5.

16. Durable mandates. At my request my partner hides the whisky and refuses to say where; when I demand it that evening, refusal is fine. The same partner, under the same standing request, refuses to tell me eleven months later when I say, sober and unhurried, that I can handle one glass. Now the refusal is not fine. Open-ended mandates seem to expire even when nothing about me has gone wrong, which connects this case to 5 and 15.

The rule I have been using: call it the epistemic-priority rule. A precommitment binds to the extent that the earlier self was better placed to decide than the later self is, and it lapses entirely once the later self is better placed. I like it because it explains 4 and 15 and 16 cleanly. But I cannot see how it explains 6, where there is no asymmetry and the commitment still binds; nor 7, where the later self seems worse placed and the commitment is still void; nor 8 and 9, since the founders were not better placed than we are about free expression and were certainly not better placed about property; nor 13, where it is unclear whose epistemic position is supposed to count; nor 14, where there is no single self across time at all; nor the ordering in 2 and 3, where the size of an escape cost seems to matter for reasons that have nothing to do with who knows what.

What I want from you.

Tell me, first, whether the epistemic-priority rule survives as a component of a better rule or has to be discarded, and say which of my verdicts the surviving rule forces me to give up. Then state the rule, or at most three candidate rules, precisely enough to apply to a case I have not listed, giving the clause that does the work in each case and the case that motivates each clause. I especially want you to say whether one principle can cover both the personal and the constitutional cases, or whether the resemblance between binding oneself and binding one's successors is only a metaphor — and if it is only a metaphor, what the real principle is on each side.

Then apply your preferred rule to three cases I have not settled. First, a living will: executed at thirty, refusing life-sustaining treatment; at eighty-five the same person, still competent, with no new diagnosis, wants treatment continued. Second, a company's founding charter locks its stated mission against amendment by shareholders, who later want to amend it by a large majority. Third, the reverse lock of 14 with the signs changed: a later electorate, clearly better informed about the costs of an entrenched right, wins a clear majority twice but cannot carry the second vote under the two-parliament lock, and so is blocked.

Finally, be explicit about what your rule does not settle — the residual question about precommitment you think my cases expose but that no principle of the sort I am asking for can close.
