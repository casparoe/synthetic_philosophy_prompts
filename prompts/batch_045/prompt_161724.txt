I convene a small interdisciplinary reading group — two philosophers, three psychologists, one psychiatrist — that has met on self-deception for roughly two years, and we have stalled. I want a diagnosis of the stall, not a verdict on the question itself.

The stall is not for want of shared data. We agree on the cases: the spouse who assembles the evidence of infidelity and then sincerely denies what it shows; the patient who interprets every symptom reassuringly; the colleague whose grant was rejected and who reports, in full sincerity, that the review process is fair and that he never wanted the money; the parent who cannot see what everyone else sees about her child. We also agree on the psychology: motivated search for evidence, asymmetric scrutiny of confirming versus disconfirming considerations, selective attention and recall, and the banal finding that people sincerely avow propositions they have strong evidence against.

Where we split is on what the phenomenon is. One camp — call them the intentionalists — insists that genuine self-deception requires what interpersonal deception requires: someone who knows or at least truly believes that p, who sets about bringing it about that the target believes not-p, and who succeeds. On their account the self-deceiver must somehow sustain both the true belief and the false one, and must have produced the false one by something like a strategy; the phenomenon worth theorising is that this ever works, and the question worth answering is how. The other camp — call them the deflationists — treats the paradox as an artefact of an over-demanding definition. On their account self-deception is simply belief acquired or retained through motivated reasoning: no simultaneous true belief is required, no intention or plan is required, and the appearance of paradox comes from theorists importing into the case the true belief that only they, from outside, possess. Build the requirement of simultaneous contradictory belief into the definition, they add, and either the state is conceptually impossible or it becomes so rare that it is no longer the everyday phenomenon we set out to explain. Each side accuses the other of changing the subject: the intentionalists say the deflationists have relabelled ordinary wishful thinking; the deflationists say the intentionalists have built a puzzle into a definition and then marvelled at it.

My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that three things are going on at once and that only one of them is substantive.

First, part of the disagreement is verbal: 'self-deception' is being used for a state and for a process, and success conditions are built into the definition on one side but not the other.

Second, the substantive part is not about the cases but about the background metaphysics of belief — about whether believing is a single unified state with one causal-functional role, in which case believing p and believing not-p is impossible and the intentionalist picture looks incoherent, or something more fragmented, a context-sensitive dispositional profile or a set of sub-personal representational states, in which case there is room for genuine conflict without paradox. If that is right, the self-deception literature has quietly been a dispute about the individuation of belief, conducted with clinical vignettes.

Third, there may be a hidden empirical question that our evidence cannot settle. Notice that self-deceivers often hedge: the jealous spouse checks the phone while describing the checking as tidying; the patient who sincerely believes she is well quietly moves the scan to the earlier date; the rejected applicant drafts the resubmission before telling anyone the rejection did not matter. Call this defensive hedging — acting in ways that make sense only given the proposition one avowedly rejects. I argued for months that hedging is the decisive datum, and I now doubt it is diagnostic: the intentionalist can read it as evidence of a tacit true belief, the deflationist as evidence of non-doxastic, perhaps merely affective or sub-personal, sensitivity to a threat, and behaviour does not seem to discriminate between the two.

So suppose, for the sake of argument, that tomorrow the following were all established: motivated-reasoning effects are robust and large across domains; physiological measures show that self-deceivers' stress responses track the threatening truth even while they sincerely avow the comforting falsehood; and no paradigm we can design distinguishes in principle between the agent's believing p while also believing not-p and the agent's not believing p while being sub-personally disposed toward p-shaped behaviour. Which parts of our disagreement would move under that supposition, and which would be untouched? Is the third item a real empirical question in disguise, or a pseudo-question generated by the second?

What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, not a survey of theories of self-deception — though I do want you to state what you take the strongest formulation of each camp to be, and to say plainly where my three-part hypothesis goes wrong, if it does. Is the impasse best explained as verbal, as a dispute about which cases or which markers count as data, as a dispute about what would count as an adequate explanation of a mental state, as a hidden question about belief individuation, or as bedrock intuitions about agency and responsibility that neither side can argue for? Is the question whether self-deception is possible ill-posed, merely hard, or two questions sharing one name? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or should we expect none and stop meeting?
