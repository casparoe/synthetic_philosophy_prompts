I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas — and I keep running into the phrase "right but early," lately mostly from people talking about Malthus, so I've been collecting arguments about it. Two friends got into a long one about it at dinner and I honestly can't tell who was winning. Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?

Elias works on long-run growth data. His opening was that Malthus got the mechanism right and the century wrong. The wage-to-population-to-wage feedback — better harvests, earlier marriages, more mouths, real wages pushed back to subsistence — is, he said, one of the better-confirmed relationships in the pre-1800 record. Malthusian arithmetic is a model of that regime. What happened after 1800 isn't a refutation, it's a change of regime: run the same model with industrial parameters and you get a different output. Right mechanism, wrong century.

Nadia, who works on prediction in science, said he didn't publish a model with a switch marked "regime." He published a forecast: that any sustained rise in income would be dissolved by population growth. That's an open-ended empirical claim, and it's been false for two hundred years in exactly the direction that mattered. "Right but early," she said, is just what you say when you won't specify in advance what would have counted as being wrong.

Elias came back with ceteris paribus — nobody discards a model because its background conditions moved — and added that there's a concrete sense in which the Malthusian test hasn't been run yet: the last two centuries were a one-off energy windfall, fossil sunlight plus industrially fixed nitrogen. That's not a background condition, that's the treatment. You don't get to call the experiment finished while the treatment is still being administered.

Nadia said that's the tell: nobody wrote the energy exemption in 1798, they wrote it after the anomaly, and an exemption written after the anomaly explains the past while licensing no new bet. She compared Wegener — continental drift was rejected, then vindicated, but not by waiting. It was vindicated when seafloor spreading produced a risky novel prediction, magnetic striping, that got confirmed. You don't wait your way out of being early; you get out by generating something new that rivals can't account for, and Malthusianism has only produced retrospective redescription.

Elias said that's unfair and offered a test with numbers: global fisheries, where effort keeps rising, catch per unit effort keeps falling, and per-capita supply sags — a prediction on data nobody had in 1798. And he volunteered a kill-criterion: fifty consecutive years of rising per-capita food supply and rising real wages everywhere while population growth is negative, and he'll call it dead.

Nadia said the fishery case doesn't discriminate, because common-pool resource theory predicts the same thing without the population arithmetic, and his kill-criterion is the escape hatch with a date stapled to it.

I have to file something and I can't referee it: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a line of justification each, then say who's more right overall — and whether "right but early" can ever be more than face-saving, and what someone would have to do to earn it.
