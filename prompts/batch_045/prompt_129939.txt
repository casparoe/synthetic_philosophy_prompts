I spent forty years in structural geology, so I have a weakness for load-bearing metaphors, and Ive only been reading philosophy properly for about two years — there's a reading group at the library. Last month two of the regulars, Miriam and Alan, got into it over Dennett's crane/skyhook vocabulary and whether an evolutionary account of human morality is a crane or a case of greedy reductionism. I sat there taking notes and honestly cannot tell who won. Here's roughly how it went, tidied up. (I'm not going to say which one I'm inclined toward, because I'm not sure.)

1. Miriam: natural selection is the model case of a crane — it hoists designed-looking complexity out of replication, variation and differential retention, all bottom-up, nothing descending from above. So when people explain our moral and conventional instincts by kin selection, reciprocity, reputation-tracking and then cultural group selection layered on top, that's a crane of the same sort. Every rung rests on the one below it.

2. Alan: that's textbook greedy reductionism. Not because it's evolutionary, but because it runs straight from fitness to 'you ought to keep your promises' and skips the levels where the work actually happens — agents, reasons, practices, institutions. As he understands the distinction, the complaint is about skipping levels, not about reducing as such.

3. Miriam: 'greedy' is just the word you use for a reduction you don't like. I didn't skip the levels, I listed them. Fill in the middle and the accusation evaporates, so the term is doing no work except registering discomfort.

4. Alan: every rung you listed is causal and descriptive, and the top one is normative. You can explain how we came to believe promise-breaking is wrong without ever showing that it is wrong. The ladder isn't missing rungs, it's leaning against the wrong wall — it hoists the psychology of moral judgement and you then announce it has hoisted morality. Call it a load mismatch.

5. Miriam: maybe that's all there is to hoist. If the supposed normative fact does no explanatory work beyond the psychology, we don't need it, and dropping it is parsimony, not greed.

6. Alan: then let's attack the crane picture directly. Every crane in the chain is itself a complex functional structure needing its own crane: genes need replication machinery, the machinery needs a metabolism, and so on down. The image is cranes standing on cranes — but what is the whole rig standing on? Either it bottoms out in something brute (the laws, the initial conditions) which does all its explaining while being unexplained itself, or it never bottoms out and it's cranes all the way down. Either way crane-vs-skyhook isn't an exhaustive pair and the victory lap is premature.

7. Miriam: brute bedrock isn't a skyhook. A skyhook is top-down, question-begging and complexity-generating; bedrock is at the bottom, minimal, and not question-begging. The axis that matters is direction of explanation, not explainedness. And your regress worry is indiscriminate — it would sink physics too, so it can't be what's special here.

8. Alan: then here's my real point, the one I keep chewing on. Some of these cranes are self-feeding loops. You need replication machinery in order to get replication. You need a language-using community in order to get language. You need creatures who already respond to reasons in order to get creatures who respond to reasons. Nothing there descends from simplicity; the output is a precondition of the input. Dennett has no name for that, so I'm going to call it a bootstrap crane, and my question is whether it's a genuine crane or a skyhook in a crane's hard hat. My suspicion: 'bootstrapping' is exactly the move the skyhook concept was invented to forbid, and that greedy reductionism always happens at the loop.

9. Miriam: every crane worth having is a loop like that. Autocatalysis runs on its own output, enzymes are made by enzymes. If a loop is a skyhook, then half of chemistry is supernatural, which drains the word of any content.

10. Alan: then give me a test. A principled way to tell a benign loop from a question-begging one. 'Loops are fine' is precisely the answer I'd expect from somebody who needs one.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? I'd like each of the ten moves rated from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification, and then an overall verdict. Three things I especially want your read on: (a) whether 'greedy reductionism' can be given a test that isn't just 'a reduction I reject' — Miriam's point in move 3 seems stronger than Alan answers; (b) whether the bedrock-and-regress worry in move 6 is a real problem for the crane picture or a confusion about what cranes were supposed to be for; (c) whether the bootstrap crane is a crane, a skyhook, or evidence that the dichotomy isn't exhaustive. And please say what both of them are missing — I have a feeling they're each assuming something about whether cranes get evaluated one step at a time or as a whole trajectory, but I can't quite articulate it. Don't tell me what to go read; I have the books. I want the adjudication.
