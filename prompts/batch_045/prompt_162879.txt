You are a professional philosopher specialising in philosophical logic and the philosophy of language, with years of work on vagueness behind you. Please work slowly and carefully: before committing yourself, sketch out for your own benefit the space of candidate principles you might offer me — the standard ones, the hybrids, the ugly ones — and run each against the verdicts below, because I would rather have two or three precisely stated candidates with their costs spelled out than one tidy story that quietly abandons a case it cannot handle. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Please write in British English.

I am finishing a thesis chapter in legal theory on how standards — reasonable care, dependant child, permanently incapacitated — get applied at the margin, and I have been trying to work out what I actually think about vagueness by going case by case rather than by signing up to a theory. My supervisor thinks the whole debate is a pseudo-problem generated by an over-literal picture of meaning; a friend in formal semantics thinks my verdicts are simply inconsistent and that I should grow up and either be an epistemicist in Williamson's style, with sharp boundaries somewhere and permanent ignorance of where, or a supervaluationist in Fine's style, with a range of admissible precisifications and determinate truth as truth on all of them. I have resisted both, and here is roughly where I land. (a) A man with eight hairs is bald, a man with a full head is not, and in the stage version — a machine removing one hair a second while he stands there — I do not think there is a hair count at which he becomes bald: no fact of the matter, not merely no way of telling. (b) Run the machine in reverse, adding hairs one a second to a freshly shaved head, and — this is the verdict I am least sure of, and the one my friend laughs at — I think the transition, if there were one, would not be at the same point: the count at which he stops being bald going up is not the count at which he starts being bald going down. (c) A committee of seven whose exact ages I know, say 34, 41, 47, 52, 58, 61 and 63, is neither determinately young nor determinately not young, and here the ignorance story seems to me to have nothing to work with, because there is nothing further about those ages to discover. (d) Likewise for a baldness judgement where I know the exact hair count, the exact scalp coverage and the pattern of loss: still indeterminate, and not through any gap in my information. (e) But when a grant panel has to decide whether an applicant is bald for a subsidy, with full photographs and counts in front of it, I think there is a determinate right answer for them to reach, one they could get wrong, and that it is fixed by what the subsidy is for even though no one could derive it. (f) Of a borderline case I will happily assert that either Jack is bald or he is not, and I will also assert that it is not determinate whether Jack is bald; I do not want to give up either. (g) If Jill has more hair than Jack, I take it that if Jack is bald then Jill is bald is determinately true even though neither Jack is bald nor Jill is bald is determinate, and this seems to me the strongest point in favour of the supervaluationist picture. (h) Yet I also think second-order borderline cases are real — there are cases that are borderline borderline — and I deny that whenever it is not determinate whether Jack is bald it is determinate that it is not determinate whether Jack is bald; that collapse of higher-order vagueness into a single sharp boundary is, to me, what sinks the supervaluationist treatment of determinately. (i) And when a legal definition tries to help by saying that bald means noticeably hairless to a casual observer, I do not think the vagueness disappears; it moves into noticeably and casual observer, and I do not believe there is a bottom level of perfectly precise predicates that the vague ones abbreviate.

The principle I have been toying with, and which I want tested rather than flattered, goes like this: a predicate is vague for a speaker or a context just in case her dispositions to apply it, together with the purpose at hand, leave open a range of admissible ways of making it precise and nothing else settles which is in force; a sentence is determinately true when true on every admissible precisification; the purpose at hand narrows the range and can in principle narrow it to one, which is what makes (e) come out determinate, while the route by which the question was reached helps settle which precisifications count as admissible in the first place, which is what is supposed to explain (b); and the range never closes for good, which is what second-order borderline cases consist in. Can you tell me, first, whether that survives (a) to (i) and where it has to be repaired — in particular whether the purpose clause can do the work I need in (e) without making every vague predicate sharp the moment it appears in a statute or a form, and whether the route clause in (b) is coherent at all rather than a muddle between my use of a predicate and the property it tracks; second, what the repaired account predicts for two cases I have not listed, say a standard whose very purpose is contested, such as dependant child in a statute whose drafters disagreed about why dependence matters, and a predicate whose users are all dead and whose use-dispositions we have reconstructed exhaustively from a corpus; and third, and most usefully, which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and which of them I ought to abandon if I want a principle worth having at all.
