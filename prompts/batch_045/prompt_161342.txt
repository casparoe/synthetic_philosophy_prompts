Hey — I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on testimony and the past, 16 students, 80 minutes, and I've got one session I want to build around the fight between a community's memory and what the record says. They've read general epistemology but nothing on memory specifically, and I don't want a lecture up front or a reading to prep.

What I want them to feel rather than be told: that when the two conflict, "which should give way" isn't mainly an accuracy question. The shared story can be doing work — holding the group together, carrying a meaning, telling them what kind of thing just happened to them — that the record doesn't do, so correcting it is a genuine loss even when the correction is right. And I want them to feel that the record's authority is borrowed: it wins only as long as nobody asks where it came from and how it was kept.

My rough plan — tell me if it's dumb. About ten minutes in, an accomplice of mine bursts in and makes a scene: I'm imagining she's furious about a grade, says something that reads as a threat, knocks over a water bottle, leaves. We then carry on with the normal seminar for half an hour. Then everyone writes down alone what happened; they pool in fours into one agreed account; the four accounts get merged at the board into one class version. My bet is that the class version comes out confident, tidier than any individual one, wrong on at least a couple of specifics, and coloured in a moral way ("she was aggressive", "this is what students are like now") that nobody was actually given.

Then I produce the record — I'll have had my phone running. It shows the scene was staged, the angry line was read off a script, the bottle went over by accident. But I want the record deliberately damaged too: audio drops for the fifteen seconds that matter, or the frame misses the bottle entirely, so they can't just default to "tape wins" and go home.

Then the forced choice: the class has to decide what goes in the seminar's own minute of the meeting, which I email out and read aloud next week. Small real stake — whatever they pick becomes the group's first memory of itself. That's where I want them stuck.

What I need: exactly what the accomplice does and says (this is the load-bearing bit, be specific), what to plant so the class version definately drifts, what to do if nobody takes the bait, rules and timeline, and a debrief that maps moments onto the ideas and only names the distinction in the last ten minutes. Also what will go wrong first time I run it, and which bit of this the activity can't produce and I'll just have to say. And a line on whether deceiving them like this is okay and how I hand it back at the end.
