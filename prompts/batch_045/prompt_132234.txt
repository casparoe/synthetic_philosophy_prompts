I have been defending a position about generational cohorts — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — that I now want stress-tested rather than supported.

The position is that these terms are not kind terms at all. They are not natural kinds, and I would also resist calling them social kinds with membership conditions. They are names for concrete historical particulars: bounded, dated slices of a population with a shared causal history. The analogy I lean on is the move in philosophy of biology that treats species as individuals rather than as classes — on that way of thinking, a species name picks out a segment of the genealogical nexus, and belonging to it is a matter of standing in the right causal-historical relation, not of instantiating a trait profile. I want to run the same move for cohorts. On my view, what makes someone a Millennial is not that they hold certain attitudes, consume certain media, or came of age under certain economic conditions as such; it is that they were born within a particular window of a particular population and so occupied a certain causal position with respect to a cluster of formative events. Two consequences I embrace rather than apologize for: first, there can be no laws about generations, only histories of them, so every empirical generalization of the form Millennials are thus-and-so is either a description of one particular or an artifact of where analysts happened to draw their birth-year window; second, the notorious vagueness about where a generation begins and ends is not a defect in the concept but exactly what one should expect, since the temporal boundaries of concrete particulars are vague in just this way.

What I want from you is a map of this view's exposure: what would have to be true, or what would have to be found, for it to be false. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed defender of my view should concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it, and on what grounds — I am more interested in what the view's friends would have to grant than in what its critics say about it.

Among the things I want addressed: the identification problem familiar from age-period-cohort research. Suppose that, once age effects and period effects are fit, adding cohort buys essentially nothing — no residual cohort effect worth the name. Does that tell against the existence of cohort particulars, or only against cohort *effects*, and is my view entitled to the distinction? Relatedly: suppose it turns out that exposure to putatively formative events is distributed so smoothly and unevenly across birth years that any stepped partition of the population is an artifact of analyst choice, and that the best-fitting partitions are unstable across different choices of event set. I would then have no fact of the matter about where the supposed particular begins or ends beyond convention. Is that fatal for a view that says cohorts are individuals, given that an individual which cannot be distinguished from its neighbors or from a slightly larger and slightly smaller candidate individual seems to have lost its claim to be one thing? Or may a defender reply that particulars need not have sharp or discoverable boundaries, and if they may, has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — and is that a problem here in the way it would be for an empirical claim?

I also want two cases handled directly. The duplicate case: a second population, causally isolated from ours, passes through a structurally identical sequence of formative events a century later. Is that a second and merely similar generation, or the very same generation again? If the honest answer is the same generation again, have I conceded repeatability and thereby conceded that I was talking about a kind all along? The near-boundary case: two children of the same parents, born a few years apart on opposite sides of the conventional boundary, with formative exposures nearly indistinguishable. My view says flatly that they belong to different generations and that there is nothing more to say. Is that a genuine cost, and is it a smaller cost for a view on which generations are property-based kinds whose membership can come in degrees?

Please also say what my view predicts that its rivals do not — I take it that it predicts generationhood can come apart entirely from any psychological or behavioral profile, that a cohort could be split into two generations by mass emigration or war, and that a set of people with identical attitudes scattered across birth years would not thereby form a generation — and then tell me whether any of those predictions are the sort of thing that could fail. Which rival does each defeater hand the win to: the homeostatic-property-cluster or social-kind treatment, the constructivist treatment on which generations are discursive artifacts produced by the discourse itself, or straight eliminativism about cohorts?

Finally: I arrived at this view mainly because it dissolves the tedious industry of pronouncing on which birth years a generation spans, and I suspect that attraction is doing some of the work. Tell me which of the defeaters you have identified I should personally find most persuasive given that motivation, and whether the view survives it.
