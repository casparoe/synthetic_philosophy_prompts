hey — I'm writing a novel and I've been tieing myself in knots over something that's half a craft problem and half a philosophy problem, so thanks in advance.

The narrator is careful, a little risk-averse, mid-forties, runs a small business, no cushion to speak of. I want to put her in two scenes maybe forty pages apart where she's secretly facing the same gamble and answers it the opposite way, and I want the reader to feel both times, immediately, that she's obviously doing the right thing. Then a sharp reader — and one character, the ex-husband, who's an insufferable rationalist — notices she can't have it both ways.

Here's the shape I'm after, and I'm pretty sure its the Allais thing, the independence axiom, but I need somebody to actually build it rather than just describe it:

Scene one: some big slice of the probability — call it the high eighties percent — pays out the same good-enough thing whichever option she picks, and the two options only differ on the remaining sliver. On that sliver she takes the safe one: she won't risk the bad outcome for the shot at the great one. Scene two: something has already gone wrong, through no fault of hers, and nothing about her has changed, but now that same big shared slice pays out something much worse. The sliver is untouched — same size, same odds inside it, same dollar figures. And now she takes the gamble, and it feels obviously right.

Constraints, and these are the ones that keep wrecking my drafts:

- No certainties. Every option in both scenes has to be a genuine gamble, at least two outcomes with real probability on them. The textbook version leans on a sure thing and I don't want that — its too easy, and my rationalist would just say "certainty effect" and the room would go flat.
- The bad outcome can be bad but it can't be special: no ruin, no zero, no death, no losing everything. Just a crappy result she'd live with.
- Same woman, same amounts, same week — ideally the same few days, or even the same afternoon, so nobody can say her tastes changed or she got tired or she learned something. The only thing that moves is what the shared slice pays.
- It has to be a real violation, not risk aversion wearing a hat. There should be no utility function over the money that lets an expected-utility maximizer give both of her answers. Don't just assert that, show me. My understanding is that the gap in expected utility between the two options comes out to the sliver-weight times the gap over the sliver, so the shared slice cancels out entirely, and that's exactly why no EU maximizer can flip. Show me that on your numbers.
- Ordinary stakes: money, or time, or the business, or a lease. Amounts a real person could plausibly come into. No billions, no hospitals, no trolleys, no sci-fi.
- One short paragraph per scene, and they should read like life, not like a survey instrument.

Also: if you can do it without making the scenes unreadable, I'd like the sliver itself to be a real gamble on both sides rather than one option being a flat payoff inside it, so I can honestly say there's no certainty hiding anywhere in this thing.

What I need back:

1. The two scenes.
2. The proof that it's the structure I asked for. Lay out the shared slice, the sliver, the mixing weight, and show that independence forces her two answers to match. Then do the check above: no expected-utility maximizer with any utility over the money can make both choices. Brief algebra is fine, I can follow it.
3. Which single feature is doing the work. My guess is that it's something about how the shared slice changes her attitude toward the same spread, but I want it stated precisely — and I want to know what collapses if you take it out: what if the shared slice paid the same in both scenes? what if it got better instead of worse?
4. How the ex-husband should attack it. Give me the strongest version of the expected-utility objection — the "you'd pay to avoid your own later choice" / money pump kind of argument, or whatever the real argument is, I don't know its name — and then give me one sentence of dialogue, zero jargon, that he could say at a dinner table and have it actually land.
5. And be honest with me about where the case is weakest. If dropping the certainties makes the reversal harder to feel than in the textbook version, say so, and tell me what you did to prop it up — the numbers, the emotional setup, whatever — instead of quietly sneaking a sure thing back in. I'd rather find out now than at a reading.

thanks!
