I have no academic background in any of this; I've just been attending the hearings of my region's standing citizens' commission on drug policy for a couple of years, and I've been trying to work out what rule it is actually running, because its decisions don't line up with any single slogan I've heard. Its record so far, as I have it:

- Cannabis: approved legalisation — licensed shops, home growing up to a few plants, plain packaging, no advertising, minimum age 21.
- Psilocybin and the other classic psychedelics: rejected general retail sale; approved access in supervised therapeutic settings; rejected criminal penalties for personal possession and small-scale growing.
- Heroin and cocaine: rejected legal sale; approved prescription maintenance, supervised consumption rooms, and making simple possession a civil rather than a criminal matter.
- Possession of any drug for personal use: approved removing criminal penalties and handling it administratively, with a health referral attached.
- Alcohol: rejected every proposal to restrict availability further, but approved a large increase in tax per unit of alcohol, mandatory labels about cancer risk, and a ban on outdoor advertising.
- Tobacco: approved ending the sale of cigarettes to everyone born after a certain year while leaving sale to current adults alone, and approved a ban on flavoured vaping products.
- Novel synthetic drugs that were sold openly as legal highs some years ago: approved an immediate ban on their sale and rejected a competing proposal to license and laboratory-test them instead.
- Naloxone and nicotine pouches: approved making both available over the counter.

The proposal now in front of them is new to me, and I can't find any earlier decision that is obviously on all fours with it. A company has developed a family of compounds — call them substitute spirits — that reproduce the subjective effects of alcohol (relaxation, mild euphoria, disinhibition) by acting on the same receptor system, but with a short and predictable duration, no hangover, no organ toxicity in the trials so far, and a dependence liability that the company's own data put at roughly that of alcohol, perhaps a little lower. The trials are short-term, so nobody yet knows whether people would substitute these for alcohol or simply add them on top of it. Two further wrinkles: current roadside breath tests do not detect the compounds at all, and there is no reliable way to tell from someone's behaviour how much they have taken. The proposal on the table is to license them for sale exactly like alcohol — in shops and bars, with advertising, with an age limit.

What I'd like from you: what is this commission most likely to decide, and what are the two or three rules that fit its whole track record about equally well but come apart on this case? The candidates I've managed to come up with on my own are: something like legalise whatever does less harm than the illegal market it replaces, and tax and label the rest; something like adults may take what risks they like with their own consciousness, unless the drug itself erodes the capacity to choose, in which case access goes through a doctor; and something like the state may legalise a practice that is already widespread but should refuse to create a new market in an unfamiliar intoxicant, so that novelty and thin evidence are themselves reasons to wait. I suspect all three fit the precedents and that they do not all fit the new one. For each rule, say what it predicts here and why; tell me which earlier decision is the closest analogue and what the analogy depends on; give me your single best prediction with the reason you prefer it; and tell me which further case — which variant of this proposal, or which other substance — would actually separate the surviving rules. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, tell me what principle the commission would be committing itself to with each of the live options.

One last thing: I'd be grateful if you'd flag where your prediction is most likely to be wrong and what would change your mind. Which further fact about these compounds, or which argument about how to read the earlier decisions, would make you switch to a different answer?
