Exam question (take-home, Philosophy of Mind). In the debate about whether dreams are experiences, Malcolm argues that dreams are not experiences. His central claim is that the only criterion for saying that someone has dreamed is the waking report (or associated waking behavior); there is no criterion that picks out an occurrence of experience during sleep itself, and the waking report is not a memory of such an occurrence. Hence, for Malcolm, to dream is not to undergo an experience while asleep. Dennett replies that Malcolm illegitimately moves from an epistemic or criterial premise to a metaphysical conclusion. Even if the waking report is our only ordinary criterion, there might still be experiences during sleep, and we could learn about them empirically. To make the point vivid, Dennett imagines a cassette theory: during sleep nothing experiential occurs, but at the moment of awakening a narrative is composed and played back into the subject's memory, producing exactly the impression of having had a dream. Because this is a possible empirical hypothesis that Malcolm cannot exclude a priori, Dennett concludes that Malcolm's conceptual argument fails; the question whether dreams are experiences is empirical, and the available neurophysiological evidence gives us reason to answer it affirmatively, even if the cassette theory cannot be conclusively refuted.

For this question, consider a different model, which I will call the Real-Time Simulation with Editorial Reconstruction model, or RTS-ER. RTS-ER agrees with Dennett that dream reports are not straightforward memories of a temporally ordered sequence of experiences. It also agrees that the waking report is assembled at or after awakening, smoothing over discontinuities and adding connective tissue. But RTS-ER disagrees with the cassette theory about what happens during sleep: it holds that during REM sleep the brain undergoes imagery-like states that are genuine experiences. On RTS-ER, those sleep experiences are the raw material for a later editorial reconstruction, and the waking report is the product of that reconstruction rather than a veridical memory of the sleep experiences.

Answer all of the following:
(a) Does RTS-ER provide an answer to Malcolm's criterion argument? Explain precisely which premise of Malcolm's argument RTS-ER accepts or rejects, and whether Malcolm could legitimately respond that RTS-ER changes nothing.
(b) Does RTS-ER answer Dennett's cassette-theory objection? In particular, is RTS-ER simply a neuroscientific version of the cassette theory, or is it a distinct position? Explain.
(c) Overall, does RTS-ER show that dreams are experiences? If not, what further premise or evidence would be needed?

In your answer, be careful to distinguish Malcolm's actual argument from a crude verificationism, to state Dennett's own conclusion correctly, and to say whether the editorial reconstruction in RTS-ER helps or hurts the claim that there are sleep experiences.

Student answer:

Malcolm's argument is essentially epistemic. He starts from the fact that the only evidence we ever have for a dream is the subject's waking report. There is no way to check, while someone is asleep, whether she is undergoing experiences, and the waking report is produced only after she has awakened. Since the report is often fragmentary and revisable, Malcolm concludes that it is not a memory of any experience during sleep. His conclusion is therefore that dreams are not experiences: the ascription of a dream is empty unless it is grounded in the waking report, and the only thing we can legitimately say exists is the report itself. In short, because we cannot verify from the outside that a sleeper is having an experience, the posit of a sleep experience does no work, and Malcolm's criterion view reduces to the claim that the dream just is the waking report.

Dennett's objection is that Malcolm has confused an epistemological point with a metaphysical one. Even if the waking report is our only criterion, it does not follow that there are no experiences during sleep; the report could be caused by such experiences without being a memory of them. Dennett's cassette theory is meant to show this: if a narrative were composed at the moment of waking and played back into memory, the subject would have exactly the same evidence she actually has, even though no experience occurred during sleep. Because this story cannot be ruled out a priori, Malcolm's argument fails as a piece of conceptual analysis. Dennett's own view, however, is that the question is empirical and that the neurophysiological evidence—especially the correlation between REM sleep and later dream reports—supports treating dreams as real experiences during sleep. The cassette theory is thus a skeptical possibility that Dennett uses to block Malcolm, not a hypothesis he endorses.

Turning to RTS-ER, I think the model is best understood as a neuroscientific version of Dennett's cassette theory. Like the cassette theory, it says that the report is not a straightforward memory and that the narrative is assembled at or after awakening; the only addition is that RTS-ER takes the raw material for that narrative to be imagery-like states during REM sleep. But this addition does not answer Malcolm's criterion argument. Malcolm's worry was not about whether there are neural raw materials; his worry was that the report is not a memory of an experience. RTS-ER makes the report even more clearly a reconstruction, so it makes Malcolm's point even stronger: if the report is edited at waking, then there is no reason to say that the sleeper had any experiences at all. Consequently, Malcolm would argue that RTS-ER strengthens his conclusion that dreams are not experiences. Nor does RTS-ER answer Dennett's cassette objection. Dennett's point was that Malcolm cannot rule out a story in which the narrative is composed at waking; RTS-ER simply accepts such a story, merely adding prior imagery states that play no role in the subject's evidence. So RTS-ER concedes the undecidability rather than resolving it. Overall, RTS-ER does not show that dreams are experiences; at most it shows that reports are reconstructed, which is compatible with Malcolm's view and with the cassette theory.
