I've been rewriting this argument all week and it still goes through, which is usually a sign that one of the steps is doing something it shouldn't. It's about the 'man of his time' defence, and it seems to show the defence is close to worthless — which I don't believe.

(1) The defence is best read as an epistemic excuse: you're not blameworthy for X if the wrongness of X wasn't available to you, meaning you couldn't have got a grip on it using the concepts you actually had.

(2) Availability has a clean test: if other people in your own society, at the time, were saying out loud that X was wrong, then the wrongness of X was available to you.

(3) For the standard cases — Jefferson, Churchill, Columbus — the test is met: there were contemporaries, arguing inside the same broad moral and religious vocabulary, saying more or less exactly that.

(4) So the defence fails for all three, and in general it only ever excuses people who had no access to any dissenting voice at all.

(5) But run (2) on us: for pretty much anything we now do wrong, somebody somewhere is saying it's wrong, and their reasons are accessible to us.

(6) So the defence fails for ordinary people today too — including the people doing the condemning, and including people whose whole social world reinforces what they're doing.

(7) So either the defence is nearly useless and historical blame is a machine with no off switch, or one of (1)–(3) is false.

I'd rather keep (1) and (3) if I can. I've been toying with splitting (2) into three seperate conditions — the better view being available to an agent, being audible (said by someone he could actually have heard, not just by somebody alive somewhere), and being affordable (acting on it wouldn't have wrecked his life) — and I genuinely can't tell weather that's a fix or a dodge.

Which step would you give up, why does it look fine until you press on it, and what does giving it up cost? Please answer in American English.
