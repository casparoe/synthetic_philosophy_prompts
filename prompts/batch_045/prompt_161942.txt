So I've been pushing a line in a reading group and I want someone to try to break it. The position: the Ramsey–de Finetti Dutch book argument is what turns "degrees of belief should satisfy the probability axioms" from a stipulation into an actual requirement. The idea isn't that probabilism is false, but that the best reason we have for it is pragmatic — incoherent credences are exploitable, in the sense that there's a finite package of bets, each of which you yourself regard as fair or better than fair, whose net payoff is a loss no matter which state obtains. Being disposed to walk into a guaranteed loss is a defect; credences that make you that disposed are defective. Call it DB-probabilism, taken seriously as doing normative work rather than as a cute illustration of a theorem we'd accept anyway.

What I want is a map of how exposed this is. Not a list of objections to Bayesianism in general — specifically: what would have to be true for this *argument* to be worthless, or at least for it to be a much weaker reason than its fans take it to be? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed defender would concede it as counting against them or would dismiss it as misdescribing the view.

Some pressure points I've been chewing on, in rough order of how much they bother me:

The bookie idealizations. The theorem needs a bookie who knows your prices, can write any finite combination of bets on anything in your algebra, settles in a common currency, at stakes where your utility is linear in money (or small enough that it might as well be), over a state space you've already fully specified, with your utility for the payoffs not depending on which state you're in. Take those apart one at a time: which of them is load-bearing, what happens to the argument when it fails, and which failures are live rather than merely logically possible? I care most about the money-vs-utility one — if my utility for money has a kink (below some amount I can't pay rent, above some amount nothing much changes), then a "sure loss in dollars" book may not be a sure loss in the thing I actually care about. Does the defender have an answer there, or do they just shrink the stakes until linearity is safe and thereby restrict the argument to small-stakes cases?

The voluntariness problem. What the book shows is that you shouldn't accept *all* of those bets together. So why is the defect in your credences rather than in your betting policy? I keep hitting the reply "the agent can just decline the bets" and I'm not sure what the good answer to it is. Is it that the disposition to accept fair bets is partly constitutive of the credence? Is it that nature hands you decision problems that are structurally bets, so you can't rely on never meeting a bookie? Is it that the defect is conditional — if you ever had to act on these credences you'd act badly, and that's enough? Which of these replies actually does work and which is hand-waving?

A case I made up that I think is the most interesting thing here, and I want to know if I'm reinventing something. Suppose an agent is required to post a single number — her fair price — at which she'll both buy and sell any bet, and she faces counterparties who sometimes know more than she does. Then trading at her own fair prices against better-informed counterparties has negative expected value conditional on a trade happening; she gets systematically picked off. Meanwhile an agent who posts a spread — one price to buy, a worse one to sell — is precisely the agent the strict single-price formulation calls incoherent, and she's the one who survives. So the idealization that makes the theorem go through is the same idealization that makes the agent exploitable, and the agent with the survival property is the "incoherent" one. Does that count against the normative reading, or does it just show the Dutch book is measuring intrapersonal consistency, a virtue orthogonal to strategic rationality? And follow the retreat: if the defender says the spread-agent isn't really incoherent, she has *imprecise* credences rather than sharp ones, and the argument was only ever about sharp-credence agents — does that retreat cost anything? Is the resulting claim much narrower than "degrees of belief must be probabilities"?

Which direction of the biconditional is the fragile one. I'm fuzzy here and would like it stated cleanly: is it "incoherent ⇒ Dutch-bookable" or "not Dutch-bookable ⇒ coherent" that has the real technical problems (does the converse need prices for everything in a complete algebra, or bets on arbitrary propositions, or a willingness to take both sides)? And if one direction is shakier than the other, does it matter normatively, or does only one direction carry the argument?

The diachronic extension. The betting arguments for updating by conditionalization seem to me to need extra assumptions the synchronic one doesn't: that you know in advance you'll learn exactly one cell of some partition, that you can now plan what your later self will do, that the later self's betting rates are even the right object. Say what those arguments assume and which assumption failing would genuinely count against the norm rather than just narrowing its scope. And if the diachronic version collapses, does that reflect back on the synchronic one, or are they independent?

The accuracy/scoring-rule rival. If probabilism can be vindicated with no bookie at all — by arguing that incoherent credences are guaranteed worse on some measure of accuracy — does that make the Dutch book argument redundant, and is redundancy a defect? Or does the accuracy route inherit parallel problems (it has to argue accuracy is the only epistemic good, or at least the dominant one, it has to pick a scoring rule, and I gather there's technical griping about how that argument is set up)? Which route is more exposed, and would a defender ever concede the accuracy route is strictly better and drop the bookie?

The insulation question. The theorem itself is mathematics, so of course it can't be false. What's at risk is the normative reading. So: what does the view actually predict that could come out false? Give me its discriminating predictions — things like "incoherent agents will, or would, do worse in some measurable way," or "every apparent case of incoherent-but-fine agency will turn out to be imprecision rather than incoherence" — and then an honest verdict on whether DB-probabilism has been qualified into something that can't lose. If it has, say whether that's a problem for a claim of this kind or whether it's fine because the claim was never empirical to begin with.

For what it's worth, the two things I personally find most persuasive are the strategic-context point above and the nagging sense that "there exists a set of bets that would guarantee you a loss" is a fact about the *package* and about an imaginary counterparty, not obviously a fact about what I believe. Tell me if those are known weak points or if I've talked myself into something. And please be willing to conclude the thing is basically fine — if the honest answer is that none of this touches it, I'd rather hear that than a symmmetrical list of worries.
