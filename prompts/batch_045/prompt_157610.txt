I have no training in philosophy. I read engineering at university and I have spent something over twenty years in transport appraisal, which means my working life consists largely of multiplying very small losses by very large numbers of people: thirty seconds added to a journey, times four hundred thousand journeys a year, times sixty years, and out comes a line in a spreadsheet that decides whether a scheme is built. I have got into the habit of defending that habit. Lately I have been turning over cases in my head where I am no longer sure it can be defended, and I would like to know whether there is a principle behind the pattern of answers I keep giving, or whether the pattern is simply prejudice with a formula attached.

My verdicts, as honestly as I can state them.

(a) A puzzle I came across years ago. Either one person is tortured for fifty years, or an unwritably large number of people — a tower of threes, the sort of number that cannot be written down in the space of the universe — each have a speck of dust blown into their eye for a second. I take the dust, and it is not close.

(b) Either a stranger is killed, or a million people each have a mild headache for an afternoon. I prevent the death. I will not add up the headaches, although I am aware that someone consistent with me in (a) probably should.

(c) The same dust, but all of it inside one life. One person has a speck of dust blown into their eye once a day, day in day out, for forty years — call it fifteen thousand specks in total — versus one person tortured for six months. I take the dust, and I am considerably more comfortable about it than I was in (a), even though fifteen thousand is as nothing beside the number in (a). That difference in confidence worries me. It suggests that what I am really adding up is episodes of discomfort rather than people, and I cannot tell whether that is a discovery or a blunder.

(d) Certainties against risks. On one side, one identified person is tortured for fifty years. On the other, every person alive is handed a one-in-a-million chance of being tortured for fifty years. The second option contains something in the order of eight thousand tortures' worth of suffering in the expectation, and I still choose it: each person's own prospect has moved by an amount too small to matter to them, and that seems to me to be the thing to look at. Yet it sits badly with (a), where tiny things happening to enormous numbers of people were precisely what I did count.

(e) Two versions of the same loss. Forty million commuters each lose twenty seconds a day for a year because of a pointless detour, nobody gaining anything from it: I count the seconds, and the total outweighs one person's permanent and serious injury. Now the same forty million people, the same twenty seconds, except that the twenty seconds is the price of a safety improvement that every one of them would have paid more than twenty seconds to have. I now count the seconds as nothing at all. Nothing has changed about the seconds themselves. What has changed is whether each person got something in return, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a deep point about what a harm is or a failure of nerve on my part.

(f) The things nobody would trouble themselves to avoid. Twenty million people each hear one mildly irritating three-second noise. Offered the chance to pay a penny to skip it, every one of them declines, because it is not worth the bother. My own working test for whether something is a harm at all has been: would the person pay anything at all to be spared it? On that test these add up to nothing, however many people there are. But the same test appears to say that a speck of dust in the eye is not a harm either, since I would not pay a penny to avoid one — and in (a) I let the dust outweigh torture. So my test eats its own best case. I would rather see it repaired than replaced, if it can be repaired.

(g) The one I go back and forth on. Take (a) again, but stipulate that the person who would be tortured is already by far the worst-off human being alive, and that everyone who would get the dust is extremely fortunate. On some days I still take the dust. On other days I take the torture, or find that I simply cannot choose, and the reason seems to be that I have two tests running at once and no rule about which one wins.

What I am asking for is this. State a principle, or a short list of competing candidates, precise enough that I could apply it to a scheme on Monday morning. For each clause, say which of my cases bought it. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate — I would much rather surrender a verdict than be handed a principle that saves all of them by being too clever to be usable. Say what it implies about (g). Say what it implies about two cases I have not been able to settle at all: harms that no one ever feels or notices, such as ninety seconds of life quietly removed at random from every adult in the country so that no statistic anywhere moves; and harms that fall entirely on people who do not yet exist and who will never overlap with us. Finally, tell me which parts of this are questions I can be right or wrong about and which are simply decisions I have to make and then live with. Please write in British English.
