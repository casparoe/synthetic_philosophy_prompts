I run a small reading group — half philosophers, half multi-agent-systems people — and we've been stuck on the problem of logical omniscience for something like two years now. You know the setup: in the standard relational semantics for knowledge and belief, an agent knows every logical truth, knows every logical consequence of what it knows, and if its information is inconsistent it knows literally everything. So there is no room in the formalism for someone who accepts the axioms of a theory and hasn't yet worked out that some hard theorem follows from them. Everyone agrees that's a bad feature for modelling anything real. And then nothing moves.

Two camps, roughly. Camp one thinks it's a genuine defect and builds replacements: worlds that aren't really possible (non-normal or impossible ones), awareness functions that filter what an agent can have attitudes toward, syntactic or justification-style setups where the object language carries explicit proof-like terms so that closure under consequence just doesn't fall out. Camp two says there's nothing to fix: the operator isn't a psychology, it's an idealization of the information available to a subject, and complaining that it doesn't model cognitive effort is like complaining that a specification of a distributed protocol doesn't model cache misses. Camp two also points out, and I think correctly, that the replacements pay for their extra realism: they give up compsitionality, or the clean completeness and axiomatization results, or they add a new primitive whose behaviour is as stipulative as the closure principle it was meant to replace — what makes an agent 'aware' of p, exactly?

Here's the bit I want diagnosed. A few months ago someone in the group proposed what she called budgeted closure: keep the standard models, add a resource parameter R, and say an agent knows every consequence of its information whose cheapest derivation costs less than R (proof steps, or something like that). Camp two's reply was that this has no predictive content, since R and the cost metric can be tuned case by case to yield whatever verdict you antecedently wanted. Camp one's reply was that it just relocates the discretion: instead of stipulating closure you stipulate a budget. Both replies seemed strong to me, and I honestly can't tell whether that shows the proposal is worthless, or whether any proposal in this space is going to get exactly this reply.

My own hypothesis, for what it's worth, is that 'logical omniscience' names three different complaints that get run together — knowing all of logic in the first place, closure under consequences of what you already know, and trivialization once your information is inconsistent — and that the argument stalls partly because each camp's favourite fix only addresses one of them. But the deeper thing I suspect is that the sides aren't disagreeing about any fact. They disagree about what a semantics for an attitude verb is for: whether its job is to predict ordinary ascriptions of knowledge to people, or to be a tractable abstraction you can reason about protocols and programs with. And they disagree about which of linguistic intuition and formal desiderata counts as evidence. That would make the dispute mostly verbal, or mostly two questions under one name, with a genuinely open formal question hiding underneath — whether there's some set of jointly unsatisfiable desiderata in this area (hyperintensional enough to seperate necessarily equivalent contents, compositional enough to be usable, and still validating a decent amount of inference), i.e. whether a no-go theorem of roughly that shape is what's really at issue.

So: is any of that right? Which kind of disagreement is this actually — verbal, substantive but empirical, or methodological? Is my three-complaints split doing any real work, or is it a red herring? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a trade-off theorem, a clean distinction between information and inferential effort, abandoning the ambition of one unified operator — or should we expect no resolution, and why? I want the diagnosis argued out, not a survey of the options, and please no reading lists or citations: I want the structural story, not pointers.
