Stylised version of the case that started it: we have an anomaly detector sitting on top of two deployed clinical models — one image-based, one that reads structured vitals plus free-text notes — that flags cases where the model's output looks out-of-distribution-ish or drifts from its own calibration. Over a couple of months both models start mis-scoring elderly patients who also have a particular comorbidity, and everyone on the rotation wants to say "it's the same bug again, we know how to handle it." The image model is sensitive to some radiographic artefact; the notes model seems to be keying on something in the wording of admission notes; the internal circuits obviously aren't the same; and we can't reconstruct the training-data regularity that produced either behaviour because the datasets are long gone. So far I've been sorting candidate individuation criteria into three: the proximal thing in the input the model is sensitive to, the internal circuit that computes it, and the upstream regularity in the training data that made the circuit. My worry is that anything we validate by "did the fix transfer between the two models" is circular — it defines sameness by the success of the intervention we were trying to predict. I've been calling this the 'grain problem' for mechanisms, but that's probably a bad name. There are two wrinkles I want the proposal to at least acknowledge rather than dodge: a mechanism can be present in a model and produce no anomaly until a distribution shift activates it, and what looks like two different behavioural signatures can come from one upstream regularity hitting different downstream heads (or vice versa).

What I want from you: a proposal, not an essay.
