I'm writing a short position statement for a decision-theory seminar on gambles whose expectation fails to exist, and before I commit it to paper I want someone to check whether the package hangs together. Assume throughout that utility is near enough linear in money over the ranges in question — I don't want to dispose of these cases by appealing to diminishing marginal utility.

Set-up, so we're working with the same objects. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time; if the first heads falls on toss n, the payoff is (−1)^{n−1}·2^n/n dollars. So the payoffs are +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5, … with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, …; the nth term contributes (−1)^{n−1}/n to the naive sum, which is the alternating harmonic series and converges to ln 2 ≈ 0.69. But the expected gain and the expected loss separately diverge, so on the standard measure-theoretic criterion there is no expectation at all: not +∞, not −∞, not ln 2. Call this the Pasadena game, P. Call the mirror game M the one with the sign reversed on the same sequence of tosses. I'll also refer to two variants: P floored at −1000 (the loss is capped), whose expectation is +∞, and P capped at +1000, whose expectation is −∞; each differs from P only on states whose total probability is under 10⁻⁴.

Here is where I've landed.

(1) I accept the measure-theoretic verdict: P has no expected value. And I take this to be a decision-theoretically substantial distinction rather than bookkeeping — a game with infinite expectation (the St. Petersburg game) poses one kind of problem, and a game with no expectation poses a different and harder one.

(2) I don't think "no expectation" entails "no value" or "don't play." I'd play P for free, I'd pay up to about fifty cents for a single play, and I'd decline at anything much above a dollar. My guide is the limit of the finite truncations (stop after N tosses, nothing if no heads by then): those expectations converge to ln 2, and I treat that as good evidence of the gamble's worth even though it isn't the expectation.

(3) Value should be a function of the gamble's payoff distribution together with my attitudes to money and risk. Two gambles that induce the same probability distribution over payoffs must be equally choiceworthy, whatever the causal or temporal order in which the chance process happens to generate the outcomes — relabelling or re-indexing states cannot change value.

(4) I am not a fanatic about tails. For a one-shot gamble, a modification confined to states of total probability ε should move the value by an amount that goes to zero with ε; correspondingly I wouldn't pay more than roughly twenty dollars for one play of the St. Petersburg game despite its infinite expectation.

(5) I want to keep two structural constraints: dominance (if Y pays at least as much as X in every state and more in some, then Y is at least as choiceworthy, and any price I'd pay for X I'd pay for Y), and additivity across independent gambles (the value of X + Y is the sum of the values, at least when both are defined).

(6) On the pair: since P and M sum to exactly zero on every toss-sequence, I hold that the pair is determinately worth nothing and that someone who sells me both for a dollar is exploiting me — while also holding that neither game individually has an expected value.

What I'd like is an audit rather than a survey. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each? Which pairs only look like they conflict and dissolve once the relevant distinction is drawn? For each real tension, say precisely what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Rank by severity, and tell me which single revision cleans up the most.

Four things I specifically want addressed. Does (6) really constrain what I can say about P and M individually, or can I keep the sure-zero verdict about the pair while denying that either member has a value? If the answer is that my fifty-cent price in (2) has to go, what is left to ground any positive price at all, and does the mirror judgement in (6) survive the loss? If instead the answer is that distribution-dependence in (3) has to go and value may depend on the order in which the process generates outcomes, then tell me what the ordering-based approach delivers for two independent plays of P and whether that verdict is additive in the sense of (5). And finally: does my anti-fanaticism in (4) make the infinite-versus-undefined distinction in (1) idle, given that flooring and capping P flip its expectation between +∞, undefined and −∞ while altering it only on states of probability below 10⁻⁴? If so, where — if anywhere — does that distinction still do work? I don't know where the fault lies and I don't have a favoured revision; tell me which commitment is the weak one.
