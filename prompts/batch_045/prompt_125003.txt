I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and I got dragged into this by an argument with my sister (she's an ICU nurse) about turning off a ventilator versus giving a lethal injection. She says those are completely different, I said the patient dies either way and the doctor intends it either way, and we went in circles for two hours. So I read a bit and now I think the whole literature is going in circles too, and I want to know why.

Roughly two camps. One says the doing/allowing line tracks nothing moral: whether the death comes from my motion or from the world finishing something already in motion is a fact about causal plumbing, not about what I owe anyone; what matters is outcome, intention, and what else I could have done. The other says the line is basic — there's a difference between being the source of a threat and merely not fixing one, and you can feel it in the cases (redirect an existing trolley, fine; shove someone into its path, not fine) even when the causal stories everyone proposes don't neatly separate them.

Here's my hypothesis, such as it is. Both sides are using one word — "killing", "doing", "causing" — to encode a whole bundle of variables that usually travel together: whether the bad outcome would have happened anyway, whether I had to actively intervene to get it, whether the threat was already moving and who got it moving, whether the death was my aim or a foreseen side effect, and whether we're asking what I'm permitted to do or how much blame/praise I get. In ordinary cases those all point the same way, so the distinction feels solid. In the weird cases they come apart and people just pick whichever variable their favorite cases load on. If that's right the debate isn't about the world at all, it's about which variable gets to own the word.

My version of this comes from work: in a diff, whether a change "introduced" a bug depends entirely on which baseline commit you diff against — pick a different baseline and the same patch is a fix. "Letting die" looks to me like what you get when you take the agent's non-intervention as the baseline, and "killing" like what you get when you take the normal expected course of events as the baseline. Nothing in the world changes between those two descriptions, so the fight is about which baseline is morally privileged, and I can't see what could settle that.

So: is that the right diagnosis, or is it something else — a real disagreement about which feature is morally basic, a verbal dispute, two different questions (permissibility vs blameworthiness vs what it says about your character) sharing a name, or bedrock intuition where neither side can argue? I half-remember surveys where people call a foreseen side effect "intentional" when it's bad and "not intentional" when it's good, which makes me suspect folk judgments here are tracking blame rather than act-type, but I might be misremebering that.

What I want is a diagnosis with reasons, not a tour of the positions. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a better causal formalism, a cleaner intention/foresight distinction, more experimental work, or nothing, because the question is ill-posed? Also tell me if my diff-baseline thing is a move someone has already made properly, or if I've reinvented it badly.
