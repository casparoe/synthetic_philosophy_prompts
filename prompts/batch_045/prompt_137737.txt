Next Friday I'm presenting a paper at an evidence-law workshop at a US law school, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the thesis.

Thesis: defeating Holocaust denial was a real epistemic victory, but it carried a standing methodological cost, because fighting deniers on the ground they chose reweighted the field's evidential portfolio toward the sources deniers cannot impeach — the perpetrators' own paperwork — and away from the sources they do attack: survivor testimony, testimony given after the war under legal pressure, and demographic inference. Call this the documentary pull. The paper trail a bureaucracy leaves systematically overrepresents killing that was centralized, formalized, and ordered in writing, and underrepresents killing that was improvised locally and authorized verbally; so, I argue, the consolidated account of how the genocide escalated is more decision-shaped and more top-down than the events were. My test case for the mechanism is a comparison of killing operations whose records survived for reasons of administrative routing unconnected to the killing — I claim the better-documented ones look markedly more centrally directed in the resulting historiography — and I extend the same prediction to the historiographies of the Armenian genocide and of Nanjing.

The audience: about fifteen people. Roughly half are Federal Rules of Evidence types, hostile to the word epistemic, who will want to know what rule, standard, or burden I am proposing and what it would do in a case. Three or four are new evidence scholars of a Bayesian or relative-plausibility stripe, sympathetic to the framing but impatient with loose talk of bias: they will want likelihoods, base rates, and a stated counterfactual. One is a civil-procedure person who works on burdens of proof and the asymmetry between proving and disproving. One is a legal historian of Nazi Germany who knows the documentary base cold and will contest my factual claims. One does First Amendment work and bristles at anything that could be heard as a reason to suppress or marginalize speakers.

Give me the eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it rather than translated into my vocabulary, each with a two-minute reply and one line on where the reply is soft. Also: what I should concede in my first two minutes, the single reply that has to land or the paper does not survive, and the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks. No literature review and no suggested reading.
