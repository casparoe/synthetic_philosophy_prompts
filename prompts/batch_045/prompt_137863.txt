I work on a platform team at a company with roughly two hundred engineers, split across about forty product teams. Over the past year we have been rolling out a shared data layer — an event pipeline plus a query service on top of it. Teams adopt it by writing an internal RFC and then migrating their services off the older path. Eleven teams have adopted so far. Mine is next.

I have a reason for doubt that I do not think anyone else has. I ran a load test against our service's actual write pattern — bursty, around forty thousand events per second at peak, with a heavy tail of late-arriving events — and the pipeline fell over at roughly half of that. The failure mode looks like it is in the query service's compaction rather than in my harness, but I would not claim more than that. My honest confidence that this would bite us in production is maybe sixty to sixty-five percent; I have been wrong about this kind of thing before, in both directions. The stakes are not trivial: reversing a bad migration costs us on the order of two engineer-months, and if the failure is real it would take down a Tier-1 service during peak traffic.

My manager's argument, which I found hard to answer at the time, runs like this. Eleven teams have now chosen this, and those teams include engineers who are better than me and who had more context than I do. If the pipeline really were broken at scale, one of them would have found it. So the sensible inference is that my load test is measuring something about my setup rather than something about the pipeline, and I should stop pushing. He added that raising it in the RFC will read as reluctance to do the migration work, and he pointed out that two people who raised similar concerns earlier are now thought of as not team players.

What I cannot get straight is whether that inference is actually as strong as it sounds. As far as I can tell, most of those eleven teams did not run their own tests at all — they adopted because the team before them had, and a couple of them told me so directly. If that is right, then the fact that eleven teams chose it does not seem to carry the weight he is giving it, even though each individual team may have been perfectly reasonable to defer. But I also cannot tell whether I am rationalizing, because "the earlier teams were not really exercising their own judgment" is exactly the thing a person would say when their own judgment is the outlier.

Three further wrinkles strike me as relevant, and I do not know what to do with any of them. First, my team is twelfth, not fortieth: whatever we do becomes visible inside the company and will be one of the data points the remaining twenty-eight-odd teams look at, so going along quietly is not only a decision about us. Second, I have a choice about what I put in the RFC — I can register a concern, which is cheap and easy to discount, or I can publish the harness, the numbers, and a reproducible failure case, which is more work, puts my credibility on the line, but is the kind of thing another team could check in an afternoon. Third, a colleague suggested we adopt on schedule but gate the actual cutover on a two-week shadow run at production volume, which sounds like it dissolves the whole disagreement, and I am suspicious of how tidy that sounds.

So: is my manager's reasoning sound, or is there a specific error in it, and is that error the kind that only looks like an error in hindsight? Given that I am twelfth rather than first or last, what do I owe the teams downstream of me, and does the answer differ depending on whether I publish evidence rather than an opinion? Does the shadow-run proposal actually resolve the problem, or does it just move it somewhere I am not seeing? And is there a general rule I should take away — something along the lines of defer to the group on judgments but always surface hard evidence — or does that rule break down in cases I have not thought of? I am not looking for career advice in the abstract; I want to know which of the arguments on the table actually hold up.
