I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology and I am writing the take-home final. I need one question on van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the requirement that an agent's present credence in A, conditional on her credence in A at some later time being x, should itself be x — together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question has to separate students who can work with the principle from students who can only state it. Reciting the definition, rehearsing the diachronic Dutch-strategy motivation, or summarizing the halfer/thirder dispute about Sleeping Beauty should earn partial credit on the technical part alone and nothing on the interpretive part. So please build the question around a stipulated case rather than the canonical one, and make it demand a general diagnosis of when the principle holds and when it breaks.

Design the case along these lines, or improve on it while keeping its structure: a fair coin is tossed; if it lands heads the agent is woken once, if tails three times, with an amnesia-inducing drug between awakenings; at each awakening she is reminded of the protocol but never told which awakening it is; after the experiment ends she is told how the coin landed; a day later a second drug erases her memory of that announcement. Ask what her credence in heads should be at each of the four stages, and what Reflection requires of each transition between them.

The question should force the following moves, and the marking scheme should be built around them:

1. A careful statement of the principle, using the conditional-credence reading and the standard restriction to values the agent assigns positive probability.
2. A proof that pure learning suffices: if the agent is certain that her later total evidence will be a cell of a partition refining her current evidence, and that she will update by conditionalizing, then Reflection follows — the union-of-cells argument.
3. The corollary that if she is certain her later credence in A will be x, Reflection forces her present credence in A to be x. A top answer must then note that the weaker no-drift reading, on which her present credence should merely equal the expectation of her later credence, is strictly weaker in general but collapses into the full principle in this case, because the later credence is certain — and must say why an appeal to averaging fails here.
4. The computation: the rule that weights a possible outcome by the number of awakenings it contains, spreading credence evenly across those awakenings, yields one quarter; plain conditionalization on her centred evidence yields one half; Reflection, together with her certainty about her own updating policy, rules out the former. So the principle is not neutral on the case.
5. The proviso dilemma: what restriction is needed to rescue the principle, and whether it is principled rather than ad hoc — noting that in the forgetting transition the later self is straightforwardly worse informed, whereas in the centred transition whether she is worse informed is precisely what is at issue, since she knows she is awake and has lost only her location in time.

Please also observe these constraints. The question must be self-contained and answerable without consulting any secondary literature on self-locating credence. The marking scheme must be neutral about which horn of the dilemma the candidate accepts: marks are for moves, not for conclusions, though the model answer may take a stand. Format the deliverable as: the question in lettered parts, with the time budget and word limit stated; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words; and a scheme totalling twenty-five marks across five must-hit points, each with the marks available, the specific move required for full credit, and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that move wrong. Close with a short statement of what distinguishes an A from a B answer and what a failing answer looks like.
