You are a philosopher of art who is also fluent in the idiom of engineers, and you treat the long debate about forgery, replication, and why the original matters as working knowledge rather than as trivia to be recited. Work carefully and in two passes. First, in private notes that you do not polish, spend a few hundred words sorting the claims in play into three bins: claims about what an object is, claims about what we can know, and claims about what a viewer's experience is worth — and flag where a move in the literature quietly slides from one bin into another. Then write the answer, addressed to me, in the vocabulary I describe below. Answer from what you already know; do not cite, quote from memory, or recommend reading.

Here is my situation. I lead the engineering team that built the provenance and authenticity registry for a consortium of museums. The vocabulary I actually work in: content-addressed storage and cryptographic hashes, append-only hash chains and Merkle trees, digital signatures over canonical byte sequences, trust roots and certificate expiry, third-party timestamping notaries, lossless versus lossy transcodes, version control with commits and diffs and blame, and access control lists. I am fluent in all of it and do not need it explained back to me. What I need is a translation going the other way.

Here is the debate as I currently understand it, in my own rough terms. Many people hold that a perfect forgery of a painting is worth less — sometimes vastly less — than the original, even when no one, not the sharpest connoisseur and no laboratory test, can tell them apart. The explanations on offer seem to come in three families.

(a) Identity. For some kinds of work, having been made by that artist at that time is part of what the object is, so a later duplicate is a different object that merely resembles it. For other kinds of work — a score, a print pulled from a plate, a photograph struck from a negative — the work is fixed by something like a notation or a template, so any correct instance is a full instance, and forgery in the strict sense is not possible; what you can have instead is misattribution. I have seen the contrast labeled with a pair of technical terms, something like autographic and allographic; if that label is right, use it, but do not build anything on it.

(b) Achievement. What matters is not how the object looks but what was accomplished in making it. The original is the residue and record of a genuine feat of invention and execution; the copy inherits the look without inheriting the deed.

(c) Experience. Standing in front of the original, knowing it is the original, changes what you notice and how long and how hard you look, and that difference in attention is itself part of the value. The forgery cannot deliver it, not because it looks worse but because the belief you hold while looking does part of the aesthetic work.

What I want is a systematic translation of (a), (b) and (c) into my vocabulary — hashes, canonical bytes, signatures, hash chains, commits, trust roots, transcodes — as faithful as you can make it, followed by a residue report. Constrain yourself as follows: do not smuggle in my framework's assumptions and call the result a translation. If a step would only work by stipulating that authenticity just is a valid attestation chain, stop and say so instead of taking the step.

Work the translation through these four cases, which are the ones my team actually argues about.

1. The molecule-for-molecule duplicate. Suppose a scan-and-fabrication process produces a second physical painting that defeats every authentication test we have: same pigment chemistry, same craquelure, same aged varnish, same weave. In my world two byte-identical files are not two files — content addressing collapses them, and the only thing that separates them is metadata attached from the outside, which anyone controlling the metadata channel can alter. The physical duplicate is, by contrast, genuinely two objects sharing every intrinsic property. Is its situation structurally the same as a bit-identical copy with a broken provenance chain, or is it a different structure that my vocabulary has no name for? Translate, and say what has no expression.

2. Continuity under intervention. A painting is relined, cleaned and revarnished; the museum insists it is the same painting, and its value does not collapse, even though material has been added and removed. Meanwhile any change in bytes gives me a new object, with lineage preserved through a chain of derivatives and one designated canonical master. Is the canonical master more like the original painting or more like the notation or template in (a)? Is a lossless transcode a copy, a restoration, or neither? Where does material continuity fail to map?

3. The gap. A painting is stylistically flawless and technically consistent with the master's hand, but its custody record has a thirty-year hole, and the notary that timestamped the earlier entries went out of business and lost its keys. In my terms: a valid hash chain that stops, a missing link, and an expired trust root. Does the object lose authenticity, or do we lose our warranty? Which of (a), (b) and (c) treats this as a change in the object, which treats it as a change in us, and can my vocabulary register the difference at all?

4. Shared authorship. A canvas was designed and blocked in by the master, with substantial passages executed by the workshop. My registry is forced to be binary: an asset is attested or it is not, a commit is signed or it is not, and blame attributes bytes but not ideas. Does the achievement account have resources for degrees of achievement that a signature model cannot represent — and is that a bug in signatures, or a sign that authorship ascriptions in art history are doing a different job from the one my system is doing?

Then give me the residue report proper, in three parts: what translated cleanly and with no loss; what translated only under a distortion you have to name explicitly; and what could not be expressed in my vocabulary at all. For the last category, take a position on each item: is it a blind spot of a registry built on hashes and signatures, or a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it looked? Close by recommending one thing my registry should record that it does not currently record, and say which of the three families it would let us say something about.
