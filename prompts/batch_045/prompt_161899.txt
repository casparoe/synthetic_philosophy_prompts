I'm a computer science PhD student in a small reading group that is working its way through Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach*. We are somewhere past the middle: we have been through the chapters that build the little formal system out of typographical rules for number theory and the material on meaning, isomorphism, and how reference gets "wired in," and we are heading toward the finale. To get oriented before our next meeting I listened to an episode of a well-known philosophy-of-AI podcast that was billed as an introduction to "the Gödelian argument" in the book, and I came away unsure how much of it to trust. Here is roughly how the host summarized the view, transcribed as best I can:

"Gödel's great discovery was that any formal system rich enough to contain arithmetic contains a sentence that says, in effect, 'I am not provable.' Either the system proves it — in which case it has proved a falsehood and is therefore unsound — or else it fails to prove it, in which case there is a truth it cannot reach. So no formal system can be both complete and consistent, and no formal system can demonstrate its own consistency from the inside. Hofstadter's move in GEB is to take this as the model for the mind. A brain is a formal system, a big pile of rules firing on symbols; once its representations get rich enough it starts to model itself, and the feedback loop that results — the strange loop — just *is* consciousness. The self is the loop catching its own tail, which is also why a computer program can never be conscious the way we are: a program is exactly the sort of formal system Gödel's theorem applies to, and Gödel showed that such systems can never fully see themselves. When your laptop hangs, that's the halting problem, which is really the same result wearing a different costume: undecidability again. So the book is, at bottom, an argument that machine intelligence is impossible in principle, and Gödel killed it before it started."

A friend in the group who is further along says the host has Hofstadter backwards at the crucial step, because Hofstadter's whole point is supposed to be that a self-modeling system is *both* a mechanism and, in some sense, outside the mechanism, and that the book is far more cagey about whether the loop really explains consciousness; but she could not tell me where exactly the summary goes wrong, and I suspect there is more than one problem. I also half-remember two things I can't reconstruct properly: that "no formal system can be both complete and consistent" is false as stated unless you also require the axioms to be effectively enumerable, since there are complete consistent theories (the theory of real closed fields, or arithmetic with addition but no multiplication, or just the set of all truths about the natural numbers) — and that the dichotomy about proving versus not proving the Gödel sentence smuggles in an assumption about *which* kind of consistency is at issue, with someone's name attached to the strengthening of the result. And we have been using a rough distinction in our meetings that I would like you to apply: we call it the two-levels problem, the question whether the isomorphism that makes a formal system's string talk about itself is supplied from *inside* the system or imposed on it from *outside*, and whether Hofstadter's analogy to a mind needs the self-reference to be in some sense intrinsic to the system rather than merely available to us as interpreters.
