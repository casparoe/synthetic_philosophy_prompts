I'm giving a talk next month at a joint session of my department's philosophy of cognitive science reading group and the interpretability group down the road in computer science, and I want to arrive prepared for the question period rather than just for the slides. My thesis is a moderate realism about internal features of language models: I want to claim that at least some directions in a transformer's residual stream — including the units you recover by training dictionary-learning methods such as sparse autoencoders on those activations — are genuinely representational, in that (i) intervening along the direction shifts the model's behaviour in the way the corresponding feature would, (ii) the same direction is reused across inputs, tasks and prompt framings rather than being a one-off pattern, and (iii) it can be identified before anyone says what it is about, and only afterwards matched to a description. I call that triple the direction's *interventional profile*, and my main positive argument is a transport test of my own devising: if a direction found in one model can be mapped by an affine map into a sibling model of the same architecture trained with a different seed and data ordering, and the transported direction plays the same behavioural role there, then the direction was discovered rather than imposed by the interpretive frame. I'll open with a hypothetical case to make it vivid — a direction that fires only on mentions of one particular suspension bridge in one particular city, and that, when clamped, makes the model drag that bridge into every answer it gives.

The room will not be friendly, and that is the point. Two of the interpretability people are outspoken instrumentalists: for them the only test of a feature is whether you can use it to steer or predict the model's output, and they will say that "representation" is ornament on a control result. Their lab leader is more sympathetic but thinks the whole question is premature until the methods stop disagreeing with each other. There is also a philosopher of science who works on measurement and modelling and will want to know whether my features are more like temperature — real, but multiply realized and only visible through a chosen procedure — or more like a choice of coordinate system, which is convenience all the way down. One person in the department is a Dennett-style skeptic about content and one works in embodied/dynamical approaches to cognition and thinks the representational framework is inherited from a bad picture of the mind, so between them they will press the question of what my realism is even supposed to add. Finally, a systems neuroscientist who is visiting and who thinks about representation in terms of decoding and cross-animal alignment will want to know why my transport test is not just representational similarity analysis with extra steps, and why I am not repeating the old "grandmother cell" and face-patch debates without noticing.

What I'd like from you is a prep sheet, not a defence: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do if I fumbled them, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it rather than in my vocabulary, each with a reply I could give in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes to buy goodwill — I'm inclined to concede up front that dictionary-learning bases are underdetermined by the data, since as you widen the dictionary what looked like one feature splits into several, and differently-sized dictionaries trained on the same model give you different units — and tell me which single reply has to land for the position to survive. I know where I'm weakest: I don't have a good account of misrepresentation, because nothing in gradient descent looks like the selection history that would make a direction *supposed* to mean one thing rather than another. Please include, at the end, the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
