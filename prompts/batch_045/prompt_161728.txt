I'm a PhD student in political theory, currently TA-ing a history of political thought course, and I attended a guest lecture by a colleague who works on early modern republicanism. His gloss of Aristotle on money and exchange went roughly like this:

"Aristotle treats money as a pure creature of law — a token with no worth of its own, whose value is simply whatever the city declares it to be, and which a ruler can therefore render worthless at a stroke. From this he gets his case against lending at interest: money is barren, a coin has no offspring, so charging someone for the use of one is charging for something that cannot exist. That is the one form of wealth-getting he calls unnatural. Really his quarrel is with exchange as such: the properly managed household aims at self-sufficiency, and the whole business of buying and selling — trade for profit, markets, commercial life generally — is a corruption of household management, tolerated only because not everyone can grow their own food."

I'd like to use some of this in section, but two things sit badly with me. I remember money entering Nicomachean Ethics V as what makes otherwise incommensurable goods measurable and exchange reciprocal, tied to need rather than to fiat; and I have a vague memory that the distinction in Politics I is between two kinds of acquisition rather than between household management and commerce outright. I don't want to correct a specialist on the strength of a hazy memory, though.

Could you audit it claim by claim — what a specialist in Aristotle's practical philosophy would accept as stated, what is a fair simplification of a more qualified claim, and what is a genuine distortion? I'm especially unsure about three points: whether "unnatural" in Politics I attaches to exchange in general or only to one species of it; whether the barrenness argument is aimed at all interest-taking or at a narrower target; and whether money's conventional status really amounts to its value being whatever the city says, or whether Aristotle anchors it in something he takes not to be up to us. Finally: once the summary is corrected where it needs correcting, does the conclusion my colleague drew from it survive — that Aristotle gives us no conceptual resources for thinking about markets, only for denouncing them?
