Please argue for the following position, writing in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — in the form of a letter to a colleague, the way one would write an email or a letter.

The position is that Johannes Kepler's reformed astrology, as set out in his Harmonices Mundi (1619), is best understood not as a leftover of superstition but as a noble failure, and that the nature of this failure is illuminated by a neo-Keplerian completion that Kepler himself could not have carried out. Kepler abandoned the zodiac, the houses, and the apparatus of planetary dignities and horoscopic fortune-telling, while retaining and expanding the geometrical aspects into a system of thirteen — including the quintile (72 degrees, from the pentagon) and the biquintile (144 degrees, from the pentagram), which have no place in Ptolemaic practice. Now suppose a modern follower tries to complete this system in Kepler's own spirit by adding aspects for every regular polygon that is constructible with compass and straightedge, including the 17-gon, which yields an aspect of 360/17 degrees (roughly 21.18 degrees); Kepler did not include this aspect, and the constructibility of the 17-gon was not proved until Gauss, long after Kepler's death, so Kepler had no way of knowing that this polygon, too, was constructible. Argue that this neo-Keplerian completion succeeds in making Kepler's project more rigorous, but that it also exposes why the project was always empty: the choice of harmonious aspects was never forced by the phenomena, because for any set of aspects one can always find a post hoc consonance, and the only thing the geometrical criterion really bought Kepler was a way of dignifying the accident of which polygons happen to be constructible. Your argument should concede that Kepler was right to reject the superstition of contemporaries who cast horoscopes from zodiac signs and planetary dignities, and should grant that his remark about the Earth singing a semitone — a rising and falling mi to fa — and about the syllables of that semitone suggesting misery and famine here at home, is a beautiful expression of his geometric Platonism. But it should conclude that the beauty of the expression is exactly what misled him, because mistaking a mathematical metaphor for a causal influence is the error that no amount of constructible polygons can repair.
