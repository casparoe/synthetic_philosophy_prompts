Our little reading group — six of us, back room of a pub, roughly a book a month — is doing Le Guin's The Left Hand of Darkness in a few weeks, and I want to pair it with Hofstadter's "A Person Paper on Purity in Language," the satire where he re-runs all the standard defences of generic "man," "mankind" and generic "he" with "white," "whitekind" and "whe" substituted in, and then has his narrator sneer at the suggestion that pronouns be marked by sex instead of race as self-evidently lunatic. What I actually want to talk about is narrower than "language and power." Here's my half-baked idea: neutralising the word is not the same as dismantling the default. Replacing "chairman" with "chairperson" leaves untouched whatever image the reader silently supplies for the chair — and Hofstadter's windbag concedes exactly that when he insists that of course the chair of the Black voters' league would be black, i.e. that an inclusive term has no content of its own and gets filled in from context and habit. Call it the prototype problem. My question is whether the novel is evidence for that view or against it, and I genuinely can't tell.

My friend says against: three hundred pages of "he" applied to ambisexual Gethenians retrains you, and by the time it's just Ai and Estraven in a tent on the Gobrin Ice you are not picturing a man, and that would mean a default is a learned habit of imagery that sustained immersion can uninstall — which a ten-page satire structurally cannot show you, because satire-by-inversion works by leaning on a default you already have (Hofstadter is counting on you finding racist language revolting, not teaching you to). I half think she's right and half think the book is doing something else entirely: Ai's whole difficulty is that he keeps failing to see Estraven as a person at all, and the scenes that cut hardest — Estraven going into kemmer on the ice, Ai's report-voice trying to describe someone who isn't a man and isn't a woman, Estraven's death at the border — are about attention and care between two people, not about which syllable English uses. The translator in our group says the entire comparison is a red herring because Karhidish marks kemmer rather than person, so the novel's own language already solves the problem Hofstadter is mocking, and the "he" is just a translation artifact. I don't think that settles it either, because the book is written in English and we read it in English. (I know Le Guin went back to the pronoun decision herself in an essay years later, in one of those essay collections whose titles I can never keep straight, and I gather she tried invented pronouns in a screenplay version — but I don't want the group's reading to depend on what she said about it, so please treat the novel itself as the evidence.)

So: give me a reading of the novel through that idea, faithful in both directions. Where does "the default person is a man, and swapping the word doesn't touch the image" genuinely illuminate a scene or a choice — Ai's narration, Estraven's kemmer, the ice crossing, Estraven's death, whatever else you'd pick — and where does the book resist the reading, or where would forcing it turn the novel into a mere illustration of a linguistics point? Which two or three scenes should I actually point the group at? And the bit I care about most: if the philosopher-of-language story is right that defaults are habits of imagery rather than semantics, does the novel add anything to that debate that the satire and the arguments can't — and vice versa, is there something ridicule-by-inversion does that three hundred pages of patient immersion can't? Please don't bother with quotations or citations; we'll have the books in front of us, and I'd rather have the argument.
