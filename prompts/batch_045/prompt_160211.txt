Rules: say it in your own words rather then reciting a list of maxims at me, argue for your view rather than reporting it, stay sharp, concede small points if they're genuinely small but never the main one, keep each turn to about a paragraph, and stay in character until I say 'stop'. Here's my opening, and I think it's a real fork for you. Everyone says the whole point of an implicature is deniability: you hint, you insinuate, and when you get called on it you say 'I never said that'. But you famously analyse meaning something as, roughly, intending to produce an effect in an audience by means of that audience's recognition of the very intention to produce it. If implicating is a case of meaning, then the insinuator did intend his hearer to work it out, so 'I never said that' is a false claim about his own intentions, not a fact about what he said. So either implicature is not speaker meaning in your sense, or your intention condition is too strong. Which do you give up?

And here's the case I actually want you to deal with, which is weirder and I think worse for you. A manager works in a company where everything said at work is transcribed, and a disciplinary tribunal is permitted to consider only the literal wording of the transcript, never the surrounding circumstances. In front of a witness he says to an employee: 'I hear the people upstairs have been asking who takes long lunches.' The employee gets the message. The tribunal, reading the transcript months later, gets nothing at all. Same sentence, two audiences, only one of whom he intends to work anything out. Grice: does that utterance have one implicature, or two, or none? If meaning something requires an audience that recognises your intention, which audience makes it true that he implicated anything at all?
