Here's my case, and I've ran the numbers twice.

A lab has a quantum coin with objective chance 0.5 of heads. Starting Monday they toss it once a day and keep going until the first heads. Every morning before the toss they make a fresh copy of me — same memories, told the protocol — and once the sequence is finished they bring every copy into one room and ask each of us: what is your credence that your toss landed heads?

I'm one of the copies. The number of copies N is geometric with p = 0.5, and exactly one copy in the room, the last one made, has a heads toss. So if I take myself to be a random copy, my credence should be E[1/N], and E[1/N] is the sum over n of (1/n)(1/2)^n, which is ln 2, about 0.69. But the chance, at the time of my toss, of my toss landing heads was 0.5, and I have no evidence about which toss I am, so the Principal Principle apparently says 0.5. This isn't an undermining case — nothing I learned touched the chances — and I can't see how my evidence is inadmissible either, since it's about the past and about who I am, not about how my toss came out.

I also tried the betting test and I don't think it settles it. Offer every copy a ticket that costs 1 and pays 2 on heads: at credence 0.5 that looks fair to each of us, and averaging over copies about 69% of us win, so each of us profits in expectation. But the bookie breaks even per run, because E[N] = 2 and he takes in N and always pays out exactly 2 to the one heads copy. So "follow the chances or get exploited" pulls both ways depending on whether you average over copies or over runs, which is why I don't think it decides anything here.

So: which step of mine do you reject — the self-location step, the claim that the chance of that de se proposition is 0.5, or the claim that my evidence is admissible? And whichever one you drop, say what it costs you: does the principle still have anything to say about the one kind of uncertainty I actually have in that room?
