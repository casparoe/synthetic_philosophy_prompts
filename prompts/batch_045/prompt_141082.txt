I convene a twelve-person final-year seminar on the ethics and epistemology of automated systems — mostly straight philosophy students with no technical background, one ninety-minute slot, a bare seminar room with a projector, index cards, envelopes and a bag of tokens I can hand out. Last year I ran a cruder version of this: I had half the room write instructions for a task and the other half execute them with deliberate literalism. It demonstrated that written specifications get exploited, but it demonstrated the wrong thing — everyone left convinced that the moral is to specify more carefully, and the distinction I actually teach never became visible, namely that between a system's doing what its operator intended and its doing what there is reason to want, where the narrow reading treats the first as the whole of the problem and merely hopes it coincides with the second. I want this year's session to put pressure on the narrow reading from the inside, and to do so before anyone has words for it.

The constraints I have in mind, which you should feel free to rework if you can do better: three kinds of participant — an operator with a partly private objective, someone who writes down what the operator appears to want on the basis of a small sample of the operator's past choices, and someone who executes only what was written down, on fresh cases, without asking questions. The scoring must pull these apart: operators are paid according to their hidden objective, writers are paid according to how well the written rule predicts the operator's verdicts on held-back cases, and executors are paid according to measured compliance with the written rule, so that a round can be maximally compliant and minimally valuable. Beyond that I want two structural wrinkles built into the rules rather than announced. First, at least one round in which the operator forms a preference only by choosing from a menu of options that the executors themselves generate, so that a student should end up unable to say whether the outcome matched an intention they already had or produced one on the spot. Second, at least one round of deliberate epistemic asymmetry, in which the executor is given information about how the domain works that the operator lacks, so that what the operator currently intends and what they would intend if they knew come apart. Nobody should hear the words alignment, intent, proxy, specification or optimisation until the debrief, and the whole thing should run on paper, a timer and perhaps a random number generator — no laptops, no software.

What I need from you is the runnable thing: exact rules and role assignments, the materials with a note on what to prepare in advance, a payoff scheme that costs me nothing but tokens, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and the verbatim text of any cards or briefing sheets participants are handed. Then a debrief that maps particular moments I should watch for onto the two readings of the problem, including what I am to do when a student says, as one surely will, that the operator should simply have been clearer. I also want your honest predictions about what will go wrong on first run — I suspect the scoring will take longer than I allow, that someone will try to be helpful across role boundaries, and that the cleverest students will read the hidden objective off the sample — with a way of handling each that does not collapse back into the specify-more-carefully lesson. Finally, tell me plainly which feature of the real problem the exercise cannot reproduce at this scale: I take it that the objective in actual systems is learned rather than written down, that the optimisation pressure is far greater, that the stakes are not notional, and that failures there are adversarial rather than merely accidental, but I would like you to say which of these the activity most badly distorts and what I must therefore supply myself in the closing minutes. If the design can be compressed to fifty minutes without losing the menu round, say how.
