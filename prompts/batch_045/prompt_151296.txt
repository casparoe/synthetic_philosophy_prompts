hey — I'm trying to nail down what a formal version of the "presumption of independence" would actually have to do. Background so we're on the same page: by a heuristic argument I mean the sort of back-of-envelope probabilistic reasoning people do when they say something like, there are a couple dozen constants in the standard model, each one seems to have to land in a narrow life-permitting window, we have no reason to think they're correlated, so multiply the windows together, the result is absurdly small, so something's up. Every argument in that family leans on a move of the shape: no evidence of a correlation between X and Y, so treat them as independent (or near enough) and multiply.

I want to give that move an actual specification, because I'm building a small evaluator that takes arguments like that apart and reports where the weight is really coming from — it should be able to say things like, this step assumes independence across these three quantities and if you relax it the conclusion swings by two orders of magnitude. So the standard I need is adequacy-for-auditing-heuristic-arguments, not adequacy as a general epistemology of probability. That's the purpose; keep it in view.

So: write me the desiderata. What does a formal account of default-to-independence have to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement; which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp; and — this is the part I most want — where the list eats itself, i.e. which subsets of the desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied.

Cases I already know need handling, so don't just restate them back at me, use them to generate criteria:

- carving relativity. Whether two quantities look correlated depends on how you slice up the possibility space (Bertrand-paradox-flavoured), and a default that silently shifts with my choice of variables is doing nothing.
- exchangeability vs. independence. For repeated trials of the same kind the honest default might be exchangeability, with independence only falling out after conditioning on a parameter. Which one should the default be, and what does that choice cost?
- deterministic-but-unknown vs. genuinely chancy. The digits of pi are fixed by their definition and I just don't know them; a coin I haven't looked at is also fixed, in some sense. Do these get the same treatment, and if not, what in the formalism marks the difference?
- selection effects. In anthropic-flavoured arguments the fact that anyone is around to observe is itself correlated with the constants even when there's no physical interaction between them, so naive independence gives the wrong number. That's a correlation induced by who's doing the observing, not a correlation in the world.
- the compounding worry. Presume independence across n quantities and multiply, and small violations compound; at n around twenty the output is meaningless unless the default comes with a slack parameter instead of a flat zero correlation.
- independence as a defeatable substantive claim. Independence across the constants is itself a physical hypothesis (no common mechanism sets them), so the default has to be visibly defeasible and not a hidden premise.
- continuous and unbounded spaces, where max-ent style defaults go improper or undefined.

One thing I want your verdict on specifically. I've been toying with a criterion of my own and I can't tell if it's a real desideratum or a confusion I've talked myself into. Call it common-cause humility: the default should never assign exactly zero correlation, but should assign a small fixed spread of possible correlations centered on zero, on the grounds that there could always be some unknown mechanism linking the two quantities, and an argument's conclusion should be required to survive the whole spread rather than just the point value. Genuine requirement, or a misunderstanding of what a default is for? Tell me if it's the latter.
