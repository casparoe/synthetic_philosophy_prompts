I convene a small interdisciplinary reading group on the history of violence — two historians, a political theorist, a public-health epidemiologist, and me. Two sessions ago the political theorist presented a paper arguing that the standard homicide-rate series leaves out most of what ought to count: deaths produced by colonial administration and by economic sanctions, harms of forced displacement, and the standing threat that secures compliance without anyone ever being struck. Her conclusion was that the familiar decline story is largely an artifact of what got counted. A member of the group who is sympathetic to the decline story ended the exchange with: "Look, everyone knows the world is safer than it has ever been; the trend just isn't seriously in dispute."

I don't want you to adjudicate the empirical disagreement between them. I want that one sentence taken apart, because I think it was being made to do work it cannot obviously do, and I want to bring a menu of its possible meanings to the next session rather than a verdict.

Three things make me suspicious. First, "safer" and "less violent" come apart: a world with somewhat more violence but vastly less disease, famine, and accident could be safer in every risk-related sense while being more violent, and a sentence that says "safer" may be quietly trading on that. Second, the quantity looks denominator-dependent: absolute violent deaths per year, violent deaths per 100,000 people per year, violence's share of all deaths, and lifetime risk of dying violently can move in different directions at the same time — a falling rate in a growing population still raises the absolute count, and a sharp fall in infectious disease raises violence's share of all deaths even if the rate is unchanged. Third, I have become attached to a measure I invented and can't get out of my head: threatened person-years, the number of person-years lived under a credible standing threat of violence, weighted by the probability that the threat is executed, counting no actual injuries at all. A long nuclear peace in which nobody is struck scores very high on it. I take this to suggest that "total violence" may not be one quantity waiting to be estimated, but I can't tell whether that makes the claim indeterminate or only makes it incomplete until a measure is specified.

Please lay out the readings as a menu, not as a single best interpretation. Do not cite figures, studies, or authors: I want the conceptual lay of the land.

Deliverable 1 — a table of the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making. Columns, in this order:

1. Reading — a short label (three to six words).
2. Precise claim — the sentence rewritten so that it fixes what quantity is meant, in what unit over what denominator, for which population, over which time range, and against which comparison class.
3. Status — exactly one of: "platitude"; "substantive and defensible"; "substantive and contested"; "false or very likely false"; "indeterminate (no fact of the matter as stated)".
4. Supports the dismissal? — "yes", "partly", or "no": whether that reading, if granted, would license telling the presenter that the trend is not in dispute and that her question about what gets counted does not matter.
5. What would settle it — the kind of evidence or argument that would actually establish or break it, in one clause.

Cover at least these: the all-risks reading ("safer" in the sense of lower overall risk of death or injury, whatever the cause); the rate reading; the absolute-count reading; the share-of-deaths reading; the lifetime-risk reading; the comparison-class readings (safer than any past moment, safer than any past century, safer than a stated baseline); the perception reading (the world is far better than it feels, because attention is biased toward vivid events); the robustness or cluster reading (every reasonable operationalization trends downward, so the choice of measure does not matter); the stipulative reading (violence is intentional physical force by an identifiable agent against a person, so structural and policy-driven harms are not violence); and the moral-progress reading. Add any reading I have missed.
