I'm writing up a paper on the rationality side of normative theory, and I'd be grateful for an adjudication of an argument that broke out in our decision-theory reading group last week and that nobody managed to settle.

The case under discussion was Parfit's hitchhiker: a traveller stranded in the desert is offered a lift to town by a driver who extracts a promise of £1,000 on arrival; once in town the traveller will never see the driver again, the promise is unenforceable, and (on the assumption that self-interest is all he has reason to care about) he has no reason to pay. Two members of the group, Yusuf and Hanne, argued as follows.

**Y1.** The interesting conclusion is not "he should pay" but that the self-interest theory is indirectly self-defeating: in the desert it tells him to acquire a disposition to pay; in town it tells him to act against that disposition. So the case shows that rationality can require irrationality — it can be rational to bring it about that you will later do what you will then have no reason to do.

**H1.** That is not irrationality; it is an ordinary commitment problem solved internally. If the disposition is simply a strong desire to keep one's promises, then paying in town maximises expected utility relative to his desires, and on the desire-based account that is what rationality consists in. Nothing has been shown about rationality condemning what rationality recommends. You have changed the inputs, not the theory, and the word "irrationality" is doing no work.

**Y2.** A reliable disposition must be one he cannot switch off at will, and the real claim concerns belief, not desire: the hitchhiker is supposed to believe that he has a decisive reason to pay, and that belief is false. So paying is irrational relative to the reasons he actually has, and the case concerns the gap between "rational" in the reason-responsive sense and "rational" in the desire-satisfying sense — rationality in the first sense requiring a violation of rationality in the first sense.

**H2.** Two problems. (a) Transparency: the device must survive his knowing that it is a device, yet he is the one installing it and he can simply reconsider in town. And if the fix is "and be disposed not to reconsider," the package becomes far more expensive and far more global — a standing policy of not thinking clearly about one's own promises. (b) Selectivity: promise-keeping dispositions are not desert-specific. The general disposition will cost him much more than the lift is worth.

**Y3.** Those are cost objections, not refutations; the claim was never that the disposition is always worth acquiring. Nor is transparency fatal: some dispositions are recalcitrant — guilt, shame, loyalty — and are not cancelled by the knowledge that they were installed. Consider the case where a benefactor pays you a million tonight merely for having formed, by tomorrow morning, the intention to drink a harmless but unpleasant toxin tomorrow, knowing that when tomorrow comes you will have no reason to drink. The question there is whether one can intend at will and whether such intentions survive full transparency, and the answer is not obviously no.

**H3.** Then take a harder variant — the "briefed hitchhiker." Suppose the driver is an excellent predictor and stops only for someone who would still pay after being told, on arrival: "you now have no self-interested reason to pay, nothing will come of not paying, and I am briefing you precisely so that you know this." No false belief can now do any work: the hitchhiker knows everything and must still pay. What the driver is selecting for is therefore not irrationality but a non-instrumental concern — with having promised, with the driver, with being a certain kind of person. If the hitchhiker has that concern, paying is rational by his own lights; if he lacks it, no amount of rational irrationality manufactures it. The case shows that some ends carry a selection advantage, not that rationality requires irrationality.

**Y4.** But "whatever you care about is rational by your own lights" empties the notion of rationality, and you cannot help yourself to your own theory of rationality in order to argue that rationality never requires irrationality — that is exactly what is in dispute. And the briefed hitchhiker refutes nothing; it describes the successful end state of the disposition. The hitchhiker begins by needing to pay and ends up caring about paying. That transformation is precisely what the rational irrationality was supposed to engineer.

**H4.** I am not assuming a theory of rationality; I am charging equivocation. "Rational" in your desert premise means "there is most reason to acquire the disposition"; "irrational" in your town premise means "does not maximise expected utility given his desires." Those are two different senses. Use either one consistently and the paradox dissolves: either paying is not irrational (desire sense), or acquiring the disposition is not rational (reasons sense — you have no reason to bring about your doing what you have no reason to do).

**Y5.** The equivocation charge assumes that a reason cannot bear on a state rather than on an act, and that reasons cannot be self-effacing. But they plainly can: there are reasons to be spontaneous, reasons to trust, reasons to have desires one should not act on. A person can have most reason to be in a state whose exercise is locally unsupported by his reasons, and that local failure is exactly what the state-level reason was a reason for. Once you allow state-given and self-effacing reasons, the two senses stop pulling apart and there is no equivocation — only a real and uncomfortable feature of practical reason.

**H5.** Self-effacement has limits, though. A reason to be in a state S is genuinely self-effacing only where S's exercise is not something you have independent reason to avoid — spontaneity and trust are like that. But here the self-interest theorist's verdict on the exercise is not silence, it is condemnation: in town the hitchhiker has most reason *not* to pay. So the state-level reason is a reason to be in a state whose exercise you have most reason to avoid, and it is far from clear that the spontaneity analogy covers that case rather than merely resembling it.
