I'm in the final year of a philosophy degree and I'm taking an option this term on theories of truth. Last week we did the classical pragmatists — Peirce's account of truth as whatever inquiry would settle on if it were carried far enough, and James's account of the true as what it is, in some broad sense, expedient for us to believe. The seminar kept falling back on the one-liner 'truth is what works', used indifferently for both, and I want to get clear on what exactly that sentence can be asserting before I write my response paper.

The reason it matters is the work the line was made to do. I raised a case where it seems to me there is plainly a truth of the matter that no possible inquiry could ever reach: the last sentence ever spoken in a language that died out along with its final speaker. A classmate replied that truth is what works, so if a proposition makes no difference to anything we could ever do or experience, there is nothing to be said about its truth, and my question isn't a genuine one. Our tutor then set a harder version: suppose that every investigator who ever runs a certain inquiry shares a cognitive blind spot, such that the hypothesis inquiry reliably converges on is false while the true hypothesis is permanently inaccessible to us. On a Peircean reading the slogan appears to say that at the limit of inquiry the false hypothesis is the true one; and that result makes truth look as though it depends on contingent facts about our cognitive equipment.

What I'd like is a menu rather than a single interpretation. Please set out the distinct precise theses 'truth is what works' could be expressing, ordered from the merely trivial through the substantive and defensible to the plainly false. For each: specify what 'works' is doing (practical success, explanatory power, survival under indefinitely prolonged inquiry, coherence with our other beliefs, social or moral expedience), whose purposes it works for, and over what timescale; and, the part I care most about, say whether the reading is a claim about the nature of truth, a claim about meaning, a claim about which beliefs to adopt, a normative claim about what we have reason to believe, or a deflationary claim that there is nothing here for a theory of truth to be about. Mark each as trivial, contentious but defensible, or false, and say which reading people who deploy the line are probably reaching for and whether that reading survives scrutiny.

Then the payoff: for each reading, does it actually deliver my classmate's conclusion about the dead-language sentence, and does it force the verdict on the blind-spot case? And does the disambiguation leave Peirce and James as two versions of a single doctrine, or as genuinely different views travelling under the same slogan?

I don't need references or chapter and verse — I'm after the distinctions themselves — and I'd be grateful if you could write in British English.
