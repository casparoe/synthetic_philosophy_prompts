You are a professional philosopher who works on formal epistemology — subjective probability, coherence norms, and Dutch book arguments. Take your time. Before giving your verdict, spend a paragraph brainstorming: list the three or four places the argument below could break, and what each of them would cost me. Then pick one and price the alternatives. I want a diagnosis, not a survey.

Six months ago I took over my company's internal forecasting pool: about forty standing questions about launch dates, hiring, and regulatory approvals, scored by Brier score. A colleague beat me badly, and as far as I can tell he didn't know more about the world than I did. What he noticed was that I had assigned 0.7 to each of three separate claims that, as he eventually showed me, could not all be true together. He then sold me three bets at my own fair prices: $0.70 each, paying $1 if the claim turned out true. I paid $2.10 and could collect on at most two of them.

I wanted to say that he exploited me because my credences were incoherent, and that incoherence is a rational defect. Then a friend who does decision theory gave me this, and now I think the Dutch book argument proves far too much:

(1) If there is a set of bets, each of which I would accept at my own fair prices, whose combined outcome is a net loss no matter how the world turns out, then my credences are irrational. This is what the Dutch book argument is supposed to establish: coherence is a requirement of rationality.

(2) Such a set of bets exists whenever my credences violate the probability axioms — and the bookie who finds it needs to know more logic than I do, not more about the world.

(3) In my case each of the three bets looked favorable to me individually; I would have taken any one of them on its own. I could not see that the three claims were jointly impossible — establishing that took my colleague an argument I had not thought of. So I was incoherent purely through ignorance of a logical fact.

(4) So the requirement is not really have coherent credences but never hold opinions about propositions whose logical relations you have not verified. For anyone with bounded time and memory who holds opinions about a few dozen interconnected questions, there will always be some such relation left unchecked: the combinations multiply, and some of them are hard to compute. No agent like me can meet the requirement.

(5) A requirement that no agent like me can meet is not a requirement of rationality at all; ought implies can.

(6) So (1) is false. The fact that my colleague could have taken money from me shows nothing about whether my credences were irrational — and by the same reasoning the Dutch book argument shows nothing about anyone's credences, since everyone is in my position.

I am confident about (3) and reluctant to give up (1): I want to be able to say both that coherence is a genuine rational requirement and that I could not have spotted the inconsistency in time. If that means paying for (5), I want to know exactly what I am paying.

Which step do you reject? If there is more than one way out, tell me which is cheapest and what each of the others costs. Finally: whatever view survives, tell me what it can still say about my case — can it call anything I did a mistake, and if so, which thing?
