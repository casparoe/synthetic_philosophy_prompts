I spent rather more than thirty years as a research chemist and have been reading political philosophy since I retired. I now sit on a small citizens' assembly on electoral reform and have been asked to produce a short position paper on who should be required to vote, so I would like the package checked for coherence before I commit it to paper. Roughly where I have landed:

(1) Voting should be compulsory in law — attendance required, sanctioned by a modest civil fine — but with a formal none-of-the-above option on the ballot, so that nobody is made to endorse a candidate or a policy they do not accept.

(2) My reason for favouring compulsion is the one that appeals to me professionally: a voluntary electorate is a self-selected sample, and self-selection is biased in a known direction — toward the old, the comfortably-off, the better-educated and the strongly partisan — so the result is a biased estimate of what the population wants. Compulsion turns a convenience sample into something closer to the population itself, much as a mandatory response rate does in a survey.

(3) Who may vote should be settled by subjection to the state's coercion rather than by nationality, identity or membership: anyone who must obey the law has a claim to a say in the making of it. This is what I take the all-affected principle to amount to when it is made precise.

(4) It follows, I think, that the voting age should come down to sixteen, and that long-term residents who are not citizens — including those with no legal immigration status — should vote in national as well as local elections, with no language or civics test attached. I would set the residence qualification at five years, on the ground that a franchise needs a determinate boundary.

(5) It also seems to me to follow that non-resident citizens should lose the vote after, say, fifteen years abroad, and that their children born abroad should not inherit it: nationality by itself carries no permanent claim.

(6) I do not want to give up the thought that elections are partly instruments for getting decisions right — that outcomes can be better or worse, and that a franchise can be better or worse at tracking which is which.

(7) Finally, I would allow a standing and permanent declaration of non-participation: anyone may register once as a non-voter and thereafter be exempt from the fine. That strikes me as the decent accommodation of conscientious objection.

I take it as obvious that (1) is no infringement of political liberty, since only attendance is compelled and no one is forced to express a view. What I want is an audit rather than a tutorial: which of these commitments actually pull against one another, which only look as though they do, and for each real tension what would have to be given up and at what cost. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equal, and tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up more than one of them at once. Please also indicate how confident you are in what you claim, separate the conceptual claims from any empirical ones you lean on, and single out the point you are least sure of.
