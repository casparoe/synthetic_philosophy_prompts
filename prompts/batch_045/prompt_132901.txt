So I'm writing a novel and I've backed myself into a philosophy-of-mind problem I definately don't have the training for. The protagonist, Mira, has a made-up neurological thing I'm calling Kestrel syndrome: after a small lesion she reports pain exactly as she did before — same burning, same location, same intensity on whatever scale you like — and she insists it still feels awful, 'just as bad as it always did' when she tries to explain it to her sister. But the pain has completely lost its grip on her: she doesn't pull her hand off the stove, doesn't lose her train of thought, never asks for anything for it, and will happily finish a conversation through what used to be a very distracting toothache. She isn't being stoical and she isn't confused or confabulating; she says, quite distinctly, that it hurts and that she doesn't mind. As I understand it this is meant to be the mirror image of the classic pain asymbolia cases in the clinical literature, where people can localize and discriminate a painful stimulus perfectly well but are strangely indifferent to it — I want Mira to be the opposite dissociation, with the sensory and (apparently) the affective dimension intact while the motivational, get-it-to-stop, this-matters-right-now dimension drops out. I need this to be right because the whole moral shape of the book turns on it: other characters keep treating her as either mysteriously brave or as no longer really suffering, and I don't know which of them is being cruel.

So give me a number and show the working. Specifically: what is your credence that the badness of pain for the person having it is entirely a matter of how unpleasant it feels — such that a state with the full sensory-discriminative and motivational profile of pain but zero unpleasantness would not be bad for its subject at all? I don't want 'well, it depends what you mean by badness.' I want the decomposition, with a rough probability and one line of justification on each term, something like: (a) that the asymbolia cases really are a dissociation within pain experience rather than a reporting quirk, an executive/frontal deficit, or global emotional blunting; (b) that unpleasantness is a separable component of experience at all, rather than an aspect of sensory intensity or a kind of content that can't come apart from it; (c) that badness-for-the-subject is a hedonic matter rather than a matter of frustrated desire, of an ignored command to make it stop, or of being disposed to avoid; and (d) that Mira's syndrome, the reverse dissociation, is even a coherent possibility — and if it were actual and well-described, whether it counts for or against the hedonic account. Then combine them into your headline number, tell me which term the number is most sensitive to and which one you're least confident about, and tell me what kind of first-person report or clinical finding would actually move you (I know you can't look anything up — I mean what sort of thing, in principle).

For calibration: I'm at about 0.4, because the more I try to write Mira's interior monologue the more the 'hurts but I don't mind' line starts to feel like it describes two things instead of one. My brother-in-law, who is an ER nurse and has seen a lot of people in pain, is at about 0.9 and thinks I'm overcomplicating it. Give me your own number, then tell me where you think the gap between me and him actually lives — which of the sub-questions we'd have to settle to bring us together, and which of us is probably wrong about how much rides on that sub-question.
