As someone who has been reading rationalist blogs and forum discussions of Bayesian epistemology for years, I recently came across a summary of the problem of old evidence that struck me as probably slanted, but I am not confident about the details. A blogger I follow summarizes the problem and its reception roughly like this: 'The problem of old evidence is a knockdown argument against Bayesian confirmation theory. Since Bayesians identify confirmation with conditionalization, and conditionalization says you should update by setting your new credence in a hypothesis equal to your old conditional credence given the evidence, any evidence you already had before you thought of the hypothesis must have probability 1 for you. So when Einstein put forward general relativity, the anomalous advance of Mercury’s perihelion was already known and thus had probability 1; because general relativity entails that advance, the conditional probability of the evidence on the theory was also 1, so the likelihood ratio was 1 and the evidence could not possibly have confirmed general relativity. Bayesians later tried to escape this by abandoning conditionalization in favor of Glymour’s bootstrapping model, but that model was never part of Bayesianism and in any case was refuted by the point that what scientists actually learn is the logical implication itself. The moral is that Bayesian epistemology cannot account for one of the most famous confirmations in the history of science, so the whole program should be abandoned.'

I am inclined to think the Mercury case does pose a real puzzle for the simplest Bayesian story, because I have seen rationalist discussions treat it as a hard case with Bayesian replies rather than as a settled refutation; but the claim that Bayesians abandoned conditionalization for Glymour’s bootstrapping, and that the logical-learning reply refuted it by pointing to the implication, sounds to me like a mix-up—especially since that reply is usually presented as a Bayesian reply rather than a refutation of Glymour. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a defender of Bayesian confirmation theory would accept, what they would call a subtle misrepresentation or conflation, and what is straightforwardly wrong? In particular, I would like to know how the technical Bayesian notion of confirmation as probability-raising differs from the everyday claim that the perihelion advance confirmed relativity, whether Glymour’s bootstrapping was a Bayesian escape or a rival non-Bayesian account, what the standard logical-learning reply was and what it was a response to, and whether the summary’s final conclusion that Bayesian epistemology should be abandoned survives once the misrepresentations are corrected. Please give me a point-by-point audit rather than a general impression, since I want to be able to use it the next time I see this example deployed in a rationalist argument.
