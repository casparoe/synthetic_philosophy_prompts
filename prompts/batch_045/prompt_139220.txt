I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in experimental physical chemistry, mostly NMR and calorimetry, so my reflex whenever the word information comes up is to reach for entropy, channel capacity, coarse-graining, free energy and irreversibility long before I reach for a moral word. I've recently started going to a philosophy reading group (my first real exposure to the stuff, so assume I know the physics cold but am shaky on the political theory) and we've landed on the question of how much citizens ought to be able to keep from the state. What struck me is that the three setups everyone compares seem to be doing three quite different things to a communication channel. The German approach looks like it's trying to narrow the channel and bind each reading to a declared purpose and then discard it — which in my terms is like compelling the observer to pay the erasure cost instead of letting it keep the record for free; I gather the phrase informational self-determination comes out of some constitutional court ruling about a census back in the eighties, though I don't know the details and don't much care about them. The American approach looks less like shrinking the channel than like insisting it run in both directions, openness about what the state does supposedly compensating for what the state knows. And the Chinese social credit arrangement looks like routing an enormous number of readings into a single very low-dimensional variable — a score — and then using it as a control signal to drive behaviour, which is a feedback loop with an aggressively coarse-grained observable. I've also been toying with two ideas of my own: a distinction between never generating the datum, generating it but severing the path to the state, and letting it through only at degraded resolution; and something I call the unpredictability floor, the minimum residual entropy rate a person has to retain relative to the state's model of them before you'd say they are being governed rather than merely predicted — on the grounds that a controller with zero prediction error hasn't measured you, it's absorbed you.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Take the strongest versions of the arguments on both sides of this — the case for extensive state knowledge of citizens, the case for keeping things back, the usual nothing-to-hide reply, the worry about function creep where data collected for one purpose turns out to be informative about all sorts of others like a demon exploiting fluctuations it wasn't supposed to be looking at — and restate them one by one in that vocabulary: correlations, mutual information between a person and a registry, coarse-graining and how fine the state's macrovariables are allowed to be, channel capacity, dissipation and the cost of acquiring and holding a correlation, back-action where being predicted changes what's predicted. Stay honest about it: don't quietly slide in a moral premise and present it as if it were the translation.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which claims come across cleanly? Which only come across bent — is consent really just an authorisation flag on a read operation, is privacy really just low mutual information, is purpose limitation really just a deadline on a memory register, is dignity really just a constraint on how coarsely you're allowed to be grained? And which claims have no expression at all in a framework that counts bits and joules? For those, tell me whether that's a gap in my vocabulary or a sign that the original claim was never as clear as it sounds. One thing I'd especially like your view on: entropy measures how much is hidden, but harm seems to depend on which bit it is — a single correlated bit about someone's health can wreck a life while a gigabyte of shopping data does nothing — so is quantity-of-information simply the wrong axis here, or is there a version of the physical picture that can tell the difference? And if my unpredictability-floor notion is doing real work, use it; if it's just thermodynamics with a political word sellotaped on, say so. Please don' t hand me a reading list or point me at papers — I can't get to a university library easily and I'd rather have the argument in my own terms anyway.
