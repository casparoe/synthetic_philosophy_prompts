The question was:

Hume wants it both ways. He agrees with the proverb that there is no disputing about taste, but he also thinks some verdicts about beauty are plainly better than others. Explain how the standard of taste is meant to reconcile these two claims. Say what a true judge is, how we are supposed to tell when a verdict is the verdict of a true judge, and whether any of this makes beauty objective.

The student's answer (unedited):

Hume starts from the claim that beauty is not a quality in objects at all, but a sentiment that objects excite in us. That is why reasoning can't settle a dispute about taste: you can't argue someone into finding a thing beautiful. But he doesn't want to be a plain relativist either, because we all treat some works as better than others, and the ones that go on pleasing people across centuries and across cultures really do seem to be the better ones.

His solution is the true judge: someone with delicacy of taste, sharpened by practice, improved by comparison, free of prejudice, and possessed of good sense. The point of the list is that most of our likes and dislikes come from the wrong place — we prefer whatever is fashionable, or whatever flatters our own nation, or our friends — and the true judge is simply the person whose response has been cleaned of all that. Delicacy of taste is the important one: a delicate palate is a refined one, so a true judge will reliably prefer subtle, understated work to anything crude or obvious, and the rest of the list is mostly there to make sure that preference doesn't get distorted.

Since beauty is only a sentiment, there is no rule or definition to appeal to. The standard of taste just is the joint verdict of the true judges, and that is what makes it objective: a work is beautiful when the qualified critics of its time agree that it is, and the agreement is the whole of the matter. Hume's test of time — if a work has pleased people in many different periods and places, its beauty is real — is only a shortcut to the same thing, since what it measures is just whether the verdict has stayed stable. This does mean Hume has no room for a work that everyone ignored when it appeared being recognised as great later on: on his account, if the critics of the day don't like it, then it isn't beautiful, and later generations are merely changing their taste rather than noticing something that was there all along.

So the view is not objective in the sense that beauty sits in the object waiting to be found, and not subjective in the sense that anything goes. It is objective in the sense that there is a fact of the matter fixed by what the experts accept, the way the correct temperature is fixed by what a good thermometer reads.
