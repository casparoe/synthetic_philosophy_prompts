I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of perception, and last week two colleagues in my department — one a common-factor theorist, the other a disjunctivist — got into an argument after a colloquium dinner that neither of them would let go of, and I was foolish enough to be asked to referee it. I'd like your help, and I'd rather you assess the arguments on their merits than point me toward the literature: no citations, no reading suggestions, no "so-and-so has a famous reply to this." The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, went as follows. Nadia opened with the causal version of the argument from illusion: the best neuroscience we have says that a veridical perception of a scene and a matching hallucination can share their entire proximal causal history — same retinal stimulation or same cortical stimulation, same downstream processing, same everything on this side of the skull — so there must be a mental kind that both episodes instantiate, and disjunctivism, which denies the shared kind, is making an empirical bet that the evidence looks increasingly likely to lose. Owen replied that sameness of proximal cause simply doesn't entail sameness of mental kind: the veridical episode is partly individuated by the object seen, in the way a genuine banknote is partly individuated by who issued it, and a perfect counterfeit from the same printing press is locally indistinguishable without being the same kind of thing; to assume otherwise is just to help yourself to the common factor. Nadia then proposed a case she called "the splice": a subject with a wireless visual prosthesis whose optic nerve has been bypassed, where an experimenter toggles between the subject's own camera feed and a synthetic rendering of the very same scene, the stimulation pattern arriving at visual cortex being bit-for-bit identical either way and the toggling being undetectable by the subject, who cannot tell at any moment, and cannot tell even on ideal reflection afterward, whether she is currently seeing or merely seeming to see. Her claim was that the disjunctivist is now committed to a subject who flips between two fundamentally different kinds of mental state several times a minute with no difference in how things are for her and no difference she could ever reflectively locate, which makes the thesis either false or trivial — distal bookkeeping dressed up as a claim about consciousness. Owen answered that the demand for introspective availability is precisely what is in dispute: a difference in what a state *is* need not show up as a further qualitative feature, and the disjunctivist can locate the payoff epistemically rather than phenomenally — in the veridical case the experience supplies a factive reason, supports singular demonstrative thought about the object, and can ground knowledge, whereas in the hallucinatory case it cannot. Nadia pressed what she took to be the fatal version of this: if the difference in kind never makes a difference to what the subject can tell, to what she is disposed to believe and do, or to what she can reason her way to, then it is an idle wheel, because everything the disjunctivist wants explained is explained by the common-factor experience plus the independently obtaining worldly fact that the scene is as it appears; and Owen's banknote analogy misfires, she added, since banknotes have a constitutive historical property supplied by an institution, while the disjunctivist has given no comparable account of what makes an experience's being a *seeing* depend on the object — an account that doesn't simply collapse into externalism about content, which the common-factor theorist is happy to grant. Owen's closing move was that the "experience plus the world" package cannot deliver the fine-grained success he cares about — acting on and thinking about *this very* object — that only a state constitutively involving the object can explain, and that if his position is forced back from a metaphysical thesis about experience to an epistemological one, that is a real dialectical cost but not a refutation.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, largely right, questionable, or clearly mistaken, and give the strongest reason for your rating, including cases where a move is right for a reason neither disputant gave. Two further things I'd especially value. First, does the splice case actually add any argumentative force beyond the ordinary hallucination case, or is it an old argument in new costume? Second, what is each of them missing — is there a coherent middle position (say, disjunctivism about the perceptual event or relation but not about experience, or vice versa) that dissolves the dispute, or is the residual disagreement substantive, and if so, what exactly is it about?
