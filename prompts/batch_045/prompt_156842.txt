You are a political philosopher whose research is in democratic theory and the normative foundations of the franchise: the boundary problem, the enfranchisement of children and of resident non-citizens, and the ethics of electoral participation rules. Work slowly and carefully. Before writing your answer, do two things privately: first, restate each of my commitments in the strongest form you can give it; second, test them against each other pairwise, marking each pair as a real conflict, an apparent conflict, or no conflict. Then give me the audit.

The context: I have been asked to write the normative section of a report for a standing commission on electoral law, and before I commit these views to print I want to know whether the package I have arrived at actually hangs together. A colleague tells me it does not, but she will not say where. Here is roughly where I have landed.

(a) The franchise is grounded in affected interests rather than in membership. What gives someone a claim to a say is that the state's coercive decisions substantially shape the course of their life over the long run. It follows that long-term resident non-citizens — including residents without legal status — should be able to vote in national elections. I take citizenship to be, in itself, no more morally relevant to the franchise than eye colour, and I do not think consent, contract, or cultural membership can do the boundary work that affected interests do.

(b) There should be no competence, literacy, or civic-knowledge requirement for the franchise. I reject epistocratic proposals, including the statistically sophisticated ones, on the familiar grounds that the aggregation results they invoke are sensitive to assumptions about voter independence, and that such tests have in practice tracked class and race. More strongly: I do not think the quality of a person's political judgment is the right kind of thing to gate the franchise at all.

(c) The voting age should be fourteen. I hold this as a substantive judgement, not as a placeholder pending better psychology.

(d) Voting should be legally compulsory: a duty to turn out, enforced by a modest fine, with easy postal and absentee options and, in my view, an election-day holiday. Two features of the scheme matter to me and I want them preserved: the ballot must contain a formal abstention option, and the secret ballot must be preserved in full. The reason is that I also think there is a genuine moral right to withhold one's endorsement — no one may be required to prefer one candidate to another, and conscientious abstention is sometimes exactly the right thing to do. A colleague says these two features of (d) cannot be had together. I think she is wrong, but I would like the objection answered properly rather than waved off.

(e) Compulsion is over-determined for me, and I would be sorry to lose any of the three grounds: it is a duty of democratic self-rule owed by the members of a political community to one another; it makes the electorate mirror the population whose interests are at stake rather than the old, the rich, and the already-engaged; and it improves the uptake of the policies that track those interests.

(f) I reject proxy voting for children — the proposal that parents cast additional votes on behalf of their minor children. My reason is that it hands parents political power in proportion to their fertility and treats children as extensions of their parents rather than as the independent agents they are becoming. I take this to be decisive, not a tie-breaker.

(g) Two boundary assumptions I have been making without much argument: that a residency-duration requirement (a year, say) is acceptable for newly arrived residents; and that citizens who have lived abroad for decades retain the vote indefinitely.

One more thing my colleague presses: she says (a) absurdly enfranchises tourists and short-term visitors, and that this shows affectedness cannot supply a franchise at all. I am inclined to think the objection is generic to any criterion that has to be implemented by a threshold, and so tells against nothing in particular — but tell me if I am wrong.

What I want from you, in this order:

1. The pairs that are in real conflict, ordered by severity, with what each conflict forces me to give up, add, or qualify.
2. The pairs that only look like they conflict, with an explanation of why they dissolve — and if a dissolve works only given some further condition I have not stated, say what that condition is and how much it costs me.
3. For each real conflict, the cheapest repair and its price.
4. One overall recommendation: the single revision that cleans up the most, and what I lose by making it.
5. Anything the package needs but lacks — a distinction, a principle, an argument I have not made — rather than only a list of what is wrong with what I have.

Where any of this turns on an empirical claim about turnout, adolescent political judgment, or parental influence over minors, say which claim it is and how much of my view rests on it. Be blunt where a commitment cannot survive. Do not give me a reading list, a literature survey, or citations: I want the argumentative work, not sources, and I have no interest in who said what first.
