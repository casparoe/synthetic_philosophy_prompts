I am finishing the last novel of a trilogy, and I have come to suspect that the book's engine rests on a moral-psychology bet I could not defend under questioning. My editor, who has been the most useful reader of the manuscript, states the bet as follows: guilt is a debt, shame is a stain. A debt has a creditor, a determinate sum, and a procedure for discharge; pay it and the ledger clears. A stain has no sum, no creditor and no discharge — it can only be covered, bleached, or cut out of the fabric. The conclusion she draws is the one my third act currently enacts: guilt is the reform-friendly emotion, because it attaches to an act, admits of reparation, and ends; shame is the regressive one, because it attaches to a self, admits of no reparation, and does not end; and a culture that wants less cruelty and less recidivism should arrange its institutions and its stories so as to produce guilt rather than shame. It is a more respectable descendant of the old anthropological habit of sorting whole societies into guilt cultures and shame cultures, because it supplies a mechanism.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on the conclusion, and I do not want the etymological point — that some languages use one word for both guilt and debt — to be doing any work. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the creditor, the sum, the unit of account, the tender, the discharge, the ledger, the fabric, the solvent, the launderer, and the party competent to declare the debt paid. Then tell me which of these the target side actually lacks, and which of the absences matter for the use my editor is making of the picture. I would rather have a ranked audit, load-bearing breaks first, than an inventory.

Two cases from the manuscript are, I think, where the picture is tested. In the first, the protagonist's guilt has no creditor: the person she wronged is dead, and there is no one left with standing to accept payment or pronounce the matter settled. Her guilt then behaves exactly as the analogy says a stain behaves — with no sum, no terminus, and no response to anything she does. In the second, a secondary character's shame lifts, and lifts permanently, in a single scene where nothing is repaired, nothing is forgiven and no one is persuaded of anything: another character simply sees what he did, sees it accurately, and goes on seeing him. My draft treats this as a discharge. If it is one, the second half of the analogy is false. So: do these cases break the mapping, or do they show that the mapping located the difference in the wrong place — in act versus self, rather than in whether repair is socially available at all?

What does the analogy quietly import that the target need not have? I have in mind a stable unit of moral account, an authority able to declare a debt settled, a self with an unstained condition to be restored, and the assumption that an emotion's object is fixed by the emotion rather than by what a surrounding community will accept as payment. Say which of these are doing argumentative work and which are decoration.

Finally, the question I actually have to answer to finish the book: can a culture choose its dominant moral emotion, and if so, through what lever? My worry is that the only lever a novelist has is not a public rite but a book read alone, one reader at a time, with no creditor in the room. Does the debt-and-stain picture misdescribe the mechanism of transmission — is the operative variable the content of a culture's stories, or the social form in which they are consumed? If there is a better analogy for that specific mechanism, I would like it; and if my editor's conclusion survives only in part, tell me what the third act must stop claiming. I would rather cut the scene than leave the novel right by stipulation.
