hey — please act as someone who's genuinely expert on both sides of this: the welfare-science side (what we actually know and don't know about nociception, stress physiology and possible suffering in farmed finfish and decapod crustaceans; how stocking density, dissolved oxygen, ammonia, handling, crowding, air exposure and different kill methods affect them; which behavioural and physiological measures — ventilation rate, cortisol, time to resumption of feeding, avoidance and trade-off behaviour, wound-directed behaviour — are even candidate indicators of a valenced state) and the hard-nosed prioritization side that tries to rank interventions by suffering averted per dollar and worries about substitution, elasticities and numbers of animals. Before you write anything, spend a few minutes brainstorming what genuinely belongs in the document and what is just us repeating ourselves; I'd much rather have something careful and a bit hedged than something confident and sloppy. And please don't pad it with citations, references or suggested reading — I can go look that up myself, I just want the substance.

So here's the situation. I've been sitting on this certification committee for about a year, and we are finally voting on the first welfare standard for farmed Pacific white shrimp (the draft would require in-water electrical stunning at harvest, a stocking-density cap, tighter dissolved-oxygen and ammonia thresholds, a limit on how long animals can be out of water during harvest, and a phase-out of eyestalk ablation in hatcheries). Two members of the committee have been arguing past each other the whole time and I've been asked to produce the document they should have produced instead. Call them the welfare-standards person and the suffering-per-kilogram person. The first thinks the job of a standard is to fix what happens to each individual animal — how long it takes to lose consciousness at slaughter, how much of its life it spends crowded or in bad water, whether its eyestalks get cut off — and is willing to treat the available behavioural and physiological evidence as good-enough warrant for acting, basically on a precautionary basis, without waiting for anyone to settle whether these animals consciously feel anything. The second thinks the unit of concern is cumulative suffering-weighted time per unit of food, that a standard is just one intervention among many and should be judged by how much suffering it averts per dollar, and is worried about three things: that stunning requirements add cost (say the pond-side stunner plus electricity and training adds a few percent to production cost) and push marginal buyers toward uncertified shrimp, toward chicken, or toward wild-caught fish with its own hours of suffering and its bycatch; that an ablation-free broodstock rule reduces post-larvae output per female (the hatchery people on our advisory panel insist it would be somewhere between a quarter and a half), so more broodstock animals are needed for the same tonnage; and that a "higher welfare" label may simply grow the market. They also both keep circling one thing I find genuinely confusing: shrimp are farmed in the hundreds of billions of animals a year, enormously more individuals per kilogram of protein than chickens or salmon, and the second person wants to multiply a modest credence in shrimp sentience by those astronomically large numbers while the first person thinks that move is exactly what's illegitimate and that we should instead just do the decent thing for the animals actually in front of us.

What I want from you is the joint statement they could both sign: a numbered section of agreed claims — including claims about the science (what the nociception and trade-off/avoidance evidence does and does not establish, why valence is hard to measure directly in animals that can't report, why chronic moderate states and brief intense states are hard to put on one scale), about what a standard can and can't reliably audit, and about the arithmetic of market effects — each phrased so neither would feel they'd sold out. Then a disagreements table, with columns for: the crux; the claim as the welfare-standards person would want it worded; the same claim as the suffering-per-kilogram person would want it worded; the concrete test, dataset, natural experiment, or hypothetical case both of them would accept as genuinely bearing on that crux; and what each of them predicts it will show, written down in advance. Please treat these as candidate cruxes but add better ones and drop any that are fake: whether the behavioural evidence supports precautionary treatment of decapods as sentience-candidates at all; whether long-duration moderate suffering (crowding, poor water quality, chronic fin and gill damage) or short-duration intense suffering (an unstunned kill, air exposure) should dominate; whether expected-value reasoning with small credences and astronomical numbers of animals is acceptable or is a form of fanaticism, and if so what the alternative weighting is (I've been toying with a "credence-discounted suffering-hour per kilogram of edible protein" index, which the first person hates — please engage with it rather than just picking a side); whether outcome-based requirements (e.g. a maximum time to verified insensibility, audited at the pond) are a better target than input mandates (a required device, a required density); and whether welfare-motivated cost increases in one sector reduce or increase total suffering once substitution and demand responses are counted. If any part of their dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive — people using "welfare," "suffering," or "sentience" to mean different things — say which part and why, in a short note after the table. Roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words is fine; density is good, filler is not.
