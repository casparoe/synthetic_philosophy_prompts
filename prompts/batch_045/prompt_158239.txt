Write a piece of in-universe official paperwork — a statutory circular or schedule of case rulings, about 700 words, headed with a form number — issued by a fictional body (say, an Office for Ontological Compliance) in a jurisdiction that has enacted Berkeley's master argument into law: to be is to be perceived, and an unperceived object is not merely unverifiable but impossible.

The circular should dispose of a series of test cases in deadpan civil-service prose: the tree in the park with nobody by, where a claimant's attempt to establish its independent existence by picturing it is ruled to be itself a perception and therefore no evidence at all; an unopened file, a locked safe, or footage nobody has watched, raising whether a recording device or a merely possible observer counts as a perceiver; an offence with no witnesses; and another person's unshared pain, which the Office must process without admitting it is in the same position as matter.

The jokes have to come from getting the argument and its weak points right rather than from inventing silly consequences: the conceiving-is-perceiving move that makes every attempted check self-certifying, the slide from our inability to form a mental image of an unperceived thing to its being impossible, the fact that the view then needs a universal perceiver to stop the world lapsing between observations (handle this as a budget line, a contracted provision, or an understaffed night shift), and the pressure toward other minds being exactly as unperceived as matter is supposed to be.

Include one quoted complaint from an exasperated materialist objector and one footnote in which the Office concedes, in passing, something it cannot concede. The document must never wink: no one in it says the view is absurd, and none of the objections should be named or explained — a reader who knows the master argument should recognise which real features of it are being sent up and find the satire fair, while a reader who does not should still enjoy the paperwork.
