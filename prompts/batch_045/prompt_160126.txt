I teach this material and I want to get clear about when the commons story is the right story, working from my own verdicts rather than from the literature.

An alpine village pasture used by eight farming families, each of whom can see roughly how many animals the others put up: I think the standard parable misdescribes this, that self-governed rules genuinely work here, and that privatising the strips would be a mistake. North Atlantic cod in the early 1990s: I think state-imposed catch limits were not merely permissible but required, and I do not believe the fleets could have arrived at them on their own. Antibiotic efficacy: I accept quite drastic restrictions on individual prescribing. Widely used open-source software that almost nobody pays for: I think leaving it alone is right, and schemes to make the maintainers' labour excludable strike me as solving nothing. The office fridge: no governance at all, and I think that is correct. Our departmental shared archive, which decays into unusable sludge: I do want someone given authority to delete and reorganise, and I cannot say why this differs from the fridge except in scale.

The pair that worries me: I accept coercive limits on fishers, but I resist personal carbon rationing, though the two look structurally identical — a finite sink, many users, effects invisible at the point of use. My tentative principle is that the remedy should track what I call the 'legibility lag': the interval between a unit of use and its detectable effect on the resource, set against the horizon over which the users can plausibly coordinate. Short lag, self-governance; long lag, external authority. I suspect that gets this pair wrong, and maybe for an unprincipled reason: fishing is an occupation one can exit, whereas emitting is woven into everything else.

Can the principle be repaired so it delivers my verdicts, or must one of them be given up? I also want its verdict on two cases I have not settled: a groundwater aquifer beneath a region of smallholders who have just acquired cheap solar pumps, and the pool of referees that a discipline draws on for peer review, supplied by a few and drawn down by everyone. And does the same apparatus handle the reverse case — fragmented rights over, say, gene sequences or urban land, where the resource lies unused because too many parties can each say no?
