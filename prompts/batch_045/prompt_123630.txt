hey — I'm a fourth-year philosophy PhD student, working on the second chapter of my diss (practical rationality / agency, with one foot in the behavioral econ lit), and I've been going back and fourth on the same knot for a couple weeks now. I'd really like someone to lay out the conceptual geography for me. Please skip the algebra — I can follow the equations, my actual problem is that I can't tell what they're a picture *of*.

The chapter asks whether hyperbolic discounting really explains weakness of will, or whether it describes a neighboring phenomenon and the literature has been sloppy about the slide between them. Three things specifically:

(1) Why exactly isn't preference reversal over time the same thing as acting against your own best judgment? At every moment the agent still picks her top option given her current preferences, so on the face of it the model says she *never* does what she judges worse — which looks like it dissolves the old Socratic puzzle rather than solving it. I want the dialectical options separated: is the traditional puzzle just misposed, is the model changing the subject, or do we have to say that the *planning-time* ranking is the one that counts as her judgment — and then owe an argument for why that time-slice gets to speak for the agent?

(2) Here are two cases I keep using on myself. First, reversal with no weakness: someone who plans to quit and never does, but who regards the planning self as the dupe and the evening self as the one who knows what matters — everything the model predicts, zero sense of failure. Second, apparent weakness with no reversal: someone with perfectly exponential discounting who judges the long-term option better, and still takes the short-term one because once the menu is actually in front of her the options present differently. Does the model have any resources at all for the phenomenology — the effort, the sense of doing it anyway? Relatedly: sophisticated agents precommit, and sometimes do the aversive thing *earlier* than planned, which reads like an excess of will rather than a deficit. So is the model really modeling failure of plan-maintenance, and calling it weakness?

(3) One diagnostic I've been toying with: add the same delay to both options, so neither one is available now. Under the (beta, delta) picture the present-bias term shouldn't kick in, and the agent should revert to the larger later option. If people mostly don't, what does that tell us — that the mechanism isn't present bias, or that hyperbolic discounting has become a bucket label for any departure from constant-rate discounting (magnitude effects, doubts about whether the later reward will actually arrive, and so on)?

Gut-level intuitions, a few paragraphs each, and please tell me if I'm conflating two questions that should be kept apart.
