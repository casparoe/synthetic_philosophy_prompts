I am drafting the allocation rule for a public body, and I want the specification settled before I look at how the philosophical literature answers it, so that I don't inherit that literature's blind spots about the use I have in mind.

The setting. The body distributes a fixed stock of goods and burdens once, to a known set of claimants, and cannot take anything back afterwards. It cannot observe the claimants' mental states and cannot ask them; it has records of what they have, what they can do, and what has been done to them. It must give every unsuccessful claimant a written statement of why their claim ranked where it did. And its allocations partly determine who the next round's claimants will be, because housing, training and care allocations affect which households form and which people arrive. I am not asking whether equality is the right aim for this body; that is settled by statute. I am asking what the currency of equality would have to be like for this body to be able to use it.

What I want is the requirements list: what any candidate currency — a candidate answer to the question of what, exactly, is to be equalized — must be able to do, with the case behind each requirement, and with each requirement marked as non-negotiable given my setting, negotiable, or a prejudice imported from some other use a theory of equality might be put to.

The cases I am confident a currency must handle, stated in my own terms:

Two claimants receive identical bundles, and one needs far more than the other to get to the same point — an impairment, a chronic condition, a caring responsibility. The currency must register the difference in what bundles do for people without simply collapsing into how satisfied those people report or appear to be.

Three claimants each end up wanting something very expensive. The first cultivated the taste deliberately, with full information and no pressure. The second acquired it because the body itself supplied the means in an earlier round — learned to sail on a publicly funded boat, say. The third no longer wants much of anything, and wanted progressively less across a long period of being given very little. The currency must say which of these generate claims, and in particular must tell me how the second case differs from the third, since both involve aims that the allocation itself helped produce.

What a claimant can do with a share depends on what others are given — a place in a programme, a priority date, a permit, a house on a particular street. So I need to know whether the currency has to be assessable on a pair of claimants in isolation, or whether it only has a verdict once the whole distribution is known; the body sometimes has to decide between two claimants before it knows the rest of the population.

Provision moves the baseline. Once lifts, ramps and interpreters are supplied, what counts as an ordinary share of access changes for everyone, including people whose access was never at issue. The currency must say whether its standard is anchored to a pre-allocation baseline or floats with what is actually provided, since a floating standard lets a body appear to equalize by redefining the target.

The written statement is itself part of the allocation. If the currency is a mental state, announcing a verdict perturbs what is being measured — being told one counts as badly off can make a person worse off. I want to know whether robustness to being announced is a genuine requirement or only a problem for one family of currencies.

And because allocations determine who the future claimants are, the currency must permit comparisons across alternatives whose claimant-sets are not the same, and must say what to conclude when the people who would be worst off under one alternative simply do not exist under the other.

Beyond the list, three things. Flag any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want, since it would mean my statute asks for something incoherent. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive candidates. And say which of the requirements are really about the currency and which properly belong to the procedure, the timings, or the unit of assessment — whether the currency attaches to individuals, households, or something else, and what it is to do with a household whose members will not say how unevenly the share is divided inside it.

Only then, and in a short paragraph each, how the three familiar families — currencies pitched at states of a person, at the means a person holds, at what a person is substantively free to do and be — do against the list. I do not need names, dates or reading.
