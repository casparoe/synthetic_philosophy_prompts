All three familiar answers to the question of what egalitarian justice should equalize — welfare, resources, capabilities — are normally treated as quantities that attach to persons one at a time: a distribution is assessed by comparing one person's quantity with another's, and the disagreement is only about which quantity to compare. I want an example that breaks that separability.

Give me a case, with named agents, in which what one person has is not fixed by her own internal states, endowments and circumstances alone, but depends on what others have or do — and in a way that is not already absorbed by the moves each view has available. So: not the price-mediated dependence that the auction version of resource equality already builds in; not a dependence that disappears once you take the person's preferences as given, of the sort the welfare view handles under headings like expensive tastes or envy; not the familiar disability case, where a bodily condition changes how efficiently means convert into functionings. I have in mind something like goods whose value depends on how many others exercise the same option, or options that exist only if others take them up too — but choose your own example and make it concrete.

Then say which of the three currencies handles your case best, and why. And say what the case shows about the site of equality: if no metric can be assigned person by person, does the three-way disagreement survive intact, or was it partly a disagreement about something else? If your example tempts you to reach for relational or democratic equality instead, say what that move buys that a distribution-level metric does not.
