I am a teaching assistant on a third-year course on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and I am assembling a seminar handout on the three stances — physical, design, intentional — and on the thought that a pattern counts as real when there is a description of the data that compresses it more than any description available at the physical level, so that the pattern is not merely in the eye of the beholder. My students have settled into a reflex I want to break: whichever stance compresses the data better is simply the right stance, and there the matter rests. A friend of mine who works in controls engineering holds the opposite reflex — that the intentional stance is nothing but debugging shorthand, useful for talking to colleagues and useless for settling anything — and I would like a case I can put to her as well as to the seminar. That is what the case is for: to pry apart 'right for predicting', 'right for fixing', and 'right for trusting', and to show that the compression criterion alone cannot deliver a verdict. So please design it to order.

The scenario: one system, one paragraph, something a competent engineer could actually build in a university lab this year — a closed-loop controller with sensors and actuators embedded in an environment is the shape I have in mind, a warehouse robot or a drone's landing controller or a greenhouse climate system, your pick. No consciousness, no qualia, no superintelligence, no perfect predictor, no adversary tampering with anything. The intentional attribution must be one a working engineer would in fact make and defend on the job, phrased in ordinary words — the system thinks the bay is empty, it is treating the sensor drift as a headwind — not a contrivance of a philosopher's, and not one that smuggles in a view about what it is like to be the system. Then I want: a single datable observable behaviour over the next few seconds about which the design stance and the intentional stance issue conflicting predictions, with each prediction stated and derived — the design-stance prediction must be reachable by someone who knows the mechanism and has never heard the intentional attribution, and the intentional-stance prediction must be the one the engineer on site would actually stake money on; the design-stance prediction is to be the one that comes true; and, crucially, a compression crossover, such that up to the critical moment the intentional-stance description has been the better compressor of this system's behaviour — cheaper to state, and generalising to contexts the design-stance description does not cover — so that the design stance wins only at the critical juncture and neither stance can claim the compression test outright. The divergence has to be generated by the case rather than stipulated, and it must not be manufactured by making the design-stance description secretly incomplete or the intentional attribution obviously gerrymandered or false for some independent reason; ideally the mechanism is working exactly as designed throughout, with no fault at all.

After the scenario, I want the design shown to work. Identify the single feature doing the work: my hunch is that it is a gap between the quantity the mechanism actually controls for and the quantity the designer intended it to control for — a proxy/target mismatch — rather than any malfunction, but if you think something else is carrying the load, say so and argue for it. Then show what happens to the case if that feature is removed (I expect the two stances to stop diverging entirely) and what happens if it is pushed to an extreme (I expect the intentional attribution to stop compressing at all and the interest of the case to evaporate), so that we can see the case sits at a genuine middle point rather than at a trivial end. Next, score the stances against the three different things 'the right stance' might mean: best for predicting this behaviour, best for repairing or reconfiguring the system, and best for deciding whether to trust or redeploy it in a new setting. My guess is that the design stance takes prediction and repair while the intentional stance takes trust and redeployment, but if your case splits them another way, say which way and why that is the more interesting split. Finally, give me the two lines of attack: what a committed instrumentalist about the intentional stance would say to blunt the case, and what someone who thinks a stance's warrant is inherited from the system's design history would say; and then tell me honestly how much of the surviving disagreement is verbal — a dispute about which question 'right stance' was asking — and how much is left over once that is set aside. Do not stipulate any of these verdicts; let them fall out of the set-up. Keep the scenario to a single paragraph, use no citations or reading suggestions of any kind (I know that literature well enough to supply my own), and keep the whole answer under nine hundred words. Please write in British English.
