I'm in a graduate seminar on rational choice — we've worked through Newcomb's problem, the medical Newcomb cases, the smoking lesion, and the one-shot prisoners' dilemma between psychological twins — and I've noticed that my gut verdicts on the "you can't causally affect it now" cases don't line up with any single view we've covered. I'd like help working out what I actually believe. Please act as a decision theorist who is equally fluent in causal, evidential, functional and updateless approaches, and who is willing to tell me that one of my verdicts is simply a mistake rather than bending a theory to fit it. Before you settle on an answer, please brainstorm two or three candidate principles and test each against the whole list of cases below rather than starting from a theory label and working forward; I've read the survey articles, so please don't spend the answer recapping the literature.

My verdicts. (1) Counterfactual mugging in its canonical form: a predictor I完全 trust — call it Omega — flips a fair coin out of my sight, would wire me $10,000 in the heads branch if it predicted that I would hand over $100 when asked in the tails branch, and now, the coin having come up tails, asks for the $100. I'd hand it over, but only weakly: I admire the person who pays and think they're doing the right thing, yet I can't bring myself to say that someone who refuses has reasoned badly. (2) Same setup, except that before the flip I could have hired a firm to confiscate $100 from me if the coin came up tails. I think hiring the firm is obvious, and part of me wants to infer from that I should also pay in (1) — though I gather some theories block exactly that inference. (3) Two versions of (1) that I feel exactly alike about, and I want a principle that also treats them alike: in one, the $10,000 is already sitting in my bank account, wired a week before I was asked; in the other, Omega still holds it and will transfer it only after I answer. (4) Omega's announcement is worthless: it always says the coin came up tails, it flips the coin in secret, and in the heads branch it wires the $10,000 first and asks for the $100 anyway; its prediction of me is still perfect. Here I'd pay, and with far more confidence than in (1) — which worries me, since the difference looks purely epistemic. (5) Accuracy: with a predictor that is 90% accurate and whose errors are noise, I'd still pay; at 60% I wouldn't; at 75% I genuinely have no verdict. (6) Mechanism, where I suspect I'm being inconsistent: if the predictor works by running a fine-grained simulation of me and asking the simulation, I'm confident I should pay; if it's an opaque black box that has simply never been wrong after studying my childhood, I'm noticeably less confident; if it made its prediction before I was born from a scan of my genome, I feel that paying would be silly even though the prediction is 100% accurate. (7) XOR blackmail: a letter arrives saying that a termite inspection was run on my house and the letter was sent iff exactly one of these holds — the house has termites and I won't pay $1,000, or the house is clear and I will pay. I'd refuse to pay, though I feel the pull of the thought that paying would be marvellous news; if the same correspondent will be writing to me every month for a year, refusing is obvious rather than merely right. (8) A moralised counterfactual mugging: instead of $100, Omega asks me to do something that costs a stranger a small real humiliation — say, posting a private poem of theirs that I know is bad — and says that in the heads branch it spared ten strangers an equivalent humiliation because it predicted I'd do it. I'm much more reluctant than in (1), and I can't tell whether that's a decision-theoretic difference or just ordinary morality overriding.

My own tentative principle is this: I ought to do whatever I would have wanted my decision procedure to be disposed to do, judged from the position I occupied before I knew which branch I was in, treating as a single decision every agent the predictor cannot tell apart. I think it handles (1), (2), (3) and (5) well, but I suspect it mishandles (6) — it seems to tell me to pay in the genome case, which I don't believe — and I can't see what it says about (8). Please repair it or replace it, and give me: the principle stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case; the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on two cases I haven't listed, namely a counterfactual mugging in which there are a thousand physical copies of me, Omega asks all of them and takes the money from one chosen at random, and a case in which Omega's prediction concerns not what I would do but what I would do after being given a pill that reliably makes people refuse; an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot save and which of them I should therefore drop; and a clear answer to whether the resulting view is just functional or timeless decision theory under another name, or something we haven't read.
