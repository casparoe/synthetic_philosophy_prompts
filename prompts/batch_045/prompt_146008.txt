I've been reading about causation by omission on my own for a couple of months now (no philosophy background, I just get hooked on things), and the case I keep coming back to is the SS Californian on the night the Titanic sank. Here are the facts as I want them stipulated, because I know a few are still argued about and I don't want the answer to turn into an argument about the history: the Californian was stopped in pack ice somewhere in the neighborhood of fifteen to twenty miles from where the Titanic went down; the Titanic's distress rockets were seen from the Californian's bridge and reported to Captain Lord, who was asleep below and was not woken; the Californian's only wireless operator had shut his set off and gone to bed roughly an hour before the distress calls went out, so they were never recieved; and — take this as given even though people still dispute it — the Californian could have worked through the ice in time to reach the lifeboats and pull people out. Also relevant, I think: there was no requirement or custom at the time for a cargo ship to keep a twenty-four-hour radio watch, and the operator was a Marconi company man whose actual job was mostly passenger telegrams. At the inquiries afterward, the failure to go to help was treated as causing deaths, and the rules about continuous watch and about answering a distress call were changed because of it.
