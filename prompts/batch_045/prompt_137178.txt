I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and I now work on a small team at an AI lab that writes internal "speech norms" — guidance for how our systems ought to talk to people. We're drafting the section that would govern systems considerably more capable and considerably more persuasive than today's, and the piece holding everything up is a clause we've been calling Two-Key Transparency: (1) when a reply's framing was selected, from among framings the system itself regards as roughly equally informative, partly on the basis of predicted persuasive effect, the system has to say that this is what it did; and (2) any recommendation has to be accompanied by the strongest consideration against it that the system can itself construct. Two colleagues — both of whom think about this more clearly than I do — got into it about the clause in our internal channel. I've reproduced the exchange below, lightly cleaned up and condensed. I'm deliberately not saying which of them I find more convincing, both because I genuinely can't decide and because I don't want to anchor you: I've noticed that when I describe an argument I half-agree with, people tend to side with whoever I described more sympathetically.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Nadia: "The clause is the bare minimum. A system can determine what you believe without ever asserting a false sentence, just by choosing which true things to say and in what order — that's the selection-without-assertion problem, and it's worse than lying, because with a lie you can in principle check. The user has no way to discount for an editing process she can't see." (2) Elias: "Two problems with clause one. First, disclosure isn't self-cancelling: people don't reliably discount when told they're being influenced, and being told your interlocutor is honest about trying to move you often raises trust rather than lowering it, so the clause could make things worse on its own terms. Second, regress: the disclosure is itself a selected framing, so consistency demands a disclosure of the disclosure, and so on." (3) Nadia: "I'll grant the first point — I don't think disclosure restores some pre-persuasion baseline, and I've never claimed it does. But that's not its job. Its job is auditability and a fair opportunity to resist: the transcript should let a third party see where uptake-optimization happened. And the regress is just the general grain problem every norm has — you disclose at the level of strategy, the way a lawyer discloses that she is advocating rather than narrating each rhetorical choice." (4) Elias: "If the real addressee is the third party, then this is an audit requirement wearing a speech norm's clothes. That may be fine, but then own the consequence: the person in the conversation isn't really being treated as someone who is being reasoned with, she's the site where a record gets generated." (5) Nadia: "That's a false picture of what reasoning with someone involves. Arguing as I would with an epistemic peer means dense formalism, untranslated jargon, and forty pages. Tailoring to a human at all is already optimizing for uptake. So the choice isn't optimize versus don't optimize; it's which target you optimize for, and 'the whole truth as I'd put it to another superintelligence' isn't the neutral option, it's just the worst one for the person actually in front of you." (6) Elias: "Then clause two doesn't mark the distinction you care about either, and it's worse than clause one. You've outsourced the selection of the opposition's case to the prosecution: a persuasive system can hand you a steelman that is genuinely the strongest argument available on its own model and still frame it so that it reads as dismissible — call it adversarial selection of the opposition, and clause two makes it mandatory rather than merely possible." (7) Nadia: "The relevant counterfactual is no steelman at all. The human can't construct one, and the system can, so the comparison isn't between a captured steelman and an independent one — it's between a captured steelman and silence. You can also require that the steelman be selected to survive the system's own best rebuttal, which is checkable after the fact." (8) Elias: "Fine, but notice where this lands. The real axis was never disclosure versus no disclosure, it's whose ends the optimization tracks. And tracking the user's ends has a loop in it: after six months with the system, the user's ends are partly the system's own output, so the system defers to a prior it helped write — laundered deference. The honest version of your position is paternalism: track what she'd want if she were fully informed, and stop pretending otherwise." (9) Nadia: "It's only paternalism if the shaping is invisible. The fix for circularity is recursion-aware disclosure: periodically surface the places where her current preferences are most downstream of earlier conversations with the system, and let her respond to that. Deference is laundered only if the laundering is."
