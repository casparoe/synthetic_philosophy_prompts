I'm in a small reading group that went through the old sea-battle case last week, and one member turned up at the next meeting with an argument he says closes the question against anyone who thinks future contingents are already true. I can't accept where it lands, but I also can't find the break. His setup is concrete: two fleets, an admiral who must decide before dawn whether to sail out and engage, and the proposition B — that there is a sea battle at dawn on the fourteenth (he insists we read B atemporally, so that its truth value, if it has one, is the same yesterday, today and forever). He also introduced a distinction I hadn't heard before and which he wants the argument to respect: recorded settledness, meaning B's truth value is already on the books whether or not anyone knows it, versus agential settledness, meaning whether a battle happens no longer depends on what anyone does from here on.

(1) Truth is timeless: if B is true at all, it is true at every time, so it was true yesterday that B (and if B is false, it was false yesterday).
(2) The past is fixed: nothing anyone can do now could make it the case that something which obtained yesterday did not obtain — whatever was the case yesterday is now out of everyone's hands.
(3) So B's having been true yesterday is now out of everyone's hands.
(4) A true proposition cannot also be false, so if it was true yesterday that B, then B cannot now fail to hold: B is recorded-settled, unavoidable in the sense that nothing will now prevent it.
(5) If nothing can now prevent B, then there is no action available to the admiral such that, were he to perform it, no battle would occur — for if there were, performing it would be a way of preventing B, contradicting (4). So whether he sails makes no difference; it is not up to him, and his deliberating is empty.
(6) Nothing in (1)–(5) mentions causation, laws of nature, determinism or foreknowledge; the fatalism is derived from truth, tense and the past alone.
(7) So for every proposition about what anyone will ever do: already recorded-settled, hence agentially settled, hence never up to anyone, so no deliberation, advice or blame is ever in order.
(8) That includes deliberation about whether to deliberate, and includes our reading group: if (7) holds, there is no reason to accept or reject the argument and no point in his having presented it.

He adds that the argument is indifferent to whether the future event is a coin toss or a human decision, which he calls a virtue; I think that indifference is a symptom, since no one is tempted to say a coin toss is up to the coin. I can't accept (7) or (8), and if forced I'd rather give up (1) than (2) or the claim that deliberation is sometimes in order — but I want to know what I'm paying. Lead with one sentence naming the step you reject and why it looked fine, then price the three ways out: denying present truth value to future contingents, keeping bivalence but restricting the fixity of the past to hard facts, and accepting that the outcome is recorded-settled while denying that being up to someone requires that things could have gone otherwise. Say in a sentence whether his recorded/agential distinction earns its keep at (5) or quietly assumes what it needs to prove. Under 500 words total.
