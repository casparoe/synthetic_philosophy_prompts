I'm writing a long essay — part memoir, part argument — about how a society should treat its successful members, and I've reached the point where I need someone to check whether the position I've assembled is actually a position. I grew up in Denmark, did a PhD and then fifteen years in the United States, and I've ended up holding a mixture that neither audience recognises as coherent: my American colleagues think I've absorbed a Nordic suspicion of individual distinction that I can't justify, my Danish family thinks I've gone soft on hero-worship. Before I publish, I'd like an audit in a specific sense: which of the following cannot genuinely be held together, which only look as though they conflict, and for each real conflict, exactly what would have to be given up and what that costs me elsewhere in the set. I'm not looking for a general lecture on envy or on meritocracy, and I have no settled view about which item I'd abandon — part of what I want to know is which items are load-bearing.

Here is the set, each stated as I would defend it:

(1) A norm or a policy is never justified by the bare fact that other people resent someone's having more; the fact that some have more than others is not itself a reason for anything. The only currencies I trust are harm to identifiable others and the position of the worst off.
(2) Even so, I think conspicuous consumption and self-advertising achievement are genuine, if minor, moral failings, and that social disapproval — ridicule, coldness, being talked about — is a fitting response. I mean the whole Nordic package: don't put yourself forward, don't fish for praise in an acceptance speech, don't use your title in ordinary life, don't tell people what you earn. My partner says this is just (1) with the sign reversed, and I don't think that's right, but I can't say why.
(3) I also think the public honouring of achievement is valuable and that a culture of enforced modesty gives up something real. I want medals, named chairs, buildings, and televised celebrations of founders, scientists and artists; I want us to be loud about people who do remarkable things, and I don't think praise has to be rationed.
(4) I don't think the successful morally deserve their success in any deep sense — who has the talents, the family and the decade that rewards them is mostly luck. I take this to be common ground and not worth arguing.
(5) I think the belief that effort and talent are reliably rewarded in one's own society is, where I live, largely false, but that a society functions better if its members broadly believe it, and that schools, media and civic ritual should encourage it. I'm not squeamish about the fact that this means encouraging something I take to be false.
(6) I think meritocratic rhetoric is corrosive, precisely because it teaches the successful to feel entitled and the unsuccessful to blame themselves. I've said this in print several times.
(7) I favour steep tax-and-transfer compression — much higher floors, much lower ceilings, small social distance between top and bottom — and I think that is a demand of justice, not merely a preference for equality.

Three case verdicts, so you can see how I apply it. A founder who posts photographs of his new yacht is being vulgar, and mockery is the right response. A founder who quietly funds malaria nets gets my gratitude and no complaint about the money itself. And a parent who spends an enormous sum placing her child in an elite school is doing nothing I want to criticise — I exempt that completely, and I don't regard it as the same kind of thing as the yacht, even though it is also a purchase of relative advantage in a competition that has losers.

What I want is the audit: go through the set and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, ordered by how much damage each does to the package as a whole; which apparent tensions dissolve once the right distinction is drawn; and, for the genuine ones, whether some distinction repairs them or whether I really have to surrender an item. Where something has to go, say what it costs me elsewhere in the set, and identify the single revision that would clean up the most. If any of my three case verdicts cannot be produced by the principles I've stated, name the verdict and the principle rather than quietly adjusting the principle to fit the verdict.
