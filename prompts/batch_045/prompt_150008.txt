I am finishing a seminar paper on causal selection in omission cases, and on one question in particular: whether the normative issue (was the agent under a duty to act?) and the structural issue (did the outcome depend on the omission?) can be pulled apart, or whether every putatively structural test is quietly borrowing a norm somewhere. I need one case that separates them, engineered to the following specification. Everything in the specification is meant to be satisfiable jointly; if you think some pair of requirements cannot be met together, say which and why before you build around it.

The scenario itself: one paragraph, realistic, contemporary, set in a hospital. No philosophers' props — no demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics. The only machinery in the story should be ordinary clinical equipment.

The dependence structure I need: two agents, A and B, each omit to perform an action; each omission is on its own sufficient for a bad outcome; the outcome occurs if and only if at least one of them omits; and both omit. So it is symmetric overdetermination with absences playing the role of the overdetermining events, which should make each omission fail the plain but-for test while both remain intuitively causal. Two further constraints on that structure.

(i) The redundancy has to be built into the hardware, not into a rule. If the reason both agents' actions are needed is a protocol, a convention, or a sign-off requirement, then a norm is doing the work and the case stops testing what I want it to test. A physical interlock — a device that will not operate unless two controls are held simultaneously — is the sort of thing I have in mind: that no single person can supply the drug or the power must be a fact about the machine, not about what anyone is permitted to do.

(ii) There must be no stepwise-dependence escape. The story should not contain any later event that counterfactually depends on the conjunction of the two omissions while depending on neither of them individually, so that no view can recover causation for the omissions by routing through an intermediate link.

The asymmetry I need: the two omissions must be structurally identical and differ in exactly one respect — A is under a duty to perform the action (assigned to the patient, on shift, the task is hers) and B is not (a nurse from another ward, off duty, in the room by accident, no role-based or institutional obligation). I do not want a Good Samaritan wrinkle; treat B's lack of a duty as a stipulation of the scenario, and be explicit that it is a stipulation rather than a discovery. Hold everything else fixed: both know what the control does, both know what is at stake, both are physically able to act, both are equally placed, and both omit for the same unglamorous reason (absorbed in conversation). No asymmetry in knowledge, ability, proximity, or timing — the omissions must be simultaneous, and the case must not quietly exploit an ordering — and no asymmetry in what the action would have achieved had it been performed.

The verdicts the case has to generate. Take the four views as I characterize them here, not as names to be looked up:

- Difference-making (DM): c causes e if and only if, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred, the counterfactual evaluated at the closest worlds with the rest of actual history held fixed so far as possible.
- Interventionism (IV): X causes Y if and only if some intervention setting X to a different value would change Y with the other variables in the model held fixed, and the dependence is invariant across a range of background settings.
- A production or process account (PA): causation requires an actual process between cause and effect — conserved-quantity exchange, or mark transmission — so absences are never causes.
- A norm-gated account (NG): an omission counts as a cause only if the agent was under a duty to perform the omitted action, or the action was the expected default in the circumstances.

Required: DM delivers no causation for either omission; IV delivers causation for both; PA delivers no causation for either, and for a reason that is not DM's reason; NG delivers causation for A's omission and not for B's. The pre-theoretic datum the case is meant to make vivid is that both omissions look equally causal to an untutored reader — the asymmetry in duty should not present itself as an asymmetry in how causal the two omissions seem.

Then the demonstration, in roughly this order:

1. Derive each verdict: the relevant counterfactual for A's omission and for B's, the relevant intervention for each, and why each view says what it says. Do not work the case for A only and assert the result for B.
2. Name the feature doing the work, and run two ablations. First, rewire the mechanism so that each agent's action is individually sufficient and the outcome occurs only if both omit: which verdicts flip? Second, remove the deontic asymmetry by giving both agents the same role: which verdicts flip? I want the ablations to show that dependence structure and deontic status are doing independent work.
3. Generalize: what does each view say for n agents with n at least 2, and does anything break at n = 1?
4. The profligacy check. A also omitted to call for help, to fetch another nurse, to press the control with her elbow, and indefinitely many other things. Say which of the four views over-generates here, why the case as designed makes the over-generation vivid, and what the cheapest repair is.
5. The redescription check. Both omissions can be redescribed as positive doings — A is chatting at the station, B is standing in the doorway. State which verdicts survive redescription and which depend on describing the cause as an absence, and say what that shows about each view.
6. Which of the four views comes out of the case worst, and what is that view's defender's strongest actual reply — the reply they would give, not one you have assigned them. If it is DM, note that the standard repair moves for symmetric overdetermination are on the table, and say why, given constraint (ii), they do not help here.
7. A closing paragraph on what the case establishes and what it does not. I would rather have a narrow scope claim I can defend in a footnote than a grand one.

What counts as cheating, since that fixes the brief: the verdicts have to fall out of the views as characterized together with the structure of the case. Stipulating the verdicts, sneaking in a second causal difference between A and B beyond the duty, letting the duty do causal work (A's action would have been more reliable, A was nearer the control), or making the outcome depend on one omission but not the other, all defeat the purpose. The point of the case is to isolate two variables and to show that they are independent of one another; a case that labels the results onto the outcome rather than generating them is no use to me.
