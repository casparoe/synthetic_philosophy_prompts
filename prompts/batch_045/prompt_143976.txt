---

Philosophers basically offer three answers to the question of what a probability is. The first, frequentism, says that a probability just is a frequency: to say the coin is fair is to say that in an infinitely long series of tosses, half of them land heads. A consequence that fans of the theory wear as a badge of honour is that single events have no probabilities at all — it is strictly meaningless to ask for the probability that this toss lands heads, or that it rains tomorrow, because nothing has a frequency until you have a whole series of it.

The subjectivists go to the opposite extreme. For them a probability is a number measuring how strongly you happen to believe something, and belief is a personal matter: your numbers are as good as mine as long as they hang together — no negative values, everything sums to one, and you are not vulnerable to a Dutch book, that is, a set of bets that loses you money whatever happens. This sounds like a licence to believe anything you like, but it isn't, because as the evidence piles up Bayes' rule forces everyone's numbers to converge, no matter how far apart they started.

Propensity theory splits the difference by making chance a physical thing. A fair coin has a real disposition, located in the coin and the flipping mechanism, to come up heads half the time: the frequency you would get if you tossed it forever, but a property of this single setup rather than of a series. Chances are then just one more physical quantity, like mass, to be measured by experiment, and the other two interpretations are really just stories about our ignorance of them.

The good news is that none of this touches the mathematics. All three camps accept the same axioms, and they all agree that your personal probabilities ought to line up with the chances when you know them — the so-called principal principle — so the whole dispute is metaphysical and makes no difference to anything anyone actually computes.

---
