I have been reading some historiography for a reason that has nothing to do with my job, and it has made me suspicious of a habit in my own field, so I want the two practices put side by side and interrogated.

Start with what I take to be standard tort doctrine — correct me if I have any of it wrong. Factual causation is normally tested counterfactually: the plaintiff must show that the injury would not have occurred *but for* the defendant's conduct. Everyone concedes that this test breaks in cases of symmetric overdetermination. The textbook hypothetical is two people who each negligently start a fire, either fire on its own sufficient to destroy the plaintiff's house, the fires join, the house burns. Each defendant can point out that the house was doomed either way; on a strict but-for test neither caused the loss and the plaintiff recovers nothing. Courts refuse that result. Many jurisdictions switch to a *substantial factor* formulation, or say outright that where there are several sufficient causes each is a factual cause of the entire harm, and hold both defendants liable for the whole loss — jointly and severally, where that doctrine still applies.

Here is the part that puzzles me. In the same kind of case, when it comes to dividing the bill, the jury is asked to assign each responsible party a percentage: sometimes described as a share of fault, sometimes explicitly as a share of causal contribution. And those percentages are supposed to sum to one hundred. So one and the same legal system treats a sufficient cause as causing all of the harm and as causing some fraction of it, depending on whether it is deciding liability or apportioning damages.

Now history. Professional historians almost never give a mono-causal explanation: a book on the origins of a war will list half a dozen conditions and say the outcome was *overdetermined*. Yet the same historians write narratives built around turning points, sort causes into underlying and immediate or primary and secondary, and cheerfully answer a contrastive question — why the rising happened in this city rather than that one — by naming one factor. Outside the profession the practice is cruder still: a plaque, a public holiday, a museum gallery, a school syllabus all single out one event or one decision as the cause. And in my own line of work, incident investigation, the report must name a *root cause*, with an explicit requirement that it be something the organisation can actually change.

What I want from you:

1. What theory of causation does the legal practice presuppose? Is the substantial-factor move a rival theory of the causal relation — causation as production or influence, something that survives redundancy — or is it the same counterfactual theory with a normative overlay about who should bear a loss? Which reading is forced by the doctrine, and which is merely consistent with it? Can a single theory really underwrite both full liability for each sufficient cause and percentage shares that sum to one hundred, or does the apportionment stage quietly presuppose that causation comes in degrees while the liability stage presupposes that it is all-or-nothing and non-rival?

2. What does historical practice presuppose? Please distinguish at least these four things a historian might mean by *overdetermined*: (i) each named condition was sufficient on its own, given the rest of the background; (ii) there were several independent sufficient routes to the outcome; (iii) the evidence does not tell us which condition did the work; (iv) the outcome was robust — it would still have occurred under most small perturbations of the circumstances. Which is the practice genuinely committed to? Which, if adopted explicitly, would make some other part of the practice incoherent? And what would have to change in historical writing under each reading? In particular: what could *primary cause* mean if the outcome really was overdetermined — temporal proximity, statistical surprise relative to what was expected in the period, salience to whatever contrast the historian is drawing, suitability as a target for intervention, something else — and how much of that list is a causal notion rather than a rhetorical or pragmatic one?

3. Here is a rule I have been toying with for dividing causal credit, and I want it stress-tested. Take the conditions that actually obtained. For each possible ordering of them, walk through the ordering and ask, at each step, whether adding that condition to the ones already present flips the outcome from no to yes; score it one when it does, zero when it does not. Then average each condition's score over all the orderings. In the two-fire case this gives each fire-starter exactly half the outcome, and it always hands out shares that sum to one. Please tell me two things about it. First, it also gives half to the back-up in a case of pre-emption — the second fire that never reached the house because the first one got there first — where the law says the back-up is not a cause at all. Is that a defect in my rule, or a reason to doubt that the symmetric and pre-emptive cases really differ in the way the law assumes? Second, could the rule reconstruct the liability stage at all, given that it can never give two sufficient causes a full share each? What would a historian have to give up to adopt something like it?

4. Is the real difference between the law and history not about causation at all, but about what the attribution is *for* — compensation, blame, control, narrative interest? Identify where each practice is underdetermined by any metaphysics of causation: the places where two or three incompatible theories of causation would license exactly the same verdicts, so that nothing in the practice settles which is right. And say what would have to be added to, or changed in, each practice before the metaphysics started to bite. My own suspicion is that the deepest disagreement is about whether causation is a fixed quantity to be divided, like a budget, or a status that each sufficient cause has fully and separately; if that framing is wrong or misses a third option, say so.

One last thing: I would rather have a map of where the disagreement genuinely lies than a verdict I have no way of checking. So at the end of each numbered section, tell me how confident you are in what you have just claimed, and at the end of the whole answer single out the one claim you are least sure of and say what would settle it.
