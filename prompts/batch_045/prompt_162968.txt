I've got a forty-minute slot with the volunteer guides at a contemporary art gallery — bright people, no philosophy, and their actual job is telling visitors what to think about paintings. I need them to get why there's something specially awkward about taking an aesthetic judgement on someone else's word: why testimony seems fine for what a work is like but not for whether it's any good.

Don't explain it to me, plan the session. What has to land before anything else does; which one example should carry the whole thing (and why the obvious one — a mate telling you a film's brilliant — is the wrong thing to build on); the misconceptions they'll arrive with, in the order they'll arrive, and where in the sequence to head each off.

Two things I want built in rather than bolted on. They'll point out, rightly, that they defer to experts constantly — mechanics, doctors, restaurant critics — so I need a moment where the disanalogy actually bites instead of just being asserted. And one of them will definately produce the case of a critic whose verdicts have matched their own first-hand judgement on every one of the last five hundred works, so tell me where that goes. I'd rather they left able to handle the welded-together cases — 'the ending's clumsy', 'the translation's leaden', where describing and evaluating look like the same act — than with a verdict on whether testimony ever works.

What should I flatly refuse to get into in forty minutes, and what one question at the end tells me the idea landed rather than that they've just been told expertise has limits?
