Hey — I'm a backend engineer (mostly Go and some Python), no philosophy training beyond a bunch of vipassana retreats and way too many dharma podcasts, and I'm trying to write down what I actually believe about no-self so I can stop going around in circles about it in my head. I want someone to check whether it hangs together, and I genuinely don't know where the weak joint is. Here's the package, roughly as I'd defend it over a beer:

1. There is no self. "I" is a convenient shorthand for a causally coupled stream of physical and mental events — the aggregates, as I understand the teaching — and there's nothing over and above the stream that has the experiences or owns the actions.
2. That doesn't make prudential concern irrational. I'm allowed to care more about what happens to me next year than about a stranger, because that future stream is causally and psychologically continuous with this one in a way the stranger's isn't.
3. I also hold that the boundary between "mine" and "not mine" is something the mind constructs, and that this is a big part of why the view is compelling to me in the first place. I take it to count in favor of being far more impartial than I actually am.
4. I reject rebirth and karma running across lives; I read that part as cultural scaffolding. But I still want to say that dying without having made real progress on the path is a genuine misfortune for the person who dies, and that's a big part of why I keep practicing.
5. At the same time I think death isn't bad for the person who dies — no subject, no one harmed, nothing to be afraid of — and that's the bit I actually find comforting.
6. I think it's right that I still feel guilty about something I did twenty years ago, and right that I apologized for it last year, even though by my own lights the person who did it doesn't exist anymore.
7. I think sitting practice gives me real first-person evidence here — you can watch thoughts assemble themselves with no thinker behind them — and I also think no-self is a claim about how things are, not just a report on what meditation feels like.

Two ground rules. First, don't give me any citations or reading suggestions: I can't follow them up and I'd rather you just argue the thing out in your own words. Second, if you're not sure whether some piece of this matches what the tradition actually teaches, say that out loud instead of smoothing it over — I'd rather know where you're shaky than get a confident-sounding answer. And if one of these commitments is just muddled, or the whole question is ill-posed, tell me straight rather than being polite about it.

Please answer in American English. Format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no sign-off. Rank them by how serious the problem is, worst first, and for each one say which of my commitments would have to give and what that costs me. Put a rough confidence on each verdict too. The last item should be whichever apparent clash in the list isn't really a clash, why it dissolves, and — if there is one — the single revision that would clean up the most of the rest for the least cost.
