I've been going to a reading group on aesthetics — mostly grad students plus one obsessive record collector who has Opinions — and I've drifted into a position I can't shake loose: that you can't come to *know* that something is aesthetically good merely from being told that it is. If a critic I trust tells me a film is devastating, I can come to know that he judges it so, and maybe that most people with a sensibility like mine will judge it so, but not that it *is* devastating — not until I've sat through it myself. Call it the pessimist view. I've been defending it for months and I've started to suspect I'm just getting good at defending it, so: what would count against it? Not the obvious stuff, and please don't give me a reading list, I can find those myself.

Here's where I actually feel the pressure, so use these as your targets. (1) The calibrated critic: someone whose verdicts I've tracked across two hundred films and who matches me almost perfectly; she's seen the new one, I haven't. If knowledge won't transfer there, why does it transfer anywhere? (2) Degraded acquaintance: I *have* heard the symphony, but once, on phone speakers, in a bad mood. Is whatever I now have knowledge from experience, knowledge from her testimony, or neither? (3) Unreachable works: an unrecorded improvised set from 1974, or a smell-based piece when I'm anosmic. Pessimism seems to entail that nobody alive, or nobody like me, can know it was good — is that a cost or a feature? (4) The one that's really bothering me: a recommender system that has never had an experience in its life but whose track record on my taste beats every human friend I have. My friend says if I'd take the word of a human critic with that record, what principled reason do I have to refuse this one? I don't have one, and I think answering it forces me to say what the magic ingredient is supposed to *be* — first-hand acquaintance with the work, a shared sensibility, or bare reliability — since those come apart in this case. Throw in jokes and wine while you're at it, since we defer about those constantly.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be straight about which ones a committed pessimist should concede count against them rather than which ones only critics think count. Two more things: I want to know wether the view has been qualified into something unfalsifiable — "you can't know, but you can truly believe, and you can rationally act on it, and really this is just a special case of a general thesis about experiential knowledge" — and, if it has, whether that's a problem for a claim of this kind or just what maturity looks like for a philosophical thesis. And when you answer, seperate what people who actually work on this treat as settled from what's genuinely contested; I'd rather you flag your own take as your own than smooth a live dispute into a consensus.
