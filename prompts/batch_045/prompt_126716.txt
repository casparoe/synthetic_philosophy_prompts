I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on happiness and the good life this term, and I've gotten pulled into an argument I can't settle, about a decision my older brother is actually trying to make in the next couple of weeks.

My brother is 28, healthy, and has been offered a place in a two-year paid study run by a neurotechnology company; the system is called Chrysalis. Participants spend most of each day in a pod while the system generates a continuous, internally coherent stream of experience — a whole life, with work, friendships, arguments, failures, losses — that is indistinguishable in the moment from ordinary waking life. It pays well enough that he could clear his student loans and cover our mother's rent for a few years, which is why he's taking it seriously, and he's described it to me in a way that seems designed to answer every objection I raise. He wouldn't be deceived in the ordinary sense: he knows he's in the pod, the company says they can't suppress that knowledge without destabilizing the simulation, and he can end any session with a deliberate act of refusal the system reads off his motor cortex, keeping everything he went through as memory afterward. He wouldn't be passive or cut off from the world either: what he does in there is mapped onto a robot that does real, useful work, so his choices have real effects. And the people he meets aren't sycophants — they're generated to be as demanding and unpredictable as actual people, so the projects he'd take up involve genuine effort and real failure.

What actually unsettles both of us is something else, and neither of us can put a name on it. His girlfriend says that even granting all of that, something is missing: whatever he does in there, it isn't really him doing it, and the people he comes to love aren't really there. He replies that he'd still be himself — same memories, same dispositions, same capacity to care — and that the people he'd come to care about would matter to him exactly as much as anyone does now. The detail I keep circling back to is this: the company reports that almost everyone, within a few weeks, switches on an optional setting that removes the standing awareness that one is in the pod, and that from then on participants report the highest sustained well-being the company has ever recorded. So the person who would decide to stop knowing is a later version of him, choosing with more information than he has right now, and it feels at once paternalistic and strangely humble to let present him overrule that.

In seminar we've been sorting accounts of a good life into a few rough families — that what makes a life good is how it feels from the inside, that it's a matter of getting what you actually want, and that there are things like real relationships, real accomplishment, and genuine contact with reality that are good for a person whether or not they make life feel better or satisfy any desire — and I notice that each family seems to hand down a different verdict on his case, with nothing yet said about how to choose between them. My professor also keeps hinting that some of what's bothering us may not be about what's good for him at all, but about something else — what a person is, or what matters as opposed to what benefits someone — and I can't tell whether that's a real distinction here or a way of dodging the question.

He asked me point-blank whether there is anything he would really be losing, or whether his hesitation is just an unreflective distaste for new technology, and I owe him an actual answer by the weekend, not a shrug. Please reply as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion: say whether his discomfort tracks something real, what exactly the loss would be if so, and which way of thinking about a good life makes sense of it; and if you think the disagreement between those accounts can't be settled, or that this case doesn't actually turn on what's good for him, say that instead and defend it.
