Not a philosopher, but I got dragged into an argument about Leibniz and the "best of all possible worlds" thing at a dinner party and I can't let it go.

Here's the pattern I keep running into. Someone claims the world we've got is the best one that could have been picked. Someone else says: no it isn't, because for any world you hand me I can describe a better one — the same world plus one more flourishing person, or minus one case of childhood leukemia, or with two more centuries of peace. And there's no ceiling, so there's no top of the pile. Fine, that part is obvious.

What I keep noticing is what happens next. The defender usually doesn't deny the no-ceiling point; they grant it and then use it. They say something like: right, so a chooser who was strictly maximizing would be paralysed — whatever it created, there was something better it could have created instead, so no act of creating is ever the best available one, which is absurd; therefore the chooser must not have been maximizing, it must have been satisficing or working with a threshold. And every time I watch that move I want to interrupt: but the word "best" in the original claim has now quietly stopped doing any work. If the pick wasn't made by maximizing, then calling the result the best possible world just means it cleared some bar, and the thesis has collapsed into something nobody would have fought about at dinner.

It also seems to be the same shape in totally unrelated arguments. My friend says it's just the "there's no largest integer" complaint in costume. I've seen a version used against maximizing utilitarianism: there's always more good you could have done, so on that rule everyone is always acting wrongly. Someone else told me it's why "the best career" or "the best move" can fail to exist even when the options are perfectly comparable.

So: is there an established name for this specific thing? I don't mean the bare no-maximum observation, I mean the two-step where a no-maximum argument first breaks maximizing and then the patch for maximizing quietly eats the original claim. Does the Leibniz literature have a name for it, and does decision theory have a different one for the same move? If it only has neighbours — satisficing, supererogation, the paradox of hedonism, dominance reasoning, whatever else — then tell me what each one is, exactly how my thing differs from it, and which fits best. Don't just hand me a label, I want the carving, including the edges: does the argument need the improvements to be unbounded, or does a dense ordering with no top do the same damage? What if there are several mutually incomparable best worlds rather than none? And does it still go through if value is only partially ordered?
