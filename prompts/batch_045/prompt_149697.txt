I sit on the standards committee of a large AI evaluation body, and I'm trying to write down what we already implicitly believe. I'd like you to reconstruct the theory of understanding that our certification pipeline presupposes, and tell me whether the parts hang together.

The pipeline as we actually run it (this is our own internal practice, not any published standard; nothing here needs checking against the literature):

1. Robustness gate. A system is credited with understanding a concept only if it answers correctly across a battery of paraphrases, reorderings, and surface perturbations of the same problem. One correct answer proves nothing.
2. Wrong-reasons rule. A correct answer does not count if the step-by-step reasoning the system produced beforehand does not contain the consideration we take to be operative. Right answer, wrong reasons is scored as failure; and a system that answers directly with no visible reasoning is scored below one that reasons aloud, even when both are correct.
3. Prompt-sensitivity discount. When a system fails a paraphrase but succeeds on a near-identical rewording of it, we attribute the failure to the prompt rather than to the system, and we do not withdraw credit.
4. Audience discount. When a system produces good reasoning only if it has been told in advance that its reasoning will be graded, we do not credit it. Reasoning produced for the record is not treated as the reasoning that did the work.
5. The distillation case. A smaller system fine-tuned on a larger one's reasoning traces passes 1 and 2 about as often as the larger system does. The committee is split on whether to certify it.

What puzzles me is that 1 and 3 pull in opposite directions: we make surface variation the test of understanding, and then we excuse failure under surface variation as an artifact of the prompt. And 2 and 4 seem to presuppose that there is a fact of the matter about which considerations produced an answer as opposed to which were merely stated next to it — but in these systems the stated considerations are literally tokens in the causal chain leading to the answer, so I cannot see what the difference between guiding and merely accompanying is supposed to consist in.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory. What must we be committed to for 1 through 4 to be more than arbitrary? Does the package require only that understanding be a counterfactual-supporting competence, or does it additionally require the right causal or explanatory history? Is the asymmetry between 1 and 3 defensible, or is it special pleading? Does 4 smuggle in the requirement, familiar from debates about acting for a reason, that the operative reasons guide the performance rather than merely being available to an audience — and if so, does that requirement survive the fact that the reasoning is part of the system's input by the time the answer is produced?

Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license it. Then say what we would concretely have to start or stop doing if we adopted a straight behavioral test, on which robustness is all there is, or a strictly mechanistic one, on which only internal causal structure counts and the trace is irrelevant in both directions. Finally, tell me how case 5 should be decided, and whether the committee's split is a genuine philosophical disagreement or a confusion.

Answer in continuous American English prose, with no headings, in at most 600 words.
