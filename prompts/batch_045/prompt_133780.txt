Me and my friend got into a massive argument on sunday evening and I'm still irritated about it, so I want to build something for our reading group to settle it, or at least to show us where exactly we're disagreeing.

The setup: two guys both drive home drunk, both weave across the centre line a bit, same blood alcohol, same everything. One of them hits a kid on a bike and kills him. The other one gets home fine. I said they're equally bad and equally to blame. My friend said of course not — the one who killed the kid is worse, deserves more blame, and has more to answer for. Two hours later we hadn't moved an inch, and I think it's because we kept silently switching between different questions: is this about the person or the act, is it about what should happen to him or about how he should feel, is it about blame or about praise.

Half the group has read Nagel and Williams on this (the two papers with the same title) and the rest are just smart people who'll show up with wine, so assume the readers know roughly what the debate is but can't be asked to self-report a theory. What I want from you is a decision tree, about eight questions, that lands someone on a named position.

Ground rules, because these are the bits that would make it useless:

- No question may use the words 'luck', 'moral', 'control', 'desert', or 'responsibility', and none of them may ask whether someone accepts the thing in the abstract. Every question is a concrete case with two or three answers, written so a clever 20-year-old who's never thought about any of this could answer it and actually mean something by it. What you're sorting on is what people say about cases, not what they call themselves.
- Every branch has to be labelled with the claim that does the splitting, so I can see the real cut — assessment of acts vs. of persons vs. of what we should do, whether blame and praise can come apart, whether an assessment has to be one the agent could have predicted, and so on. Each leaf gets a short name and one sentence on the most awkward thing it commits you to.
- Build it out of the logical space rather than out of a list of views: give me the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then place the positions people actually hold into that space. Tell me about the empty combinations — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody has got round to it?
- Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate. I'd much rather know than have it smoothed over.
- These four cases have to appear somewhere in the tree: the two drunk drivers; the careful lorry driver who kills a child he could not possibly have seen; the man who abandons his family to go paint and whose work turns out to be great versus his twin who does exactly the same thing and turns out mediocre; and someone who votes for a policy that ends up causing a famine versus someone who votes the same way and nothing bad happens.
- Don't attach names or citations to leaves. If you're not confident who holds a view, call it 'the view that X' and move on. I don't want a literature survey and I don't want anyone guessing at references.

One more thing, and it's the part I care about most, because it's where I think my friend was cheating. We came up with a distinction mid-argument that I want in the tree. Call it input luck versus output luck. Output luck is luck in how things turn out after you've acted — whether the kid was on the road. Input luck is luck in what turns up while you're deciding: which considerations occur to you before you turn the key, what you happen to remember, what mood you're in when you weigh them. My claim is that anyone who refuses to let output luck affect blame is still stuck with input luck, because what it occurred to the drunk driver to think about was just as much a matter of chance, and you can't scrub that out without giving up on assessing agency altogether. So: give me at least one branch that turns on that cut, and a leaf or an empty slot for the person who swallows output luck but not input luck, and another for the reverse. And if you think the distinction is phoney or just collapses back into one of the standard four kinds, say so in a note instead of quietly dropping it.

Epistemic bit, since I don't want to wave this around at the group as if the map were agreed: for each branch, tell us whether the distinction it turns on is something nearly everyone who works on the topic accepts, or whether it's one of the genuinely fought-over points. Say explicitly where the tree is imposing a tidy structure on a mess, and where you personally had to make a call that a specialist would dispute. And please don't let it come out looking like there's an established answer on whether the two drunk drivers are equally blameworthy — if there is, my friend and I wasted a whole sunday.
