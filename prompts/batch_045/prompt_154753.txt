I'm a journalist working on a long feature on whether the laws of nature really govern anything, and I'm writing to you because I would rather be corrected now than in the correspondence column afterwards. I don't need citations or a reading list — I'll do that legwork myself. What I need is to know whether the frame I have built the piece around is sound, or whether it quietly assumes the thing at issue.

Here is my current map of the dispute. On the Humean side: you take the complete history of the world, every particular matter of fact, the mosaic; the laws are whatever regularities the most economical true summary of that history would have to state — the best-system account, as I have seen it summarised. Nothing pushes anything around. A law is the shape of what happens, not a producer of it. On the anti-Humean side: over and above the mosaic there are relations of necessitation, or powers, or primitive nomic facts, and the mosaic has the shape it has because of them.

Two consequences seem to me to follow from the Humean picture, and both strike me as close to fatal, which is why I want them checked. First, if a law is only the shape of what in fact happens, then Humeanism is a species of antirealism about laws — the metaphysical cousin of instrumentalism about electrons. The Humean keeps the word and surrenders the thing. Second, and more damaging: on that picture the laws of nature hold by accident. The mosaic contains what it contains and nothing in it rules anything out, so the statement that no signal propagates faster than light and the statement that every coin in my pocket is dated after 1990 would differ in scope but not in kind — one is simply a larger accident than the other.

The physicists I have interviewed put a sharper version of the same worry, and it is the one I find hardest to shake. They say that the laws we actually have are dynamical: they are given as evolution equations that take the state of a system at one time and generate the state at the next, and that is why they underwrite intervention and explanation. A summary of a finished film does not project the next frame; a dynamical law does. So the Humean, on this telling, is not offering a rival metaphysics of laws but describing the film instead of the projector.

My own attempt at an organising idea, which I have not seen anyone state quite this way: I have been sorting the work that laws are wanted to do into five jobs — licensing induction from observed to unobserved; being the thing you cite in answer to a why-question; supporting counterfactuals and telling you what you could bring about; ruling some histories out as impossible; and grounding objective chance, which our best physics seems to assign. My thesis was going to be that governing is simply the name for the fourth job, that the Humean gets the first, second, third and fifth for free from the structure of the best system, and that the entire debate therefore reduces to whether the mosaic needs a separate modal job done to it.

Am I right that the Humean is committed to laws holding by accident? Is the five-job distinction real, or have I manufactured it by slicing one thing five ways? And if the dispute really does come down to the fourth job, is it in the end verbal — a quarrel about whether the word govern earns its keep — or is there something a working physicist would notice if the Humean turned out to be right?
