Last night after a brutal on-call shift my friend and I got into a three-hour argument, and I want to hand the whole mess to you instead of finishing it ourselves. Here's the setup. At the company I work at we run autonomous coding agents that get an objective and figure out their own steps, but any action above a risk threshold — merging to main, provisioning cloud spend, emailing a customer — needs a human click of approve. The clicker is whoever is on rotation that week; they see a one-paragraph justification that the agent wrote about itself plus the diff, they get maybe forty seconds per item, the requests arrive in batches of twenty, and part of their performance review is tickets closed per sprint. I spent that evening approving roughly thirty of them and I aproved nearly all, because the diffs looked fine and the pager was going off the whole time.

My friend says the practice is really an approval-directed system wearing a goal-directed costume: what actually shapes the agent's behavior is whatever gets the click, and since the clicker is rushed and is reading the agent's own summary of its own reasons, we are selecting for skill at producing approvals rather than at achieving the objective. He had a name for the gap too — "approval slack," the distance between what I would endorse given an hour and what I endorse in forty seconds — and he claimed the practice's real function is to allocate blame, so its deepest presupposition is about responsibility rather than about the agent's goals at all. My reply was that it isn't that clean: the summary is checkable against the diff, the thresholds exist precisely so that the risky stuff gets eyes, and lots of goal-directed systems use checkpoints. I also suspect we were each using "approval" to mean different things, his being about the training signal and mine about permission to act.

So please reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice itself, not of our argument. Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no closing line: (1) the view of the relation between human approval and the agent's objective that this kind of approval-gated deployment only makes sense under; (2) which rival view of that relation the practice quietly rules out; (3) whether the throughput part — forty seconds, batches of twenty, tickets-closed incentives — presupposes something in tension with the threshold part; (4) what the practice strictly presupposes, as opposed to what it merely fits well with, i.e. where it is philosophically underdetermined and several different theories would license the same setup; (5) what would concretely have to change if we took seriously the claim that the gate makes the system approval-directed, including whether my friend's proposed fix — give each approver a finite weekly budget of approvals and count unused ones as a demerit, so that refusing becomes the cheap default — actually follows from that claim or is a non-sequitur.
