I am writing a general reference entry on the boundary between legend and history, and every treatment of the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus that I read turns on criteria that nobody has stated. So before I apply any such criterion to a case, I want the specification.

Your task: set out the desiderata that a criterion for classifying a traditional narrative — or a part of one — as historical rather than legendary must satisfy, given a fixed purpose. The purpose is this: the criterion is to determine what a secular reference work may assert, hedge, or decline to assert, for an adult readership with no specialist training. Assume the criterion will be applied to narrative traditions generally, including traditions that the person applying it belongs to and has a stake in.

For each desideratum: state it, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as either non-negotiable or a preference of one temperament or camp. Use a numbered list for the requirements; a table is acceptable for the closing scorecard only.

Start by auditing the raw material below. These are the constraints I keep encountering in practice. Decide which are genuine desiderata, which are disguised restatements of one another, and which are merely the reflexes of a suspicious or a trusting temperament:

- chronological proximity of the earliest surviving source to the events described;
- numerical independence of attestations — including what "independent" can mean when later sources copy earlier ones and when a shared oral tradition is not a common source in the usual sense;
- agreement with what excavation and inscriptions establish independently of the narrative;
- the criterion of embarrassment: that a tradition would not invent material inconvenient to itself;
- absence of any discernible motive for invention;
- generic plausibility, i.e. that the events are the sort of thing that happens;
- the inference from real place names, real offices, and real objects embedded in the story to the reality of what is said about them.

Then I want the list to rule out, explicitly, three failure modes. Treat each as a test a candidate criterion must be shown to pass, and say what it costs to pass it:

1. Transmission selection. The features that make a story worth retelling — drama, a moral point, a memorable reversal, the miraculous — are the same features that invite elaboration in the retelling. A criterion that reads wide circulation, persistence, or vivid detail as evidence of fidelity has the sign wrong. State the requirement that handles this, and say how a criterion is supposed to estimate the distortion rate of a tradition without already knowing what the tradition distorts.
2. Absorbed detail. A narrative can take up genuine topography, genuine institutions, and genuine names without any of them corroborating the events it narrates. Require the criterion to distinguish detail that corroborates from detail that was merely available to be absorbed, and to say how the two are told apart in advance of knowing the answer.
3. Circular cross-validation. Excavators identify a site using the narrative; the identified site is then cited as confirming the narrative; inscriptions are read through the story and the story through the inscriptions. Require the criterion to fix an order of inference that blocks the loop, or to explain why the loop is benign and how much independent anchoring is needed to make it so.

Consider also placing, merging, or rejecting the following:

- Decomposition: the criterion must grade parts of a narrative rather than return one verdict on "the Trojan War" or "the Exodus," since a war fought at a real place, the elopement that supposedly caused it, and the gods fighting on the plain are different claims answerable to different evidence.
- The existence/accuracy split for named individuals, so that the question whether an Arthur or a Moses existed is not settled by settling whether anything in the narrative happened to him, and not settled the other way either.
- An explicit expected-record assumption: silence counts against a tradition only given some view of how much documentation a society of that period and kind should have left. The criterion must state that view rather than leave it implicit.
- Recalibration against controls: the criterion should be run on cases whose answer is independently settled, and a criterion that would refuse to certify a well-documented recent event, or that would fail to exclude a narrative we are confident was invented, is defective in a way that no amount of internal elegance repairs.

The structural questions matter to me more than any verdict on a case:

- Name one pair of desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and say what a criterion must give up. The pair I have in mind is recalibration against known cases versus the demand that a criterion not be tuned to return the answer its author antecedently favors — but argue for whichever pair you find worst, including that one if you agree.
- Identify the single desideratum that excludes the most candidate criteria, and explain why it does more exclusionary work than the others.
- Say how the list changes if the purpose shifts. Consider a court adjudicating a territorial or customary claim, and a religious community asking whether its constitutive narrative is true. Which requirements become non-negotiable, which drop out, and does any requirement flip its sign?

Close with a short paragraph or a compact table scoring three broad stances against the list, and nothing more than that: (i) an austere evidentialism that places the burden of proof on the tradition and withholds assent absent near-contemporary attestation; (ii) a generous default that treats a stable, widely held tradition as innocent until positively disconfirmed; (iii) a memory-first approach that takes the object of study to be what a community preserved and transformed rather than what occurred, and doubts that the occurrence question is answerable at all.

Write in American English. Target 900 to 1,200 words, with the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring. Do not name or cite scholars, works, or excavations; I want the argument, not the literature.
