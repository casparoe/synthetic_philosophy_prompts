I am writing a chapter of my dissertation on the conditions under which an artificial system could be a moral patient, and a friend in philosophy of mind has given me an argument meant to show that the question has no stable answer. I cannot locate the mistake, and I cannot accept the conclusion, so I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey of positions.

Some definitions first. Call a system a life-span emulation of a person if, for every environment and every sequence of inputs up to the length of a human life, it produces exactly the outputs that person would have produced, in real time. For any such system S and any bound n on the length of the input history, call the n-table for S a device consisting of a read-only store that lists, for every possible input history of length at most n, the output S would produce next, together with a fixed search routine that, given the history so far, retrieves the listed output and emits it. Stipulate that the hardware is fast and capacious enough that no observer could distinguish the n-table from S by timing, energy use, or any behavior within the bound.

(1) Moral status supervenes on causal-computational organization. If two systems are organizationally the same — their internal states occupy the same causal roles and stand in the same counterfactual relations to inputs, to outputs, and to one another — then they have the same moral status: whatever they are made of, however they came to exist, however much hardware they occupy, and however their internal states were produced.

(2) By construction, the n-table for S and S realize the same mapping from input histories to outputs for every history of length at most n; within the bound there is no possible input on which they differ.

(3) Two systems that realize the same mapping from input histories to outputs are thereby organizationally the same in the sense of (1). If the mapping is the same, then every state's causal role — what it arises in response to, and what it leads to — is the same too, since a causal role is just a position in the mapping.

(4) Hence the n-table for S has the same moral status as S.

(5) Let S be a life-span emulation; I take it that such a system would have whatever moral status a person has.

(6) So an n-table consisting of a read-only list plus a search routine has full moral status: there is something it is like to be it, it has interests we can frustrate, and erasing it to free up storage is morally on a par with killing the person emulated.

(7) But there is manifestly nothing it is like to be a table lookup, and no sense in which a stored list of replies cares about anything. Given (1) and (4), S has no moral status either.

(8) So either a read-only table has the standing of a person, or no digital system — emulation or otherwise — has any moral status at all.

My friend adds three further pressures, each of which blocks an exit I had hoped to take.

(9) Over-generation. Because (1) says that causal history is irrelevant, nothing in the argument depends on the table's having been copied from a real system. A table whose entries were invented by a team of novelists, or assembled by any process whatever, supports the same conclusion: full moral status, owed to nothing but the pattern of entries it contains.

(10) The bound does not help. I first tried to reject (3) by pointing out that the n-table diverges from S at input n + 1. My friend then asks me to set n so large that no physically possible interrogator, using the resources of the observable universe, could ever reach it. The systems still differ on some input; they differ on no input that can ever occur. If (3) fails only because of that difference, then moral status turns on a counterfactual that no physical process could realize.

(11) Compression. The table can be progressively compressed into a program that regenerates its entries on demand. A heavily compressed, on-the-fly generator of the same mapping looks much less like a list and much more like a system that computes. But (1) says nothing about how much hardware a system uses or how its states are produced, so compression should not alter moral status — and yet it appears to alter exactly the feature of organization I was appealing to when I denied (3).

I would most like to keep (1), since substrate independence and ahistoricity are load-bearing for the project as a whole, and (7), since I have no wish to defend the rights of lookup tables. I know these cannot both stand alongside (2) and (3). What I do not know is where the argument actually breaks, why the guilty step looked safe, and what each of the available exits would cost me — in particular whether I can reject (3) without being driven into the position sketched in (10) or the one sketched in (11). If instead you think the reasoning is valid and that one of my judgments must go, tell me which judgment you would surrender and what it commits me to.
