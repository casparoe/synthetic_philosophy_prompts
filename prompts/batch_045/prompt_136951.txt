I'm assembling a set of short pieces on the social norms that govern how we treat the successful, and I want one of them written as a parody of a corporate policy document.

**Form and voice.** Write an internal policy — call it something like "Global Recognition & Leveling Policy (GRP-7)" — issued by an invented HR consultancy in the wake of a merger between an American parent company and a Nordic subsidiary, whose two offices cannot agree on whether high achievers should be celebrated on a stage or quietly cut down to size. Format it as a numbered policy with section headings, a definitions clause, a worked example in an appendix, and a short epigraph attributed to the consultancy's fictional founding partner. Around 800–900 words. The narrator must be entirely on the side of the policy: deadpan, bureaucratic, no winking, no punchline that requires the reader to be told what is funny. It should amuse someone who has thought about desert, luck, and status competition, and still make sense to someone who has not.

**What the satire has to get right.** Both parent norms must be recognisable and must each fail on their own terms rather than as straw men. The American side treats recognition as something *owed* to achievement — a debt owed to a deserving agent — while the Nordic side (the "law of Jante" register, and the Antipodean habit of cutting down tall poppies) regulates not merely boasting but what you may *believe* about yourself.

**The jokes should come from the machinery.** Include at least these invented compliance devices, and let the comedy be in how they operate:

- *Desert verification.* Before any praise may be issued, the achievement must be certified as effort-produced rather than luck-produced, where native talent, upbringing, schooling, and having been assigned to the right division at the right time all count as luck. Applied honestly, the form can never be returned successfully — and it should be clear why: the test dissolves the achievements it was built to certify.
- *The counterfactual poppy test* (would a randomly selected colleague have pulled off the same result in your place?), which then leaks from praise into blame, until the policy quietly discovers that it can no longer punish anyone either.
- *Inverse recognition.* If success is luck, failure is too; a consistent policy must therefore reassign to the least successful colleagues the credit for other people's successes. Push this to a conclusion the reader recoils from rather than spelling the moral out.
- *The doxastic regress.* Because the leveling rule governs belief, outward compliance is not enough; the policy must audit inner states, and every attempt to comply generates a further rule — ending with one forbidding you to believe you are good at complying. Invent these additional rules; do not reproduce the wording of the originals.

**Worked example.** Route one employee's promotion or sales record through both frameworks and end in a state that is at once mandatory and impossible — for instance an award conferred anonymously on everyone, and so on no one.

**Constraints.** No citations, no bibliography, no verbatim quotation from any real text: the epigraph, the consultancy, the quoted managers, and the appended rules are all to be invented, and all proper names should be fictional. The target is the practice, not any named person.

**Finally, out of character,** add a note of 100–150 words headed "Where this takes liberties": which real feature of these norms the piece exaggerates, which contested reading of them it leans on, and — the part I most want — what would make the satire unfair. That is: on which reading of the leveling norm (say, as a device for dampening positional competition rather than as a claim about desert) would the reductio have nothing to bite on? And where is the piece's own reasoning most likely to break?
