Last night I got into a two-hour argument with a friend about Pascal's wager and I lost it on points even though I think I'm right. His whole case was the many-gods objection: there are lots of candidate deities, some of whom punish whoever bets on the wrong one, so the expected values wash out to zero or worse. I kept saying that objection is overrated — it's just a complaint about the probability assignments, it doesn't touch the machinery — but I couldnt say what the real problem is. I think it's about timing, and I want you to argue that.

The idea: the payoff is infinite and, as far as the argument is concerned, undiscounted — same eternity whether you convert at 20 or at 79. The cost is a finite stream: the churchgoing, the guilt, the wine. But nothing in that math says start now. Delay a day and you save a day of cost; the only thing you risk is dying before you get around to it. So expected-value maximization looks like it says convert as late as you can get away with, which for a healthy twenty-five-year-old is decades out, and maybe it says something stronger, like convert only once your hazard rate starts climbing. That reads like a reductio to me, and a better one than my friend's.

So: argue that the deferral problem is the strongest objection to the wager, and argue that the many-gods objection is doing less work than its fans think. Take on the two escapes I'd expect. One: sudden death, so waiting isn't free and the calculus does say something after all. Fine — but then the conclusion turns into an empirical claim about your own mortality curve, it tells the young and healthy to wait, and it's strange that a proof of religious urgency should hinge on actuarial tables. Two: a God who damns strategic or deathbed converts, or who demands sincerity, or who rewards the lifelong faithful more even though both rewards are supposed to be infinite. Argue that each of those is really the many-gods objection in a costume — a claim about which deity is on offer rather than about the structure of the argument — which is exactly why the many-gods objection is the weak one and not the decisive one.
