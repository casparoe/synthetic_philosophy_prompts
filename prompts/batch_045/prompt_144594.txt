Bit of background so the question makes sense: I ran a wet lab for about thirty years (protein chemistry, nothing glamorous) and since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local uni. We did James and Clifford a couple of weeks ago and I came away annoyed in a way I can't quite localise.

In my old lab the standing line at group meeting was that you've got to believe in your project. Not fund it, not take it seriously — believe it. The joke version was that a hypothesis is like a self-fulfilling prophecy, or like a marriage: nobody has the evidence at the start, you commit before the evidence is in, and the commitment is part of what produces the outcome, because if you don't believe the thing is going to work you won't do the three years of tedious failed attempts that are the only route to finding out whether it works. And people used that to say something about Clifford: that his rule — you're never entitled to believe past your evidence — is fine for a seminar room but no working scientist obeys it for a day, so it can't be a serious ethics of belief. The stronger version, which our group leader was pushing, was that scientific practice vindicates James: letting your passional nature decide where the evidence runs out isn't a regrettable exception, it's the engine of inquiry.

I want that analogy audited rather than admired. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the hypothesis, the evidence, the three dead years, the grant renewal, the PI's conviction, the paper at the end, and the colleagues who have to be persuaded — and then tell me where the mapping fails and, more usefully, which failures actually matter for the use it's being put to and which are just noise.

Three things I think are load-bearing but can't get anyone in the group to address. First: in the lab case the proposition believed is one that the believing itself helps make true — there's a causal loop, my deciding it'll work is part of why it does. In the cases James actually cares about, the truth of the thing believed doesn't seem to depend on my attitude at all, or only does in a weird second-order way. So isn't the analogy smuggling in the very feature that makes the permissive conclusion look easy? Second, this one I care most about: couldn't Clifford just say fine, act as if — run the experiment, talk yourself up at group meeting, take the bet — and call that acceptance rather than belief, and insist he only ever objected to believing? My group leader said that was cheating but couldn't say why, and I suspect the analogy is what's making it look like cheating. Third, the risk structure: in Clifford's story about the shipowner who reassures himself the old ship is sound, the people who drown never signed up for his optimism. In my lab the people who paid for my optimism were my students and my funders, who sort of did sign up, and there was a machine at the end (other labs, replication, the literature) that eventually corrected me. Is that difference doing all the work?

And here's my own attempt at a distinction, which you should feel free to scrap: cases where your believing causes the truth (the lab, the prophecy), cases where the evidence is just not in yet and more waiting is the answer, and cases where the fact believed is partly constituted by somebody's attitude — trusting a colleague, being in a collaboration, sticking with a marriage — so that there's no fact of the matter prior to the commitment for your evidence to track. Which of these buckets do James's genuine options actually fall in? My suspicion is that the lab analogy only supports the permissive conclusion for the first bucket, that James's own examples are mostly in the third, and that the analogy therefore makes his argument look more like the scientist's excuse than it is. But I'm not confident, and I'd like to know whether a better analogy does the job — something that isn't a prophecy and isn't a marriage.

Last thing: I don't want a survey of positions or a reading list, I want you to actually take a stand on whether the conclusion survives. So label how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one you're least sure of and say what would change your mind about it — I'd rather have that flagged explicitly than have it buried three paragraphs down in a hedge.
