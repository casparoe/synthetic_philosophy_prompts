After thirty-odd years as a research geophysicist — most of it spent on radiometric dating and on the stratigraphic correlation of cores taken from widely separated basins — I have begun sitting in on a graduate seminar in the philosophy of history, and I keep tripping over a word I would have defined wrongly had I met it in my own field. In metaphysics, presentism is the doctrine that only the present exists; in historiography, as my seminar uses it, it is the fault of judging past agents by the standards of our own time. I assumed at first that these were merely homonyms, but I am no longer sure, and I would like help seeing whether the physicist's sense is doing any quiet work underneath the historians' sense.

What prompted the question is a pair of practices that seem to pull in opposite directions. On the one hand, the professional norm in my seminar is emphatic: we are to reconstruct what an agent could reasonably have believed given the evidence available to her, to resist anachronism, to treat the past as a foreign country. On the other hand, the institutions around us do the reverse constantly and with great moral seriousness: statues of nineteenth-century slave traders and imperial proconsuls are pulled down, colleges and schools named for benefactors are renamed, plaques are bolted on, parliaments and churches issue retrospective apologies, and politicians accuse their opponents of standing on the wrong side of history — a phrase I find curious, since it launders a thoroughly contemporary judgment as though it were a verdict of time itself. Meanwhile the scruple seems strictly one-directional: no one reproaches us for praising an abolitionist or an early advocate of women's education as having been ahead of her time, though that is the very same anachronism running the other way, and the same seminar that insists we must not fault a seventeenth-century naturalist for failing to be a Darwinian will happily describe him as a forerunner of population thinking.

I have been toying with a way of framing this that I cannot get to work properly, and I would like it tested rather than indulged. In my old field, when a quantity looks different depending on the frame in which you measure it, the useful move is not to ask which frame is correct but to ask which quantities are invariant under a change of frame, and then to argue about the conventions that make one frame appropriate for a given purpose. So I have been treating the choice of *which era's* standards to judge by as a kind of frame choice: the question would then be which moral judgments are invariant under a shift of temporal standpoint, and what conventions govern when it is apt to adopt the standpoint of the agent versus that of the judge. Does that analogy illuminate the situation or does it smuggle in something? More concretely: what view of moral judgment, and of historical explanation, do these commemorative and condemnatory practices actually presuppose? Does condemning a past figure require thinking moral standards are timeless, while the anti-anachronism norm requires relativizing blame to an agent's epistemic situation — and are those consistent, or does one need something like a distinction between an act's being wrong and the agent's being blameworthy? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license the same renaming? In particular, is it a real answer to say that commemoration is not a verdict on a person at all but a decision about what a community currently wishes to honour and to teach — so that taking down a statue no more judges the dead than choosing a reading list does — or is that a dodge? And what would have to change in these practices if one took the anti-presentist norm seriously all the way down, or if one took the frame-relativity picture seriously instead?
