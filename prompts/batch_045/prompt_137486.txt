I work in security engineering, mostly detection engineering and insider risk: my job for years has been to assume that some component of the system, or some person holding legitimate credentials, is already hostile, and to arrange the environment so that this does not turn into an incident. My native vocabulary is: assume breach; least privilege and blast radius; egress filtering and data-loss prevention; canary tokens and honeypots; detection coverage, dwell time and time-to-remediate; the trusted computing base; red and purple teaming; alert fatigue and the base-rate arithmetic that makes rare-event detection nearly worthless; and, no less load-bearing, the deterrence half of insider risk — vetting before access, attribution afterward, and consequences for people who get caught.

Lately I have been reading people who work on advanced AI systems argue that safety work splits into two strategies that are usually conflated. Call the first alignment: try to build a system whose goals are the ones we want, and verify that they are. Call the second control: decline to rely on any claim about the system's goals, treat the system as a potentially adversarial insider with real capabilities, and arrange the deployment so that even a misaligned system cannot do durable damage — containment, restricted capabilities, monitoring, planted opportunities to defect, and the practical ability to shut it down. The case I care about is an agent that writes and runs code, has tool and network access, and is given a budget, not a chat system answering questions.

I would like the second position restated as carefully as you can in my vocabulary, measure by measure, with no security term doing work that the corresponding AI-control term would not do. The measures I have in mind are: (1) restricting what the system can touch and how much of it, the way one restricts a service account; (2) restricting what it can find out, in particular whether it is currently being evaluated or really deployed, on the theory that a system which can tell the two apart can behave well in one and badly in the other; (3) monitoring its actions with a weaker, more trusted system rather than a stronger, less trusted one — including monitoring by copies of the suspect system itself, and deliberately resampling or rewriting the inputs the suspect system sees so that it cannot infer that it is under test; (4) tripwires: deployments indistinguishable from real ones to the system but in which the opportunity to defect is planted and harmless, with the base-rate problem this raises for treating a tripwire hit as evidence; (5) an explicit safety case of the form: a bound on the probability of an escape per action or per deployment, multiplied by the number of actions or deployments, held under some budget; and (6) containment and shutdown, including the problem that a system which anticipates being shut down for misbehaving has an incentive to ensure that the shutdown does not succeed.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually want. Which of these translate cleanly into what I already do; which translate only after you have quietly shifted the meaning of one of my terms or helped yourself to an assumption I would not grant; and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? My guesses about where the seams are: canary tokens and tripwires come apart once the adversary is better than I am at telling a canary from the real thing; the trusted computing base idea fails, because my monitoring would be performed by another statistical model rather than by a root of trust I can reason about; and the entire deterrence half of my work has no analogue, which would mean that a large part of what makes insider-risk programs function is simply unavailable here. Tell me whether those guesses are right and where else the translation strains.

Where something does not translate, say which way the fault runs: is my framework missing a concept that this work has had to invent, or is the control-side claim less determinate than it sounds once it is restated in terms I could actually operationalize? Keep the translation honest — if a step requires an assumption my discipline does not license, flag it rather than presenting it as a restatement.
