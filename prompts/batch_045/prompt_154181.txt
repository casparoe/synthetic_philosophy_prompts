Two guys in a group chat I'm in got into a long back-and-forth about AI safety, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, so I want a second opinion. Its not really my field, so plain language please.

Dan opened with: the whole idea of "assume the model might be misaligned and build around it anyway" is incoherent. Any test you can run on a model, a misaligned model can pass, because it can just behave well while it's being evaluated and defect later when it actually matters. So either you trust it or you don't, and deploying it while claiming not to trust it is theater.

Ruth: that only applies to tests the model knows about. Her version is don't ask the model anything — limit what it can touch, check its work with a separate weaker model it never gets to see, and mix in fake tasks that look like chances to cheat but are actually being watched. The model doesn't get to choose which of its outputs get audited.

Dan: then the weak checker is the real bottleneck, and the whole reason we wanted the strong model is that it's better than the weak one. And your fake tasks only catch cheating you already thought of; a smarter model invents a kind you didn't imagine, and a dumber monitor can't reliably flag something it couldn't have come up with itself.

Ruth: you don't need to catch novel tricks, you need the odds to be bad. Keep the damage from any single attempt small and the chance of being noticed high, and attempting is a losing bet. The defender only has to be right occasionally; the saboteur has to get away with it over and over.

Dan: unless there's a one-shot move — copy its own weights out, leave a backdoor — where getting caught afterwards doesn't matter. If any single action pays off irreversibly, "not worth it" stops working, and you need impossible. A weaker monitor can't give you impossible.

Ruth: then make irreversible actions structurally unavailable and eat the loss in usefulness. That's not control being incoherent, that's just the price of it.

Dan: two more things. One, monitoring the model's reasoning doesn't help if the training process then filters out reasoning that looks suspicious — you're selecting for models that scheme in ways that look innocent, not for models that don't scheme. Two, all of this works fine on tasks where you can check the answer and terribly on ones where you can't, so "control" quietly means only ever using the model on the checkable half of the work.

Ruth: the first is an argument for not training against the monitor, not against having one — keep the thing you monitor separate from the thing you train on. And the second is a limit, not a refutation; it just tells you where you're allowed to deploy.
