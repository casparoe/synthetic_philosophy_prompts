I am writing a 1,200-word piece on Kavka's toxin puzzle for a student philosophy magazine, and I want the piece to argue that the puzzle has been oversold. Before I write it, I would like you to stress-test my argument and tell me where, if at all, it goes wrong.

The puzzle, as I understand it: an eccentric billionaire offers me £1m if, at midnight tonight, I intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make me painfully ill for a day and will do nothing else. The money arrives hours before the time for drinking, and I need never drink. The puzzle is supposed to be that I cannot intend to do what I know I will then have no reason to do.

My solution is what I call the forfeit device. At 11pm I sign a costless, perfectly enforced contract under which £900,000 is transferred to charity if I have not drunk the toxin by noon, the contract being enforced automatically against the million when it lands. Assume that a day of painful illness matters far less to me than £900,000. Now, at noon, drinking is plainly worth while; so at midnight I can straightforwardly intend to drink; the billionaire pays; I drink; and I end up with the full million and one unpleasant day. Nothing in this requires any special psychology, and no one has to fool themselves about anything.

Three conclusions I want to draw, and which I would like you to assess:

First, that the toxin puzzle is just an instance of the Ulysses problem. The moral is the same as for any pre-commitment case, and the literature has manufactured a mystery by stipulating that no commitment device is available.

Second, that structurally this is Newcomb's problem over again: in both cases the reward is keyed to a mental state rather than to an action, so the real work is being done by the debate about causal and evidential decision theory, and nothing specifically about intention is left over.

Third, that when people say the real question is whether intentions are reducible to beliefs about what one will do, they are asking a merely verbal question, and the forfeit device lets us set it aside.

I am writing this away from my library, so please do not rest anything on citations or on who said what: argue the points directly. Because the audience is undergraduates, I also need to be scrupulous about what is and is not agreed: tell me which claims are settled background for anyone working on this, which are live controversies, and which are only your own inclination, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
