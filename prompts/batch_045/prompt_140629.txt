hey — I'm in a metaphysics seminar this term and we've spent the last two and a half weeks on the special composition question (when do some things add up to a further thing?), and I've realised I have a pile of gut reactions that don't seem to add up to any of the three views we were handed (everything always composes / nothing ever does / composition happens but only sometimes, and only when...). I got into an argument about it in the dining hall last night and lost, mostly because I couldn't say what my own principle was. So here are my verdicts, straight from the gut:

- my coffee mug: there is a mug, not just particles arranged mugwise.
- a heap of sand on a beach: no, just grains.
- a statue and the lump of clay it's made of: one thing, not two — 'statue' and 'lump' are two descriptions of it, and I really don't want to say there are two solid objects occupying exactly the same place at the same time.
- my watch, which my brother took apart and left in pieces in a drawer for a month before putting it back together: no watch-thing existed during that month, but it's the same watch afterwards — it survived being taken apart, it wasn't replaced by a duplicate.
- the tree on the lawn in front of the library: exactly one tree, even though there are thousands of overlapping near-candidates (tree-including-that-flake-of-bark, tree-minus-that-leaf…). I refuse to say there are thousands of trees there, but I also can't say which one it is, and this bugs me more than the statue case does.
- my body minus a single hair: no such thing — which looks structurally just like the tree-minus-one-leaf candidates I just denied, so one of those two verdicts has to go.
- my left shoe together with the Eiffel Tower: obviously not.
- one of those colonial jellyfish-ish things (a Portuguese man o' war) whose bits are physically separate but work as a single animal: yes, one organism.
- a wolf pack spread over a valley: no.
- a ship whose planks are replaced one at a time while the old planks are immediately assembled into a raft floating alongside: the ship is the repaired one and the raft isn't, even though the raft has all the original parts. But then, if the ship burns and ten years later someone rebuilds it plank by plank from the stored originals, I find myself wanting to say it's the same ship again — which I suspect is inconsistent of me.

Someone in my seminar floated a principle I keep turning over: the parts compose something exactly when they stand in a single causally self-maintaining system, where the self-maintenance is allowed to be realized indirectly — through a designer's intentions, or through stored plans — so that a watch in pieces in a drawer still counts (its unity is 'hosted' by the plan and by what everyone intends to do with the parts), while the very same parts in a random pile don't; and the colonial jellyfish counts because its parts maintain one another, while a wolf pack doesn't, because its coordination is behavioural rather than metabolic. I like this alot, but I'm pretty sure it isnt finished: it seems to say yes to some things I want to say no to, and I can't tell what it does with the tree.

Can you do the reflective-equilibrium thing with me? Take my ten verdicts and see whether that principle, or a cleaned-up version of it, or a completely different one, gets them. Please state the principle precisely, tell me which clause is motivated by which verdict, be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and whether I should just drop them or whether the principle is worse off for dropping them, and say what it predicts for a couple of cases I haven't listed — I'm especially stuck on whether a person with a transplanted mechanical heart has the pump as a part of them, and on whether a gravitationally bound system like a solar system turns out to be an object on this view. Also: does my watch verdict secretly commit me to thinking of things as spread out in time rather than wholly present at each moment, or can I keep it without that?
