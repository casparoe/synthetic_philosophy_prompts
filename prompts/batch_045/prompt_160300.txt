A friend's startup spent most of last year in a nasty dispute with a former contractor over who "had" a design, and the experience left me unable to say what I actually think about when one person owes another something for using an idea. I don't want a reading list or a survey of what the law currently holds anywhere — I want to know whether my own verdicts on concrete cases add up to a principle, and if so which one. Here they are, as honestly as I can report them. A former line cook opens his own restaurant and reproduces his old employer's signature dish exactly — same technique, same plating, same name on the menu: not fine. But a cabinetmaker's apprentice who spends ten years in a shop absorbing the master's way of joining drawers, then sets up across the street and builds in that idiom: fine, and I'd think less of a master who tried to stop him — that pair is the one I can't shake, since both look like carrying away something learned at someone else's elbow. A graduate student emails her advisor an idea that ends up in his keynote without her name on it: not fine, and worse than the restaurant case, though nothing was copied in either. Two teams with no contact prove the same theorem the same way: the later one owes the earlier nothing. A rival journalist learns only that a story exists and digs it up himself: fine. A biographer spends two years in an archive and establishes that a famous novelist wrote a certain anonymous review; another biographer uses that finding in her own book without so much as a nod: not fine. A lab spends years and a lot of money measuring a decay constant; a rival lab uses the number the week it is published: fine — and this pair, two costly-to-produce new facts treated completely differently, is the one that makes me doubt I have a principle at all rather than a set of habits. A comedian does another comedian's joke more or less verbatim on television: not fine; another comedian takes only the structural trick — the misdirection setup, the callback timing — with wholly different material: fine. A fashion label prints a pattern that a weaving village in Oaxaca has used for generations, marketing it as the label's own signature: not fine, except that I cannot identify anyone in particular who is owed. A company buys a rival's product on the open market, takes it apart, and copies the geometry: fine; the same company fishes the rival's discarded prototypes out of a dumpster and copies those: I genuinely don't know. Finally, the case I keep returning to: a living poet's complete published works are used to build a system that produces new poems in her manner, no line of hers reproduced, sold by people who never spoke to her — some days this seems like the open-market teardown, other days like the advisor's keynote.

The only formulation I've been able to make satisfy me even partly is this: you owe the originator of an idea something only when the idea reached you through a channel that they had a stake in controlling — a confidence, an employment or apprenticeship relation, a shared workspace, a manuscript handed over for a purpose — and never merely because the idea was in their head first. Please test that against the cases rather than just restating it: where does it give the verdicts I gave, where does it have to be bent to do so (I suspect the archival-fact case and the poet case are where it strains, since a published book is a channel the author chose, but not for that use), and which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon? I'd like the simplest principle that saves most of the verdicts and the more elaborate one that saves all of them, side by side, with an explicit statement of what each costs me — and I'd especially like to know whether the thing I'm calling ownership of an idea is really about ideas at all, or only about relationships and the expectations they create. Then tell me what the principles you offer say about two cases I haven't settled: an employee who invents something on company time and with company equipment but well outside anything he was hired to think about, and a novelist who takes from a novel she read at fifteen, and only half-remembers, the bare shape of its plot — a death disclosed in the first chapter, the rest of the story told backwards.
