I am revising the section on exploitation and alienation in a second-year course on Marx, and I want Ford's Highland Park plant in 1913–14 to do argumentative work rather than serve as decoration. Take these facts as established: in 1913 Ford introduced the first moving assembly line there, having broken complex jobs into short, repetitive, largely unskilled operations, with chassis assembly time collapsing from many hours to roughly an hour and a half; annual labour turnover in the plant is usually reported as approaching 400 per cent, with daily absenteeism near 10 per cent. In January 1914 Ford announced the five-dollar day, roughly double the prevailing factory wage and for an eight-hour day, with a profit-sharing component conditional on sobriety, thrift and approved domestic conduct, policed by a Sociological Department that inspected workers' homes. Turnover and absenteeism fell sharply, thousands of men queued at the gates, and the Model T's price kept falling through the same years, so that the men who built it could increasingly buy one.

What I want is the case run in both directions; I do not need the narrative or the theory restated. First, the mapping: which Marxian categories attach to which features of the case — formal versus real subsumption, absolute versus relative surplus value, the moral-historical element in the value of labour power, the reserve army, the four dimensions of alienated labour — and what each category actually predicts about the observable outcomes, including the queues and the collapse in turnover. Second, the return direction: what the case does to the theory. Three cruxes matter most to me.

One: I distinguish in my lectures between the extraction complaint — workers surrender unpaid surplus labour time under a formally free and fair wage contract — and the command complaint — workers have no say over what they do, how fast, or to what end, and are disciplined into another's purposes. Ford raised pay and deepened command. Does Marx's apparatus treat these as one wrong or two, and does the case suggest that what actually stings about Fordism is the second rather than the first?

Two: I distinguish alienation as a structural property of the production relation from alienation as an experienced harm. Men queued for jobs that, on Marx's own description, are paradigms of alienated labour. Is that an anomaly, a confirmation, or evidence that the distinction is one Marx himself cannot sustain? Fold in the worker-as-consumer point: does it touch alienation from the product at all that the producer can now buy what he makes, or is that simply irrelevant on Marx's terms — and if irrelevant, does that tell against the theory, against its critics, or against Marx's remark that a small house becomes a hut once a palace is built beside it?

Three: with wages doubled and output per worker multiplied, can Marx's concepts deliver a determinate verdict on whether Ford's workers were more or less exploited in 1914 than in 1912, or does the verdict depend on a choice of numeraire the theory does not itself settle? Mark any point where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. About 800 words, please, with the space spent on the mapping rather than on summary.
