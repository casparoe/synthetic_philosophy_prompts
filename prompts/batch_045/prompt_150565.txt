hey — I'm chewing on something about norms of assertion and I keep going in circles, so I want someone to just untangle the conceptual knot for me. No citations or reading recs needed, I've read around in this stuff plenty; it's the reasoning I'm after.

Setup so we're on the same page. Call KNA the claim that you may flat-out assert that p only if you know that p — and then the stronger version, where that isn't just a rule you ought to follow but something like part of what makes an utterance an assertion at all rather than guessing out loud, or acting, or ventriloquizing someone. Assume for argument's sake that knowledge itself isn't stakes- or hearer-sensitive (set aside pragmatic encroachment stuff), so that KNA is a norm about the speaker's own epistemic position and doesn't shift depending on who's listening.

Here's the knot. Take some people who all say out loud "the meeting's at noon," and it is in fact at noon:

- A: sincere, knows it, normal case.
- B: lying. B checked the calendar, knows it's noon, doesn't believe it, and wants the hearer to show up at midnight.
- C: sincere and knows it, but C also knows the hearer will take "noon" in the wrong timezone and quietly lets that happen.
- D: sincere, knows it, true — but D is saying it to the person who scheduled the meeting, who obviously knows better than D does, and it's weird in that "let me explain your own job to you" way.
- E: doesn't know it at all, just heard a rumor, asserts anyway — and the hearer happens to have the calendar open in front of them, so nobody is misled and nothing bad happens downstream.

B, C, D and E all feel defective to me in different ways, and I can't get clear on what's doing the work. So:

1. B is the one case KNA might handle, but only if knowing p requires believing p. Is that a genuine feature of the view — the sincerity condition falling out for free — or is it a fudge that smuggles in a second norm under the label "knowledge"?

2. C, D and E are what really bother me. Whatever they violate looks like it has to do with the hearer: with how your utterance lands, or with whether you're in any position to tell *this* person *this* thing. But then there's an asymmetry I can't get past: KNA is addressee-independent, while whatever C/D/E violate isn't. If assertion is essentially something you do *to* someone — its whole point being to hand the hearer a reason, or to put something into the conversational common ground — how can its most basic norm be one that ignores the hearer completely? And going the other way: if the basic norm really is speaker-side, then what exactly is wrong with "asserting" to a brick wall or into a diary, and is that even assertion? Does settling that question settle anything, or is it a standoff?

3. And separately: how can a norm be *constitutive* of assertion when people violate it constantly and still plainly assert? I have the rough regulative/constitutive distinction (a regulative norm governs an activity that could exist without it; a constitutive one partly defines the activity), but I don't have a grip on what violating a constitutive norm is even supposed to consist in. Is B's utterance a defective assertion or not an assertion at all — and why do we have no trouble reporting "B asserted the meeting was at noon"? Compare promising: an insincere promise seems to still be a promise, just a rotten one, whereas a "promise" made by an actor on stage isn't a promise at all. So which of those is a KNA violation more like, and does the answer change depending on which version of KNA you hold?

What I want is the machinery: the distinctions I'd need in order to place these five cases and see which of them are really assertoric defects rather than just ordinary moral or etiquette failures that happen to involve speech. And if you think one of my cases is doing no work — that it's not a genuine problem for KNA, or that it collapses into another one — tell me which and why.
