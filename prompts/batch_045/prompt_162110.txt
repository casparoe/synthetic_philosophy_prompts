hey, I need a piece of satire for a philosophy-and-economics reading group I put together, and I want it to actually land rather than just being 'haha, togas'.

Write a mock SEC-style mutual fund prospectus excerpt, 600–700 words, for a retail fund called something like the Oikos Fund, whose stated investment policy is to make money strictly the way Aristotle says money should be made in Politics Book I, plus his bit about proportional reciprocity and money as a conventional measure in the Nicomachean Ethics. Format: a short 'Principal Investment Strategies' blurb followed by six or seven numbered 'Principal Risks of Investing', all in proper prospectus boilerplate voice — deadpan, heavily hedged, lots of 'may', 'there can be no assurance', 'investors could lose money'.

The humor has to come from the doctrine being right. Things I want recognizable and quietly exaggerated rather than invented: the split between managing a household toward enough and wealth-getting aimed at no limit; a thing's proper use versus its use as an object of exchange (a shoe worn versus a shoe sold); the claim that the unnatural kind of wealth-getting is the one where money itself breeds money, since money came about for exchange and not for breeding — the Greek word for interest meaning offspring; money being a measure that holds by convention rather than by nature, the thing that ties a polis together through proportional return. And the Thales olive-press story from the Politics, where the philosopher corners the presses to prove he could get rich if he bothered.

Two invented wrinkles you have to build the piece around, because that's where the fun actually is:

1. The Fund has solved the no-interest problem by reclassifying every return as a custody and storage fee: a payment for safeguarding the principal while its in the borrower's hands, not a payment for the use of the money. The fee of course scales with the size of the principal and the length of time, and the compliance language keeps betraying that this is interest in a costume. The reader should work that out without anyone ever saying so.
2. The Fund's alpha strategy is a 'Thales clause': it only takes positions where it can corner a real input with a genuine use, and the Adviser explicitly reserves the right not to exercise a winning position, in order to demonstrate that philosophy isn't useless. Disclose that as a risk, in the same flat tone as market risk.

Also, holdings are supposed to be natural wealth-getting — herding, farming, fishing and so on — so I want one risk factor on how the Fund classifies modern assets into those boxes (a semiconductor fab as pastoral, index futures as fishing) and one on the problem that the Adviser must certify each holding is held for its use and not for resale, which sits very badly with the Fund being diversified.

Constraints: don't quote Aristotle, paraphrase him in your own words; Greek only where your confident (tokos, chrematistikē are fine). No footnotes, no suggested reading, no explaining the joke. Keep it deadpan — no winking, no 'as any classicist will tell you'. Half my group are philosophers and half work in finance, and I'd like the finance half to be the ones who end up squirming a bit.
