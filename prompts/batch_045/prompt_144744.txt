I help run a monthly reading group that grew out of an old rationalist-community meetup — a dozen or so people, heavy on steelmanning, base rates, and tabooing contested words before arguing about them — and for about a year our two most useful members have been deadlocked on scientific realism. Dana defends something like selective or structural realism: she grants that the pessimistic meta-induction works against the naive package deal, but insists that what typically survives theory change is the structure — the equations, the symmetries, the quantitative relations — rather than the ontology, so the induction only looks devastating if you score every abandoned posit as a verdict on the whole theory it appeared in. Ivo runs the induction straight: take the posits that a period's best-confirmed theories treated as real, note how many are now regarded as fictions, and conclude that our own best-supported posits are probably fictions too. They have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to draft the document they would both sign. I cannot do it: every formulation I try is either vacuous or quietly smuggles in one side's framing.

Two things I want built into the document, because they are where I think the actual argument has been living. First, Dana has recently stopped disputing the base rate and started modeling it. Her proposal: the quantity that matters is not the fraction of past posits that were eventually eliminated but the instantaneous hazard of elimination as a function of a posit's age. If the hazard is strongly decreasing — a posit that has survived a century of heavy interventional use is far less likely to be eliminated next year than it was in its first decade — then inferring a high death probability for current posits from the aggregate historical discard rate is a base-rate fallacy applied to a survival process, structurally the same mistake as quoting the lifetime failure rate of startups at a firm that has been profitable for thirty years. Her toy numbers: annual elimination hazard of a few percent during a posit's first decade of heavy use, roughly halving each subsequent decade, which would put a century-old survivor's ten-year survival far above what the raw discard rate suggests. Ivo's counter is that a decreasing hazard is just what you would expect if only structurally preserved content survives, so the model presupposes the selective realist's conclusion; that the record is too short and too theory-laden to fit a hazard curve to, since we largely know which posits died because later science recorded the deaths; that eliminated is itself a contested grade, with reduced, reidentified, and quietly-demoted-to-useful-fiction being three different fates that get lumped together; and that even a constant, modest hazard compounds into unflattering numbers over a few centuries. Second, both of them gesture at something like a counterfactual test for whether a theory's success was really sensitive to a given posit — would the predictions still have come out right if that posit had been false, or was the posit doing bookkeeping? — but neither has written the test down, and I suspect their disagreement about whether the historical cases pass it is doing most of the work.

So please write the thing. Give me a numbered section of agreed claims — claims both could sign about what the no-miracles argument and the meta-induction each establish on their own, about what retention of structure comes to, about what would have to be true of the historical record for it to license any induction here at all — each phrased so that neither side would wince. Then a table of the residual disagreements, each stated in the wording its own side would accept: the hazard-rate question, the grading question, the sensitivity question, and whatever else you think is load-bearing. For each, name the crux such that settling it would resolve or dissolve the disagreement; give a test, case, or argument both would accept in advance as bearing on that crux, including forward-looking ones if useful — predictions about which current posits each of them would bet on being reidentified rather than eliminated, scored a few decades out; and record what each of them predicts the result will be. Where some part of the dispute looks verbal — about approximate truth, about belief, about what a realist is claiming — say which part and why. No citations or reading lists, please; I want the argument, not the literature.
