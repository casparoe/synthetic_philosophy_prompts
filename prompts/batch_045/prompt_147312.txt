I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, been covering algorithmic fairness for years, sat thru enough conference talks to know the standard impossibility results by heart (calibration plus equal false-positive rates plus equal false-negative rates can't all hold once base rates differ, and so on). I still can't tell you what I actually think. So here are my gut verdicts on a set of cases — all fictional, all close to things I've reported on — and I want the principle that cashes them out, or the news that some of them have to go.

My working theory, which I'm increasingly sure is wrong: a model can't be unfair in itself, only the composite thing can be unfair — model plus threshold plus whoever acts on it. Test it, break it, repair it.

1. A county buys a recidivism score. It's calibrated the same way in both groups (a 60 means 60% rearrest whoever it's about), but at the single cutoff the county uses, one group is flagged who-wouldn't-reoffend at twice the rate of the other. My gut: not OK. Somebody has to move a threshold.

2. Same score, same county, but it goes to a pretrial-services officer who sees the score and the person's file and makes her own call, and her releases come out with equal error rates across groups. Gut: fine. Genuinely fine. Even though the number on her screen is exactly as lopsided as in 1.

3. Same score again, and now the officer's decisions come out *more* lopsided than the score alone would have been, because she trusts it more for one group than the other. Gut: this is worse than 1, and it's the same model. I realize 2 and 3 together say something strange about 1.

4. A hospital ranks patients for a care-management program by predicted cost, and cost tracks need badly for one group because they get less care at the same level of sickness. Even if the hospital tuned enrollment so both groups got slots at equal rates, I'd still say the score is broken: within the program it ranks sicker people in one group below healthier people in the other. Gut: unfair, full stop, no deployment story needed. This is the case that makes me think my working theory is dead.

5. A predictive-policing-ish model uses prior arrests as a feature, and one neighborhood has been policed harder for a decade, so the feature partly records what the department did rather than what people did. Dropping it costs accuracy. Gut: unfair anyway, and I feel that even when the resulting disparity is small — which is the same shape as 4.

6. Two vendors, both with a modest disparity at the cutoff the city actually uses. Vendor A's disparity stays roughly flat if you slide the cutoff anywhere. Vendor B's triples if you move it a few points, and the city has already floated moving it because of jail overcrowding. My gut: B is the unfair one. I've started calling this "threshold-proofness" — judge a score across the whole range of cutoffs, not at the operating point, because cutoffs drift. Problem: I also feel sort of fine about a score that's lopsided only in a stretch of the range nobody will ever use, which I think contradicts my own idea.

7. A vendor solves a disparity complaint by deleting the group variable. The disparity gets worse, and also nobody can audit anything anymore. Gut: worse than before, and not just because the number moved.

8. The one I go back and forth on. The same calibrated score, same base-rate gap, used to hand out 200 slots in a diversion program: one group gets 40% of the slots, the other 20%. My stomach says fine, that's just who needs it more, leave it alone. But it's the identical math as 1, where my stomach says not fine. Benefit versus burden? I don't have a story that survives flipping the sign.

So: give me the principle, or two or three rival candidates, stated tightly enough that I could apply it myself, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me which of my calls a candidate forces me to drop — I assume one of 4 and 5, and one of 2 and 3, has to go. Then tell me what your best candidate says about cases I haven't listed: a resume screener that's simply more accurate for one group than the other; two counties buying the same score where the base-rate gap is twice as wide in one of them; and a parole score in a state where the base-rate gap in the data was partly *caused* by ten years of using the score. And say what it says about 8.
