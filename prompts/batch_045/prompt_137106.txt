I have been asked to run a single ninety-minute session on the inefficacy objection — the argument that, because my own contribution to a large collective harm makes no difference to whether the harm occurs, I have no reason to refrain — for a mixed adult audience of about fifteen people at a public library. None of them have studied philosophy. Two or three will follow a simple expected-value multiplication without difficulty. Several arrive already in the habit of saying that one person cannot make a difference. One or two arrive hoping to be told that they are therefore under no obligation to change anything. I do not need the objection explained to me; I need a plan for explaining to them why it is harder to settle than it first appears.

My own tentative map, which I would like you to correct rather than ratify, is that what travels under this one name is really three different shapes of case. First, a threshold that is fine-grained and unknown to me, where my contribution has some small but real chance of being the one that tips it. Second, an institution whose response is quantised: it reacts in batches, with rounding, so that my unit of consumption or my signature changes anything only when the running total happens to sit at a boundary. Third, a case of outright redundancy, where enough others will act that my contribution is not improbably efficacious but certainly inert, and no expected-value calculation can assign it any effect at all. My conjecture about the second is that batching does not shrink the expected size of my effect; it merely relocates it into a lottery, so that I matter less often and more when I matter. Tell me whether that is right, whether it is teachable in ninety minutes, and whether an audience like this one will hear it as a conjuring trick.

Given that, what I want from you is the dependency structure rather than the explanation itself. What has to be in place before the question is even intelligible to them? In particular, must I get them to prise apart "makes no difference to the outcome", "is not among the causes of the harm", and "bears no responsibility" at the very start, or can that three-way separation be drawn out of them later, at the point where they actually need it? Where in the sequence does the expected-value move belong, and at which case should I let them see it fail?

On the example: I am inclined to discard the standard philosophical case in which a thousand people each turn a switch and each adds an imperceptible increment of suffering, because this audience disengages from anything with dials in it; and I would rather not use voting, since it recruits people's political allegiances before the structure is on the table. Tell me which single case should carry the hour and a half, what it must contain, what it must lack, and whether it should be a case these particular people could find themselves in.

On misconceptions: I expect them to collapse "vanishingly unlikely" into "impossible", to produce some version of "but if everyone reasoned that way" within the first ten minutes, and at some point to slide straight from "no difference" to "no reason" without noticing that they have taken a step. Tell me which of these to head off and exactly where in the sequence, which further ones I have not anticipated, and which one I should deliberately let them sit inside for a while because it is doing useful work.

Finally: give me one question to end on that would show me they had grasped the structure rather than learned a slogan — I would prefer one that requires them to apply the distinctions to a case we have not discussed — and tell me what I should openly decline to go into at this level. No reading list, and please do not propose anything for them to take away.

Write your reply as continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you would write a long email to a colleague — and keep it under nine hundred words.
