Question. Say what seperates a reductionist about testimony from an anti-reductionist. Then consider a case where the thing doing the asserting isn't a person — a language model that outputs the sentence "the meeting is at four", which happens to be true, and a hearer who accepts it. Does the anti-reductionist have to say the hearer thereby knows? Take a position and defend it.

Student's answer:

Reductionists — Hume is the classic case — hold that our reliance on the word of others is itself justified by experience: we have observed that reports and the way the world is tend to line up, and that is our reason for accepting any given report. Testimony therefore isn't an independent source of knowledge; it reduces to perception, memory and induction. Anti-reductionists (Reid, and more recently Coady and Burge) deny this: we have an original, non-inferential entitlement to accept what people tell us, so testimony is a basic source in its own right, alongside perception.

It's sometimes said that the two views only differ about whether the hearer needs positive reasons, and that way of putting it makes the dispute look verbal. It isn't verbal, and the real difference is that the reductionist is committed to a much narrower extension for testimonial knowledge: on her view we don't really know, say, when the Battle of Hastings was, since nobody has the experiential evidence that alone could support reliance on the reports that have come down to us.

The interesting test case is a non-human source. Suppose a language model outputs "the meeting is at four" and it's true. Does a hearer who accepts it thereby know? I say the anti-reductionist has to say yes. Her view is that no reasons drawn from the hearer's own evidence are required, and that's exactly the situation here. And it follows that she can't object that the model has no intentions, no understanding of what it says, and can't be held responsible, because any such objection is a demand for reasons about the source — which is just what anti-reductionism says we don't need. The model also isn't the sort of thing that can be more or less trustworthy, so there's nothing here to be trusting about; the anti-reductionist is stuck treating it as an informant on a par with a person.

So anti-reductionism is too permissive. The reductionist can say instead that any warrant here would have to come from evidence about the system's track record, and that seems right. Reductionism handles the machine case better, and that's a reason to prefer reductionism.
