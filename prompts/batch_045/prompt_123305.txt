I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation chapter on practical reason in cases of exact ties: what, if anything, happens when an agent chooses between options she has no preference between. I keep coming back to the medieval example of the ass placed between two equally attractive bundles of hay, the one usually pinned on Buridan, and I've become convinced that both standard moves in the literature miss what's interesting about it. The first treats it as a puzzle about whether rationality supplies or permits a tie-breaker; the second dismisses it on the grounds that real agents are never exactly indifferent, so the case is empty. I want to write a piece of short fiction that gets at what neither move touches, and I'd like you to draft it with me.

Here is the setting and cast I have in mind, which you should feel free to reshape. In a near-future city, a public office — call it the Bureau of Equipoise — hears deadlock petitions. A citizen who has been unable to decide something may, after a certified period of supervised deliberation, have the state settle the matter for him by lot; the Bureau grew out of the allocation lotteries the city already uses for school places and conscription, and was quietly extended to private life. The protagonist is a junior assessor who interviews petitioners and certifies whether a deadlock is genuine. She uses a distinction I want the story to honor and to make dramatic: thin ties, where two options really are equal in expected value and a coin settles everything worth settling; and thick ties, where the agent is not undecided about the outcomes but unsettled about what he values — where the tie sits at the level of who he is rather than at the level of what he wants. Her worry, which she cannot get her supervisor to take seriously, is that the Bureau manufactures the condition it treats: once a lottery is available, petitioners stop cultivating the fine-grained discriminations that would once have let them choose, and so the availability of the tie-breaker induces the ties it exists to resolve. Her supervisor holds the opposite view, a thoroughgoing skepticism about indifference: no one is ever truly indifferent, every apparent tie conceals an unacknowledged preference, and the Bureau is a machine for laundering cowardice into a ceremony. The case that forces the issue is a petitioner deadlocked between staying in the city to care for his mother and taking a research post abroad — a case that is not obviously thin and not obviously thick, and where certification means the difference between a man who acted and a man to whom something happened.

What I want, in about 1,200 to 1,800 words, is a story that earns its ending rather than announces a thesis: the assessor must decide whether to certify this petition, and the reader should be left genuinely unsure whether her decision is an act of respect for the petitioner's agency or a paternalistic theft of it. Please avoid the two cheap resolutions — do not reveal, in the final scene, a hidden preference that was there all along, and do not let any character deliver the argument in lecture form; if the distinction between thin and thick ties is to do work, it should do it in what the characters do, in the ceremonial details of the Bureau (the two identical folders, the supervised draw, the certificate), in what the protagonist notices and fails to notice about herself. It would help me if the story dramatized, without asserting, the thought that the capacity to be moved by considerations one cannot rank is itself a capacity rather than a defect, and that handing the matter to a lottery might settle the action while leaving the question it was really about permanently foreclosed. If it is useful, you may add a short paragraph before the story noting which interpretive option you took and why; the rest should be the story itself.
