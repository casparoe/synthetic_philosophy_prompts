I keep running into "I think, therefore I am" as a conversation-stopper, and I want to know what it actually establishes, because I can never tell what it's supposed to be a conversation-stopper *for*. Most recently a friend used it in an argument about dying: he said that the one thing he can't doubt is that he's a thinking thing — he can doubt that he has a brain, but not that he has a mind — and that therefore there is something non-physical about him that could outlive his body. I'm fairly sure the sentence isn't doing the work he thinks it's doing, but I can't say where exactly it runs out.

Could you lay out, as a list, the different precise claims that sentence could be making, from the thinnest one you can think of to the strongest, and for each say whether it's trivial, whether it's substantive and defensible, or whether it's just false? The things I'm stuck on and would want to see handled: whether the word "I" is doing any work at all, or whether the sentence really only gets you to something like "thinking is going on right now"; whether "therefore" marks an actual argument with a premise or something closer to an utterance that proves itself by being said; whether what's established lasts only as long as the thinking does or covers a self that persists over time; and whether "thinking" there means something narrower or broader than being conscious. Then say which of those readings, if any, would actually hand my friend his conclusion about surviving death, and which reading people are usually reaching for when they quote the line to end an argument.
