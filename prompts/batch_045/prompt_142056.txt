I write novels — four out, the fifth in the miserable middle — and over the last few years I've drifted into something like a steadfast view about my own work. When my editor, my agent, or the two friends who read drafts tell me a chapter isn't working and my gut says it is, I don't split the difference. I listen, I usually change something, but I keep whatever it was I was committed to, and I don't really lower my confidence at all. I've got a small pile of arguments for this and I want to know what would knock it down. So don't be gentle, and don't bother naming papers or who said what first — I can't look anything up and I don't care about priority, I care whether the position holds.

Here are my arguments, so you can aim at them individually rather than at "steadfastness" in general.

1. I'm not a peer of my readers about my book, because I have a different kind of evidence, not just more of it. I know the two hundred pages I cut, the character who got folded into another character, the thing the whole book is quietly building toward that hasn't been written yet. Their evidence is the text; mine is the text plus the process. The usual conciliationist reply is that I should count my extra evidence once and then still treat the disagreement as evidence — but my claim is that process knowledge isn't more evidence about the same question, it's evidence about a different question: not does this work but is this the book I'm making. If that's right there may be no disagreement to conciliate, just two compatible verdicts.

2. The object moves. In the standard cases the disputed proposition is fixed — the bill, the diagnosis, the forecast. My chapter isn't. Whatever I decide becomes part of the book that the next person reads. So there's no stable fact about what the book is like if I defer, independent of whether I defer, which makes splitting the difference feel not just unwise but ill-defined.

3. Deference atrophy. I make maybe fifty of these calls a year. If I defer each time someone whose taste I respect disagrees, I erode the thing that made me worth deferring to. This is obviously a cousin of the spinelessness worry people raise against the equal-weight view, but I think it bites harder for me than in the seminar cases, because the calls compound — deferring isn't a one-off cost, it's training.

4. Belief versus action. Even if I should lower my confidence that the thing works, I'm not choosing a credence, I'm choosing a revision. And the costs are lopsided: keeping a chapter that's secretly broken is usually fixable two drafts later, whereas rebuilding a book around someone else's instinct can quietly wreck it and I won't find out for a year. So the decision can go my way even if the belief shouldn't.

What I want: what would count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether someone who already believes what I believe should concede it as counting against the view, or whether they'd reasonably shrug it off.

Some things I want you to specifically chew on.

There's an obvious empirical test available to me that almost nobody in the peer-disagreement literature has: I have a decade of drafts. If I went back and found that the passages I was most confident about are the ones I later cut, and the ones I was talked into keeping are the ones that survived, that looks bad for me. Would it actually be decisive? Or is there a natural way for a steadfast person to explain that pattern away — and if there is, is that a sign the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability?

Relatedly: am I insulated? Every time I turn out to be wrong I can say I wasn't really confident, or that reader wasn't really a peer, or that it was a special case. At what point does that stop being a fair description of exceptions and start being a view that can't lose? And is that automatically a problem, or are there positions of this shape where insulation is fine?

There's also a test I'm slightly afraid of: if a reader who shares my tastes, has read the whole manuscript, and has also read the cut material still disagrees — I've basically manufactured a peer by handing over my evidence. Does argument 1 survive that? And separately: would I react differently to one reader disagreeing than to six? If honest answer is no, then what I have isn't a view about evidence, it's obstinacy with footnotes.

Finally, the thing I'm least sure about, and maybe the most interesting: is the book I'm making a legitimate object of judgment at all? My whole position leans on the idea that there's a fact about what I'm building that isn't on the page yet. Readers tend to act as though the book just is what's on the page. If they're right about that, argument 1 and argument 2 both collapse and I should probably be a conciliator after all. Say what you think, and say what would settle it.

Practical hook, since it's live this week: my editor and one of my first readers both say the last thirty pages of book five collapse. I think they're right about the mechanism and wrong about the fix, and I want to keep the ending and repair the approach to it. Don't just tell me what to do with the ending — I want the structure of the argument, and then you can tell me what it implies.
