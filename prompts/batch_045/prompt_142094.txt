I've been asked to give the laudatio — the five-minute tribute speech — at the banquet where my doctoral mentor, Professor Marion Ashgrove, receives our professional association's Vance Medal for Distinguished Contribution. Complication: Marion is the first recipient chosen under the association's new procedure, which she herself argued for and which is announced from the podium every year — the committee shortlists the finalists whose dossiers it judges cannot be ranked, then draws the winner by lot, on the grounds that the medal certifies a standard reached rather than a ranking, and that pretending to rank people who have all cleared the standard misrepresents what the committee actually knows. I think she is only half right: the randomization launders luck out of the last step while leaving untouched the luckier matter of who became a finalist at all — native speed, a stable department, a fellowship application that got misfiled into the right pile in 1986, which is a story she has always told on herself. Her catchphrase, which generations of her students can recite, is that nobody is self-made, we are all carried. Half the room are philosophers, legal theorists, and education economists who will hear every move I make; the other half are her family, a dozen of the first-generation undergraduates she spent thirty years hauling through a summer programme, and Hal Vance himself, eighty-eight, at the head table, whose endowment letter says the medal exists to honour those who earned distinction by talent and perseverance. One of the other shortlisted finalists is at the next table and speaks immediately after me.

So I need the speech to do something structurally awkward: praise a forty-year career, and a person, in the idiom of an award whose whole genre is desert-ascription, without claiming that she earned the medal in any deep backward-looking sense or that she beat the others; without implying to her family and her students that her life's work was a coin flip or that the medal means nothing; and without saying anything that lands, in front of the students she carried, as the condescending thought that their achievements are luck too. Write me the speech, about 700 words, in a register that works aloud after dinner — warm, concrete, at most one inside joke the non-philosophers can still follow. Assume I mean what I say about luck and desert and cannot simply assert what I disbelieve. After the text, add a short note flagging the two or three places where the luck problem forced a particular piece of phrasing, and naming the tempting phrasing each one was avoiding.
