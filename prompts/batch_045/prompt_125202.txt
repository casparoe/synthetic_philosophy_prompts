I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about presentism in historiography — the kind that begins after dinner and ends with neither of us able to state the other's position in a form the other would accept. I am not asking you to adjudicate who won. I want to know why a chain of reasoning I conceded step by step ends somewhere I am certain is wrong.

We had agreed to set aside the metaphysical version of presentism (the thesis that later events alter what was true of earlier ones) and to argue only about the methodological version: whether historians can, should, or even could avoid describing and appraising the past by the standards of their own time. He then offered the following, and I granted each step as it came.

(1) Framing is inescapable. Any history requires a selection of subject, a periodization, a vocabulary, and a working sense of which differences among the surviving materials are significant. None of these can be drawn from nowhere; they have to be drawn from concepts available to the historian now. A history written from no present vantage point is not an ideal we fail to reach; it is not a possible object.

(2) So a historian cannot avoid the framework she in fact employs. Whatever she writes, she writes it under some present set of standards, and there is no external position from which that framework could have been chosen or refused.

(3) Ought implies can. If she could not have avoided writing under that framework, she is not open to criticism for writing under it.

(4) Therefore presentism is not a vice. It is the condition under which history gets written at all. The familiar complaint that some historian has illegitimately imposed her own period's categories on the past — the Cambridge-school strictures against anachronism, the old objection to narratives organized around what led to us — is, taken strictly, a demand no one could meet, and a demand no one could meet is not a demand.

(5) The reasoning is temporally symmetric; call it the Diachronic Parity Principle. If a historian's present framework cannot ground a criticism, then no historian's can, including those who will one day write about us. He made this concrete: imagine historians of 2320, scrupulous and evidence-hungry, who describe our liberal democracies as a regime of managed appetite, our medicine as a systematic cruelty, and our environmental policy as a form of negligence so ordinary that no one noticed it — and who organize their entire narrative around the catastrophe they believe we were sleepwalking into. On the argument so far there is no methodological defect to point at; there is only a framework we happen not to share.

(6) Yet we do criticize historians of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries for letting the racial and civilizational categories of their moment do the descriptive and explanatory work in their accounts of, say, the Roman Empire or the Atlantic revolutions — and we treat this not as a difference of taste but as a defect in the history, sometimes the most important thing there is to say about it. Relatedly, we credit those same historians for what they established about the sources, which seems to require that something in their work floats free of their framework.

(7) So either the practice in (6) is an illusion — our criticisms of earlier historians are only expressions of our own framework, and there is no notion of progress in history-writing left to appeal to — or one of (1) through (5) is false.

I find that I want to keep (1), and I want to keep the practice in (6), and I am not willing to pay for both with the claim that history is only power. That means something has to give at (2), (3), or (5), and I cannot see which, or what the giving-up costs.

So: where exactly does this go wrong? Name the step and explain why it looked innocent. If more than one step is load-bearing, tell me which rejection is cheapest and price the others — in particular whether even the cheapest rejection forces me to abandon the idea of progress in (6), and whether my friend's argument can be repaired once the flaw is removed. Please argue for a diagnosis rather than survey the positions available on presentism; I can read those myself.
