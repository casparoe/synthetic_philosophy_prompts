I am writing an opinionated overview of dialetheism for a general philosophical readership, and I want the spine of the piece to be a ranking rather than a catalogue: instead of listing the considerations that have been offered for taking some contradictions to be true, I want them ordered by how much dialectical work they actually do. Below are six arguments, stated in my own words. Please do not attribute them to anyone, do not cite or point to any literature, and do not treat familiarity or prestige as a consideration: evaluate the arguments exactly as stated.

(a) The semantic paradoxes. A fully transparent truth predicate — one for which a sentence and the statement that the sentence is true can be substituted for one another in every non-opaque context, so that both directions of the naive equivalence and full intersubstitutivity hold without restriction — can be retained only if the sentence that says of itself that it is not true comes out both true and false. Rival treatments each pay a different price: gap treatments lose the ability to state, within the language, that the paradoxical sentence is not true; hierarchical and contextual treatments give up the single unrestricted equivalence; treatments on which the sentence gets no stable status give up bivalence in a way that cannot be expressed in the language itself. Only the glutty treatment keeps all three, and the price it pays is one the dialetheist was willing to pay anyway.

(b) Naive comprehension. The same shape of argument applied to sets: unrestricted comprehension plus standard syntax generates the Russell contradiction, and every classical response restricts comprehension in a way that is motivated by the need to avoid the contradiction rather than by the concept of set. A paraconsistent set theory keeps comprehension unrestricted and contains the contradiction without triviality, because the consequence relation is not explosive.

(c) Structural uniformity. The semantic and set-theoretic paradoxes, together with the paradoxes that arise at the limits of thought and of description, all instantiate a single structure: a totality, a diagonal or self-applicative construction defined on it, and a clause guaranteeing that the object so constructed both falls inside and falls outside the totality in question. Because the recurrence is structural, patching each case with its own restriction treats symptoms; the uniform response is to accept the contradiction that the structure generates.

(d) Vagueness. Borderline cases of a vague predicate are cases in which the predicate both applies and fails to apply. This preserves the individual Sorites premises, explains the characteristic oscillation of judgment and the felt arbitrariness of any sharp cutoff, and does the work without a third truth value that behaves like a gap for some purposes and like a glut for others.

(e) Normative conflict. Legal codes, constitutions, and systems of obligation routinely generate conflicts that survive every available technique of resolution. In the hard cases a person both has and lacks a given permission, or both is and is not required to perform some act, and no interpretation, priority rule, or revision restores coherence except by deleting part of the normative data. The correct description of such a case is a true normative contradiction.

(f) An argument of my own. Suppose two theories are each the best-confirmed account of their own domain, and suppose further that the bridge principles connecting their vocabularies are themselves well confirmed. Then the conjunction of everything we have most reason to accept is inconsistent. If acceptance is closed under conjunction, and if there is no principled basis for demoting either theory or the bridge principles, we are committed to an inconsistent total theory; and since the inconsistency is not traceable to any removable error, we are committed to at least one true contradiction.

Two ground rules. First, assume each argument's formal machinery is in good order: do not object that the relevant formalisms are unsettled, that the details are contested, or that the argument needs to be spelled out more rigorously. The question is what the argument would establish if it worked. Second, score the arguments on two dimensions and weight them as indicated:

A. Alethic yield (weight 0.6). Does the argument, if sound, reach the full thesis — that some contradictions are true, in the sense that the correct total description of things is inconsistent — or only a weaker one: either (i) that some bodies of information are inconsistent and must be reasoned from without triviality, which is a thesis about consequence or about rational acceptance, or (ii) that our representational apparatus generates contradictions we cannot eliminate, which may be an artifact of the apparatus rather than a feature of what it represents? Score 9 or 10 for arguments that reach the full thesis; 4 to 6 for arguments that reach only (i) or (ii); 0 to 3 for arguments that reach neither.

B. Evasion-resistance (weight 0.4). How much of the argument survives once the standard replies are applied: for (a) through (c), gap, hierarchy, contextualist, and revision-theoretic treatments; for (d), supervaluationist, epistemicist, and many-valued competitors; for (e), priority rules, excision, and the distinction between genuine and merely apparent obligations; and for all of them, non-adjunction (accepting each of two claims while declining to accept their conjunction), compartmentalization, and classical recapture, i.e. the result that over any region one can show to be free of contradiction the paraconsistent reasoner can reason exactly classically.
