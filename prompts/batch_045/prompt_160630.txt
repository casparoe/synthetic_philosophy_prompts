I work as a research assistant to a retired diplomat who now writes and broadcasts on the ethics of force, and I am the one assembling a table of everything she has publicly committed to, because she has agreed to give evidence to a parliamentary committee on humanitarian intervention next month and has a bad habit of saying that each case must be judged on its own facts. The track record I have managed to reconstruct from her columns, her memoir and two interviews is this. On Kosovo in 1999 she backed the NATO air campaign despite the absence of Security Council authorization, and she continues to insist that legal status and moral justification can come apart; she also always notes that the point was to stop the expulsion of the Albanian population and that the Serbian leadership was left in office afterwards. On Iraq in 2003 she was opposed from the start: no authorization, no ongoing mass atrocity, regime change announced as policy in advance, the threat assessment overstated, and the aftermath catastrophic. On Libya in 2011 she gives what I think of as her split verdict: the initial air campaign, authorized by the Council to protect civilians, was right; the arming of the rebels and the pursuit of the leadership turned protection into a war of overthrow; and she treats the collapse that followed as part of the verdict rather than as bad luck. On Syria in 2013 she opposed strikes after the chemical attack outside Damascus, on the grounds that they would have been unauthorized, that nobody had a plan for what came next, that escalation was likely, and that some of the rebels she did not want armed would have benefited. On Rwanda in 1994 she calls the failure to reinforce and re-mandate the mission already on the ground one of the great moral failures of her professional lifetime, and she does not treat the lack of authorization as any kind of excuse. On the Indian intervention in East Pakistan in 1971 she has said it was on balance right, though she never fails to add that it ended in the dismemberment of a state, and I cannot get her to say how much that counts against it.

The case she will be asked about is not a historical one. The government of Vardania has for fourteen months been driving the Orran minority out of two northern provinces, using regular army units together with allied militias; UN monitors on the ground estimate forty thousand dead and six hundred thousand displaced, and their public forecast is several hundred thousand more deaths if the campaign continues through the winter. A Security Council resolution authorizing force to protect civilians was put to a vote and vetoed by a permanent member that supplies the Vardanian air force and has a basing agreement with it. A regional organisation of neighbouring states then authorized an air campaign, which its members are now flying, with no ground component; the mandate as written covers only the aircraft and artillery being used against civilians. The intervener has publicly refused to arm the Orran self-defence militias, though a third country is doing so quietly and everyone knows it. In the fifth week, after the government began directing attacks on civilians from the national command bunker under the presidential palace, the bunker was struck and the president and much of the high command were killed; the regular army then defected to the rebels rather than dissolving, so unlike Iraq the officer corps and the ministries survived more or less intact, and the capital fell three weeks later. Revenge killings of civilians belonging to the formerly dominant group have since claimed, by the monitors' count, something in the low thousands. The intervening state also has a second reason for being there: it wants to keep the port access the old government granted it, and it has said nothing to dispel that impression.

I have to write the briefing note predicting what she will say, and I would rather get the structure of her thinking right than the bottom line. As I read the table, at least four rules fit all six of her verdicts about equally well: a rule requiring multilateral authorization with a narrow exception for ongoing mass atrocity the Council has failed to address; a rule that counts authorization only as evidence and asks whether the force was necessary, proportionate in its means and expected to leave things better; a rule that turns on whether a new political outcome was imposed rather than merely foreseen; and a rule that asks whether the intervener's motives were sufficiently clean. My sense is that these come apart sharply on Vardania, because it is at once unauthorized, atrocity-stopping, means-limited, lethal to a head of state, outcome-imposing, aftermath-tolerable and motive-mixed, and the surface analog is Libya while the deeper ones are Kosovo and 1971. So: what verdict should I expect her to give, and should I write it up as approval, condemnation, or another split; which of those rules is she most plausibly running, and what does each of the others predict here; where exactly do the two closest candidates diverge and how confident should I be; if her track record genuinely underdetermines the case, what principle would she be committing herself to with each of the available answers; and finally, what hypothetical variant should I put to her in the prep session to force the surviving rules apart?
