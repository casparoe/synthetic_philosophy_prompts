I retired two years ago after forty-one years as a claims examiner for a health insurer, and since then I have been reading Thomas Szasz on his own terms — The Myth of Mental Illness and a good deal of what both admirers and critics say about him. In my working life a diagnostic code was never a discovery about a body. It was a permission slip: the thing that made a service payable, a claim defensible, a disability finding possible. That is probably why Szasz's position lands on me the way it does — that mental illness is a myth rather than a disease, that the people so labeled have problems in living rather than lesions, and that psychiatric diagnosis is chiefly an instrument for the exercise of state-backed power over adults who have done nothing to anyone. I want to test that framework against an episode I remember from the newspapers as a young man: the American Psychiatric Association's removal of homosexuality from its diagnostic manual in the 1970s. I have never seen the two run together at length, and I would like it done carefully, in both directions — Szasz on the episode, and the episode on Szasz.

The facts as I have them, so that nothing here turns on your being able to look them up: homosexuality was listed in the first DSM in 1952 and again, unambiguously, as a sexual deviation in the DSM-II of 1968. Activists disrupted the APA's annual conventions in the early 1970s; in December 1973 the board of trustees voted for a compromise, and in 1974 the membership confirmed it by referendum. The seventh printing of the DSM-II accordingly replaced the homosexuality code with sexual orientation disturbance, covering people whose same-sex attractions left them disturbed, in conflict, or wanting to change — accompanied by a note that homosexuality on its own is not a psychiatric disorder. The DSM-III of 1980, produced under Robert Spitzer, who had drafted the 1973 compromise, carried a successor category, ego-dystonic homosexuality, whose criteria turned on unwanted homosexual arousal and absent heterosexual arousal; it was dropped in the 1987 revision. The World Health Organization kept an equivalent until the ICD-11 removed it.

Here is what I want mapped rather than asserted. On Szasz's account, who were the parties and what were they actually doing to each other — the trustees, the protesting activists, the rank-and-file who voted by mail, the researchers whose work on homosexual men was cited, and the people who ended up in the residual category? What did the framework say each of them should have done? I ask because it looks to me as though every available move conceded the thing Szasz denies: demanding a favorable certificate from the profession acknowledges its authority to issue certificates, and a vote on whether a desire is a disease concedes that there is such a question.

I have also been playing with two devices of my own, and I want them tested rather than flattered. The first is the repeal test: a genuine disease cannot be voted out of existence, so whatever a professional body can strike from its manual was never a disease to begin with. Does Szasz need that test, does it belong to him, and does it prove too much — would it make every contested somatic diagnosis a myth by the same argument? The second is a distinction among three ways a profession can lose a diagnosis: outright repeal, which returns the person to the status of an agent; relocation, where the category reappears as a disorder of distress or volition and jurisdiction is quietly retained; and re-description, where the conduct is declared normal but only with a residual clause. Which of the three happened here, in which order, and does Szasz's framework predict relocation rather than repeal?

Two things strike me as places where the framework may be silent rather than merely untested. The ego-dystonic category was, by its own criteria, a diagnosis that patients asked for, and Szasz's settled line was that consenting adults may contract for any help they want — which seems to leave him unable to object to a category whose main historical effect, as critics tell it, was to keep conversion therapy inside medicine for another decade. And Szasz has almost nothing to say about the apparatus I spent my career inside: if a code is the only way to get paid, then declassifying can injure the very people it liberates. Is that an anomaly for his theory, or an external complication it never claimed to handle?

Please give me an honest verdict on where Szasz's account explains the episode, where it misdescribes it, and where it has nothing to say; whether the episode on balance confirms his framework or is an anomaly for it; and whether some other lens — a sociology-of-professions story about jurisdiction, or an account on which diagnoses allocate resources and exemptions — does better on the parts he cannot reach. I do not want reading recommendations or a bibliography, only the analysis.
