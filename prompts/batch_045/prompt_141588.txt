I'm a fourth-year PhD student in comparative-historical sociology, and this term I'm the TA for a graduate seminar on macro-causal explanation whose enrolment is, awkwardly, about a third historians, a third economists, and a third evolutionary biologists. For the first session I want a handout that does something I have never managed to find in the literature: a decision tree that starts from what students say about concrete cases and ends at a named position on whether history is, in any interesting sense, locked in — whether, had some small thing gone differently, we would have ended up somewhere else entirely, or whether the same kind of outcome would have arrived anyway by another route. I want them to fill it in during week one, before I hand out any of the canonical readings, so nothing in the tree may depend on knowing a label or an author's name: every branch node has to be a question about a case that a capable first-year could answer cold, with no prior exposure to the debate. Seven to nine branch nodes and six to eight leaves, please; I have the readings and citations already, so what I need is the instrument itself, not a bibliography.

I have become convinced, after a long argument with a friend in evolutionary biology, that a large share of the disagreement here is really disagreement about which counterfactual is being run and about how fine-grained the description of the outcome is, and I would like the tree to track three distinctions explicitly. First, the counterfactual operation: removing a single event and letting everything else run forward (rewind); re-running an entire sequence from an early starting point under the same initial conditions (replay); or nudging one parameter slightly and watching whether the trajectory diverges (perturbation). For each node, say which of these it presupposes, and flag any leaf that is really just another leaf's position under a different counterfactual. Second, the identity of the outcome: the same token outcome; the same function served by an institution built very differently; or sameness only in some coarse respect while distributional and welfare-relevant properties diverge for decades. The phrase "the same thing would have happened anyway" should never get through the tree without being forced into one of these. Third, the mechanism of persistence: a genuinely self-reinforcing ratchet that makes reversal costlier the further along you are; a one-off redirection that nothing subsequently reinforced; a sensitivity to small differences that washes out at the aggregate level because the process has attractors; or path dependence located not in outcomes at all but in the administrative categories and data infrastructure through which later actors perceive their situation. I would rather you build the tree from that space of combinations than from a list of received positions, so please also mark the combinations nobody occupies and say in a line whether that is because they are incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored — and flag any two leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate rather than manufacturing a distinction between them. Each leaf should carry a name, or "unclaimed" where you think nobody holds it, plus one line on the commitment its holder has to swallow.

The cases I want used are all invented or anonymised so that nobody can recognise a famous example and answer from memory: the harbour gauge, where a port authority in 1888 adopts standard X over Y on a vote swung by one member's late arrival, after which cranes, training regimes and supply contracts co-adapt within a decade and reversal becomes unthinkable for a century; the delayed envoy, where a treaty collapses because a negotiator's ship is becalmed for three weeks, war resumes, and the settlement forty years later reproduces the rejected terms on territory while departing from them entirely on the fiscal clauses; the two valleys, where two regions in similar hydrological conditions and with no contact independently build near-identical hierarchical irrigation bureaucracies within a century of each other; the pension convergence, where two provinces both reach near-universal old-age provision by 1960, one through a tax-funded flat pension and the other through occupationally fragmented insurance funds, so that coverage rates converge while replacement rates and gendered outcomes diverge for fifty years; the slow follower, where a fiscal innovation appears in one polity in 1720 and independently in another in 1790, with a different institutional design and a different coalition behind it; the founding memo, where a filing and classification scheme adopted without discussion by a mid-level official becomes the inherited grid for all later administrative statistics and renders one large category of hardship invisible for three generations, while substantive policy changes repeatedly and the categories do not; and the rotating chair, where which of twelve reformers happens to chair a committee wildly changes the next five years of policy but every variant converges on a professionalised, exam-recruited civil service. Finally, work two example paths through the finished tree: one for a student who says the harbour gauge and the founding memo are contingent but the two valleys and the slow follower are not, and one for a student who treats every case as contingent in its details but inevitable in its broad function. Tell me where each lands, and where the tree gives a determinate answer and where it does not.
