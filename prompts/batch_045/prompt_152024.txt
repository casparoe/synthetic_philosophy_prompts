I take minutes for the release review board at the AI lab I work at, and I've started to notice a pattern in how we argue that I can't stop thinking about. Every time someone proposes a safety measure with a real cost attached — hold the checkpoint until the interpretability eval is done, don't ship the fine-tune, spend three months on scalable oversight before the next run — somebody says some version of "that's an alignment tax we can't pay, the other labs won't pay it, we just lose the race, and nothing gets safer because the capability shows up anyway." That argument always wins. But the same board happily ate a nine month delay last year when a hardware vendor couldn't deliver compliant chips, and most of the people making the competitiveness argument are also cheerfully in favor of export controls and mandatory reporting requirements, i.e. for rules that force everyone to go slower together. So the futility argument gets deployed against safety measures and basically nothing else, and I want to know what you'd have to beleive for that to be coherent rather than just convenient.

My reading group keeps going back and forth between two reconstructions and I can't tell which one the practice really needs. One is straight causal: if the risky capability is overdetermined — someone else builds it whether or not we do — then our restraint changes no outcomes, it costs us the prize and buys nothing, whereas the hardware delay changed something real. The other isn't consequentialist at all: it's a fair-play or conditional-cooperation idea, that duties of restraint are owed only against others who are restraining too, and a unilateral slowdown is just us subsidising everyone else. What I can't figure out is whether either of those explains the selectivity. Both look like reasons that would also tell you to skip clinical-trial-style testing or to quietly ignore an export control if you could get away with it, and nobody on the board believes that. So is the practice secretly resting on something specific about this technology — that leads compound, that being first is winner-take-most, that safety research leaks to competitors so whoever pays the tax doesn't even capture the benefit — and if so, which of those claims is actually doing the work?

Here's the wrinkle that makes me think the board might be contradicting itself. A friend of mine has this distinction between a deferred alignment tax (you get the capability later, plus the safety property) and a permanent one (you end up with strictly less capability, forever), and he points out that "we lose the race" only really bites for a deferred tax if the race is decided by who arrives first and the lead compounds — if it's decided by where everyone ends up eventually, then only permanent taxes cost you anything at all. And a deferred tax that everyone pays is just a slower schedule for the whole field, which is more or less what the export-control crowd wants anyway. So: what does this practice strictly presuppose, what does it merely fit well with, where is it underdetermined so that several theories would license the same behavior, and what would have to change in how we actually run these reviews if we dropped the competitiveness framing and took some rival view seriously — say, the view that you shouldn't be the one who imposes a certain kind of risk regardless of what everyone else is doing?
