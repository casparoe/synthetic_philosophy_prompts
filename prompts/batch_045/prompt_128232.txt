I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on comparative political institutions: 24 students, one 90-minute session, no budget, and nothing beyond a room with movable chairs and a stack of printed cards. We have already been through the standard textbook contrast between presidential and parliamentary government — separate origin, fixed terms, fusion versus separation of powers, the mechanics of confidence — and what I want now is for students to confront something that contrast papers over: it bundles together at least two logically independent variables (whether the executive and assembly are chosen separately, and whether the executive's tenure depends on continuing assembly support), and the incentive consequences they care about seem to track one of these much more than the other. I want them to feel this rather than be told it.

Please design a runnable simulation for that session. My requirements:

The same 24 students play three short rounds back to back, with preferences held fixed across rounds and only the constitutional rules varied, so that any difference in outcomes is attributable to the rules and not to renegotiated tastes. Students are dealt into five parties of four or five, each party receiving a printed payoff schedule over three policy packages, and each party additionally receiving a private "electoral exposure" figure: the change in its payoff if an election were held immediately. Some parties should gain from an immediate election and some lose heavily. That device is what makes dissolution either a threat or a gift, and it needs to be heterogeneous enough that no single majority is uniformly election-averse or election-hungry.

The three regimes should be: (A) an executive selected by the assembly and removable by it, where a successful removal also dissolves the assembly and forces an election; (B) an executive separately chosen for a fixed term, unremovable, whose preferred package passes unless the assembly overrides it by a supermajority; (C) an executive separately chosen, whom the assembly may dismiss once, at no cost to itself and with no election, in favour of a caretaker drawn from a pre-agreed roster whose policy preference is publicly known in advance. Please supply actual payoff numbers, the supermajority threshold, the caretaker's specified preference, and a rationale for each number.

Students will arrive predicting that assembly influence rises monotonically from regime B to regime A to regime C, on the grounds that a dismissal power is simply more power. I want a design in which that prediction is genuinely contestable rather than obviously right or obviously wrong. So: tell me honestly whether, under the numbers you actually choose, the naive monotonicity prediction holds or fails; if it fails, identify precisely which feature of the numbers breaks it and what would have to be different for the prediction to come out true; and if it holds, say so and explain which part of my expectation about the mutual-destruction rule was mistaken. I would rather have correct reasoning about your own design than a rigged demonstration of the point I expected.

End with a ten-minute beat in which each party drafts a one-line constitutional amendment that would best serve its own interests, and the amendments are then clustered on the board. The aim is for the clusters to fall into two families that map onto the two variables above, with students noticing the mapping themselves.

Also include: a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 90 minutes including setup and teardown; a debrief that maps specific moments in the rounds onto the two-variable decomposition, explains what the mutual-destruction rule does to the credibility of a threat, and makes the point that "who can dismiss whom" is far less informative than "who pays what when the government falls"; a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students treating it as a personality contest, parties failing to coalesce in the time allowed, the executive player being either ignored or allowed to dominate, the election mechanic never being triggered so that the electoral-exposure device never bites — with a fix for each; and an honest account of what the simulation cannot reproduce about real presidential and parliamentary government, which I will have to supply afterwards.

Finally: the materials handed to students should not use the words "presidential," "parliamentary," "semi-presidential," or "vote of confidence." Call the three arrangements Regime A, B, and C, and let the labels arrive only in the debrief.
