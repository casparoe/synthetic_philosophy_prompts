I've been arguing about panpsychism on a rationalist-adjacent forum for months and have ended up holding a position that nobody there will attack properly — every objection I get is either the one about introspective access to micro-experiences (which I think is a dud) or a restatement of the combination problem I was trying to solve in the first place. So I want you to attack it. Please answer as a professional philosopher of mind would to a colleague who genuinely wants to be wrong: before writing anything, brainstorm candidate defeaters beyond the standard set, drawing on grounding, material constitution, and causation as well as philosophy of mind, and then rank them. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — how exposed the position actually is, and which single defeater does the most damage — then give the reasoning without preamble and without restating my question back to me.

Here is the position; I call it constitutionalist panpsychism. Macro-experiences are not *composed of* micro-experiences, they are *constituted by* them, in roughly the sense in which a statue is constituted by, but not identical to, the clay that makes it up at a time. The micro-subjects and their experiences are the total grounds; the macro-subject's experience is nothing over and above them, yet it is neither one of them nor a mereological sum of them. The appeal, for me, is that this looks like it converts the combination problem into somebody else's problem: whatever makes it true that the clay constitutes a statue rather than two objects or no statue at all is supposed to do the corresponding work for experiences. The sub-principle doing the real work is a grain-tracking claim: the grain of experience follows the grain of causal integration. There is a subject at — tentatively, only at — each local maximum of causal integration in the physical world, and the contents of that subject's experience are fixed by the causal structure of the system at that maximum; phenomenal structure mirrors causal structure not by stipulation but because that is what it is for a plurality of micro-experiences to constitute a single macro-experience.

So: what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with each item marked as one a committed constitutionalist should genuinely concede versus one they would be right to dismiss, and marked again as to whether it is distinctive to my view or shared with every non-eliminativist about macro-experience. Three things in particular. First, the collapse worry, which I personally find most persuasive: if constitution is not identity and the macro-experience is a further fact about the arranged micros, haven't I simply bought the brute emergence I was trying to avoid, and is there any daylight left between my view and the emergentist rival? Steelman that argument, and then tell me honestly whether it lands. Second, the subject explosion: if there is a subject at every local maximum of integration, a human brain plausibly contains many nested and overlapping integrated systems, and I get far more subjects than I bargained for. My patch is an exclusion principle — subjects only at maxima, so that sub-system subjects are shadowed, their experiences real but constituents of nothing further. Tell me whether that patch is ad hoc, and test it on the case I have been using: three micro-subjects A, B, C, where A+B and B+C are each causally integrated, A+C is not, and A+B+C is also a single integrated system. How many subjects are there, whose experiences are shadowed, and is a view that answers the way mine must still recognizably panpsychist? Third, tell me plainly whether I have qualified the view into unfalsifiability: "causal integration" is doing a great deal of unspecified work, and if I can always relocate the subject to whichever level makes the answer come out right, then do I risk anything at all — and if the answer is no, say whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of something this abstract. For calibration: I would be moved most by a result showing that reported unity of experience comes apart from causal integration (split-brain subjects are the case people reach for, though I am not confident what the data actually show), and least by anything resting on what we can and cannot introspect.
