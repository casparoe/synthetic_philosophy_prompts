ok so I had a three hour argument with my housemate last night about dutch book arguments, and Ive got to teach them in a section tomorrow morning and I realise I still dont have a plan, just a lot of resentment.

Setup: second-year philosophy students, one 60 minute section. Theyve had a week of basic probability (the axioms, conditional probability, bayes' theorem as a formula) and nothing at all on what probability means. So I have to introduce credences as a thing and then run the betting argument for probabilism inside the same hour, and then next week someone else does all the updating stuff. My housemate (econ phd, extremely annoying) says the whole thing is circular: that a dutch book only shows you shouldnt take sucker bets, that it assumes betting behaviour tracks belief and then pretends to conclude something about belief, and that you will lose money is a prudential reason dressed up as an epistemic one. I think hes wrong about the first bit, half right about the last one, and I want the section to actually land on that instead of just doing the algebra.

So dont explain dutch book arguments to me. Give me the teaching plan.

- The order the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what. Do I define a betting quotient as a fair price before or after I say what a dutch book is? Non-additivity and the price on a tautology: separate cases, or one example seen two ways? And where exactly does it go, that the bookie gets to pick both the direction and the stake, and can bet against you as well as with you? That feels like the bit the whole argument secretly rests on and also the bit easiest to lose the room on.

- The one example that carries everything. I want a single event space and a single table of prices I can leave on the board and reuse for each violation (non-additivity, then the tautology / certainty case, then the ratio or conditional-probability one if there is time). Tell me what that example has to have and what it has to lack. I keep coming back to a horse race, or else a coin drawn from a bag that might be double headed, but I dont think either does all three jobs and Im not sure why.

- The misconceptions, in the order they will actually form, and the point in the sequence where each gets killed. My list: (a) this is about money, or about gambling; (b) it only shows you can be exploited, and being exploitable isnt the same as being irrational; (c) surely you would just decline the bets; (d) the sneaky one, where they assume a dutch book requires you to accept both sides of every bet offered; (e) mixing up a synchronic dutch book with a diachronic one, or with a money pump; (f) thinking the result says any incoherent person does lose money in real life. Which of these am I missing, which ones are actually the same mistake wearing two hats, and which should be pre-empted before they form rather than corrected afterwards?

- What I should flat out refuse to get into at this level, and what I should name out loud as something Im refusing. The converse direction, whether coherence arguments get you all the way to probabilism rather than just to some constraints, whether betting quotients measure credence at all, scoring rules as an alternative route, and the why be coherent regress. Id much rather say these exist, say Im not doing them, and move on than get dragged into one with ten minutes left.

- And the check at the end. One question I can put to them whose answer would show me they got the dependency structure rather than just the algebra: that whatever normative force the argument has rides on the bridge between prices and credences, and on the bookie being free to choose, and not on the fact that losing money is bad. Ideally the question is such that a student who only followed the algebra gives a visibly different answer from a student who got the point, and I can tell them apart in about thirty seconds.

If it helps: Id rather they leave slightly unsettled about whether the argument really proves anything than confident that it proves something it doesnt.
