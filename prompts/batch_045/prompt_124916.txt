You are a professional philosopher with research-level command of philosophy of mind, metaphilosophy, and the philosophy of cognitive science. I would like you to think carefully and slowly about the question below, and to begin with an explicit brainstorming pass: generate five to eight candidate diagnoses drawn from different areas (philosophy of mind, metaphilosophy, philosophy of language, philosophy of science, social epistemology), including several that you will then reject, and say plainly why each is rejected. Please distinguish throughout between established terminology, your own proposal, and what is contested in the literature. I am not asking for a label; I am asking for the carving. If the honest answer is that the phenomenon has no established name, say so and give me a name, a definition, and the boundary cases.

Here is the pattern I keep running into. I work in human-computer interaction on assistive cognitive technology, which is why I read philosophy of mind at all, and the pattern is sharpest for me in the debate about whether a person's mind can literally extend beyond the skull — the literature that grew out of the much-discussed case of a man with a memory impairment who carries a notebook whose contents are as reliably and automatically available to him as a neurotypical person's biological memories are to her.

The first part of the pattern is familiar. A proponent says the notebook is literally where some of his beliefs are stored; the boundary of the cognitive system runs out into the world. A critic replies that at most he has shown causal dependence, and that causing is not constituting. The proponent replies that the critic is helping himself to undefended criteria for what counts as cognition, that nothing in contemporary cognitive science respects the skull, and that where the boundary falls is an empirical and theoretical question rather than one to be settled by intuitions about what looks mental. The critic replies that in that case the original claim was never the empirical discovery it was advertised as, but an invitation to redraw a line and call it a finding. The proponent replies that there are good explanatory, practical and ethical reasons to redraw it there.

What interests me is the next bit, which I have not seen discussed. Take a dispute that runs in the opposite direction. Someone argues that a choice made under the influence of a brain tumour, a deep-brain stimulator, a hormone, or an automated subpersonal habit system is not really the agent's: the mechanism sits inside the skull, but it is not part of her. Now the theorists who, twenty pages earlier, insisted that the skull has no privileged status and that the location of the boundary is a matter for empirical theory often say something different in spirit — that the question is not straightforwardly descriptive, that it depends on what we want our notion of a person or an agent to do for us, on our practices of holding people responsible, on what we care about. And the theorists who in the first dispute insisted that boundary questions are interest-relative and pragmatic now say the reverse: you cannot excise a mechanism from the agent merely because you dislike what it does, and whether it is part of her is a factual matter about how the system is organized. The same participants seem to treat Is X part of the system? as an empirical question when the answer they favour expands the boundary, and as a normative or conceptual question when the answer they favour contracts it. I do not think anyone is being dishonest; each side appears to find the other's shift transparent and its own principled.

A second feature: the standard dissolution does not work on these disputes. When I have tried the obvious move — point out that the parties are answering different questions, one descriptive and one normative, and that they should disambiguate and go home — both parties say, in effect, that they win on both questions: the descriptive question resolves in their favour and so, independently, does the normative one. Pointing out the shift does not end the argument, which is what I would have expected if this were merely a verbal dispute. That is why the nearest thing I have found, the literature on verbal disputes, seems to fit only imperfectly: it accounts for the shift but not for its direction, and not for the dispute's immunity to being dissolved once the shift is pointed out. I have seen structurally similar arguments about whether a group, a firm, or a machine learning system can be said to know or believe something, and about whether a contested psychiatric category picks out a real condition or a useful fiction.

So: does this have an established name, or is it discussed in a literature I have not found? If not, what are the nearest concepts, how does each differ from what I have described, and which fits best? My colleague's deflationary explanation is that I am pattern-matching noise: boundary-expanding claims are surprising and therefore attract a demand for evidence, while boundary-contracting claims are usually made in contexts with high practical stakes and therefore attract a demand for justification of consequences — which would make this a burden-of-proof artifact rather than a register switch. Tell me whether that is right, and how one would distinguish the two empirically. Finally, tell me what a dispute with this structure could come to: whether settling it would be a discovery, a decision, or something else, and what would count as progress rather than as another turn of the same cycle. Please also flag anywhere you think my description is confused or my example misdescribed.
