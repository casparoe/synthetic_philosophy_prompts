I'm running a 75-minute session of my intro ethics seminar, 16 students, on whether everyday conventions create real moral obligations and whether a visitor has to follow the local ones. Design a runnable activity for it: rules, secret roles, small but real stakes (nothing that needs money beyond maybe a chocolate bar), and a debrief. The main constraint is that they should hit the problem before I name it — no mini-lecture up front, and nobody should hear words like coordination, convention, or expectation-dependence until afterwards.

Build it around this invented case so nobody can look anything up. In the town of Vell, the bakery queue works through a notebook by the door: you write your name, leave, come back, and notebook order is treated as binding, but if you aren't physically there when your name is called you drop to the bottom. Separately, in Vell nobody thanks anyone for being let ahead — thanking implies the person did you a favor rather than what they owed, so it lands as an insult. Half the room is secretly assigned locals (they recieve both rules on a card), half visitors (they get a seperate card that misdescribes one rule — say, that the notebook is only a rough waiting list). Stagger arrivals so visitors start outnumbered and end up the majority.

The two rules should be doing different philosophical work: I want the notebook to make conformity feel genuinely owed because people plan around it, and the no-thanking rule to make conformity feel owed even though nobody seems harmed by breaking it. So arrange the payoffs and timing so students feel both pulls, then argue past each other about whether they're the same pull.

Also tell me: how I stage the tipping point where visitor numbers dissolve the notebook convention and whether locals can blame newcomers for it; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the student who says at the debrief that it's only a game and none of it is binding; and, honestly, which part of the real question the game can't reproduce and I'll just have to say myself.
