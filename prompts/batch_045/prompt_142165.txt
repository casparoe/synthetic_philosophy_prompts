I'm a second-year PhD student in robotics, and my group is building what we call the Household Preference Benchmark: about forty simulated kitchen environments plus one teleoperation rig, from which we collect demonstrations by roughly 150 participants doing everyday tasks (loading a dishwasher, clearing a counter, putting away groceries). For each participant we fit a maximum-entropy (Boltzmann-rational) inverse reinforcement learning model with a fairly expressive neural reward, with the eventual aim of using the recovered reward to drive an assistive robot in that person's own kitchen. My difficulty is knowing when we have succeeded. Everyone agrees that IRL is underdetermined, in the sense that many reward functions rationalize the same trajectories, but the line I keep hearing from my advisor is that this is essentially a finite-data problem: the ambiguity is supposed to be bad only because our datasets are small and our environments too similar, so that if we scale up to tens of thousands of trajectories spanning many varied environments and tasks, the posterior over rewards should concentrate and the residual ambiguity should become negligible, very much as a confidence interval shrinks as the sample grows. I have been trying to turn that into a single reportable number per participant — my working name is the preference identifiability coefficient, defined as the expected divergence between the demonstrator's policy and policies sampled from the reward posterior, evaluated on held-out trajectories from the same forty environments — and my plan is to show that this coefficient falls toward zero as I add environments, and then to treat a value below some threshold as license to say that we have identified that person's preferences well enough to act on them.

Two things make me uneasy, and I would especially like help with the second. First, someone in the theory group pointed out to me that if you add a state-only potential term to the reward — the transformation that sends r(s, a, s′) to r(s, a, s′) plus a discounted multiple of Φ(s′) minus Φ(s), for an arbitrary function Φ on states — then the optimal policy is unchanged, and a neural reward is certainly flexible enough to absorb such a term; she also mentioned that the identically-zero reward explains everything, so that some kind of regularizer or heuristic is doing real work in picking a solution. That makes me doubt whether more data drawn from the same dynamics can ever pin down what I care about, or whether what I actually need is a different kind of variation (different initial states? perturbed dynamics? rewards tied to shared features rather than fitted per environment?). It also sits oddly with the fact that the more expressive I make the reward network, the more solutions there are, so that "more data" and "more flexible model" seem to be pulling in opposite directions.

Second, and more important for how I write this up: suppose the coefficient does come out low. Is it then correct to say that we have recovered the participant's preferences? In the draft I have been sliding between "the learned reward," "what the participant wants," and "what the participant would choose in new situations," and I no longer think those come to the same thing — the reward is, after all, just the parameter that best explains observed behavior under an assumed noise model, and my participants are sometimes impatient, habitual, or plainly mistaken about how to do a task, while the number that comes out also seems to depend on the temperature of the Boltzmann policy in a way I cannot interpret. So my questions are these: what, if anything, would have to be true of my data for it to be legitimate to say that we have identified a given person's preferences rather than merely fitted their behavior; does collecting far more demonstration data of the kind we already collect actually close the gap, or does the remaining ambiguity have to be closed by assumptions or by a different experimental design; and how should I report the residual ambiguity so that a careful reviewer will not object that the paper overstates what was measured — given that the whole point is to deploy the reward in a kitchen with different dynamics from the ones it was fit in?
