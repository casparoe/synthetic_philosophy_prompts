I am writing a paper on whether the doing/allowing distinction can carry the weight it is asked to carry in end-of-life ethics, and I need a matched pair of cases built to order — not an illustration of the distinction. Please construct two variants of one realistic scenario: same hospital, same patient, same clinician, same drug and dose, same time of death, same aim on the clinician's part (to end the patient's life at that moment). The only stipulated difference is that in Variant A the fatal mechanism is already in motion and beyond anyone's control before the clinician acts, while in Variant B it is not; any further difference between the variants, including any difference in what the clinician knows, must be a downstream consequence of that one.

Apply three criteria in my formulation rather than anyone's published one (if a label below misdescribes the view it names, use the definition):

- Sequence criterion (the causal, Foot-style view): the clinician kills just in case her bodily movement initiates a causal sequence terminating in the death; she allows the death just in case she merely lets an already-initiated sequence run its course, her own movement not being a link in it.
- Baseline criterion (the rights-based, Quinn-style view): she kills just in case the patient is thereby left worse off than he would have been had she never existed; she allows the death just in case her agency merely fails to improve a situation it did not create.
- Difference-making criterion (the counterfactual, Moore-style view): she kills just in case, holding fixed everything not under her control, there is a relevantly close possibility in which she does not move and the patient does not die when and as he does; otherwise she allows the death.

Note that these return classifications — doing or allowing — not verdicts about permissibility. I want classifications only.

The design requirement that makes this hard: within Variant A, the death the clinician brings about must be outcome-matched to the death that would have occurred anyway — same death to the minute, same observable clinical course, no additional suffering. Variant A is therefore not allowed to be a case of shortening a worse death; the match has to be engineered into the scenario (deep sedation, a lethal mechanism with an indistinguishable terminal course, a patient who is no worse off along the way either route) rather than asserted.

Constraints on both variants: ordinary clinical setting, no exotic technology and no science fiction; no consent, request, advance directive, or waiver from the patient; no appeal to law, professional codes, or hospital protocol; no second patient waiting for the ventilator and no other trade-off that would let consequentialist reasoning settle the case; and the narration must not itself say which description applies. If your scenario has to announce the classification, it has failed — I need the verdicts generated by the criteria, not stipulated.

What I am hoping the pair shows, and what you should tell me plainly if it does not: the sequence criterion returns the same verdict across A and B, making it the one criterion that cannot register the difference everyone will feel between the variants, while the baseline and difference-making criteria flip from allowing in A to killing in B.

Deliverables:

1. The two variants, each a single paragraph of at most 80 words (160 words total).
2. A table: one row per criterion, with columns for the A verdict, the B verdict, and one sentence of justification.
3. Which single feature of the design is load-bearing, in at most 60 words, and what happens to the verdicts if it is removed, in at most 60 words.
4. The strongest internal reply available to whichever criterion comes out worst — a defense that keeps the criterion and accommodates the case rather than abandoning the criterion — in at most 80 words.

Keep the entire response under 600 words.
