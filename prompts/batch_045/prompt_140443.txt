I am not a philosopher, but the last few months have made one philosophical question impossible for me to postpone. In March I spent five days in intensive care with septic delirium, and I came out with a set of memories — a corridor, a long conversation with my dead mother, an immense machine I was convinced I had been sealed inside — as vivid and as emotionally loaded as anything I remember from the rest of my life, and false in every particular. I have another procedure ahead of me that will require deep sedation, and I have found that I cannot think clearly about consenting to it until I have decided what a report of experience is worth when the machinery that produced it was, at the time, demonstrably producing nonsense. So I have begun reading about the meta-problem of consciousness: not why consciousness is as it is, but why creatures like us say the things we say about it — that experience is immediate, private, ineffable, that there is a further question left over once the functional questions are answered.

Before I read anyone's solution I want a specification of my own, so that I am not simply adopted by whichever writer is most persuasive. Please write me the five requirements a proposed solution to the meta-problem would have to satisfy to be worth my assent, in this form: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, each stating the requirement, the case that shows why it is a requirement, and a tag of either LOAD-BEARING (I would abandon a solution for failing it) or PREFERENCE (a reasonable partisan could reject it). No preamble and no closing summary; start at 1 and stop at 5.

Three constraints I want built into the list, without any of them becoming an item of its own. First, a solution should have to earn its keep on the dissociation cases rather than on the canon: I care less about red and pain, which have been polished by a century of argument until they fit any theory, than about the cases where the report comes apart from what it reports on — the blindsight patient who denies seeing and still guesses correctly, the anosognosic who confabulates a story about a paralysed limb, the dream report, the failure of ownership in thought insertion. A mechanism that generates phenomenal reports should be expected to misfire in specifiable ways, and predicting the shape of those misfires should be part of what is demanded. Second, a solution should have to explain its own inability to convince: it is apparently common ground that one can fully accept a debunking account of why one finds experience ineffable and still find, in the next moment, that it seems ineffable. That inertia is a datum, and it is the one I am living with; a requirement that predicted the intuition should by now have dissolved is simply wrong about the phenomenon. Third, and this is the part I most want, I suspect two of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and I want that stated inside whichever item the conflict lands in rather than in a sixth item: if phenomenal reports are produced by a mechanism we can show to be a fallible heuristic, then coming to know that should loosen its grip on us, and it plainly does not, and everything I can think of that closes that gap either stops being a neutral explanation of the reports or stops being able to account for the misfires. Say so plainly if the real incompatibility lies somewhere else.

Do not score the existing positions against the list; I will do that once I have read them, and I want the standard rather than the league table. And please do not start by telling me what consciousness is: part of what I am asking for is a specification that does not quietly settle that question before any argument begins.
