I have agreed to give the guest address at Speech Day at Anning College, a co-educational sixth form in Warwickshire, on the last Friday of the summer term. The address follows prize-giving, in a marquee, in front of roughly four hundred people: the upper sixth leavers, their parents and grandparents, the teaching staff, the chair of governors, and — in the first two rows — the head of philosophy, two epistemologist friends of mine who have come expressly to catch me out, and the head teacher who invited me. Please write it.

One complicating fact. The leavers are seven weeks from A-level results day, and several of them are holding conditional university offers that turn on grades they do not yet have. The head has asked me in writing to promise them that everything will work out. I am an epistemologist, sympathetic to Nozick-style tracking accounts — very roughly, that a belief counts as knowledge only if it would not survive the falsity of what it is about (sensitivity) and would survive its truth (adherence) — and I take seriously the consequence that knowledge is not closed under known implication. So I owe the head something genuinely reassuring that I can say without asserting what I do not think I know, and without turning Speech Day into a seminar.

Three things I want the structure to do.

First, somewhere near the middle, the speech should state, in plain words that an eighteen-year-old and a grandmother could both follow, the inference I am not entitled to make: that a good school taught them well; that pupils taught well end up all right; therefore they will be all right. I want the failure located precisely rather than gestured at. The conclusion is what tracking withholds, since I would produce it in exactly the same words whatever happens on results morning — I have started calling this cheerful insensitivity — and I would add that the premises are weaker than they look: the first would probably still be believed in the nearest worlds where it is false, and the second rests mainly on the alumni who come back to speak at Speech Days, a sample whose composition would look much the same if it were false.

Second, the denial of closure should be what the speech stands on, not merely what it forbids. Because knowledge does not transmit across known implication, my not knowing how results day goes does not spread backwards and infect what I do know about the seven years in front of me: that the work was done, and taught by people who meant it. I would like that asymmetry to be the speech's positive move, and I would like it put as the practical discipline tracking actually recommends — not "will it work out?" but "what would have to be the case for it not to?", asked about something near at hand rather than about a life.

Third, constraints on what may be said: no promise about the future dressed up as knowledge; no providential or "everything happens for a reason" consolation, which I do not believe; no sceptical scenario or brain-in-a-vat material, which would lose everyone outside the first two rows; nothing that treats the head's request as foolish. Do not use the words "tracking", "closure" or "sensitivity" anywhere in the speech, and do not name Nozick: the philosophers should recognise the shape without being told it. There should be one sentence the epistemologists in row two would accept as accurate, and one sentence a grandparent would repeat in the car park.

Length: 1,000–1,200 words, about eight minutes, in British English, written as it would be delivered — no stage directions beyond the odd bracketed beat, no bullet points.

After the speech, a note of no more than 200 words: where the speech leans on a contested reading of the material — whether sensitivity really is the right condition for claims about the near future, whether a safety-based rival would let some version of the reassurance back in, whether refusing closure actually buys me the insulation I claim — and one sentence on what would change my mind: the single argument that, if I found it persuasive, would make me rewrite the passage where I refuse the head's request.
