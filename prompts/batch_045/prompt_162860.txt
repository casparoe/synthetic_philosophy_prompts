I teach a seminar on causation and historical explanation, and there is a pattern in the disputes over "Great Man" versus structural accounts that I have never been able to label properly. I would like help identifying it, or having it named and defined if it has no name.

The pattern is easiest to show with the case my students always start with: would there have been a National Socialist regime in Germany without Hitler? Whenever I press the question I find that the answer flips, not with any new evidence, but with what I imagine holding still. If I delete Hitler and keep the Depression, the settlement at Versailles, the calculations of the conservative elites, and the party apparatus he inherited, I find myself imagining some other demagogue stepping into the vacancy, and the outcome survives. If I delete Hitler and also let everything downstream of his particular gifts come apart — the oratory, the tactical sense, the hold over the movement — the outcome dissolves entirely, and I get no Nazi Germany at all. The same flip shows up when we ask whether Britain would have fought on in 1940 without Churchill: hold the cabinet, the service chiefs, and the state's capacity fixed and you get one verdict; let the person vary and you get another. My sense is that the two camps in this literature are not really disagreeing about the facts; they disagree about which of these questions is the honest one, and neither side says so.

What makes me think this is a distinct thing and not just sloppiness is that the two descriptions of the removal are not contraries: no one denies that there was a slot to be filled, or that Hitler filled it, or that the filling mattered to how the regime looked. The disagreement is about whether the counterfactual removes the occupant of the slot or the slot and its occupant together, and — this is the part I find genuinely puzzling — each side is remarkably consistent about which things it is willing to vary. Structuralist explanations reliably hold the structure fixed and vary the individual; Great Man explanations reliably do the reverse. I have been calling it baseline smuggling in my notebooks, but I suspect there is something more precise, and possibly two things rather than one: the dependence of the verdict on what is held fixed, and the asymmetry in what each explanatory style is prepared to hold fixed.

Several candidates have been suggested to me and none seem right. It is not ordinary overdetermination, since nothing here is a matter of two actually operating sufficient causes. It is not the underdetermination of theory by evidence, which operates at the wrong level. It is not merely the vagueness of counterfactuals, since each of the two questions seems to me to have a determinate answer; the problem is that there are two questions. A colleague said it is just a choice of similarity metric over possible worlds, which may be true but relocates the difficulty rather than carving it.

So: is there an established concept for what I am describing, or for either half of it? If there is, I want more than the label — I want to know exactly how the thing I described differs from the nearest neighbours, including any distinction it is routinely confused with, and where its boundaries lie. If there is not, please coin a term, define it tightly, and give me the boundary cases it should exclude. I do not need a bibliography; I can find the literature once I know what to look for. Please close by saying how confident you are in each of your main claims, distinguishing what is standard from what you are reconstructing or proposing yourself, and single out the one point you are least confident about.
