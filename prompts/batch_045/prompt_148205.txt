I build backend services for a living and have basically zero philosophy background, but me and two friends got into one of those 2am Discord arguments that started about Conway's Game of Life (someone said the glider 'wants' to reach the other side of the grid, someone else said that's just us talking) and ended with one of them bringing up Dennett's three stances — physical, design, intentional — and his idea of real patterns. I read a bit and now I sort of get the picture: you can predict a system by simulating its physics, by assuming it was designed to do something, or by treating it as an agent with beliefs and desires, and a pattern counts as real if it lets you compress/predict the data better than chance. What I can't find is anything that tells me what a *good* version of that account would have to do to count as good. I don't want another overview of the three stances, and I don't want a book report. I want a spec, the way I'd write an RFC before picking a library: what does an adequate theory of real patterns and stance-adoption have to deliver, and what's the concrete case behind each requirement?

Here are the cases I keep getting stuck on, which is probably where the requirements should come from. (1) Some patterns are real but leaky — a traffic jam, a retry storm, a market trend — they hold ceteris paribus and break constantly, while other patterns are projections (constellations, faces in clouds, the pattern I found in a RNG output seeded with my birthday). A spec has to say how the two come apart without making patternhood all-or-nothing. (2) The gerrymander problem: for any finite dataset some compression scheme will find a pattern in it, so 'compressible' alone can't be the test, or literally everything has real patterns — my guess is the theory owes some constraint on the complexity of the scheme itself, maybe something like a minimum-description-length budget, and I want to know whether that smuggles the predictor's interests into what's supposed to be a fact about the world. (3) Granularity: a pattern in a packet trace that only shows up if you bucket by 100ms and only on the retry stream — is it real or am I just looking at the right resolution? (4) Content: a thermostat and a chess engine both get predicted well by the intentional stance, but there seems to be no fact of the matter whether the thermostat believes it's 20C or 68F or 'too cold', so does a theory have to deliver determinate belief contents or is it allowed to punt? (5) Reflexive prediction: predicting something that predicts you (an opponent, a front-running trading bot) — the intentional stance seems to both work best and break down there, and my hunch is that's a case an adequate theory must handle rather than a corner to ignore. (6) The LLM case, which I don't think existed in this form when these ideas were written down: intentional-stance descriptions of a language model are wildly more predictively useful than for a thermostat, yet the training signal was literally 'produce text like something with beliefs', and unlike the thermostat we don't have a clean design-stance story for what it's for. Is that a new kind of case or the same old one? (7) Do real patterns have to do any causal work — is a shadow or a stadium wave a real pattern, or does the theory need them to have causal powers? (8) Does the theory have to tell me what patterns are, or only when I'm licensed to use agent-talk — because for my purposes the second is what I actually need, and I suspect a lot of the arguing is about which of those is even being asked for.

So: give me the desiderata list with the motivating case for each, mark which ones are non-negotiable for any adequate account and which are just prejudices of one camp (I suspect 'must bottom out in fundamental physics' is a prejudice and 'must be usable by a computationally bounded agent' is only a requirement if the theory is prescriptive rather than metaphysical), and be explicit about any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied — I'd rather get a proof that the spec is impossible than a spec that secretly contradicts itself. My own guess is that 'patternhood is objective, independent of any predictor's interests and compute budget' and 'patternhood is a matter of compressibility relative to a scheme' can't both hold, but you tell me. Then, briefly and only at the end, score three contenders against the list — something like Dennett's own mild realism about patterns, the view that patterns are purely in the eye of the predictor and agent-talk is just a useful fiction, and an information-theoretic view on which real patterns are objective features of the data — and say which single requirement knocks out the most candidates. The reason I care is dumbly practical: I need to know when it's legitimate for me to write internal docs saying a service 'thinks' its cache is stale or 'decides' to shed load, and when that's just me fooling myself with a bad abstraction.
