ok so I could really use help drafting something. I'm on the recovery committee of the Cedar Basin Wildlife Trust — we allocate breeding and habitat money for a bunch of desert spring systems — and we vote on the 14th. Last night I got into a long, kind of dumb, three-hour argument with my friend Dana about our main agenda item, and I woke up still not knowing what I think, but I promised to circulate a written opinion before the meeting.

The item: two populations of a small spring fish. Call them the Ash Creek springfish (one protected spring, census around 4,000, stable) and the Salt Wells springfish (a second spring about 90 km away in the same basin). They've been separated since the basin dried out; genomic work puts the split at roughly 180,000 years, with maybe one or two reconnections in wet periods. Salt Wells has collapsed: 61 fish in last month's census, down from about 900 five years ago, because of aquifer pumping and an introduced crayfish. The hatchery fish are a mess — spinal deformities, ~80% juvenile mortality, and sibling crosses in the lab basically fail. Field team says extinction in the wild inside six years without intervention.

Two complications. First, in 1954 somebody cut an irrigation canal that joins the two spring outflows in wet years, and in that canal we find fish of mixed ancestry: fertile, and in lab trials about as fit as either parent type. So under one way of thinking they're clearly the same species and always were; under another they're separate lineages and the canal is an artificial leak we caused. Second, our enabling charter says we exist to conserve "native species, subspecies, and distinct population segments," and never says which of those trumps which or what makes a population distinct.

The proposal on the table: move 30 Ash Creek fish into Salt Wells over two breeding seasons, monitor for a decade, and accept that in twenty years the Salt Wells fish will be something like 30–40% Ash Creek ancestry. There's also a third option someone floated late: do the translocation but first cryopreserve gametes from as many of the remaining pure Salt Wells fish as we can, so the "original" genome is banked even if the wild population becomes admixed. Half the committee calls that genetic rescue; the other half calls it extinction-by-hybridization and points out that our biggest donor's grant is written around "the Salt Wells springfish" and that a state listing might not cover hybrids.

Dana's position, which I can't shake, is that species are human bookkeeping — that what exists are individuals and lineages and ecosystems, that the fish we'd be "saving" by doing nothing are the ones who actually die, and that the option I keep circling back to is us conserving a name rather than an animal. My colleague Rae says the opposite: that what we owe the future is the distinct thing, that a 40%-admixed population is a new animal we manufactured, and that the frozen-gametes idea is literally conserving a label in a freezer.

Can you draft the memo I circulate? It should land on a concrete recommendation — approve, deny, or approve with conditions — with a timeline and whatever conditions or monitoring you'd attach; say plainly what the disagreement is actually about underneath the biology, including whether the thing we'd destroy by admixing is the same kind of thing we'd destroy by letting them die; and give the losing side its strongest version rather than a strawman. And if the charter's vagueness about "distinct population segments" is doing real work in the decision, say so instead of papering over it. Don't cite anything — I just need the reasoning, and I need to be able to defend it out loud in a room where both Rae and Dana are going to be there in spirit.
