Sorry if this is long. I'm on a small behavioural insights team at a public pension agency and we're finally writing an internal ethics guidance note for the stuff we actually ship, and going through it I've realised my own views here are probably less tidy than I keep pretending in meetings. We had a proper fight about it at our offsite last month and nobody convinced anybody. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Option-preserving is basically the whole moral ballgame: rearranging the choice environment is fine, taking options away (bans, mandates, stiff penalties) is a different category and I want no part of it.
(2) I'm attached to what I've seen called asymmetric paternalism — an intervention is justified if it helps people who are making an error and leaves the people who aren't making an error no worse off. What I like about it is that it seems to let me do good without ever having to win an argument about whose values or preferences are better than whose.
(3) But I don't think actual choices are the welfare standard. Revealed preferences are shot through with framing, defaults and inertia, so what people actually pick can't be the thing I'm trying to promote; I want to say the target is something like their informed or "laundered" preferences.
(4) I reject the transparency condition. If an intervention only works while people aren't noticing it, and would stop working the day it got disclosed, I definately don't count that against it. Where people end up is what matters, not whether they could in principle endorse the mechanism.
(5) I think targeting is good, not creepy: use a predictive model and give different defaults to different people depending on how likely they are to make the relevant mistake, instead of one blunt default for everyone. Uniform defaults waste the effect on people who'd have been fine anyway and under-help the ones who most need it.
(6) I also think the nudger's aim matters: the very same intervention is fine when it's aimed at the person's own good and objectionable when it's really serving somebody else's bottom line, even if it happens to help the person too. Our own procurement people keep pushing an auto-enrolment tweak that would genuinely help staff and also cut our admin costs, and I cant get myself to be neutral about that.
(7) Auto-escalation, where your contribution rate creeps up every year unless you actively opt down, is the best tool we have and I'd defend it to anyone.
(8) But I also want to say that interventions which work by farming people's inattention or procrastination are manipulative and not OK; the legitimate ones remove real obstacles like hassle costs and confusing forms, they don't just sit on people's inertia.
(9) And here's what started the fight: we've begun running what we call "re-setters" internally — instead of a default that fires once and then sticks, the default re-applies every year, so if you opted out last year you get put back in unless you actively opt out again. I think these are better than sticky defaults, because everyone gets a fresh chance each year to act on what they actually want, and the group still enrolled at the end is closer to the group that genuinely wants to be in.

Can someone audit this for me? I'd like the tensions ranked by severity rather than a list where everything is flagged as equally bad, and for each real one I want to know exactly what would have to give and what it would cost me to give it. Please also tell me which pairs only look like they're in tension — I have a feeling at least one of these is actually fine and I'm just hearing it as contradictory because of how I've worded it, but I can't tell which. And if there's one revision that would clean up the most, say what it is. Be concrete about the re-setters specifically: I need to know whether (9) survives (1)–(8) or whether I have to pick.
