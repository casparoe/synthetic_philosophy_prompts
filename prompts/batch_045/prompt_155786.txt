hey — I run the ML side of a mid-sized eng org, and as a side project we run an internal prediction market for things we actually care about: whether a given eval target gets hit by a date, how many pager incidents next sprint, whether a migration ships before the freeze. It's a cost-function automated market maker with a subsidy out of our team budget, so the maker eats the losses and anyone can always trade against it. Traders see the current price and each others positions live, and separatley we log every trader's own forecast and feed those into an ensemble. Two things bug me and I suspect they're the same thing, so I want a translation.

The ask: there's a family of results in economics and epistemology that I only know secondhand — the agreement result (agents sharing a prior can't agree to disagree once their posteriors are common knowledge) and the no-trade results built on top of it (if the prior is common, the information symmetric, and everyone's rationality common knowledge, nobody should want to trade, so any volume you actually observe has to come from somewhere else: adverse selection about who knows more, differing attitudes to risk, genuinely divergent priors, or plain noise and liquidity needs) — plus the bigger claim these get wheeled out for, that prices aggregate dispersed information and are therefore epistemically special. I want that whole package restated in the vocabulary I actually think in: priors as model priors and as shared training distributions, posteriors as calibrated probabilities, information as features and training data, updating as training on more data or conditioning on more context, proper scoring rules (log score and Brier), ensembles and stacking, bias vs. variance, overfitting and distribution shift, the winner's curse in which datapoints or which traders self-select into being counted, adversarial data collection, and expected loss under a known loss function. Assume I'm fluent in all of that and allergic to econ jargon — but don't flatter me: wherever the translation has to cheat, say so.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Three things to chew on specifically.

(1) Our ensemble of trader forecasts beats the market price itself on Brier score, consistently, and the traders are all looking at the same dashboards and the same data. Naively that looks like a violation of the no-trade idea: information is roughly symmetric, so there shouldn't be a gain left lying on the table, and yet there is one. My guess is that a mixture gains by averaging away idiosyncratic error even when every forecaster is risk-neutral and shares information, because the loss is assessed on the mixture rather than on the individuals — and that the no-trade results have no slot for that, since they're about reallocating an asset between people rather than about combining outputs into a new one. Am I right, or am I smuggling something in (an implicit diversity assumption, a decorrelation assumption) that the theorem would just call a difference in information after all? Where exactly does the analogy break?

(2) Our market always trades, because the subsidised maker will take the other side of anything. So observed volume tells you nothing about whether anyone thinks they have an edge, and you can't read willingness-to-trade off the tape the way those arguments want to. Does that destroy the price's epistemic status? I can't tell whether the subsidy makes the market dumber (noise swamping signal) or smarter (people reveal what they think even when no human counterparty would take the bet). Say what the no-trade framework would predict here and, more usefully, what it can't express at all.

(3) The bit I think genuinely has no counterpart on my side: in these results, the fact that someone is willing to trade is itself evidence you should condition on, and conditioning on it destroys the gain — the trade is self-defeating in a way I don't have words for in terms of inputs, outputs, losses and updates. Is that just adverse selection wearing a hat, or is it something about actions-as-signals that my vocabulary really cannot say? If it's the latter, is that a defect in how I think, or a sign that the original argument is doing something fishy?

Format: restate the position first, faithfully and in my vocabulary; then the residue report taking on 1–3; then a short closing section on what would change your mind. For that last bit I want you to name the one empirical finding about our setup, or the one move inside the argument, that would flip your verdict on (1) in particular — and to say plainly where your translation is most likely quietly cheating.
