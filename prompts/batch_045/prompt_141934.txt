1. Plain fair coin, nothing else: 0.5, full deference, no hestitation.
2. Same coin, but a device that has called the last 200 flips correctly and says heads: ~0.99, and the chance is irrelevant now.
3. A different device that never sees outcomes, only reads off objective chances, and is reliable; it says the chance of heads on this flip is 0.83: 0.83 — and I feel like this is deference while case 2 isn't.
4. A laundering device: it peeks at the outcome, then reports a number that is a real objective chance, but of some seperate chancy process it rigs up — 0.83 if it saw heads, 0.17 if tails. Me: I treat it like case 2 and go high, ~0.99, not 0.83. The fact that the number it says is a genuine chance of something doesn't seem to matter.
5. I'm told the chance of heads is 0.5 and I'm equally confident that this same coin comes up heads 80% of the time over the next million flips. I go 0.8 and I stop believing the chance claim.
6. Physics says the chance this atom decays within the hour is 0.999999: I don't go there, I cap around 0.99, because I might have the setup wrong.
7. A device reports genuine second-order chances: 0.6 that the chance of heads is 0.5, 0.4 that it's 0.9. I mix and land on 0.66, and I don't feel like I'm deferring to anything, just averaging.

My tentative rule is: defer to whatever the chance of the event in question is, unless my evidence is really about the outcome rather than about the chance, or gives me positive reason to doubt that the number I've been handed is that event's chance. But case 6 looks like it breaks that — I have no specific doubt there and I still hedge, while in case 1 I don't hedge at all.

Give me a principle that handles these, or tell me which verdict has to go.
