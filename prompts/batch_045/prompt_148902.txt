I'm working on a piece about how funders decide a field has stopped going anywhere, and I keep circling back to one person — a senior programme officer at a big biomedical foundation who talks about this stuff in public more than anyone in her job has to, and who does it in Lakatos's vocabulary: hard core, protective belt, progressive versus degenerating problem shifts. Here's her track record as near as I can reconstruct it from her talks and from panel decisions people have described to me.

She says phlogiston was degenerating: it bolted on auxiliaries, swallowed every anomaly, and never stuck its neck out on anything new and been right about it. She says Wegener's original drift theory was degenerating at the time it was being argued about, even though it turned out to be onto something, and that plate tectonics after 1960 was progressive, because the seafloor-spreading and magnetic-striping business was content the theory called in advance. She says the neutrino was progressive, and she's explicit that it started life as a rescue device for energy conservation in beta decay — it outgrew its origins. She says Vulcan, the planet Le Verrier parked inside Mercury's orbit, was degenerating: same move, no payoff. She calls string theory theoretically progressive and empirically stalled, and keeps a small slice of the portfolio in it anyway, which not everyone at the foundation is happy about. And she cut a programme in her own portfolio that spent ten years explaining away null results by saying the effect only shows up under conditions nobody could specify.

The one I can't call is also in her portfolio, and I've changed the details so she won't recognise it. About fifteen years old, a couple hundred labs, hard core intact and unchanged: that a certain cognitive capacity is carried by a particular kind of slow synchronized oscillation, and that the oscillation is the mechanism rather than a readout of something else. Every failed replication in the last decade has been absorbed by a new auxiliary added after the fact and tailored to the failure it was saving — the oscillation is real but transient, or it's in a different cortical layer than anyone sampled, or it only appears in a behavioural state nobody was recording. Then, about eighteen months ago, one of those add-ons — the one about phase relationships across layers — was fed into a large simulation and spat out an odd quantitative prediction: a specific asymmetry between the rising and falling phase of the cycle, at a specific ratio, in the local field potential. It wasn't what anyone was looking for. They built a new electrode array to go find it, and it's there. As far as I can tell the result is real.

Three complications. The array was calibrated against simulated data generated by the same model. The signature was picked out of a scan across a few thousand candidate summary statistics, so there's the usual look-elsewhere headache. And the field has quietly split into three variants that disagree about whether the oscillation is a mechanism or a readout — they've started calling that disagreement interpretational and counting each other's positive results as support for the framework as a whole. Meanwhile the analysis tools the programme built are now standard across neuroscience, including in the labs that generate its confirming data.

So: what does she say, and what should she say if she wants to be consistent with herself? I'm less interested in the answer than in the rule she's running. Is it a strict novel-fact rule where the prediction has to come before the observation? Or Lakatos's own weaker version, where a fact can count as novel even if it was already known, so long as it wasn't used in building the theory — which would make the timing of the prediction beside the point? Is it theoretical progress plus patience, so a programme that keeps generating content and instruments gets funded even when the empirical scorecard is muddy? Or is she really running something more like an institutional-hygiene rule, about calibration circularity and search spaces and whether a framework that can't say what its own hard core is is one programme at all? Which of those rules fit all nine of her previous calls, where do they come apart on this one, what's your best guess and why, and what single further case would actually tell them apart?
