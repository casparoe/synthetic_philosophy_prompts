I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of perception, and I am trying to break a stalemate in a paper I am writing on cognitive penetration. The stalemate is a familiar one. The argument from perceptual learning says that the expert birder, the radiologist, the wine taster come to see something in a scene that the novice simply does not see, and that no story about redirected attention or shifted post-perceptual judgment captures the difference. The standard reply from defenders of the informational encapsulation of early vision is that learning merely retunes the input system: the cognitive state does its causal work at training time, and what happens at the moment of the experience is ordinary processing by a system that has already been rewired, so nothing genuinely central, belief-like, or inferentially integrated is operating on perception when perception happens. I have been using a case of my own construction to get past this exchange, and I would like you to help me argue for the reading of it that I favour.

Call it the Sterile-Batch case. Marta has spent fifteen years as a plant pathologist inspecting wheat seedlings for early-stage stripe rust. Her performance is near-perfect and fast, and she reports, consistently and in forced-choice terms, that infected leaves look different to her: there is a sick look — a faint dulling and mottling — that is as immediately present to her as the leaf's colour, and she sees it in a single glance at a tray of a hundred seedlings, at a size and exposure at which she cannot name any low-level feature that carries it. Now a colleague, as a practical joke, tells her — falsely — that the tray in front of her came from a sterile growth chamber and cannot possibly be infected. While she believes this, two things happen: the sick look vanishes from her experience (she says the leaves now look uniformly healthy, and is startled, and a little embarrassed, by how total the change is), and her detection accuracy on that tray drops from near-perfect to chance, with no change in her response times, no change in her willingness to say infected elsewhere, and no change in her confidence. Fifteen minutes later the joke is revealed, and both the look and the accuracy return at once. Nothing about Marta's eyes, the leaves, or the lighting has changed, and — this is the feature I care about — the expertise itself is intact throughout: she has forgotten nothing and needs to relearn nothing. What is switched off by an occurrent belief, and switched back on by its retraction, is the *expression* of a perceptual sensitivity she still fully possesses. My psychologist colleagues object that this is just a shift in response criterion rather than in sensitivity, or that a gate is not a penetrator, or that the whole thing is demand characteristics and report bias.

Here is the position I want you to argue for: the Sterile-Batch case is a genuine case of cognitive penetration, and what it shows is that penetration has been systematically misdescribed in the literature — a cognitive state can be an in-the-moment causal determinant of what experience is like without contributing any of experience's representational content, because the penetrating state here functions as a gate or enabling condition on a stored perceptual sensitivity rather than as a supplier of content, and the gate-vs-content-supplier distinction, not the influence-vs-no-influence distinction, is where the real theoretical work is. Please work as a professional philosopher of mind and an expert on perception would: spend some time first brainstorming the strongest available deflationary explanations of the case — criterion versus sensitivity shifts in signal-detection terms, attentional reallocation, slow retraining, post-perceptual reinterpretation, response bias, the possibility that a mere enabling condition is not the right kind of cause to count as penetration — and then set out the argument for my position against the best version of those objections, rather than against a strawman. I do not want a literature survey or any citations or references; I know that material, and I am asking you to reason, not to source. I also want a committed response: take the position, give your reasons, and tell me where the argument is most vulnerable and what would have to be true for it to fail, but do not hedge or finish by leaving me to choose between readings. Conclude with the verdict stated in a fixed, machine-readable form on the final line: \answer{a five-to-eight-word statement of your verdict, e.g. "gating is penetration without content-supply"}.
