I am a novelist, and a scene I am drafting turns on a technical result I only half understand; before I put the words in a character's mouth I would like an accuracy audit of the version of it I am working from.

A popular book on clear thinking summarizes the result — I gather it is called Aumann's agreement theorem — roughly as follows:

"Two perfectly rational people who begin with the same prior cannot remain in disagreement. A prior is simply what you already believed before the evidence came in, and since no two people have lived through the same evidence, no two real people ever literally share one — which is why the theorem is elegant but almost never applies. The argument: once each person has stated how confident she is about some event, and each knows the other has heard, and knows that the other knows she has heard, and so on without end, the mathematics forces the two numbers to be identical. So a disagreement that survives an honest exchange of evidence and of confidence levels proves that somebody is either reasoning badly or keeping something back. Philosophers have objected that requiring a common prior simply assumes the answer, since sharing a prior is only agreement in disguise, and most economists now treat that objection as fatal."

My instinct is that the core is sound and the framing around it is shaky, but I cannot tell which sentences are which: some of it may be fair simplification that a careful theorist would accept, and some may be the sort of thing that would make a professional wince. Please go through it claim by claim with that distinction, and say plainly whether the two conclusions drawn at the end follow from the parts that survive.

Two questions bear directly on the scene. First, my mathematician character invokes the result to close an argument with her sister about whether their late father's unfinished novel was meant to be published — a disagreement that is not, on its face, about the probability of anything. Her move is that any dispute can be given a number. Is that an abuse of the theorem, or does it bite once credences are attached, and if so what is doing the work? Second, in the scene the sisters state their confidence levels to each other once, in person, and the mathematician treats that single exchange as settling it. Is she entitled to that, or does the result require something neither of them has done yet? If the popular version cannot bear the dialogue I want, tell me what she would have to say instead to be defensible.
