hey — I need a spec, not a reading list. I'm helping put together an internal auditing checklist for a hospital group, and the recurring mess is this: an analyst pulls a treatment comparison out of our own records, finds that the new option looks worse overall but better inside every severity band, and then three people look at the same table and reach three different conclusions about which number is the effect. I want the requirements list that any rule for that situation has to meet — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Assume the rule is meant to be run by a data steward who has the table, a rough causal story, and a decision to make, and who has to write down one sentence at the end saying which number to act on. That purpose is the point: I don't want adequacy in the abstract.

Cases I definately want covered, at minimum:

- the standard one: A beats B among mild patients and among severe patients, loses overall
- the same shape but where the thing you'd stratify on is caused by the treatment (a complication, a follow-up measurement) rather than a pre-treatment severity marker
- a stratifier that's a noisy stand-in for the real confounder, measured with error
- a reversal that shows up in the odds ratio but not in the risk difference, with no confounding in the setup at all
- a continuous severity score where whether you get a reversal depends on where you cut it into bands
- a reversal driven by a band with four patients in it
- the case where the aggregate and the stratified numbers are both correct but answer different questions, because of how much the decision-maker will know at the moment she acts
- a case where the mild/severe mix in our hospitals differs from the mix in whatever dataset the numbers came from

Also: I've been sorting these into 'paper reversals' (the kind that dissolve once you're explicit about the causal question) and 'live reversals' (the kind that still change what you should do after the question is fixed). Tell me if that cut is the right one or whether it slices across the cases badly, and if it's wrong give me a better one.

Two more things. First, mark each requirement as non-negotiable versus a preference of one camp, and call out the requirements that are just my side's prejudices — I'd rather have six real ones than a wishlist. Second, flag any subset of the requirements that can't be jointly satisfied (my guess is that "uses only what's in the table" and "tells a confounder apart from a variable caused by the treatment" can't both hold, and that binning-invariance fights using the full score, but check me on that). Then say which single requirement knocks out the most popular heuristics.

At the end, briefly — a short table is fine — score four candidate rules against the list: report both sets of numbers and move on; adjust for everything measured before treatment; adjust only for the variables that block the confounding routes; and set it up as a decision problem, picking the action that does best given what's known when the action is taken. Please write it in American English.
