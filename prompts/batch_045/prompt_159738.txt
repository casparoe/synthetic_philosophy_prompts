I sit on the internal review committee for a care-management program at the regional health system where I work, and I want a consistency check on my own position before I write the one-page fairness rationale I have to sign in about six weeks. The setting matters, so here it is: the program has a fixed number of slots each month, far fewer than the number of eligible patients, so a model score is used to rank patients and a care manager then chooses from the top band. This is a ranking under a single global capacity, not a threshold decision, and we set no per-group quotas. The outcome we actually care about — whether enrolment prevents a readmission — is observed only for patients who were enrolled. The model is moderately accurate, nowhere near perfect. And the base rates for that outcome differ across the groups our audit reports on.

Here is where I think I have landed.

(1) The familiar impossibility results apply and they are load-bearing. When the groups differ in base rates and the model is imperfect, no score can be calibrated in every group and also have equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups. So no setting of this model is fair on every axis, and any dashboard that reports several fairness metrics side by side is concealing a choice someone already made.

(2) Which axis to give up is a moral and political question, not a technical one, and it should be settled by what the affected parties could accept rather than by whatever metric happens to be implemented in the library we audit with, or by what is easiest to compute.

(3) Fairness is a property of the decision procedure rather than of the statistical pattern the procedure produces. What matters is that the process gives public reasons, is contestable by the people it ranks, and does not route through proxies that have nothing to do with the point of the program.

(4) It is therefore not merely permitted but often required to use group membership directly when building and auditing the model. Deleting the protected attribute is not fairness; it usually makes allocation worse and it destroys our ability to detect a disparity at all.

(5) The deep notion, of which the group-level statistics are only crude proxies, is individualized: similar people should be treated similarly under a similarity metric appropriate to this task, which we specify ourselves and can defend.

(6) The two criteria I actually want for this program, and have been quietly assuming we can have at once: (6a) wasted-slot parity — across the groups we report on, the same share of the slots that go to each group should go to patients who would not have benefited; (6b) miss parity — across the same groups, the same share of patients who would have benefited should end up without a slot. Treat (6a) and (6b) as separable.

(7) We should deploy, with the audit and an appeal route, because the status quo — nurses picking from a list — is worse on everything we can measure and is not auditable at all. Not deploying is also a decision, and it is the worse one.

I do not know which of these I would give up, and I would rather you not assume I will drop whichever one is least convenient for the rest. Assume I mean what I said, not the most defensible version of each thesis.
