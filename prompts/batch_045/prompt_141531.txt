In most parliamentary democracies a legislator who votes against their party on a whipped vote faces real sanctions — loss of committee seats, demotion, deselection — and the party, not the voters, fixes the order of names on its list of candidates; yet the same systems treat the free vote or vote of conscience as the honourable exception, describe a representative's office as one of judgment on the common good rather than of transmitting instructions, and criminalize the sale of a legislator's vote even to a constituent. Simone Weil's essay arguing for the abolition of all political parties says this is no accident of bad characters: an organization whose constitutive aim is to win power for itself must, she argues, manufacture collective passion and place the thinking of each of its members under collective pressure, so that a party becomes a machine for generating error and for subordinating truth and justice to its own growth, however sincere its founders were. What does the institution of the disciplined party actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: what view of collective judgment, of representation, of the relation between winning and being right, and of the moral division of labour among legislators does it only make sense under — does it strictly presuppose that political disagreement is not answerable to inquiry, so that coordination and the delivery of a promised programme matter more than getting the answer right, or could a defender give discipline an epistemic footing, on the model of deferring to a panel that aggregates information better than any single member? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with — a Burkean trustee view, responsible-party government, pluralist interest aggregation, or something like epistemic deference — and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, licensing several theories at once.

Then test Weil's claim that the corruption is structural rather than contingent. Suppose a redesigned party: members bind themselves only to a published and general statement of principles; an independent body of non-members adjudicates whether a given vote really follows from those principles; and leaders are chosen by lot from among the membership for a single non-renewable term, so that nobody has a career stake in the organization's growth. Would that escape her argument, or does her argument rest on something such a design still fails to satisfy — for instance, the claim that any body with a shared programme and the ambition to govern must eventually come to treat its own victory and persistence as ends in themselves, whatever its internal constitution? Finally, what does Weil's own alternative presuppose: does replacing parties with unorganized, independent judgment require a view on which individuals who attend properly to a question can converge on the truth without the machinery of organized opinion-formation, and does the critique of parties survive if one rejects that epistemology? Say what would concretely have to change in a working legislature if parties were abolished, and whether anything recognizable as electoral representation would be left.
