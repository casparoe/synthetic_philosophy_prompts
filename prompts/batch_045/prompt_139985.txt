My wife's uncle died in February, and because I am the family's only philosopher I have been asked, informally, to help settle a dispute over his estate before everyone meets with the attorney on Friday morning. The two relevant clauses read: "my 1962 E-Type, the car I drove to Monterey in '79," which goes to her cousin Reed, and "whatever else is left in the barn," which goes to her cousin Colin. The facts are these. He bought the car new in 1962 and restored it, obsessively and continuously, for the next forty-five years: the engine, the gearbox, the body tub, the front subframe, the interior, and the wiring harness were each eventually replaced, until nothing that came out of Coventry in 1962 was still on the car he drove. He never threw any of it away. From the parts he took off, over the same decades, he assembled a second car in the barn — never registered, never driven, but complete, and, everyone agrees, closer to factory specification than the one in the driveway. There are two further complications I can't tell whether to weight heavily or dismiss. First, for roughly two years in the mid-nineties the road car did not exist as an assembled object at all: the body was at a shop in Oregon, the engine was crated in a friend's garage in Reno, and the rest was in the barn, before it was all brought back together. Second, in 1989 the car was badly damaged in a fire, the insurer declared it a total loss, paid out, and issued a salvage title; he bought it back and rebuilt it from that point. The data plate with the original chassis number is bolted to the barn car, since it stayed with the old body; the registration, the plates, and the shoebox of receipts have always traveled with the road car.

Colin's argument, as he puts it, is that matter is what makes something the same thing, and that the car in the barn simply *is* the 1962 E-Type, however long it sat in pieces, whereas the thing in the driveway is a different object wearing its identity like a costume. Reed's argument is that what his uncle meant to leave him was the thing that has been in the family photographs for four decades, the thing that was driven to Monterey and rebuilt after the fire, and that a heap of original parts that has never been on a road is, at best, the raw material of that car. My own suspicion is that each of them is tracking something real and that they are arguing past one another, but I can't make that suspicion precise enough to be useful to them, and I don't want to walk in on Friday with nothing better than a shrug.

So let me ask you for three things. First: what exactly would have to be true of the world for Reed to be right, and what would have to be true for Colin to be right — that is, what is each of them implicitly committed to about when a collection of parts makes up a single further thing at all, and about what it takes for such a thing to survive the loss and replacement of its parts? Please separate the question of whether there is one thing there or many (the barn apparently contains, besides the second car, the original tool roll, an extra wheel, and an axle he replaced twice, so even Colin's candidate is not a single tidy pile) from the question of which thing, if any, is the one that was driven to Monterey. Second: is there a determinate fact of the matter here that the family is simply failing to see, or is the bequest genuinely unsettled — and if it is unsettled, is that because reality itself is indeterminate at this point, because our word for the kind of thing in question is, or because there are two things present where everyone assumed there was one? I should say that I have never been satisfied by the move of declaring such questions merely verbal; I use the case of the statue and the bronze it is made from in my own teaching, and it never seems to me that the right thing to say is that nothing is really at issue. So if that is where you land, tell me what would have to be added to make the answer satisfying rather than evasive. Third, and most pressingly: I have to help these people reach some actual resolution. Is there a principled way of dividing the estate — one that follows from how the question is properly framed rather than from splitting the difference — or does the metaphysics, worked through honestly, actually favor one of them? And does the paperwork change anything, the salvage title, the two years in crates, the registration: are those evidence about the car, or merely instruments by which a legal system settles a question that metaphysics leaves open?
