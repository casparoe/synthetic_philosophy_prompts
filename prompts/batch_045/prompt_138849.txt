I co-host a tiny podcast for policy and political-theory people (think: one guest, one hour, no ads, mostly listened to by other grad students) and next week's guest is a political theorist who's spent the last few years defending a variant of futarchy she calls "audited-settlement futarchy". I want to not waste the hour on setup, so I need questions that start where the interesting part starts.

Setting her up for you, since I'll have to do this on air anyway. In the basic Robin Hanson picture, elected officials democratically fix a measure of national welfare and then conditional prediction markets decide which policy maximizes it: assets that pay off depending on both which policy is adopted and what the welfare index turns out to be, and the policy whose branch trades higher gets adopted. The slogan is "vote on values, bet on beliefs". Her complaint is that in that setup the market is never actually scored on the thing it's supposed to have an opinion about: only one branch of the conditional market is ever realized, so traders get paid for forecasting the world, not for getting the causal effect of the policy right. Her fix: five years after adoption, a standing panel of economists issues an official estimate of what the index *would have been* under the rejected policy, and the conditional markets are settled against that counterfactual estimate rather than against realized welfare. To keep the panel honest she adds a second-order market that trades on what the panel will say, and a big enough divergence between the two triggers a review. (When she presented this in our department someone asked "and who audits the second-order market" and she said "that's a measurement question, not a governance question", which I think is a dodge.)

Give me nine or ten questions, each with the answer she's most likely to give and then a follow-up keyed to that specific answer. Constraints:

- Nothing that lets her just restate the mechanism — assume people read the abstract.
- At least one question on whether conditional market prices are causal claims at all. She'll probably say that a well-informed trader prices the conditional asset by reasoning about the counterfactual, and that market prices are expected counterfactuals rather than correlations. I want the follow-up that asks what it would even mean for such a market to be *wrong* about the counterfactual, and whether a market can build a track record on a quantity that is never observed.
- At least two questions that make it hard for her to say "that's a measurement question, not a governance question". One route: the panel has to choose a counterfactual method (some kind of synthetic control, a structural model, straight expert elicitation) and rival methods give different numbers, so whoever picks the method is deciding, retroactively, which policies would have won — and traders know that in advance and will price the panel's habits rather than the world. Another route: the second-order market on the panel's report just pushes the same problem up a level; if she answers that the regress stops somewhere at a boring fixed rule, follow up on whether the whole system then inherits whatever credentials that terminal rule has, and why we shouldn't just evaluate *that* directly.
- One on how manipulation changes once settlement is counterfactual. Her likely reply is that it's harder to sabotage an index nobody observes. Follow-up: you can't move the realized number but you *can* move the estimate — fund the studies, capture the panel, publish the models — so does the attack surface just relocates, and who has the incentive to do it? Also worth pressing her on the more familiar worries: someone with a big position making the adopted policy fail, and the fact that the state is both the decision-maker and the counterparty that has to honor a payout five years and one election later.
- One on what the contract is even written on. Voters are supposed to keep control of the welfare measure, but the measure can be revised between the trade and the settlement. If she says you settle on the definition frozen at the time of the trade, follow up on whether that means the market is pricing a definition we've since rejected, and whether revising the measure now expropriates people holding open positions.
- One on horizons and liquidity: settlement five or ten years out, thin or nonexistent markets, original traders gone, and bets whose winners and losers are different generations. If she says the measure can just include long-run components, press her on whether the discount rate inside the index is a value choice smuggled in as measurement.
- One on the value/belief line itself — the standard objection that values and beliefs don't come apart cleanly. Concrete version: some policies change who is in the population or how the index weights them (migration, fertility, people who don't exist yet), so the market is doing value work. Her answer will be that this is what the measure is for; the follow-up should be about how detailed the measure would have to be to settle all of those questions in advance, i.e. whether she's really proposing a constitution written in the form of an index.
- One closing question about what would count against the view. She likes to say "run it on city councils and compare", but if the whole point is that the counterfactual is unobservable then the pilot can't discriminate. Ask her to name the observable proxy she'd accept and the result that would make her give the thing up.

Keep them in spoken English, no jargon dumps, and if a question needs a one-line setup for listeners put it in parentheses after the question. No "how did you get into this" stuff. Typos fine, I'll proably clean it up before recording.
