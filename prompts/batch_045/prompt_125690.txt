I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether the practical stakes facing a believer can make the difference between knowing and not knowing, and we got nowhere — for a reason I only noticed on the walk home. Every case either of us reached for was one we had both encountered before, and each of us already knew which way the standard examples are supposed to cut. Her position: a resident who has checked a chart twice knows the patient has no allergy to the drug when the dose about to be given is routine, and does not know it when that same dose will kill the patient if the allergy is real; same evidence, different knowledge, and nothing about this turns on who is doing the attributing. Mine: the difference is entirely in which error possibilities are live for the person hearing the attribution, and building the subject's stakes into the truth conditions is a category mistake. We could not produce a case the other found probative, because we kept generating vignettes whose intuitive verdict one of us had already memorized.

So I want to build something better for the epistemology reading group I help run, and I would like you to design it. Most of the group knows this debate only from a survey article and one seminar, and we meet in a room with no internet access: nothing in the instrument should require looking anything up, and I do not want any citations, reading suggestions, or "who defended what" questions. Sort people by what they say about cases.

What I am asking for:

**A ten-item sorting quiz.** Each item is a short concrete vignette followed by three answer options (one of which should be something like "the case is underdescribed — it depends on a further fact," where that is genuinely the honest answer). Every item must be answerable by someone who has never heard the labels: no question may ask whether one is a contextualist, an invariantist, a relativist, or anything of the sort. One item may be a calibration case — a low-stakes/high-stakes pair with identical evidence, in the spirit of the classic bank, airport, and train examples — because I want to see how people anchor. The other nine may not be thin reskins of those examples, and may not be reskins of each other.

**Cases I want represented, with the wrinkle each is meant to introduce.** A system holds the evidence and the stakes fall on a third party, while the person saying "it knows" is an auditor with small stakes of her own. Stakes that belong to the believer's much later self, who does not share her current preferences. Stakes that are purely epistemic: if the belief is false, she will use it as a premise in thousands of downstream inferences, with no practical or moral cost at all. Stakes the believer does not know about and could not reasonably discover, because a stranger has secretly wagered on her being right. Stakes the believer deliberately created by betting, versus stakes imposed on her. Stakes that are high for one party and low for another party with opposed interests, when both hinge on the same proposition. Huge stakes attached to a minute probability of error, alongside modest stakes attached to a substantial probability of error. Stakes that differ by contrast — high for its being p rather than q, negligible for its being p rather than r. And many small decisions, each individually low-stakes, whose errors compound if the belief is false.

**A decision tree underneath the quiz, built from the logical space rather than from a list of names.** I want the branch nodes to be at least: whether any practical factor makes a difference to the truth of a knowledge attribution at all, or whether the appearance of one is explained pragmatically; whose situation the difference tracks, among the subject's, the attributor's, and the assessor's; whether the difference lies in the standard to be met, in the content of what is said, or only in what may properly be asserted; whether practical factors operate by making an error possibility relevant or by the cost of being wrong, and if the latter, whether it is cost alone or cost discounted by probability; whether the encroachment, if there is any, is into knowledge, into justification, into the rationality of action, or into whether the subject counts as believing at all; and whether the effect runs in one direction only or can also lower the bar.

**Leaves that must appear:** classical invariantism with a pragmatic or error-theoretic treatment of the data; relevant-alternatives contextualism; attributor contextualism of the Cohen–DeRose sort; subject-sensitive invariantism; interest-relative invariantism of the Fantl–McGrath sort; the view that stakes affect belief or actionability but not knowledge; a credal view on which stakes set the threshold credence for knowledge; assessment-sensitive relativism; and contrastivism. Add further leaves, or an "unnamed" leaf, if the space requires them.

**The diagnostics I actually care about.** Give me the scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves, and mark the patterns that are internally unstable or that no one could hold coherently. For the combinations of answers that are coherent but unoccupied, say whether that is because the view is incoherent on reflection, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Then flag the pairs of leaves your items cannot pry apart — I strongly suspect subject-sensitive invariantism and interest-relative invariantism collapse under any case I can think of, and I cannot construct anything that separates attributor contextualism from assessment relativism except by asking directly about disagreement, which I do not want to do — and tell me what further case would do the work if one exists.

Give me the items first, then the tree, then the key, then the diagnostics.
