I'm in my fourth year of a PhD in comparative historical sociology — dissertation on why a couple of countries locked in pay-as-you-go public pensions in the interwar period while neighbors with basically the same fiscal capacity and demography went with funded schemes — and the same two analogies keep doing opposite work in my department, and I've never once seen either of them actually audited.

My supervisor's is the landscape: history is a ball rolling over corrugated ground. Small early kicks decide which valley it ends up in, after that the walls of the valley hold it there, and the valleys aren't ranked, so there's no sense in which one outcome is better than another. He uses it to license two things at once: that the institutional forms we live with are frozen accidents, and that counterfactual questions like "would a funded system have showed up here anyway, thirty years later?" are unanswerable in principle, not merely hard. The labor economist down the hall runs the reverse picture: history is water. Rivers take wildly different routes but they all reach the sea, so contingency shapes only the path and, given enough time, the efficient arrangement wins — which he uses to tell me route-dependence is a short-run curiosity I can basically ignore in a dissertation about a 1920s decision. What I want is an audit of both rather than a verdict on my case: lay out explicitly (a table is fine) what plays the role of the ball, the terrain, the kick, the valley walls and the downhill direction in the first picture, and of the water, the watershed, the gradient, the sea and "enough time" in the second; then where each mapping breaks. My own list, so you know where I'm coming from: the landscape imports a terrain fixed before anything moves, whereas my terrain is made of other actors who are themselves moving; it imports one ball where I have a population of ministries, unions and insurers; and it imports friction without a scale, since "locked in" has to mean locked against some size of shove. The water picture looks worse to me — the sea is a destination, so it builds the efficiency claim into the plumbing instead of arguing for it, and I can't see what would count as the sea where there's no agreed welfare metric. I'm also trying to keep apart three grades that both analogies blur: same endpoint reached by a different route; a different endpoint that's functionally equivalent to the one we got; and a different endpoint that persists. And I've been toying with a fourth that neither picture has room for — a self-eroding lock-in, where the same increasing-returns mechanism that pins you in the valley (coordination benefits, sunk complementary investments, everyone else having already adapted around it) also slowly lowers the walls, because it turns the arrangement into one conspicuous target and makes exit cheaper the moment a few visible actors move. Does that fourth thing deserve to be its own category, or is it just ordinary lock-in plus a slow trend? Please don't just re-run the standard textbook examples at me (the keyboard layout, the videotape format war, rail gauge) — I know them, and I know the first is contested now; I want the mechanics.

Two more things that would help. Any "would it have happened anyway" claim has to be indexed to a perturbation class, and my suspicion is that a lot of the inevitabilist talk in my literature slides quietly between replaying the tape with a butterfly's wing and replaying it with, say, the fiscal shock of a war removed — say how much of the dispute in this area is really a dispute about wich replay is being run. And tell me whether either analogy can bear any weight at all for the question I actually have to answer, or whether I should reach for something else (I keep coming back to scaffolding being rebuilt while people are standing on it, but I don't trust it); if there's a better comparison, name it and say what it buys that these two don't. Could you also flag how confident you are in each substantive claim you make and single out the one you're least sure of? American English please. happy to be told I've framed the whole thing wrong.
