Before I decide whether any evolutionary debunking argument in metaethics should move me, I want a specification of what one would have to deliver. Write the desiderata an adequate evolutionary debunking argument must meet on the following reading of adequacy: it is addressed to a reflective moral realist who already grants the empirical genealogy and wants to know why that genealogy is epistemically significant at all. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp.

The list has to settle at least these: which target the argument must actually hit — moral belief, moral knowledge, moral realism, or only the contents of some moral judgments — and whether reaching only the last of these counts as a success; why the genealogical story must be fine-grained enough to predict the content of the judgments we actually have rather than merely that we would have some moral judgments or other; what it owes on scope, i.e. why the same style of explanation does not discredit perception, memory, or logic, or the epistemic norms the argument itself invokes; whether it must avoid normative premises that its own genealogy would undermine; and, if it makes an empirical claim, what finding would refute it. It should also say what an argument owes to the reply that selection shaped a general capacity for normative judgment rather than any particular judgments.

Two things I especially want flagged. First, whether any pair of requirements is jointly unsatisfiable: I suspect the demand that the argument rest on no normative premises sits badly with the demand that it yield a conclusion about what we ought to believe, and that full generality across moral judgment sits badly with content-specific genealogical detail. Second, whether an argument that debunks only a proper subclass of moral judgment — judgments about purity, about partiality to kin, about retributive punishment — faces the same list or a weaker one; I think the local case is where the real work is, and I want to know whether the desiderata change shape there.

Briefly, at the end, score two contenders against the list: the Darwinian-dilemma style argument (Street, 2006) and the explanatory-superfluity style argument (Joyce, The Evolution of Morality). Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the full set is jointly satisfiable, and which single requirement excludes the most candidates — then the requirements with their cases, then the scoring; no preamble and no restating the question.
