I am writing a paper on whether the blame of a hypocritical blamer can still be in order, and everyone I talk to reaches for the same picture to settle it: blame is like a complaint brought before a court, and there is a maxim in equity that a plaintiff who comes to court with unclean hands in the very matter complained of gets nothing. On that picture the hypocrite's blame is not merely rude or unfair; it is void, and the blamee may set it aside without answering it. I want that picture audited rather than asserted.

Please do the following.

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the plaintiff, the defendant, the court, the cause of action, the transaction at issue, the unclean conduct, the remedy sought, and the dismissal. Mark which of these have a genuine counterpart in ordinary interpersonal blame and which exist only because the analogy imports them.

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter for this use from the ones that do not. I have in mind, among others: that a court is an authority with a duty to hear only certain complaints, whereas a blamer has no office; that equity's maxim is confined to the same transaction rather than to the plaintiff's general character; that the court's output is enforceable and blame's is not; and that blame characteristically has audiences beyond the person blamed.

3. Apply the audit to three cases, where the blamer's own fault differs in kind rather than only in degree. (a) Ada blames Bo for taking money from an industry-funded think tank; Ada took the same money years ago, has disclosed and renounced it, and now campaigns against the practice. (b) Cara, who never took such money but whose pension is invested in the same industry, blames Bo on the same ground; Bo says her hands are dirty too. (c) Dan blames Bo for the same thing while himself doing something he admits is worse. Does the clean-hands logic give a verdict in each, and does it scale until no one has standing?

4. Say which conclusion the analogy can actually carry. In particular, distinguish three things it might be used to establish: that the hypocrite's judgment is likely to be mistaken or biased; that the hypocrite is being unfair in blaming; and that the blame itself lacks the status it purports to have. I suspect the analogy is strong for one of these, weak for another, and silent on the third, but I want that argued rather than assumed.

5. If the equity picture fails, say what a better analogy would be — for instance blame as a demand for an account within an ongoing relationship, or as the withdrawing of goodwill, or as the pressing of a claim one is not oneself prepared to honor — and say which of the three conclusions that alternative can support.

Finally, tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong, and what single consideration, if I could establish it, would lead you to a different verdict on whether the hypocrite's blame is void.
