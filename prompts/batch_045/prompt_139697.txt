I'm a senior in high school, and last summer I had a paid internship at the small art museum in the town where I live — mostly photographing and inventorying the collection. That is how I ended up in the middle of a problem I don't know how to think about, and I have to speak to the board on Thursday for about three minutes, so I would like to know whether I am wrong about any of this before I open my mouth.

Here is what I found. Since it opened in 1976, the museum has shown a large oil painting of the river bluff north of town, catalogued and labelled as the work of Delia Vance, a painter who was well known in this part of the state in the 1930s and 40s and who is the reason the museum exists: her family gave the town the building and most of the collection. While I was going through a box of her papers I found letters from her younger sister Margaret, who lived with her and worked in her studio for roughly fifteen years. In them Margaret discusses having painted and signed a number of pictures in Delia's name, with Delia's knowledge, because the dealer would not look at anything under Margaret's own name. In one letter Delia writes that her sister's skies were better than anything she could manage and that she sometimes went over them herself. The conservator the museum consulted thinks the bluff painting is one of these: the underdrawing, the handling of the sky, and a pigment that otherwise appears only in Margaret's own signed work all point to her, and she thinks one passage of the sky may be Delia's hand after all. The important part, though, is that this is not a copy. There is no painting by Delia that it reproduces. It is Margaret's own composition, in Delia's manner, signed with Delia's name.

Two more things about the collection. The museum also owns a portfolio of etchings printed from Delia's own copper plates in the late 1970s, a decade after her death, by Margaret. Those are catalogued as Delia's, and no one has ever treated that as a problem; when I asked why the painting was different I was told prints are simply different and that I was splitting hairs. And Margaret's own signed work — three canvases — has been in the museum's storage since 1976 and has never been hung; the museum's records list her only as a studio assistant.

The director's position, as he put it to me, is that we should change almost nothing: a small printed card at most, and certainly no show of Margaret's paintings. His argument is that it is the same object, the same paint on the same canvas, that people in this town have stood in front of for fifty years; that nothing anyone sees is any different from what it was in June; that taking the name off would be taking something away from the town in exchange for a fact about paperwork; and that hanging Margaret's work now would turn the collection into an argument about a dead woman's family rather than a collection. He also said that whatever I think I now see in the picture, I am only reading the label into the paint.

That last part is the part I keep turning over. I have looked at this painting maybe two hundred times, and after reading the letters I see the brushwork in the foreground differently: what I would have called loose and confident now looks to me like someone working inside another person's manner. He says that is just my imagination, and that if the picture were by Delia after all I would feel the old way again, which shows the feeling is not about the paint.

My own proposal for Thursday was going to be that we hang the bluff painting as Margaret's and hang her three stored canvases next to it. But another board member pointed out that if we do that we are valuing the picture because of the signature, which is exactly the mistake the original label made, and I could not answer her. Delia's granddaughter is on the board and has said privately that she will resign if the name comes off.

So: is the director right that a fact about paperwork cannot change what the object is worth to look at, and that my change in perception is just a prejudice I should discount? If he is wrong, where exactly is he wrong, given that the painting copies nothing, that Delia may have touched the sky herself, and that nobody minds the posthumous etchings? And what should I actually ask the board to do on Thursday, in three minutes, with one vote?
