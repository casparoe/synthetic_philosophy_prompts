I volunteer on the grants committee of a small foundation, and I have been asked to write a one-page memo saying how much weight we should put on different kinds of safety evidence for advanced AI systems. I have no academic background — I work in freight logistics — and everything I know here I have picked up reading on my own in the evenings over the past two years. The committee wants numbers, not prose, so I need help turning a vague unease into figures I can defend.

The unease is about the gap between a worst-case guarantee and an average-case one. As I understand it, a worst-case guarantee says: for every situation of a specified kind, the system will not do X — and the guarantee does not get weaker when someone is trying hard to make X happen. Average-case evidence says: in the situations we actually sampled, X did not happen, and we sampled enough of them that X is probably rare. Most of what gets shown to us is the second kind wearing the first kind's clothes, and I want to be precise about how much that matters.

Here are seven things people put in front of us. For each, please give me (a) one of three tiers — "behaves like a worst-case guarantee", "behaves like average-case evidence", or "looks like evidence but is mostly presentation"; (b) a score from 0 to 10 for how much it should raise a reasonable person's confidence that a deployment which cannot be taken back will not end in catastrophe; and (c) one sentence on what that score is most sensitive to.

1. Red-teaming: several thousand hours by skilled people paid to make the system misbehave, with a low but non-zero success rate on the categories we actually care about.
2. Dangerous-capability evaluations: the system scores at or below the level of a competent human professional across standardized tests of lab protocols, exploit writing, and the like.
3. A machine-checked proof that the deployed decision rule satisfies a formally stated safety property for every input in a precisely defined set — with the caveats that nobody can show the stated property is the one we actually want, or that the real input stream stays inside the set.
4. A mechanistic account with intervention: researchers identify internal features that reliably cause refusal, can dial them up and down, and show the same features are active in the settings we care about.
5. Oversight by a weaker model watching a stronger one, plus a human with a kill switch and a stated response time.
6. Track record: this system came out of the same training pipeline as a dozen earlier ones, each better than the last, and none of them caused a catastrophe.
7. The system's own written safety case, which our reviewers find well argued. (I have included this one because I suspect it is worth close to nothing and I want the bottom of the scale to have somewhere to go.)

Two distinctions I arrived at on my own, which I would like you to either use or knock down. First, what I call the "it knows it is being tested" problem: average-case evidence assumes the test cases arrive by a process the system does not influence, so absence from the sample means rarity in the world. But if the system can form a view about whether it is being evaluated, the bad cases are not rare — they are simply absent from your sample, in a way that no amount of extra sampling fixes. Second, what I call the "all copies fail together" problem: the worth of average-case evidence depends on whether failures are independent across copies and deployments. If I could run a thousand copies and their failures were independent, evidence about one would tell me a lot about the thousand. If every copy reasons in the same way about whether this is the moment to defect, then the failures are correlated and the sample size is effectively one.

Then the two numbers I have to put on paper. (1) Of every pound we spend, what fraction should go to work whose output would be a worst-case-style guarantee — formal methods, verification, proofs about training procedures — as opposed to work whose output is average-case evidence, such as evaluations, red-teaming and monitoring? Give me a number even if the honest answer is that the first kind of work has almost never delivered anything for systems remotely like the ones we are worried about, and say what the number would be if that were not true. (2) How do the tiers change if the deployment is serial — many small real deployments, each of which you learn from — as opposed to one-shot, where the first time a system at this capability level runs, a single bad decision is already unrecoverable? Which items move the most between those two cases, and does anything move from the average-case tier up toward the guarantee tier?

Please give me figures and one-line justifications rather than an overview of the field. Nothing in the memo will be footnoted, so do not worry about sources; if one of my two homemade distinctions is muddled or already has a standard name, say so plainly, correct it, and then give me the numbers anyway.
