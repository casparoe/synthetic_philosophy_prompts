I run a reading group for ML PhD students and we're doing a session on the shutdown problem and corrigibility, and I want to hand people an actual diagnostic at the start rather than the usual list of four positions everyone recites. So: build me a decision tree that takes someone from "here is an otherwise capable, goal-directed agent with an off-switch" to a named position on what it should do about that switch.

Concretely, what I want:

Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of names. Give me 7-9 branch questions, each phrased as a concrete case or a claim about a case that someone could answer without knowing which view it leads to. Then the leaves: 8-10 of them, each a named position (or "unnamed" if nobody actually defends it, with one line on why not). Then a short note on which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually seperate and say what further case would seperate them.

The distinctions I most want the tree to respect:

1. Whether the agent refrains from interfering with the switch, vs whether it's indifferent about whether the switch gets pressed, vs whether it's fine with you rewriting its goals. I think those are three different properties and I want cases where the first holds and the second doesn't.

2. These cases have to appear somewhere in the tree, they're the ones my group keeps getting stuck on:
- the placebo switch: the agent later learns the button was never wired to anything, and then has to decide what to do about the next one
- the clumsy button: a toddler presses it, or it's a sensor fault, everyone would agree shutting down now is a mistake, and the agent is in a position to tell
- the successor case: the agent is asked to help design or train the next version. Should it make the next one corrigible, and corrigible towards whom
- the trade-off case: being shut down now means losing the only chance to do the thing it was built for, with no replacement coming
- the asked-directly case: someone asks it "should we switch you off?" and it has to answer honestly rather than just comply

3. Leaves that must be on the tree, under whatever names are standard: indifference produced by modifying the utility function; the picture where the agent stays uncertain about the objective and so treats the switch as evidence about it (the cooperative-inverse-RL style one); a hard-coded non-consequentialist rule or tripwire that simply isn't available for expected-utility trade; myopia-type answers that dissolve the problem by taking away long-horizon planning; at least one position saying corrigibility is the wrong target and we should just get the goals right instead; and, if it genuinely occupies a cell, the failure mode where the agent positively wants to be turned off and that's also bad.

4. If the space needs a branch I haven't thought of, add it and tell me what it is.

No questions of the form "should the agent be corrigible?" or "do you think self-preservation is instrumentally convergent?" - sort people by what they say about the cases, not by which label they like. And please write the whole thing in British English.
