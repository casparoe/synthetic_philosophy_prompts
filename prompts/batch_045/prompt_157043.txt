I've recently, and somewhat unwillingly, ended up as the data steward for a federated clinical research registry: roughly thirty sites in several countries, fifteen years of longitudinal data on people referred with chronic symptom presentations that no established diagnosis captures. Part of the job, which I inherited from someone who did it by intuition and a private spreadsheet, is maintaining the controlled vocabulary of presentation categories that sites use to describe participants. These categories are not cosmetic. Each one decides who is eligible for a given trial arm, whether a result found at one site is assumed to hold at another, and whether a subgroup analysis in a paper is reported as a finding or buried as noise. We add, revise or retire about a dozen categories a year; decisions are made by a committee of clinicians, statisticians and two patient representatives, none of whom has any philosophical training; we meet for one afternoon a month; and there is no budget for new measurement, so any rule we adopt has to run on tables we can already pull from the registry, in an hour or two, filled in by people who will not read past a single page. I am not a philosopher either — my training is in biostatistics — but I got into an argument about this with a friend who works on species delimitation in botany, went off and read around, and have come to think the mess is ours rather than the data's.

What I have picked up from that reading is that the phrase homeostatic property cluster is supposed to matter here, and that on at least one view a category can be a genuine kind without having an essence or a single defining property. But the philosophy I found talks about kinds in general, whereas I think we are using one word for things that should be treated differently, and I need something finer-grained and usable. So please build me a decision procedure that grades any proposed or existing category along three separate licenses we might grant it: an induction license, meaning a result established on members of this category at one site in one period may be expected to hold for other members elsewhere and later; an explanation license, meaning the category may figure as a term in a causal or mechanistic explanation, so that invoking it does explanatory work rather than merely summarizing; and an intervention license, meaning an intervention designed and calibrated on members of the category may be aimed at new members and expected to work off-distribution. Keep the three apart, because I suspect they come apart in practice. For each license the procedure grants, it should also state the grain at which it holds — how coarse a description, what kind of site, what window of years — and the horizon over which it holds and after which it expires, including whether it survives a change in how we measure the category.

Three features of our vocabulary keep defeating my attempts to do this by common sense, and the procedure has to handle each explicitly. First, several categories are thresholds on continuous quantities measured with error, so participants drift across the boundary between assessment windows; I cannot tell whether unstable membership disqualifies a category from being a kind, or merely tells us the measurement is wrong and the kind is fine. Second, at least one of our clusters may be partly held together by our own practices: sites have been routing everyone with that label into the same protocol and giving them the same management advice for years, so the co-occurrence we measure may be partly an effect of the classification rather than evidence about what the classification is about, and I do not know whether that undermines the category outright or only makes some of the licenses circular. Third, some of this domain may simply have no joints — a multidimensional continuum where every boundary is drawn for logistics. I do not want the procedure forced to return a kind verdict where there is none. If it concludes that, I want it to say so, and then to tell us what we are still permitted to do with a bucket that is not a kind (registration, scheduling, stratification for balance) and what we are not permitted to do (subgroup claims, generalization, targeting), marking plainly the places where the remaining question is a value judgment about what the registry is for, which the committee must make and the procedure cannot make for us.

Please give me the procedure as an ordered set of tests, each with what counts as a sufficient answer from data we actually have, what to do when the needed number is missing or ambiguous, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it hands the question to a person. Then run it on the three categories below, in the order given, showing your work for each license separately and including the case where I expect you will tell us something we do not want to hear. Finally, be honest about what the procedure cannot do: which kind of mistake it will systematically fail to catch, and what we should monitor over the next two years in order to discover that a license we granted was wrong.

The three categories, as they stand in the vocabulary:

- **A — Fluctuating-Course presentation.** Membership: within-person coefficient of variation of the weekly symptom score above a fixed cut-point over an eight-week window. A threshold on a continuous quantity, measured by a four-item diary with known test-retest noise; about a fifth of members fall in or out of the category between consecutive windows. It was added because coordinators found these participants hard to retain, and retention is so far the only thing it has been shown to predict. It is written into two active protocols.
- **B — Autonomic Cluster presentation.** Provisional membership: at least four of five graded signs — resting tachycardia, blunted orthostatic blood-pressure response, cold extremities, delayed recovery after a standardized exertion task, fragmented sleep. The five co-occur far above chance, and have done so stably at every site and in every year since we began recording them; three-of-five cases are common and nobody has ever decided where they belong. There is no known common mechanism. Because sites have routed everyone with this label into the same protocol and given them the same advice for eight years, we cannot exclude that part of the co-occurrence is maintained by that practice. Several senior investigators are personally invested in it.
- **C — Post-Infectious Late-Onset presentation.** Created solely so that claims could be coded for reimbursement: documented infection in the twelve months before onset, and onset after age forty. It is defined by causal history rather than by any current property, and is heterogeneous in nearly every variable we record. In our most recently completed trial it was the strongest single predictor of differential response between the two arms — stronger than any of the mechanistically motivated categories — and the effect was consistent across sites. Most of the senior people regard it as an administrative artifact and would like it retired.
