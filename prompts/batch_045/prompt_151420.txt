I serve on the five-person panel that adjudicates breach complaints for a mutual-aid network I will call the Pledge Assembly. Members file pledges on a public register — I pledge to contribute twenty pounds a month; I pledge to drive any member to a medical appointment; I pledge to support any member who falls into hardship — and other members may name themselves as beneficiaries of a given pledge. When a member complains that a pledge was broken, we have to rule, and we cannot rule by saying that it depends on which theory of promising you like. So before we argue about which theory is true, I want the specification: what would a theory of promissory obligation have to get right in order to be usable by a body like ours?

Write it as a list of desiderata, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as either non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. I would rather the list be driven by the cases than by the literature, so here are the ones we actually face: the redundant pledge, where someone pledges to do what they were already obliged to do; the pledge no one relied on, because the beneficiary forgot it, never cared, or never knew; the coerced, fraudulently induced, or half-understood pledge, including pledges whose wording was vague; the indexed pledge, whose content is fixed later by a formula the pledger does not control and cannot currently predict, so that it is unclear whether the obligation attaches to the outcome or only to compliance with the procedure; the pledge to future or unidentified beneficiaries, as when someone joins the Assembly after a pledge to support any member in hardship was filed; the pledge whose beneficiary has died, where heirs want to press the complaint; the grading problem, where a trivial pledge seems genuinely binding and an enormously costly one seems not to be; the immoral pledge, and the pledge to help someone else do something wrong; the released pledge, where a beneficiary cancels with a word, and the refused release, where a beneficiary spitefully declines to cancel; the re-pledge, where someone pledges again to do what they already pledged; and the pledge to one's own future self, where there is no second party at all.

Three further things. First, some of the desiderata should be contrastive: a theory must distinguish promising from announcing an intention, predicting, threatening, consenting, swearing an oath, and signing a contract, and it must not quietly collapse promissory obligation into reliance-based or expectation-based duty. Second, include adjudicative desiderata, since a rule we cannot apply publicly is useless to us: determinacy, publicity, and not turning on facts about the beneficiary's inner states. Third, tell me which of the demands I am tempted to make are really only partisan: that the obligation be derivable from non-normative facts alone; that it be reducible to a single underlying moral reason such as harm or fairness; that every valid pledge bind with the same pro tanto force; that the theory work without appealing to any social practice; that it yield a verdict in every case above; and that it explain why a small welfare gain never defeats a promise. Flag any pair of genuine desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories. A brief scorecard at the end — practice and convention theories, reliance theories, expectation and assurance theories, and the view that promising is a normative power to alter one's own obligations — is welcome, but keep it to a few lines per view; the criteria and their cases are what I need.
