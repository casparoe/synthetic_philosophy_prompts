I'm finishing the third chapter of my thesis, which is a defense of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness in the strong form — no phenomenal properties at all, and introspective judgment systematically misrepresents our own sensory states as having a qualitative character they don't have. My supervisor is broadly sympathetic but she keeps saying the view is "built to survive everything," and I want to actually answer that rather than just insisting that no, it's a substantive claim with real exposure. So: what would count against it? I don't need the standard objections listed back at me — I know them (the isn't-the-illusion-itself-a-phenomenal-state move, the collapse-into-weak-realism-about-seemings worry, whether the theory gives any non-circular account of what we're misrepresenting our states as being, whether it can keep the desiderative and normative weight of pain without sneaking qualia back in the back door). What I need is a map of which of these actually has teeth, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and split between the ones an illusionist would herself concede as counting against the view and the ones we'd dismiss as question-begging.

The bit I most want stress-tested is an objection I've been kicking around and genuinely can't tell whether it's new or just confused — call it the dispelling asymmetry. The visual-illusion analogy illusionism leans on doesn't seem to survive being looked at closely: with a Muller-Lyer-type illusion, learning it's an illusion changes things even though the look persists — you stop trusting the comparison, you'd bet differently, you'd act differently if you had to act on it. Whereas learning (and fully believing) that your experience of red has no intrinsic qualitative character seems to change nothing downstream at all: not your bets, not your preferences, not what you'd pay to skip a pain, not how you'd choose between two futures that differ only in what they're like. If the analogy's other term behaves like that, is the analogy doing any work? And is the asymmetry itself evidence for realism, or is this just "but it still seems like something" in decision-theoretic costume — i.e. does an illusionist have a decent story about why this would be exactly the kind of illusion that survives full intellectual correction?

I'd also like an honest verdict on the empirical side, since I sometimes claim this is an empirical position. Say we got a case where people reliably report a difference in what things are like for them while every first-order discriminative and functional difference we can measure stays fixed, and where the report pattern looks nothing like response bias or a generic tendency to over-report richness — I've been calling these silent metamers in my head. Would I count that as decisive, or would we (rightly) say that reports are just the output of a misrepresentational system and shrug? Rank that alongside the conceptual defeaters. And if your conclusion is that the view has been qualified into something insulated from any possible finding, say so plainly, and tell me whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just what a metaphysical position looks like when it's going well. I'd find a concrete counterexample-style case most persuasive, and big-picture methodological hand-waving least.
