I am taking an advanced seminar on collective cognition and the philosophy of mind, and last week we spent a rather frustrating session on the Ant Fugue dialogue from Hofstadter's 1979 book Gödel, Escher, Bach, featuring Aunt Hillary—the ant colony who, in the story, holds up her side of a conversation with Achilles and the Tortoise even though no individual ant knows English or grasps the whole exchange, and whose apparent intelligence survives the loss of many ants but dissolves once too many have been removed by the Anteater. Our discussion stalled because everyone agreed on the basic dialectic—emergentists say the colony has a mind because the right kind of organization can constitute a subject of mental states, while skeptics say we are merely confronted with a distributed control system that behaves as if it had beliefs and desires—but nobody could agree on how far the Aunt Hillary intuition should extend to harder, less anthropomorphically scripted cases.

After class, while discussing this with my friends, I proposed a variant that my study group has been calling the palimpsest colony. Imagine a colony, whether engineered by future biologists or discovered on another continent, whose pheromone-based communication network does more than coordinate present activity: it also lays down durable chemical records of past colony-wide patterns of trail-laying and signal exchange, and it can differentially reinforce or suppress those records, so that at a later time the colony's global behavior comments on and revises its own earlier collective choices in a way no individual ant represents or could represent. Suppose, moreover, that this colony passes a demanding colony-level test for unprompted error correction: after being experimentally tricked into laying a trail to a dead end, the colony not only abandons that trail but, days later and before any single ant encounters the relevant dead end again, reliably slows its advance and lays an anticipatory detour trail when placed in a merely similar choice architecture. My friends are split on whether this shows the palimpsest colony has crossed the threshold from mere feedback to genuine mentality, or whether it is just a more intricate version of the same mindless optimization that a sophisticated routing network exhibits.

Please write an essay, in continuous prose, that weighs the arguments for and against attributing a genuine mind—or, if that is too strong, genuine mental states such as colony-level beliefs, desires, or a rudimentary form of self-directed agency—to the palimpsest colony described above. The essay should not end on a non-committal note: I want you to reach and defend a verdict, saying either that the palimpsest colony does have a mind (or the relevant mental properties), that it does not, or that it does so only under further conditions you specify. Please be concrete about what you take having a mind to require (for example, whether it requires consciousness, a unified subject of experience, the capacity for self-reference, or merely the right functional organization), and apply that standard to the case without relying on vague appeals to emergence. Because our professor insists that we not disguise hand-waving as depth, you should explicitly address the strongest skeptical objection—that we are illegitimately projecting a self onto a system with no center and no sentience—as well as the strongest argument from the Aunt Hillary side—that if interactive complexity of the right kind can make a mind out of neurons, it can make a mind out of ants and chemical trails. Finally, please write the response as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or email, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
