I'm in a seminar on statutory interpretation and we've been working through Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457 (decided February 29, 1892) — the case in which a New York church was sued for a penalty after contracting, in 1887, with an English clergyman named E. Walpole Warren to relocate and serve as its rector, seemingly in violation of an 1885 act making it unlawful, in any manner whatsoever, to prepay the transportation of or otherwise assist or encourage the migration of any alien into the United States under a prior contract for him to perform labor or service of any kind here. The passage my professor flagged is the middle of Justice Brewer's opinion for the Court:

"It must be conceded that the act of the corporation is within the letter of this section, for the relation of rector to his church is one of service, and implies labor on the one side with compensation on the other. Not only are the general words 'labor' and 'service' both used, but also, as it were to guard against any narrow interpretation and emphasize a breadth of meaning, to them is added 'of any kind;' and, further, as noticed by the circuit judge in his opinion, the fifth section, which makes specific exceptions, among them professional actors, artists, lecturers, singers, and domestic servants, strengthens the idea that every other kind of labor and service was intended to be reached by the first section. While there is great force to this reasoning, we cannot think congress intended to denounce with penalties a transaction like that in the present case. It is a familiar rule that a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit nor within the intention of its makers. This has been often asserted, and the Reports are full of cases illustrating its application. This is not the substitution of the will of the judge for that of the legislator; for frequently words of general meaning are used in a statute, words broad enough to include an act in question, and yet a consideration of the whole legislation, or of the circumstances surrounding its enactment, or of the absurd results which follow from giving such broad meaning to the words, makes it unreasonable to believe that the legislator intended to include the particular act."

I can follow the opinion as advocacy, but I can't reconstruct it as reasoning, and I'd like help with that rather than with the labels ("textualism," "purposivism," "absurdity canon") that I've so far found more confusing than helpful. What is the logical form of the move from "within the letter" to "not within the statute"? Is the conclusion a claim about what the statute means, about what its legal effect should be, or about whether this case falls under a statute that means just what it says — i.e., is Brewer narrowing the text or carving a non-textual exception out of it? Relatedly, what work is done by conceding that the fifth section's enumerated exceptions give the government's reading "great force" and then ruling against it anyway: what does that tell you about the weight the Court assigns to inferences drawn from the text itself? And is "not within its spirit nor within the intention of its makers" one test or two, and if two, how do they differ?

The specific thing my study group split over — and the thing I most want your view on — is what sort of fact "the intention of its makers" is supposed to be. I suggested it could mean at least three things: (a) what the words would have conveyed to a competent reader at the time of enactment; (b) which applications the lawmakers actually expected the words to have; or (c) the end they were aiming at and whether applying the words here would advance or frustrate it. My friend argued that the maxim as stated only needs (a), so that it is perfectly compatible with a fairly strict textualism: general words get their ordinary meaning, but that meaning is fixed against surrounding context, and "spirit" is just a name for that posture. I claimed Brewer is doing something stronger, and that his list — "a consideration of the whole legislation, or of the circumstances surrounding its enactment, or of the absurd results" — is evidence for (b) or (c), since none of those three items looks like a source of ordinary meaning. Where exactly in the quoted sentences does each reading find support, and where does it strain? Does Brewer's treatment of absurd results — apparently as evidence of what the legislator intended rather than as an independent license to disregard the text — favor one of us?

Finally, here is a hypothetical I built to test the readings, and I'd like you to run the maxim on it. A state legislature, alarmed in the 1970s by high-pressure door-to-door sales fraud, enacts: "No seller shall solicit or conclude a contract for goods or services at any place other than the seller's established place of business unless the buyer is given three days to cancel." An amendment expressly exempting contracts concluded by telephone was proposed and voted down — not because the legislature wanted telephone contracts covered, but because it could not agree how far such an exemption should reach and preferred to leave the line-drawing to courts. Decades later, a firm concludes contracts entirely by video call with a buyer sitting at her kitchen table. The text plainly covers it, the mischief that prompted the statute (a stranger's physical presence pressuring someone at home) is at best partly present, and the legislature demonstrably declined to write the exemption. On each of readings (a), (b), and (c), what result follows, and why? Then consider the variant in which the only fact available is that the 1970s legislature never so much as imagined video calls — no refusal to exempt, no expectation, nothing — and say whether the maxim yields anything determinate there. If it doesn't, I'd like to know what you think that shows about what the maxim was really doing in Brewer's hands.
