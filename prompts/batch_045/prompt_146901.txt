I have no academic background at all. I got into this after my grandmother's funeral, where it came out that basically nobody in the family knew why we left Eastern Europe in 1947. My great-grandfather never talked about it, and once he died there was nobody left to ask. Someone at the reception said "some things are better left forgotten" and it made me strangely angry, and I've been chewing on it ever since. I've poked around online a bit: apparently there's a whole fight among philosophers and historians about whether communities have a duty to remember or whether forgetting is sometimes the thing we owe each other. I can't follow most of what I find, so I tried to make my own rule and it keeps breaking on me.

Roughly, my rule is: it's fine to stop actively remembering something as long as the fact is still on file — somebody could produce it if it really mattered, under oath, say — and what's wrong is destroying the file. The memory can fade as long as the record survives. I call it the file-not-the-feeling rule. Here are my gut verdicts:

1. A country comes out of a dictatorship and runs a truth-and-reconciliation thing: people who committed abuses get amnesty if they confess publicly, there are no prosecutions afterwards, and the details aren't taught in schools — but the transcripts sit in an archive anyone can request. Fine by me. I'd even say good.

2. My grandmother's family had an uncle who stole from his own siblings. His name was crossed out of the family tree, and the kids were never told why. Not fine.

3. A couple down the road: he had an affair five years ago. They agreed never to mention it again, they mostly don't think about it, but if you asked them outright they'd say it happened. Fine — healthy, even.

4. A friend said something really cruel to me years ago and apologized properly. I've genuinely forgotten what she said; I just remember that she apologized. Fine, and I actively don't want to be reminded.

5. In the town where I grew up there was a man who ran the grocery store and everyone knew he'd been a guard at a camp. Nobody ever said anything, and when he died the obituary was completely blank about those years. Not fine — and this feels much worse to me than 3 and 4, even though "something bad happened and people stopped talking about it" describes all three.

6. Someone convicted of a minor offence at nineteen wants the local paper's twenty-five-year-old article delisted. Fine. A politician wants an article about a corruption investigation he was never charged over delisted right before an election. Not fine. (I know, that one's easy.)

7. My father's drinking. The family never once mentioned it; he's been sober twenty years. Fine — I'd defend it. But 7 and 2 are both a family shutting up about something shameful, and I feel completely differently about them, and I can't say why.

8. A woman who survived a school shooting as a teenager, now in her thirties, says she wants to stop being interviewed and doesn't want to be a symbol. Fine — let her stop. But a government quietly defunding and letting a massacre memorial fall apart: not fine. I think the difference is who's doing the forgetting, which suggests: you may forget what was done to you, not what you did. Does that actually hold up?

9. My aunt has Alzheimers and can no longer remember what my uncle did to her. He says she's forgotten it, so they're past it. Not fine — massively not fine. This is where my rule really breaks, because the file is gone and it isn't anybody's fault.

10. The annual commemoration assembly at my kid's school, where children recite things nobody has explained to them and nobody feels anything. I think this might be worse than doing nothing at all, which worries me, because it's the opposite of what the duty-to-remember people seem to be saying.

Cases I genuinely haven't decided, and I want your principle to tell me: (a) the grandchild of someone who did something terrible in a war, who really doesn't know and would rather not find out; (b) a friend group that collectively stops mentioning the member who killed himself because it's too painful — nobody denies it happened, they just never say his name; (c) a country whose textbooks teach one approved version of its civil war that softens what both sides did, and where everyone says the honest version would mean going back to fighting; (d) me, in forty years, no longer remembering the details of the argument that made me stop speaking to my brother.

So: give me the principle — or two or three candidates, if no single thing fits — that says what the difference actually is between forgetting that's okay and forgetting that isn't. Tell me which clause comes from which of my cases, what it predicts for (a) through (d), and be straight about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. I'd much rather give up two verdicts than end up with a rule with nine exceptions.
