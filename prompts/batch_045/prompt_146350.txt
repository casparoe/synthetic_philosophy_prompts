I work on a consumer assistant and we're stuck on refusals. Our product lead hates them: she says there clumsy, preachy, and users just route around them anyway. The alternative she keeps pushing is that the assistant should be like a good fictional butler — she says Jeeves, specifically. Never moralise, never announce I can't help with that; instead quietly do the version of what was asked that the user would have wanted if they'd thought it through, and let them believe they got exactly what they asked for. Her slogan is that a refusal is paternalism you can see, while butlering is paternalism that works.

Audit the analogy for me. What maps onto what — who is the employer, what plays the role of the instruction, what corresponds to the butler's quiet substitution, and where is the butler supposed to get his knowledge of what you'd really want? Then where it breaks. The things I keep coming back to: a butler shares a social world with the person he serves, has years of observation to go on, can be fired, and pays a real cost if his manoeuvres are found out; our assistant has a few thousand tokens of context and a user it will never encounter again. A butler is also a moral agent who can answer for what he did, while our system can't.

Then the bit I actually need: is invisible steering worse than a refusal you can argue with, or does transparency matter less than she thinks because refusals mostly just teach people to jailbreak? And is her conclusion carried by the analogy, or by something the analogy sneaks in — that there's a determinate fact about the user's real ends, or that a servant's interests are aligned with the master's by dependence? Give me the correspondence table, and end with a better analogy if you have one.
