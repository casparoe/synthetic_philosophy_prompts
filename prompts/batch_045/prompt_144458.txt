I work as a disability benefits assessor for a national scheme. I see eleven or twelve claimants a week, forty minutes each, one appointment only; before the appointment I have the standard self-report form, which asks about a fixed list of activities such as walking fifty metres, preparing a meal, and washing. I write a report that goes to a decision-maker who never meets the claimant and who signs off every award, and a sample of my reports is read by a quality assurance auditor whose main test is whether the written evidence in the file supports the recommendation. A wrong denial can strip most of someone's income and push them into work that makes them worse; a wrong award costs the fund money and, in sufficient numbers, costs the scheme its political licence. Some claimants do misrepresent their condition. I cannot fix any of this in the room; I can only change how I weigh what I hear.

I want the literature on epistemic injustice turned into a procedure I can actually run in those forty minutes. The distinction I have in mind is the one Miranda Fricker draws between two failures: a hearer giving a speaker a deflated level of credibility because of prejudice tied to the speaker's identity, and a speaker being unable to make their own experience intelligible — to themselves as much as to the hearer — because the shared vocabulary has no place for it. I want the procedure to say at each step which of the two it is dealing with, because they call for different repairs, and I want it to be usable by someone who has never read the theory.

The procedure should give me, in order: the questions to ask myself in the room and then again while writing; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which it stops with a no; and the point at which it hands the decision to someone else because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. It has to survive four constraints: it must fit on one side of A4 so it can be laminated and kept on the desk; it must produce a report an auditor will accept; it must not pretend the assessment is a forty-minute psychotherapy or a re-examination; and it must be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing.

Five distinctions I want preserved if they can be, and want told honestly cannot be if they cannot.

One: the difference between recording a claim as not established and recording it as not credible. I suspect a large share of what my file currently calls low credibility is really unestablished, and that the two deserve different sentences in the report.

Two: the difference between using a claimant's identity prejudicially and using it as evidence. When a former warehouse picker tells me he cannot lift a crate, his occupational history is doing real epistemic work; when an accent or a diagnosis of a personality disorder moves my estimate, something else is. I want a test — the one I have been using informally is to ask whether the same datum offered by a claimant of higher status would have raised their credibility; if not, the datum is a proxy for status — but I want it stated properly and I want to know when it gives the wrong answer.

Three: proxy credibility, my label for credit routed through a higher-status intermediary so that a specialist's letter, or a support worker's rephrasing, or even my own tidier prose in the report, ends up carrying the epistemic weight that the claimant's own account should carry.

Four: scripted fluency. Some claimants have learned the form's vocabulary from a support group or a benefits adviser and produce beautifully fitted accounts; some have not and ramble. It is tempting to treat fluency as coaching and rambling as honesty. Both temptations are errors, and I want the procedure to block both rather than replace one with the other.

Five: credibility excess. I want the procedure to catch the cases where I believe someone too readily because they are like me, because I like them, or because we share a background — not only the cases where I believe them too little.

There are hard cases the procedure has to handle rather than bracket: the claimant who gives inconsistent numbers across three assessments; the claimant seen once through an interpreter and once without; the claimant who answers I don't know to nearly everything and whose adult child answers for them; the claimant who has clearly been coached; and the claimant who withholds information because previous disclosures were used against them.

Then apply the procedure to three files and show the work.

A. Mr H, fifty-four, former warehouse picker, chronic lower back pain, no imaging finding that matches the severity he reports. Recorded walking distance: two hundred metres, then fifty, then depends on the day. One of three assessments was conducted through a Punjabi interpreter, two were not. The auditor has flagged the inconsistency.

B. Ms K, twenty-nine, formerly a copywriter, reporting fatigue and post-exertional crashes. Highly articulate, uses the form's own phrasing precisely, brings a two-page letter from a hospital specialist, and turns out to have read the same subject at the same university as I did. I like her and I believe her; I expect this is the case where your procedure tells me something I will not want to hear.

C. Mr O, sixty-one, monosyllabic, I don't know to most questions, daughter answering for him, no diagnosis in the notes but some suggestion of cognitive impairment.

Finally, I want the parts the procedure cannot do. I already suspect three: it cannot repair a gap in the shared vocabulary, only decline to punish the claimant for it; and it cannot tell me how to set the threshold between wrongly denying and wrongly granting, which looks like a values question that should be owned by whoever sets policy rather than by me or by the theory. Tell me if there are others, and tell me where the procedure will misfire — in particular whether a quarterly log of my own credibility ratings against claimant demographics would actually detect bias in me or would simply teach me to equalise the numbers.
