I've got a shot at a small six-month independent-project grant: part-time, call it 10 hrs a week, deliverable is a ~6000-word working paper plus a one-page thing a practitioner could actually use.

The topic I want to work on is the old fight about whether it's fine for a scientific model to rest on assumptions everyone knows are false. The Friedman version from the 1950s: he says, roughly, that the assumptions of a really important hypothesis will be wildly inaccurate as descriptions of reality, and that this doesn't count against it — what counts is whether it predicts (I've seen people call this the F-twist). His defenders say the unrealism is a feature, not a bug; his critics say a model built on known falsehoods can't really explain anything, only save the phenomena. Every summary I read of the debate has it going around the same loop, and I want to know if there's a way in that isn't just picking a side.

My half-formed idea comes straight from my day job. I think both camps are treating "false assumption" as one kind of thing, and in software we learned to split it:

- Inert: the falsehood is like a stub or dead code. Nothing the model gets right depends on it.
- Compensated: the falsehood is load-bearing, but it's cancelled by another falsehood somewhere else — two bugs cancelling. Right answers, wrong reasons, and it collapses the moment you move the model to a new context.
- Constitutive: the false bit is doing the actual work. Take it out and you don't get a better model, you get no model.

The reason I care: in my job the difference between the first two is invisible as long as you stay inside the regime you tested in, and then it bites hard when you port the thing. I suspect the philosophers' fight is really three fights and nobody noticed.

My own tentative principle, which I half like and half don't: a false assumption is harmless iff you could disclose it to whoever's using the model — tell them plainly which bit is false and how — and they'd still be entitled to the inferences they're drawing. I worry it's circular (entitled by what standard?) and I'm not sure it handles the constitutive cases at all.

What I need in the proposal: the question stated tightly enough that it has a determinate answer; why the existing positions leave it open; what I'd actually do month by month; why this route rather than the obvious ones (a general theory of model-world similarity? just cataloguing idealizations? a formal measure of truthlikeness?); what each possible conclusion would look like and what would decide between them; what would count as the project having failed; and an honest scope.
