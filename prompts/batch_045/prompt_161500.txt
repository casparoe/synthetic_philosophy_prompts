I build control software for warehouse robots — the tote-shuffling kind, multi-agent, mostly model-predictive control with a hand-tuned cost on top plus a few learned bits — and a friend who's doing a PhD in philosophy got me into an argument about free will. She sent me this old parable about a digger wasp (I think the word for it is "sphexishness"; I have a vague memory it comes from some pop-science book from decades ago, and I've also heard people say the original wasp observation was exaggerated or never properly replicated — so please don't rest anything on the anecdote, it's the concept I care about). Roughly: the wasp runs its nest-checking routine, you move the prey a few inches while it's inside, and it runs the whole routine again instead of just grabbing the prey. The move is then: maybe human behaviour is like that, just with a vastly bigger state space, and our sense of deliberating is a readout on a machine we have no introspective access to. The usual reply is that we aren't rigid, we're flexible, we respond to reasons — and the argument then just turns into a fight about how flexible is flexible enough.

What I'd like you to do is translate that whole cluster into the vocabulary I actually use: open-loop vs closed-loop, feedforward vs feedback, precompiled motion primitives, hierarchical and multi-rate control, fixed cost functions vs learned policies, preemption and interrupt handlers, replanning vs executing, metacontrollers and anytime algorithms, safety and liveness specs, graceful degradation, mode confusion, and the difference between a system that adapts and a system that was designed to adapt. Do the skeptical claim first (human behaviour as fixed action pattern, agency epiphenomenal), then the standard flexibility reply, as faithfully as you can — and don't cheat by just defining "free" as "closed-loop" and declaring the problem solved. I want to know what the translation loses.

Three wrinkles I want run through the same translation, because I suspect this is where the real disagreement lives and I don't see them in either the pop version or my friend's version:

1. Layer-relative flexibility. A policy can be enormously expressive and adaptive while the objective it optimizes and the rule that updates it are hardwired and unreachable — all the adaptation happens one level below the level that would matter. In my world that's completely normal and nobody treats it as a defect. Does the free-will worry care about the top of the stack? What would "the stack can rewrite its own cost function" even cash out as, and is that supposed to be the thing the wasp is missing?
2. Interruptibility vs revisability. For me these are seperate mechanisms: I can bolt a watchdog onto a rigid sequence so it aborts when the world changes, and I can also build a system that re-derives its whole plan from scratch but never stops mid-execution. The wasp fails the first; a lot of what I build fails the second. Which one is the free-will debate actually asking about — or is it asking about something that's neither?
3. Grain. "Rigid" is only rigid relative to a timescale and a description language: the wasp's routine is fixed over seconds, the species' routine is shaped over evolutionary time, the individual's over development. At what grain are we supposed to evaluate a human, and does the answer change if you evaluate at 200 milliseconds versus ten years?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want: which of these claims survive translation cleanly, which only survive if you fudge them, and which have no expression in control vocabulary at all. My guesses for the last category are the counterfactual "could have done otherwise with everything else held fixed," reasons as causes with normative content, and the idea of the agent being the source or author of the action rather than just the box the controller sits in — but tell me if I'm wrong. And for each bit of residue, say whether it looks like a hole in my vocabulary, something engineering has no business representing, or a sign that the philosophical notion was doing less work than it sounded like it was doing. I can read a control diagram fine, but my philosophy is basically zero, so keep that side in plain words.
