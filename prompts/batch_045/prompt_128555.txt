A podcast episode on philosophy of language that I listened to recently summarized Putnam's Twin Earth argument roughly as follows, and I would like to know how much of it to trust:

Imagine a planet exactly like ours where the clear, drinkable liquid in the lakes and taps is not H2O but some other compound, XYZ, indistinguishable from water by any test available in 1750. Oscar and his molecule-for-molecule duplicate Twin-Oscar are in the same psychological state, yet Oscar's word refers to H2O and his twin's refers to XYZ; so a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of you can mean something different and believe something different, and Twin-Oscar's remark that the water is cold is simply false. The point of the argument is to break the assumption that knowing what a word means is just a matter of being in a certain psychological state: meaning is not in the head, it is fixed by the stuff in your physical surroundings. The same holds for thought, since Burge's arthritis case is the very same argument applied to belief, and shows that the content of what you think is fixed by how the world outside your skull is. What this really comes to is that every natural-kind term has a hidden microstructural essence that science eventually uncovers, which is why ordinary speakers have to defer to experts. The upshot is unsettling: because content is fixed by the environment rather than by anything you can survey from the inside, you can be systematically mistaken about the contents of your own mind, and introspection cannot be trusted to tell you what you think.

I am reluctant to dismiss the episode, since it is a professional production and most of it matches what I half-remember, but two things bother me. I had thought semantic externalism was meant to leave self-knowledge intact — a thesis about what determines content rather than about our access to it — and the claim that Twin-Oscar's sentence comes out false strikes me as running backwards. I also tried applying the episode's rationale to a variant of my own: imagine a Twin Earth with no naturally occurring water at all, where the taps run H2O that was synthesized in a laboratory and has a completely different causal history from ours. Taken literally, the way the episode states the argument seems to predict that 'water' would mean something different there too, and I cannot see which step generates that prediction.

Please audit the summary for me: say which claims a defender of externalism would accept as stated, which are subtle misrepresentations and what the corrected version of each would be, and whether the unsettling conclusion survives once the misrepresentations are fixed. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no concluding paragraph.
