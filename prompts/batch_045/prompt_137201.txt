I am writing a novel, set on a working saltmarsh farm, in which two estranged sisters must agree on a single joint valuation of the low field before the autumn tides, or the land passes to a trust under their mother's will. Their mother, before she died, ran an informal sweepstake on whether the spring tide would top the sea wall, kept the whole thing in a ledger, and every year made her daughters each post a price for the same event and then keep trading prices until the two numbers matched. One sister stayed and farmed the marsh and reads the water; the other left and prices flood risk for a reinsurer and reads tables. The scene I am stuck on requires a poem I have been unable to write.

I would like a sestina, discovered in the back pages of the mother's ledger and read aloud by the two daughters in alternation — odd stanzas the sister of the marsh, even stanzas the sister of the tables — with the three-line envoi spoken by both together. Use these six end-words: PRIOR, GIVEN, SALT, LEDGER, HALF, ODDS. I want the standard thirty-nine-line form respected properly, unrhymed, with a correct rotation through the six stanzas and the envoi carrying the six words again, two to a line.

The philosophical content I want the form to carry is the common prior assumption and the agreement result that goes with it. Two people who, before either had learned anything, would have set the same price on an event cannot, once each knows the other's price and knows that the other knows hers and so on, rest permanently at different prices — every scrap of their disagreement has to be traceable to what each has since taken in, and the trading itself drives them together. The sestina should make this felt structurally: the two women are stuck with the same six words, and what differs is only the order in which the words arrive to each of them, so that the poem's machinery enacts a single shared starting point refracted through two different histories of evidence.

Three constraints on accuracy, since the poem should survive a philosopher's reading. First, the convergence must come from the exchange itself and not from any new information about the sea; nobody looks out of a window and learns something. Second, the poem should register, somewhere and without pedantry, that the whole mechanism is hostage to one thing — that there was a time before either of them looked, and a price they would then have shared — so that a reader can see why the argument would collapse if each had begun from her own prior. Third, the closing should not pretend that landing on the same number settles what to do with the land: agreeing how likely the flood is leaves each sister's reasons for wanting to stay or to sell exactly as they were, and I want the last line to hold one number and two women still facing different ways.

The voices should be distinguishable without speech tags — different idioms, different kinds of knowledge — and the mother who supposedly wrote this was a bookmaker rather than a philosopher, so the thought should move in prices, debts, and odds rather than in technical vocabulary. Do not name the theorem or its author anywhere in the poem. It should also work as a poem for a reader who has never encountered the idea. If the rotation fights the content at some point, tell me in a line or two where and adjust the scheme as little as you can rather than abandoning it.
