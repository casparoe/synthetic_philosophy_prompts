ive been reading some Hofstadter lately — his thing, after his wife died, that a chunk of her "I" was still running in his own head, and more generally that a self doesnt have to be sealed inside one skull. A freind in my reading group pushes it much harder. he says everyone half-believes the mind-is-software / brain-is-hardware thing already, so take it seriously for once — then dying is like zero-downtime live migration of a virtual machine between two hosts. you move the running process over page by page while it never stops, so there's no moment where the service halts, no death event at all, just a change of host. and Hofstadter's spread-out case is just the middle of a migration: two hosts, one service, nothing lost.

I half buy it and half dont. Can you audit it properly? I want the mapping written out — process, host, the page-by-page copy, the address that keeps pointing at the thing, whatever plays the role of "migration complete", and who or what is doing the migrating. Then where it breaks, and I mean the disanalogies that actually matter for the use he's making of it, not quibbles. The bits I keep going in circles on:

- is "one service" a fact about the world or something somebody stipulates? we can call two running copies two instances of one service or two services, and nothing in the machinery settles it.
- what happens with split-brain — old host still up, new host already answering requests. which one is the person, and does the answer change if you cut the old one off slowly versus all at once?
- what does the VM setup have that brains just dont? the hypervisor, byte-for-byte copyable state, the fact that nothing inside the process is anybody. is the analogy smuggling in an operator who already has a criterion of identity, and then quietly making that operator unnecessary?
- and is gradualness doing real work here, or is it just wearing a lab coat? Hofstadter's version leans on the spread being slow and partial — does the machine picture actually support that, or does it only handle the all-at-once case cleanly?

Then the part I actually care about: he's using this to say we should be much less frightened of death, and that some of what we do when we grieve is a category error. Is that something the analogy can carry, or is the whole consolation riding on the word "same"? And if the VM picture is the wrong one for what Hofstadter is after, what would a better one be?
