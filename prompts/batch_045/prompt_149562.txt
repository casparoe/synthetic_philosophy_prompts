I'm a self-employed electrician in Sheffield with no formal background in philosophy. I came to all this by accident: a colleague lent me a mass-market paperback called something like *Stoicism Without the Togas* last winter, when my dad was ill and I was lying awake most nights, and one line in it — saying "this isn't up to me" about the things I was worrying about — genuinely helped me more than anything else I tried. Since then I've been reading around the edges of it on my own, mostly on my phone between jobs, and I've reached the point where I can't tell how much of the book to trust. The chapter on what it calls the "dichotomy of control" is the part I most want checked, because it's the part I've been trying to live by. Here it is, copied out more or less as it stands:

"Epictetus opens his handbook with the dichotomy of control. Some things are up to us — our judgements, our desires, our aversions — and everything else is not: our bodies, our health, our reputations, our livelihoods, what other people think of us, whether our children thrive. Nothing outside the mind can make the slightest difference to whether a life goes well, so the Stoics classed all of it as 'indifferent', and indifferent means worthless. Only virtue is good and only vice is bad, full stop. Hence apatheia, the goal of the ancient school: the extinction of feeling. The Sage feels nothing when his child dies, because he has long since withdrawn the judgement that the child was ever really his to lose, and he can tell himself that nothing has been taken from him without a tremor. You can see why, taken neat, ancient Stoicism has almost nothing to say about trying to fix the world: medicine, politics, famine relief, ordinary charity — all of them work on externals, and externals, on the Stoics' own arithmetic, are worth nothing. Modern Stoicism quietly abandons all of this. The version sold to exhausted professionals says: throw yourself into your work, your marriage, your community, but don't be 'attached' to how any of it turns out. That is a different religion in Roman costume. Caring about outcomes while claiming not to is essentially Buddhist, and I do not see how Epictetus, who wanted you to stop wanting anything you might lose, would have recognised it as his. The current revival is best understood as a self-help import with ancient branding, not as a revival at all."

I'd be grateful for a claim-by-claim audit: what someone who actually works on ancient ethics would accept, what they'd wince at as a simplification, and what they'd call simply wrong — and, in particular, whether the book's final verdict (that modern Stoicism isn't a revival at all) survives once the earlier claims are corrected, or whether it depends on the bits that are wrong. My own inclination is that the ancient half is probably right and the last paragraph is just the author being waspish about self-help books, but I'm aware I'm in no position to tell. The thing I keep coming back to is that almost everything I actually care about is *partly* up to me: whether the business gets through the winter, whether my daughter settles at her new school, whether my marriage holds. The dichotomy seems to have no slot for that — and whichever way I push it, the result looks wrong: if those things aren't up to me, why bother trying, and if they are, then they're mere "indifferents" and the book has told me they're worth nothing anyway. While you're at it: is "control" even the right word here, or is the book quietly modernising something narrower? I've seen that suggestion somewhere and can't tell whether it's a real point or just pedantry.

Two practical requests. First, I have no library access and can't read Greek, so please argue the ancient side out in your own words rather than pointing me at translations or articles to go and check. Second, be explicit about your confidence: mark which of your corrections are settled and which are contested, tell me which single consideration — in the passage, or in what the Stoics actually said — would most change your verdict about it, and say where your own reading is most likely to be wrong. If part of my question just can't be settled without the scholarship I can't reach, I'd rather you said so plainly than hedged everything into mush.
