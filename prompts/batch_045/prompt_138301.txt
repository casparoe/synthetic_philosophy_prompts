I build distributed systems for a living and have never taken a philosophy course, so please don't stall on my vocabulary — fix the reasoning, not the words.

I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about whether the size or complexity of a brain has anything to do with how much a being morally matters. Here are my gut verdicts on a set of cases:

1. A fire at a facility; I can carry out either an adult human or an adult chimpanzee, not both. I choose the human, and I think that's right — but I also think it would be wrong to explain the choice by pointing at skull volume. I want some other reason to do the work.
2. A human born with a brain a third of the usual size, whose measured capacities (learning, planning, social responsiveness) are roughly a chimpanzee's. I give this person exactly the same moral status as any other human — no discount at all.
3. An adult has a large tumor resected and loses a substantial fraction of their cortex. Scans confirm the loss; nothing changes in what they can do, remember, or report about their experience. Verdict: their moral status does not move by even a little.
4. Hypothetical near-future: a graft of lab-grown cortical tissue roughly doubles someone's cortical neuron count, again with no change in any capacity or in anything they report. Verdict: no increase in moral status either.
5. A clump of the same kind of cortical tissue, grown alone in a dish, no body, no behavior, no signs of experience. Verdict: it counts for something — I wouldn't dispose of it on a whim — but far less than a mouse, and I can't say why this tissue counts at all when the tissue in case 4 counted for nothing.
6. An elephant, whose brain is several times the mass of mine. Verdict: not several times my moral status; mass is obviously the wrong unit.

My tentative principle: brain size matters only as evidence about the richness of a being's conscious life, and only up to the threshold at which there is a conscious life at all — above that threshold it goes flat and stops counting for anything. I don't think that principle survives cases 4 and 5 together, but I don't know which verdict to drop.

So: state the principle you think captures these verdicts, precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one you'd overturn. Then commit to the two I can't settle: the dish-grown tissue, and a large AI system with far more parameters and total compute than my brain has neurons, behaving intelligently but with nothing biological going on — does size talk apply there at all? Give me a definite answer with reasons, not a menu of options. Finally, tell me what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: an adult whose brain slowly atrophies over a decade, losing capacities gradually and continuously.
