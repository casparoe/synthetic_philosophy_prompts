Write a parody of a government form together with its line-by-line instructions: "Form UC-1, Application for Anticipatory Self-Binding Order," issued by an invented Office of Anticipatory Autonomy. Roughly 800 words, in deadpan bureaucratic prose — the register of tax-form instructions or an insurance policy, complete with cross-references ("see the Continuity Worksheet, line 14"), defined terms, and a fee schedule. The form exists so that a person can file, now, an order that will later be enforced against them even while they are asking, loudly, for it to be lifted. Part I covers individual applicants; Part II covers collective and intergenerational applicants — states, parties, constituent assemblies — seeking to entrench a rule against future majorities.

The target is the discourse of Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment: the habit of treating self-binding as a costless, self-executing remedy for weakness of will and for majority tyranny. The satire should land by having the fine print quietly take positions on the following, without any character ever stating them as objections.

- Continuity. The form must treat the applicant as the same person for purposes of giving consent and as a different person for purposes of being restrained, and it should say so in a Definitions section ("Filing Party," "Bound Party") as though this were ordinary drafting. Add a Continuity Worksheet requiring a percentage of psychological overlap with the future self whose request to be released is being discounted; the thresholds should generate absurd dispatch — a client whose projected overlap falls below the cutoff is informed that his order will be processed as a bequest, or as a restraining order against a third party, rather than as an act of self-binding.
- The enforcer. Ulysses needed a crew. Require the applicant to name an enforcer who is not himself bound and who is instructed to disregard the bound party's later pleas, and let the form handle — without registering that there is a difficulty — what entitles the earlier instruction to override the later one, and who checks whether the filer was himself impaired at the moment of filing. Give it both a cooling-off rule that voids filings made in the aftermath of the regretted act and a symmetric rule that voids filings made while the applicant was feeling unusually resolute.
- What success consists in. The Office should guarantee compliance and never agreement: a properly processed order leaves the bound party wanting exactly what he is prevented from doing, struggling and protesting. State this as the product working as designed, and decline refunds for continued desire.
- Recursion. Include second-order binding — binding oneself against future attempts to unbind — and let the Office's precedence rule for conflicting instructions from the same filer be applied literally, so that the form cheerfully binds the applicant out of ever filing Form UC-1 again.
- Part II, the political half. An entrenchment schedule under which a generation may bind its successors for longer than it may bind itself; a certification that the bound parties would have consented had they been asked, and were not asked because they did not yet exist; a supermajority exit whose own amendment procedure is entrenched by it, with a note that the Office presumes entrenchment clauses protect themselves, on the ground that a rule which cannot protect itself is not a rule. Let the ratchet asymmetry — filing easy, release harder than filing — show up in the fee schedule, with fees calibrated to the applicant's later willingness to pay, which prices self-binding out of reach of the poor and charges most to whoever needs it most.

Constraints: keep the internal logic literal and consistent in application even where the rules embarrass each other, don't let the prose wink, and never name an objection to self-binding. Cite no authors, papers, cases, or statutes; the only authorities invoked should be the form's own sections and its fictional administrator. Someone who works on precommitment and constitutional entrenchment should concede the satire is fair — that it exaggerates real features of the practice rather than inventing them — while a reader who has never opened the literature should still understand what the form does and why it is funny. Close with a short Privacy Act-style notice whose boilerplate quietly reveals what the Office takes a person to be.
