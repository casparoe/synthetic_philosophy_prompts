You are a philosopher of history and a historian of science with long experience teaching first-year undergraduates how explanations are built. Please think carefully and work in two stages. Begin with a short brainstorming phase in which you list the candidate cases you considered, the obvious-but-wrong opening moves, and your reasons for rejecting each; only then write the teaching plan itself. Do not explain the concept to me, and do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations. I want a plan for an explanation that I will deliver, and I want you to name and reject alternatives rather than present one polished option as if it were the only one.

The setting: I am a graduate instructor and I have a fifty-minute slot next week in the first-year *How History Works* seminar. The students have read some narrative history, no philosophy of history, and no science beyond school level; a whiteboard is available. They are at ease with board-level sentences such as: the Black Death broke the back of the manorial system; the Little Ice Age pushed settlers off the land. But they have no vocabulary for causation, condition, or agency. My aim is narrower than teaching them to think critically in general. By the end I want each of them to be able to hold three claims apart: that a non-human thing — a pathogen, a climatic shift, a volcano — was causally relevant to some outcome; that it was the determining or sole cause; and that it can properly be written into a narrative as a character without either of the first two collapsing into the third. I also want them to be able to hear the word actor applied to a germ without concluding either that the word is empty metaphor or that the germ had plans.

Provisional device I want you to pressure-test: I have been using a two-sentence rule when revising my own lectures. Whenever I let a non-human entity appear as a character in an explanation, I require myself to produce a mechanism sentence, stating how the entity made its causal difference — the pathway, not merely the correlation — and an uptake sentence, stating what the human beings in the story did, failed to do, or could have done differently such that the entity's difference became the outcome being explained. If the mechanism sentence cannot be written, I am writing poetry rather than history; if the uptake sentence is empty, or reduces to the claim that nothing could have been done, I have slid into determinism. Tell me whether this rule does the work I want at this level, whether it needs sharpening or a third sentence, and exactly where in the fifty minutes it should be introduced and applied. I suspect it has to arrive before I turn them loose on any case, but I want your reasoning about the dependency rather than agreement.

Now the plan.

1. Order and dependency. Give me the phases of the session in the order they must occur, with rough minutes, and for each phase state what later phase depends on it. I care about the dependency structure and not merely a plausible sequence: tell me which move, if placed earlier, would make a later move unintelligible or would invite a misconception that I then have to undo. Place in particular the separation of causally relevant from determining, the defusing of the agency-requires-intentions reflex, the moment actor-talk is first licensed, and the two-sentence rule.

2. The carrying case. I am inclined to build the whole session around the potato blight of the 1840s in Ireland, using the contrast with the same blight in places where it did not produce mass mortality. Tell me whether that is the right carrier, and specify what a carrier for this idea must have and must lack. My own guess is that it must contain a non-human entity whose causal role is localized and legible, a human institutional component that students can recognize as uptake, and the same entity present in another setting with a different outcome; and that it must lack any need for specialist biological or climatological detail, and must not be so catastrophic in scale that it hypnotizes students into treating the non-human thing as the whole story. If the Irish case fails any of these, say so and propose the better carrier. Then tell me why each of the tempting alternatives is a trap at this level: the Black Death, the 1815 eruption of Tambora and the following year's summer, and the 1918 influenza pandemic. I want the reversal in each case — what the alternative makes easy and what it quietly makes impossible.

3. The misconception map, in the order the students will actually form them, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. The ones I expect are: that only beings with intentions can be agents, so that calling a germ an actor must be metaphor or must be nonsense; that once a germ or a volcano is cited as a cause, human decisions become decoration; that saying the environment mattered just is saying it determined everything, so that students conclude either that any environmental causation is determinism or that none of it is; and — I expect this one only after the cross-case contrast — that since the same blight did not produce famine everywhere, the blight did not really matter in Ireland, which is a slide from not sufficient to not necessary. Add any further misconception you think is predictable in this audience, and for each tell me the sentence or board move that defuses it and the phase in which that move belongs.

4. The check. Give me one question to ask in the final five minutes that would show the idea has landed rather than that the students have merely learned my vocabulary. I would rather it be a transfer task than a request for a definition. I am happy to put a fresh, minimally described case on the board — either the failure of the French canal effort in Panama in the 1880s, in which mosquito-borne disease was central, or the Tambora case held back from the main exposition — and ask them to produce the two sentences, or to diagnose a one-line board explanation as determinist and repair it. Tell me what a correct answer would look like and, just as importantly, what a superficially correct answer would look like and why it would not count.

5. Refusals. State explicitly what I should decline to explain at this level and say aloud that I am declining. I take it that this includes any general metaphysics of agency, any formal account of causation, the internal biology or climatology of the entities, and any named theoretical framework about non-human agency. Say what I should say when a student presses on one of these.

Write the brainstorming section first and keep it visibly distinct from the plan that follows.
