I have recently been co-opted onto the Medals Committee of a learned society, and I find the exercise more interesting than I expected, so I should like your help with a case that comes before us next month. Our remit, as the statutes have it, is to reward "distinguished conduct beyond the call of ordinary obligation". What turns on our decision is small: a medal is struck, and a name is read out at the anniversary dinner. That is all.

The trouble is that our precedents will not settle the new case, and I want to know what principle we should take ourselves to be applying before we walk into the room. Here is the run of decisions I have been able to reconstruct from the minutes, with the grounds recorded in them:

1. A man who found a wallet containing £380 in cash, went somewhat out of his way, and returned it intact. No medal: "ordinary decency".
2. An off-duty consultant physician who gave emergency CPR to a stranger collapsing in a railway station, successfully. No medal: "any competent bystander with her training would have done the same, and the cost to her was negligible".
3. A woman who made an anonymous, non-directed kidney donation to a stranger. Medal awarded.
4. A man who gave roughly a third of his income to famine relief for fifteen years. Medal awarded on a split vote; the dissent minuted that "if that is genuinely what beneficence requires, we do not strike medals for doing one's duty".
5. An off-duty firefighter who entered a burning house and carried out a neighbour's child. No medal: "his training and equipment made this what his occupation prepares him for". Dissent: he was off duty and under no obligation to intervene.
6. A finance officer who leaked evidence of systematic fraud to the regulator, was dismissed, and never worked in the sector again; the fraud was in any case uncovered by a routine audit nine months later. Medal awarded. One member is recorded as having argued explicitly that the point of the award was to encourage others in that position, and not merely to mark what he deserved.
7. A young woman who, after her parents died, cared full-time for four years for her severely disabled younger brother, giving up a university place. She said she did it because there was nobody else and she could not have lived with herself otherwise. No medal: "she describes herself as having had no alternative".
8. A soldier who threw himself on a live grenade to shield others and was killed. Posthumous medal, unanimous, though one member observed that the services have their own gallantry awards.

The new case: because of a gap in the regulatory framework, Dr Ruth Adeyemi was, at the material time, the only person in the country both licensed and competent to certify the cold-chain protocol for a vaccine. Without certification the vaccine could not be distributed, and the Ministry's own estimate was that the resulting delay would cost some two thousand children's lives over the following two years. A specialist from abroad could in principle have been brought in, but not for fourteen months. She did the work unpaid over nine months — some seven hundred hours — and in order to do it she had to relinquish a research fellowship abroad that she had already been awarded. Her own account is that someone had to do it and that she could not have lived with herself otherwise. She has since declined all publicity and asked not to be named in any announcement.

I am less interested in being told what we should morally conclude about Dr Adeyemi than in the structure of the problem. Please do the following: set out the two or three rules that fit the whole run of our precedents about equally well, and say what each of them predicts here; give me your best guess as to what the Committee will in fact do, with the reasons for preferring that guess; identify which of the earlier cases is the closest analogue and how much weight it carries; tell me what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and state plainly which principle we should be committing ourselves to under each of the two or three available answers; and then name the one further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between the surviving rules — that last item is the part I care about most, since I mean to put it to the Committee as a test.

Two features of the case seem to me to be doing the work, and I should like you to deal with them explicitly. First, whether her being the only person able to do it converts what would otherwise have been beyond duty into a duty, so that there is nothing left to honour. Secondly, whether the "could not have lived with myself" line disqualifies her by parity with the carer in case 7, or whether it is simply what modest people say and should be ignored. I should also like you to consider whether our practice is best understood as tracking desert at all, rather than as an incentive mechanism or as the management of a scarce currency whose value depends on our saying no most of the time; those readings come apart here, and I suspect they come apart in ways we have so far been able to ignore.

One request: I have read the standard literature on supererogation — from Urmson's "Saints and Heroes" onwards, through the shallow-pond argument, to the work on moral options and agent-centred prerogatives — so please do not give me a reading list, a literature survey or any citations. I want the argument in my own terms, and I will supply the references myself if I ever write this up.
