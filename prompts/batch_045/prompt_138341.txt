I got nerd-sniped the other day by this puzzle someone posted in a work Slack (I think it's Kavka's, but I can't check and it doesn't matter): an eccentric billionaire hands you a vial of stuff that will make you miserably, painfully sick for about a day with zero lasting effects. He wires you a huge sum of money at midnight tonight on one condition only — that at the stroke of midnight you genuinely intend to drink the vial tomorrow afternoon. You never have to actually drink it. The money is already in your account by the time the drinking would happen, so at drinking time you have no reason whatsoever to drink and every reason not to. Question: can you do it? Can you form that intention tonight, knowing that tomorrow you'll have no reason to act on it and will in fact not act on it?

I've landed on what feels like a deflationary answer and I want it stress-tested. Here's my version, in my own vocabulary because I don't have the philosophy one. I build distributed systems, and to me an intention looks like a durable commitment record: you write down "we are doing X" in a place that survives process restarts and lost network connections, and the entire point of that write is that it keeps binding even after the reasons that prompted it stop being live, or after the local cost of executing goes up. Two-phase commit is the clean example — the prepare-phase vote is supposed to hold even when, at commit time, the node would really rather not. So on my reading, the toxin case isn't a paradox about intending at will at all. It just strips away every other feature of a plan until only the bindingness is left, and then we stare at the naked bindingness and find it weird. The deep-sounding question "can you intend to do something you'll have no reason to do" is, on this reading, the same question as "can you set an alarm for 5am when you know 5am-you will hate the plan" or "can you decide tonight to send the Difficult Email tomorrow". Yes, obviously, all day long, that's what plans are for. The supposed solution route where you trick yourself into believing you really will drink is just one hacky implementation of forming the intention, not the interesting part.

The thing that would actually make me drop this is if the deflation is hiding a real distinction, so I made up some variants to probe it and I want to know which of them are real diagnostics and which are noise:

1. The dial variant: instead of a day of agony, make it ten seconds of mild nausea, and crank it up gradually to a day of agony. If there's a point where people stop being able to form the intention, then the "can't" claim is a threshold claim about predicted future aversion blocking intention-formation, not a categorical claim about reasons — and that's a different animal than what I'm deflating.
2. The mirror variant: same deal, but the billionaire pays you if at midnight you intend to *decline* something tomorrow afternoon that will be free, harmless and delightful — a perfect ice cream, say. If intending-to-refrain is symmetric with intending-to-act, fine for me; if it isn't, my story needs a reason why and I don't have one.
3. The nightly variant: he runs the offer every night for a year and pays per night, still never requiring you to drink. Night two, you now know for a fact you didn't drink on night one. If you can still form the intention, then forming an intention clearly doesn't require believing you'll follow through, which kills the version of the "can't" answer that says intending is partly a self-prediction. If you can't on night two but could on night one, that's evidence the self-prediction thing is load-bearing and my commitment-record model has no slot for it.
4. The amnesia variant: at noon tomorrow you get a drug that wipes your memory of tonight entirely, so tomorrow-afternoon-you is a continuation of you with no access to the record. Can you still intend tonight? My model says the record is in *your* state, not the future self's, so yes — but I'm not sure that's a feature or a bug.

So: what would count against my reading? I want it sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I specifically want the stuff a *defender* of my reading would concede as counting against it, not only what someone who thinks the puzzle is deep would say. Rank it. And please tell me honestly whether I've insulated myself: I've noticed that on my view, any report of someone failing to form the intention can be explained away as ordinary weakness of will or as not-trying-hard-enough rather than as impossibility, and any report of success is a win. If that means my position has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability, say so, and then tell me whether that's actually a problem for a claim of this kind or whether it's just what a philosophical claim about what we can do looks like.

A few more things I'd find useful, in rough order of how much they'd move me:

- Someone in the thread said this is structurally the same as Newcomb's problem, with forming the intention as one-boxing and the "of course you can't, and you know you won't drink" answer as two-boxing. I don't know enough to judge whether that's deep or a coincidence of shape. Is the analogy actually load-bearing for my deflation, or can I drop it?
- Which of my four variants genuinely split my reading from the readings that treat the puzzle as deep, and which do both sides absorb for free? I'd rather have two sharp cases than four fuzzy ones.
- The thing I'd personally find most persuasive is a case where my reading and the "can't" reading predict different answers to something I can settle by sitting quietly for thirty seconds and introspecting. I trust that much more than armchair claims about what is possible "at will", because introspection is at least data I can gather myself. Give me that case if it exists, and if it doesn't exist, tell me why not.
- And a mechanical question for my own model of the thing: does intending have to include a belief, or even a weak expectation, that you'll actually do it? If it does, my commit-log picture is missing a field and I'd like to know what breaks when you add it. If it doesn't, say what intending is instead, in terms a person who writes state machines would accept.
