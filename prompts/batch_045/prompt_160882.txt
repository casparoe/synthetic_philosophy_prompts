I'm assembling the final problem set for my graduate seminar on welfare economics and population ethics — a packet of "applied" exercises rather than straight arguments — and I'd like one item in it to be a piece of imitative writing. Over the past few weeks we have worked through the standard machinery: Pareto rankings and why they almost never certify anything, the slide into potential (Kaldor–Hicks) improvements where compensation is only ever hypothetical, expected-utility comparisons under model uncertainty, and the non-identity problem, where the very people a policy harms are the people it brings into existence, so that "they are worse off" apparently fails to be an objection. What I want students to feel is how much extra work the word "safe" quietly does: a policy gets certified as a Safe Pareto Improvement when the improvement holds only in expectation, only under the modeler's own probability function, only for a population defined to include the people the policy creates, and only on a metric nobody outside the ministry would recognize as tracking how well a life goes. Every one of those moves has a serious defense somewhere in the literature; the satire is that they stack.

So: could you write a parody official form — an "Application for Certification of a Safe Pareto Improvement" — issued by a fictional Office of Distributional Review, pitched somewhere between a tax return, an environmental impact statement, and a software end-user licence agreement? Around 800–1000 words, laid out as numbered sections with sub-clauses, checkboxes to be ticked, and blank fields to be filled in. It should include at least: (1) a Definitions section in which "Affected Party" is defined so as to exclude precisely those people whose objection would defeat the application — be inventive, and make the exclusions the sort a real ministry would draft with a straight face; (2) a Compensation section offering the options "Actual," "Hypothetical," "Retroactive," and "Posthumous," each with a short note on when it is deemed to apply; (3) a Discount Rate and Probability Field section, where the applicant specifies the model under which the expected improvement is computed and attests that the model was specified after the results were known; (4) a Non-Identity clause dealing with persons who would not exist but for the policy, and with how their consent is to be registered; (5) a Safety attestation covering variance, tail risk, and the maximum acceptable probability that literally everyone is in fact made worse off; and (6) a short worked example in the footer in which some recognizably bad policy — a compulsory relocation, a fertility incentive, a large infrastructure project, your choice — sails through on a technicality.

Keep it deadpan throughout: no editorializing, no winking, no voice stepping outside the form to tell the reader that something is corrupt, and no named individuals, living or dead. The comedy should come entirely from the fact that each clause is a recognizable exaggeration of a move that actual welfare economists and policy analysts make — compensating in principle, discounting the future, defining the affected population to suit the conclusion, treating deep uncertainty as if it were risk. Someone who knows the literature should laugh because the form is technically competent about the machinery and quietly monstrous about its use; someone who doesn't should still be able to say exactly what is being satirized. Any invented terminology should read as though it belongs in a government register.
