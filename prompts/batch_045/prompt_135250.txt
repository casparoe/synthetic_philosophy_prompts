I have no academic background in philosophy or law. I left school at eighteen and have supported myself for fifteen years as a freelance surface-pattern designer — prints for fabric and gift wrap, the occasional small brand identity — so I have skin in this game, but everything I believe about intellectual property I worked out on my own, mostly in arguments online. I've finally written down where I've landed, and I'd like you to audit it rather than explain the topic to me.

Here is the set:

(1) There is no natural property right in an idea. Intellectual property is a bargain: society hands out an artificial, temporary monopoly because doing so gets more work made and published than would otherwise exist. Nobody deserves to own something merely for having thought of it first, and the fact that I sweated over a design gives me no claim in itself.

(2) So the term should be short and the scope narrow — ten or fifteen years from publication — and everything on the idea side of the line stays free for anyone to use commercially: facts, techniques, colourways, genres, functional features, and styles.

(3) Drug patents are justified even though they are twenty-year monopolies that price people out of medicine. My father has MS; the drug that keeps him walking costs more per month than my first car did, and I still think the arrangement that produced it is worth it.

(4) Software patents should not exist at all. Software is already covered by copyright, software patents mostly fund litigation, and they stop people from building on each other's work.

(5) Training image generators on the portfolios of working illustrators, without consent and without payment, was a wrong done to those illustrators, and they are owed something for it — even granting that the model does not store their files or reproduce any particular picture.

(6) A separate and worse wrong is one person fine-tuning a model on, say, eight hundred pieces by a single living illustrator and then selling new pictures in her style, undercutting her commissions. This happened to a friend of mine and I thought it was obviously wrong. I also found myself rooting for the plaintiffs in that lawsuit a few years ago about a hit song that had borrowed the feel of an older one, which now worries me given (2).

(7) Fan art, pastiche, homages and remixes of characters and worlds you do not own are a good thing, should not require permission, and may be sold, provided you are not simply reselling someone's actual work. Part of my own living comes from reworking public-domain wallpaper scans from the 1930s, and I do not want a world in which that kind of borrowing has to be licensed.

(8) An acquaintance lifted a paid pattern file of mine for a small community theatre poster without asking. I still think that was wrong after she pointed out that she would never have bought a licence and that I lost no sale.

Two people have told me opposite things: one says (5) and (6) quietly betray (1) and (2), while the other says (3) and (4) are flatly inconsistent. I cannot tell who is right, and I would rather revise as little as possible.

So please do the following. Rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally serious. For each, say exactly what would have to give, and what giving it would cost me — if I drop or weaken (6), for instance, do I have to accept that anyone may flood the market with work in my style? Separate out the pairs that only look inconsistent, and say why they are not. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what I would end up unable to say afterwards. Please do not settle for telling me that intellectual property is a balance between competing interests; show me where my own set does the same work twice, where one commitment quietly cancels another, or where a case verdict my principles cannot deliver has smuggled itself in.
