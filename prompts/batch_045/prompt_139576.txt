I am drafting an essay on dialetheism and would like comments on it. I have written it as notes rather than prose, since the argumentative joints are what I want feedback on.

The target claim: any proposed dialetheia outside the semantic paradoxes should be required to pass an externalization test. For a sentence to be a genuine case of being both true and false, there must be a decision problem in which treating it as glutty mandates a different action from every non-glutty treatment of the same case: true only, false only, neither (truth-value gap), or true but underdetermined.

Rationale: outside semantics, arguments for gluts are usually abductive. The glutty treatment is said to best explain why we are inclined both to assert and to deny the sentence. But if two treatments of a case prescribe exactly the same action in every decision problem, the difference between them is idle, and the abductive argument has nothing left to explain.

Why most candidate cases fail: take conflicting legal provisions, where two statutes of equal authority impose incompatible requirements. Treating both requirements as genuinely in force, in a logic in which explosion fails, still only tells you that both hold; it does not, on its own, tell you what to do. Treating the situation as one where no requirement determinately holds, and falling back on doing what is reasonable, yields the same available actions. The same holds for borderline cases of vagueness, where the glutty treatment and the gappy treatment both leave you doing whatever is sensible.

The case I claim passes: the marshal case. You are an aide. Two written orders of equal authority arrive simultaneously: advance, and hold. Doctrine says both remain in force; there is no appeal and no tie-breaking rule. Your options are advance, hold, or split the force. If both obligations genuinely hold, then a principle of maximal satisfaction—satisfy as much of your obligation set as you can when you cannot satisfy all of it—prescribes splitting. If instead no obligation determinately holds, the maximizing principle has nothing to maximize: everything is permitted, nothing is required, and there is no reason to split rather than pick arbitrarily. The glutty treatment is also the only one on which, afterwards, you can cite both orders as your reasons for what you did.

The objection I cannot shake: a classicist can say that both orders generate pro tanto obligations, that there is no contradiction anywhere, and that the same maximizing principle yields the split. The mixed response then seems to be doing all the work, and the glut none.

What I would like:

1. Is the externalization test one a dialetheist should accept, or is it a category mistake—a pragmatic demand placed on a semantic thesis? Is there any principled reason a truth-value glut should surface in prescriptions?
2. Does the marshal case really pass, or does the pro tanto reply deflate it? Can you construct a variant that resists that reply?
3. Does the test backfire by applying to the liar sentence, the dialetheist's home case? If the liar fails it, is that a reductio of the test, or only a reason to restrict the test to non-semantic cases?
4. The test only has bite where mixed or partial responses are available. Is that a fatal limitation or an informative one?
5. Which part of the argument is weakest, and what would a sympathetic dialetheist say that I have not considered?

If the interesting contribution is somewhere other than the externalization test, tell me that instead.
