I have finished a novel taken closely from my family's history. Its central scene reconstructs an episode of humiliation in my mother's life — she is alive, she has asked me not to write it, and she has spent forty years trying not to think about it. My editor's case for publishing is that the book will give a great many readers an intense and lasting pleasure, and I cannot deny an awkward corollary: that pleasure is not independent of the wound. The scene is what would make the book worth pressing on a friend; without it the book is a competent family chronicle, and much of the good it does would not exist.

I keep appealing to a principle I cannot state cleanly: that milder hurts can simply be weighed against the good a book does and, past some point, outweighed by it, whereas there are degrees of suffering for which no quantity of other people's enjoyment is any compensation at all. Three things worry me. I cannot say where the line falls, and I notice it tends to fall just above whatever I most want to do. I am not certain my mother's distress crosses it — she may be less wounded than I fear, or more. And the good and the bad here are entangled rather than separate, so I am not sure I am comparing two items at all.

I owe my publisher an answer by the end of the month. Is the principle coherent; can it be stated so that it does not merely relocate my own preference; and does it decide anything in a case where I am unsure both where the line lies and whether this case crosses it? I would rather be told that it decides nothing than be advised on how to talk to my mother.
