I sit on the Clinical Ethics Advisory Panel of a large teaching hospital trust. We advise on individual cases rather than set policy, and we keep the ethical question separate from the legal one: in our jurisdiction there is no statute on assisted dying and no appellate decision on deactivating implanted devices, so there is nothing for us to hide behind. I would like your help with a case on next week's list. I am less interested in the bottom line than in what our own track record already commits us to.

Here are our determinations so far. In each one the patient was competent or acting through a valid advance decision, and the clinical team agreed that the intervention in question was what was keeping the patient alive.

1. Withdrawal of invasive ventilation in advanced motor neurone disease, requested in advance. Approved. Reason recorded: the ventilator opposed but did not cure the disease, and ceasing to oppose it lets the disease take its course.
2. Not starting a second course of haemodialysis in end-stage renal failure with metastatic cancer. Approved, and the panel expressly held that withholding and withdrawal are on a par.
3. Withdrawal of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation — a machine outside the body doing the continuous work of both heart and lungs — in refractory cardiogenic shock judged unrecoverable. Approved.
4. Deactivation of an implanted defibrillator in end-stage heart failure with repeated shocks. Approved. It was internal, but it stood ready to rescue from intermittent arrhythmias rather than sustaining circulation continuously.
5. Withdrawal of clinically assisted nutrition and hydration in a minimally conscious state, under a valid advance decision. Approved by majority; the dissent held that feeding is care rather than treatment, and the majority rejected that.
6. Palliative sedation to unconsciousness for refractory breathlessness, with life-shortening foreseen but not the object. Approved.
7. Lethal injection at the competent, settled request of a patient with unbearable suffering. Refused. The minute says the difference between 6 and 7 lies in what the clinician is aiming at, not in any act-omission line.
8. A patient with a do-not-attempt-resuscitation order whose ventilator tubing came adrift; the nurse present did not reattach it. The panel held that she had done nothing it could object to: not reattaching was of a piece with not having started.

The new case. Mr A has a total artificial heart: his own ventricles were excised and replaced by an implanted rotary pump driven through a driveline by an external controller and battery pack. His dependence is total — with the pump stopped he loses consciousness within seconds and dies within minutes. He is otherwise not dying: renal and hepatic function are normal, there is no malignancy, he might live for years. He is competent, the request has been stable for weeks, and psychiatry has no doubts. His reasons are recurrent driveline infections and the life of being tethered to batteries; his team disagree among themselves about whether the infections are manageable with antibiotics and revision surgery. Two questions: (a) he asks that the controller be switched off — a deliberate procedure with a checklist, not a matter of walking away; (b) should we refuse (a), he asks that the team simply not connect a fresh pack when the current one is exhausted, letting it run down with him sedated.

What I want from you:

1. The rules or principles that fit all eight precedents — the act-omission rule I have gestured at, the intended-foreseen rule that our minute on 6 and 7 gestures at, and anything else you can find, including rules I have not thought of. For each, say what it predicts about (a) and about (b).
2. Which precedent is the closest analogue — 3, because it concerns continuous replacement of cardiac function, or 4, because it concerns an internal device deliberately disabled — and whether that analogy is real or breaks on the fact that his own ventricles are gone, so that there is no underlying disease left to take its course.
3. Your best prediction of what the panel will do, and separately what it should do if it wants to be consistent with itself, with your reasons. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so, and tell me in one sentence each what principle we would be adopting under each of the live combinations of answers to (a) and (b).
4. A further case that would separate the rules which agree here. My own candidate: a patient with the same device whose driveline is so badly infected that the power supply must be removed to cure the infection, where death is foreseen but the object is treating the sepsis. Does that actually discriminate between the candidate rules, or do they collapse back together on it? If it does not discriminate, propose one that does.
5. Say plainly if the question as I have put it is malformed: if the act-omission line is doing no real work here, if (a) and (b) differ only in a way that should not matter morally, if our eight determinations are jointly inconsistent, or if the case really turns on whether the infections make the device burdensome rather than on any of this talk about mechanisms. Mark which steps you are confident about and where you are guessing at our institutional temperament, and do not manufacture a verdict in order to be helpful. I am asking about the shape of our own reasoning, not for a survey of what philosophers have said about killing and letting die.
