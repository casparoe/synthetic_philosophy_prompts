I'm in a decision-theory seminar this term and we've been working through a family of exchange cases, and the verdicts I arrive at by gut don't line up with each other. The setup in every case: two sealed envelopes, one holding twice what the other holds, I'm handed one at random and offered the other. The stakes are small enough that I'm happy to treat money linearly unless I say otherwise. What I want is a principle precise enough to test, together with an honest account of which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

1. Both envelopes stay sealed, I'm told nothing about how the amounts were chosen, and the exchange is free. I'm indifferent, and I wouldn't pay even a token fee to switch. The reasoning that the other one is either half or double mine and therefore a quarter better on average strikes me as broken somewhere, but I can't say where.

2. I open mine and find $40, having been told the pair was produced by drawing a number from a wide but bounded range and doubling it. I'd switch.

3. I open mine and find a very large amount, with nothing I've been told ruling out larger ones. I'd switch as well. This is the verdict I trust least.

4. I don't open mine, but the host tells me truthfully that the mechanism was biased: the other envelope holds half of mine two times in three, and double mine one time in three. I want to switch, since the upside looks twice the size of the downside, though I'd like that checked rather than taken at face value.

5. The envelopes hold numbers of tokens, and what tokens are worth to me goes as their logarithm: every doubling gains me a fixed amount, every halving costs me the same. Here I'm indifferent, and I wouldn't pay to switch.

6. I switch, the envelopes are shuffled out of my sight, and I'm offered the exchange again on the same terms. I feel no pull at all towards taking it, and this makes me suspect that assessing each offer on its own is the wrong unit of assessment.

My own candidate principle, which I can't make fit: switch just in case my probability, given everything I have learned, that I am holding the smaller envelope exceeds one third. As I read it, it calls case 4 a break-even rather than a switch, which is not my verdict, and it tells me to switch in case 1, where I am indifferent and won't pay a cent.

So: test the candidate, repair it or replace it, and state each clause of whatever you end up with alongside the case that motivates it. Then give me the resulting principle's verdicts on two variants I haven't listed — one where the smaller amount is drawn from a distribution on which small values are much likelier than large ones, and one where switching costs me ten percent of whatever is in my envelope — say which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say whether the principle ought to govern individual acts or whole policies.
