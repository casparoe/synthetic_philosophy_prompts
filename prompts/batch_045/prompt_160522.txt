Our department's graduate committee publishes a short satirical insert in the termly newsletter — one piece, roughly 700 to 900 words — and the readership is mixed: philosophers (faculty and graduate students), the department's administrators, and a scattering of subscribers from other faculties. So whatever goes in has to be funny for someone who knows the moral-responsibility literature and still legible for someone who has never opened it. The next issue lands at the end of the week in which our seminar finishes P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," which is what I'd like the insert to take on: specifically the contrast between the participant stance, in which we answer one another as moral agents with resentment, indignation, guilt and gratitude, and the objective stance, in which someone is instead set aside as a creature to be managed, handled, treated, or trained.

The form I have in mind is a parody of corporate internal communications: an announcement and FAQ issued by the People department of a mid-sized freight company rolling out a company-wide program called the Objective Stance. All colleague-to-colleague interaction is to proceed on the objective model; blame, praise and thanks are retired in favour of scheduled reinforcement and "formative-circumstance audits"; gratitude is banked in a Gratitude Deferral Account and redistributed at the end of each quarter; managers complete a half-day of mandatory training; and there is a short appendix of sanctioned scripts ("in place of 'you missed the deadline,' try…"). Please also include a sidebar describing the pilot study, a note on which grades of staff are expected to adopt the stance toward which others, and a closing paragraph from the CEO. Invent freely — company names, consultants, module titles — but keep a deadpan corporate register throughout, with no winking at the reader and no explanatory frame at the beginning or end.

What matters most is that the humour comes from the content rather than from office-satire clichés. A reader who knows the essay should be able to notice, without being told: that Strawson presents the objective stance as apt only in a narrow range of cases — very young children, people with certain psychological impairments, someone who did not know, or was pushed, or could not have been expected to resist — and as something we could not adopt toward everyone at once without losing the interpersonal relationships in which our practices of holding one another responsible have their point; that the company is therefore prescribing a stance it cannot itself perform, since the rollout is mandatory, someone is blamed for failing to adopt it, and the CEO plainly still wants to be thanked for the program; and that the reactive attitudes come as a package, so a policy that tries to keep gratitude while abolishing resentment is not one anyone can actually follow. Don't quote Strawson, and don't name other theorists or their views; the piece should stand on its own.
