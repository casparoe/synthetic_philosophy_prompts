hey — could use a hand with something a bit odd. I build and validate clinical prediction models (mostly prognostic scores: risk of deterioration in the next 48h, that kind of thing), so my whole working vocabulary is losses and scoring rules — Brier, log-loss, calibration curves and calibration-in-the-large, discrimination / AUC / c-index, sens and spec at a threshold, net benefit and decision curves, threshold shift when prevalence changes, likelihood ratios, label noise and censored follow-up, averaging forecasts across an ensemble. I'm comfortable with proper scoring rules and with why you'd want them. Philosophy: basically zero, i'v never taken a course.

I keep running into people talking about accuracy-first epistemology / epistemic utility theory, and from what I can gather the position is roughly this: (1) the only fundamental epistemic good is having accurate beliefs, i.e. being close to the truth — everything else (evidential support, coherence, explanatory power) is good only instrumentally; (2) belief comes in degrees, and the norm that your degrees of belief have to satisfy the probability calculus is supposed to follow from the fact that any incoherent credence function is beaten by some coherent one *in every possible world* — closer to the truth however things turn out — where the measure of "distance from the truth" has to be a strictly proper scoring rule, the same incentive-compatibility condition I use when I want a model to report its real probabilities; (3) the norm of updating by conditionalizing is supposed to follow from maximizing expected accuracy, with the expectation taken using your own current credences; (4) and the whole package is sold as a vindication of these norms that doesn't lean on pragmatics at all — not that coherent believers do better in life, just that they're closer to the truth, full stop.

Can you restate that package in my vocabulary, thesis by thesis, as faithfully as you can? I want the honest version, not the "it's just scoring rules, move on" version. (And if I've got the position wrong, correct me — but do it in my terms.) Some specific things I want you to actually work through rather then gesture at:

- Step (2) is a pointwise / worst-case claim — for every way the world could be, some coherent assignment beats you — not an average-case one. In my world that's the gap between a robust guarantee and an expected-loss guarantee. Is that the right frame? And is the dominance step doing something my vocabulary can't do, given that the only distribution over "possible patients" I ever have is my own training sample?

- We deliberately do *not* use proper scoring rules for most of what ships. The real deployment metric is net-benefit-ish: flat below the treatment threshold, and it cares a lot more about false negatives than false positives. It's improper on purpose. Does accuracy-first have to say that metric has simply stopped measuring accuracy and now measures something else (usefulness? decision value?), or is there room in the framework for accuracy itself to depend on stakes? My hunch is it has to say the former, and that this is a real cost to the view — but I'd like the argument either way.

- We weight cases: some patients count more toward the score (paediatrics, or the rare outcome we actually care about). The weights are fixed — they don't depend on what the model outputs. Does the framework survive importance weighting? If inaccuracy is scored per possible world and you're allowed to say some worlds matter more, does the dominance argument still go through, or does it quietly break? Work it out rather than telling me what the literature says.

- Truth is messy in my world: outcomes are defined by proxies, follow-up is incomplete, patients get censored and we just never learn what happened. The framework seems to need a determinate truth value for every proposition so that "distance from the truth" means anything, and it seems perfectly happy to score you against truths no one will ever observe. Feature or bug? I have no way to express "closeness to a truth that will never be observed anywhere, ever" — every loss I use is eventually computable from data someone collects.
