"Heritability is the proportion of the variation in a trait, within a specified population living in a specified range of environments, that is statistically attributable to genetic variation. It is not the proportion of any individual's trait that is caused by that individual's genes: saying that height is around 80 per cent heritable does not mean that 80 per cent of how tall you are came from your DNA and 20 per cent from food and illness. Two things follow. First, a trait can be highly heritable and still be thoroughly preventable or treatable by an environmental change. Second, a high heritability within each of two groups tells you nothing at all about why the averages of those groups differ. So heritability is not a measure of how fixed a trait is, and it gives no comfort to anyone who wants to say that social policy is fighting biology. There is a flip side, though: where heritability is high, interventions that try to shift the trait are pushing against the grain of the genome, and their effects will tend to wash out once the intervention stops. And the newer 'response heritability' statistic finally gives us the number we actually wanted. It is the share of the variation in how much people change under a given intervention that is associated with genotype, and so it tells us how much of the intervention's effect is genetic rather than environmental — and therefore how much room policy really has."
