I am not a decision theorist by training; I come at this from building multi-agent systems, where questions about what one agent's choice tells it about another's show up sooner than one might expect. I have been mulling over the family of arguments in which an agent treats its own choice as evidence about the choices of relevantly similar agents, and so acquires a reason to cooperate in a one-shot prisoners' dilemma despite having no causal influence on anyone. I am not puzzled by that basic argument. I keep tripping over something else: in every version of the case I can build, whether the argument goes through depends on two things that the argument itself does not seem to constrain, and I cannot find a settled way of talking about either.

The first is about how the comparison class is carved. Suppose the world is large and the population is heterogeneous. If I count as relevantly similar only agents running my exact decision procedure who also had coffee this morning, then my cooperating looks like near-conclusive evidence that they cooperate, and the evidential case for cooperating is very strong. If I widen the class to every agent even loosely similar to me, the evidential link thins to almost nothing and the case collapses. Nothing in the setup tells me which carving is the right one, and the narrow carving is self-certifying in an uncomfortable way: the more details I condition on, the stronger the correlation I appear to manufacture, so the argument seems to reward gerrymandering the class until it delivers the conclusion I wanted. It reminds me of Simpson's paradox, and of what people say about the reference class problem for single-case probabilities, but in both of those the question is which set of frequencies applies to a particular case, whereas here the question is which similarity class is permitted to ground a correlation in the first place, and the answer changes what it is rational for me to do.

The second is about who gets to break the correlation. In the smoking-lesion style cases, the correlation is downstream of a fixed common cause, and no amount of deliberation on my part touches it; that is what makes the evidential recommendation there look merely odd rather than circular. Here the correlation is downstream of my own decision procedure, which I can in principle alter: an agent who, before choosing, verifiably commits itself to a public coin flip seems to dissolve the evidential reason to cooperate, and so seems to have an evidential reason not to adopt the very arrangement that would make its choice uninformative. Relatedly, if I already know my own procedure and know that the other agent runs it on the same inputs, then I already know what it will do, and there is no uncertainty left for the evidence to remove; the argument appears to work only within a narrow band of ignorance about whether the other agent is really relevantly like me. And I cannot tell whether the argument gets stronger or weaker as the population grows, since in a large world my single action is one noisy draw from a frequency I am trying to estimate.

So: does this pattern have an established name, or am I wrongly lumping two separately named things under one description? The nearest things I can think of are Hofstadter-style superrationality, the broader literature on acausal or evidential cooperation in large populations, Newcomb's problem and its medical variants, and the reference-class and Simpson's-paradox material above; none of these seems to be about the dependence of the reasons themselves on a carving choice plus a corrigible correlation, but I may be misremembering the scope of each. I do not want a bare label and I am not asking for citations or further reading: what I want is the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, which established concept comes closest, how my cases differ from it, and where the boundaries fall, including cases my description might seem to cover but should not.

5. One nearby configuration that the concept from item 4 should not be taken to cover, and why it falls outside.
