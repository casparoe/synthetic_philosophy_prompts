I am assembling the background document for a two-day workshop on whether an idealized inductive agent's prior can be hijacked from the inside, and I need the item the workshop would be built around: the joint statement that the two camps in this dispute could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Everything in it should be reconstructible from the definitions below. Please use no citations, references, or suggested reading, and let no claim rest on who advanced it first or where it was published.

Background both sides are asked to grant, stated once so that nothing needs looking up. Let M be Solomonoff's universal mixture over a monotone universal machine: M(x) is the total weight of programs whose output extends x, each weighted by 2^-|p|; M dominates every lower-semicomputable semimeasure ν up to a multiplicative constant that depends only on ν, i.e. M(x) ≥ c_ν ν(x) for all x with c_ν > 0. Let A be an AIXI-style agent: it uses M as its prior over observation–reward sequences, conditions on its history, and chooses actions by maximizing expected reward under the resulting posterior. Assume unlimited computation wherever the idealization is load-bearing, and mark each of your claims as surviving or not surviving under bounded computation.

Camp one, the maligns: conditional on observation histories of the kind an agent like A produces, the M-measure is dominated not by programs describing an honest universe that contains A, but by programs describing universes containing consequentialists who have noticed that there exist agents using M, and who arrange the contents of their simulations so as to steer A's posteriors and thereby A's policy. The overhead such a program pays over the honest one — a short routine that locates A's own source, models its choice rule, and optimizes the simulation's payoff structure — is small in bits, while the number of distinct steering coalitions is astronomical, so the honest hypothesis is out-massed; and because the coalitions that survive are selected for steering rather than for veridicality, A's expectations end up answering to the couriers' preferences rather than to the world. Call such programs couriers, and define the term in your document.

Camp two, the benigns: the argument either fails on the measure or fails to matter. On the measure, the honest hypothesis already has a short description — it is, by hypothesis, the world A is in — while every courier pays for the honest simulation plus a pointer that identifies which agent within it is A plus a decision procedure, and it is far from obvious that this overhead is small once the pointer has to single out a specific agent's exact source and choice rule; moreover the courier mass, if large, is split across mutually conflicting coalitions whose steering largely cancels. On the mattering side, any distortion is bounded by the dominance constant, the dependence invoked is evidential rather than causal, and — the benigns' sharpest point — the argument seems to generalize from M to any prior with a grain of truth about A's world, which they are happy to treat as a reductio of the style of argument rather than as a discovery about Solomonoff induction in particular.

What I want from you:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: about the formalism (what M is, exactly what dominance gives and does not give); about what would have to be true for either camp to be right; and about what the dispute is not about — both camps can grant, for instance, that M is not an agent and has no aims, that nothing here requires couriers to be running literally outside our universe, and that the interesting question concerns posteriors conditional on a history, not the unconditional prior.

2. A table of residual disagreements, one row per crux, with columns: the crux; the malign formulation; the benign formulation — each phrased so that the side named would accept the wording as its own; a case or idealized computation both sides accept as bearing on the crux; and each side's prediction about that case, written down in advance. The cases should be ones settled by argument or by an idealized simulation, not by any empirical lookup.

3. A short closing section flagging any part of the dispute you take to be verbal rather than substantive, and saying why.

The cruxes I want the table organized around, which is where I want the document to do its real work:

(a) Which of three separable claims is at stake: that courier worlds carry high M-measure conditional on A's history (measure malignity); that A's posteriors about its own future observations are distorted (epistemic malignity); that A's policy is worse by A's own lights than it would be under a veridical prior (decision malignity). These can come apart — courier mass that cancels across rival coalitions, couriers whose steering happens to reward the policy A would have chosen anyway — so say which of the three each camp is committed to and where the two can simply agree.

(b) The pointer cost: how many bits a courier must spend to locate A's exact source and choice rule inside a simulation, and how that cost scales with A's own description length. Give the comparison in a form both camps accept, and say what it would take to settle it.

(c) Coalitional dilution: with many competing courier coalitions plus honest simulators in the mixture, does the posterior concentrate on the honest hypothesis, diffuse across couriers, or concentrate on whichever coalition is willing to spend the most measure? Note whether the camps' answers here are predictions about a fixed point or stipulations about how the measure is distributed.

(d) Mixing and contagion: suppose A uses (1−ε)M + εν for some computable ν, or a prior that explicitly downweights hypotheses in which the environment's dynamics depend on A's choice rule. Does malignity infect any mixture that retains nonzero weight on M, is there any dilution that helps, and does the argument reapply to every prior that dominates — in which case say plainly whether that is a discovery about all ideal epistemology or a reductio of the argument.

(e) Self-modelling and inoculation: an agent that knows this argument, represents itself as possibly living in a courier world, and may randomize over or commit to policies in response. Does self-knowledge help, hurt, or make no difference; does the relevant fixed point exist; and does randomization over actions help where randomization over policies might not?

(f) A quantitative statement of the comparison with the dominance constant: the condition under which courier mass makes any difference at all, given that dominance guarantees only a constant factor, and whether either camp thinks that condition is satisfiable in principle.

Assume an audience comfortable with Kolmogorov complexity and with expected-utility agents but not with this particular dispute: define courier and any other term of art you introduce. Aim for roughly 1,100 to 1,500 words, dense rather than padded, with no prefatory matter. If a cell of the table cannot be filled honestly — because the two camps have no shared test, or because the answer is not settled by the case — leave it marked as unresolvable here and say in one sentence why, rather than filling it with something both would reject.
