I build data infrastructure for a machine learning platform group, and the person on our team who decides whether we are allowed to trust a model off distribution is a staff engineer I will call R. He is also our resident skeptic: he maintains that the claim that the future will resemble the past cannot be established without already assuming it, and that averaged over all possible mappings from inputs to labels no learner beats any other, so every claim of generalization has to be paid for with an assumption about which mappings are actual. He adds that you do not buy your way out of that result merely by narrowing attention to the problems you happen to care about; you escape it only if the ones you care about share real structure rather than being an arbitrary selection. Yet he licenses models constantly, and I cannot reconstruct what he thinks he is doing. His rulings so far:

1. A classifier trained on a large iid sample, with the model family fixed before anyone looked at the test set and a large held-out gain: licensed.
2. An identical pipeline, except that the architecture and hyperparameters were chosen by checking test performance: not licensed.
3. A forty-parameter polynomial fitted to fifty noisy measurements, with no account of the mechanism: not licensed, even though it fits.
4. A three-parameter model derived from a well-confirmed physical mechanism, fitted to fifty measurements and used only for interpolation inside the measured range: licensed.
5. A trend fitted to observed data and then extrapolated an order of magnitude beyond the data, with no mechanism: not licensed.
6. A network with vastly more parameters than the textbook story permits, no mechanistic interpretation, trained on a very large iid corpus, with a large held-out gain on a task nobody has a theory of: licensed.
7. A three-parameter demand-forecasting model fitted on rolling-origin backtests, which has beaten every rival method we have tried on that task for six consecutive quarters, with no theory of why: licensed.

The case he has to rule on next: a grasping policy trained entirely in a simulator whose dynamics come from rigid-body mechanics with friction coefficients we measured ourselves, with no real-world trajectories used in training, and which succeeded on its first twelve real trials. Our company's sim-to-real record is three transfers that held up and two that quietly failed, and nobody can tell in advance which kind a given one is. The question is whether he will say we are licensed to expect it still to be working on the ten-thousandth real trial.

What I want from you: first, the two or three rules that fit all seven earlier verdicts about equally well — I can make out a mechanistic one, a statistical-learning-theory one, and a track-record one, but I want each stated sharply enough that they disagree somewhere. Then what each predicts about the simulator case; your best guess at his verdict, with the reason for preferring that rule over the others; and the single further case you would put to him to separate whichever rules survive. If none of the candidate rules actually answers his own circularity worry rather than restating it, say so, and say which of his verdicts that undermines.
