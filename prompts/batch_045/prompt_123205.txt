After forty years in experimental physics I retired and began reading philosophy of mind seriously for the first time, and I have become stuck on a question that no amount of reading settles, because the person I would most like to ask has been dead since 1954: what would Alan Turing have said about John Searle's Chinese room argument, and about the large language models we now actually have?

I am not asking for pastiche and not for an imagined monologue. I want a prediction argued from what Turing really committed himself to: the 1950 paper in Mind where he replaces the question whether machines can think with the imitation game; the 1948 report on machine intelligence he wrote for the National Physical Laboratory (if my recollection is right it is there that the ideas of unorganised machines and of training rather than programming appear); and anything else of his you are reasonably confident about. Name the commitments you lean on, and mark how confident you are that each is genuinely his; where you are recalling rather than certain, say so rather than smoothing it over. Give me no bibliography and no citations — I can find the texts myself, and I would rather have the reasoning than a list of references.

Four things in particular I would like handled.

First, the line I keep meeting is that Turing simply exchanged a question about minds for a question about behaviour, and therefore has no quarrel with Searle at all, since Searle grants the behaviour and denies the understanding. That strikes me as too quick. Turing replaced the question for methodological reasons while continuing to think that internal organisation matters, that learning matters, and that simulating the adult mind directly would be the wrong way to build a thinking machine. So I want his answer to the man in the room stated as an answer, not as a refusal to answer, and I want to know what in his view it costs him to give one.

Second — and this is the part I care about most; it is my own construction, so you will not find it in the literature — consider the following ladder of cases, each producing exactly the same Chinese input-output behaviour as the others, and tell me at which rung, if any, Turing would locate understanding, and why that rung rather than its neighbour:

Rung 0: Searle's original room, a rule book, a man with paper and pencils.
Rung 1: the same function realised as an explicit table of every possible conversation up to some length, stored in filing cabinets occupying a warehouse the size of a city.
Rung 2: the same function realised as a statistical model with no rule book and no table, a large set of numerical parameters fitted to a corpus of Chinese text — the sort of thing we in fact now possess.
Rung 3: that same model, additionally equipped with cameras and the capacity to act, trained by reward for what it does rather than for what it says, which is roughly the arrangement Turing himself gestures at when he proposes giving a machine the best sense organs available and then teaching it.

For each rung give me his predicted verdict, the feature of the rung doing the work in his eyes, and whether Searle's intuition retains any grip there. If his verdicts pull apart in a way that exposes a strain in his own position, say that plainly; I would rather have the strain than a tidy story.

Third, a complication Turing could not have foreseen and that I think cuts against the spirit of his own test. We can now open the box and inspect what a system's internal states encode. Suppose that on one branch we find the trained system has built something functionally akin to a structured model of the situations its sentences are about, and that this model is doing the causal work in producing its replies; and that on the other branch nothing of the kind exists, the behaviour arising from shallow matching against a memorised corpus, with correct answers emerging for reasons no one can state. Would Turing have cared which branch we are on? And, more sharply: given that his criterion was deliberately black-box, was he entitled to care? Make the tension explicit rather than settling it by fiat.

Fourth, the large language models themselves. Somewhere in the 1950 paper Turing ventured a quantitative guess about the storage capacity a machine would need and how long an interrogator could question it before being deceived, with a target date around the year 2000. If you can recall that guess, restate it; if you cannot, say so instead of inventing figures, since I intend to check them. Then tell me whether he would regard present systems as the vindication of that guess or as a way of winning the wager while abandoning its spirit: they are not programmed but trained, which is the route he preferred, yet they are trained almost exclusively on text, without the sensory education he imagined, and no one can say in detail how they do what they do.

Finally, be candid about the limits of the exercise. Distinguish the predictions that are forced by what Turing wrote from those that are projections onto a man who did not live to see any of this, and for the latter say what sort of evidence or argument would settle the fork. Tell me also what would have moved him: what result, shown to him, would have led him to abandon the imitation game as a criterion.

Write this as a formal memorandum of roughly twelve hundred words, in sections, addressed to a careful non-specialist — me: no technical term without a gloss, no staged dialogue, no quotations put into his mouth. If some part of my question is ill-posed, identify the part, reformulate it, and then answer the reformulation.
