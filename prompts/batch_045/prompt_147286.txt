Hey — I've been chewing on the "what is life?" question on and off for years and I've hit the point where I don't think more reading is going to help me, so I want a diagnosis instead.

Here's the shape of it. The positions I keep running into: the astrobiology crowd with their working definition (roughly, a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution), the autopoiesis people who think the real mark is a self-produced boundary that maintains itself, the far-from-equilibrium thermodynamics line where the interesting object is a dissipative structure holding itself together, the evolution-only people for whom heredity, variation and differential reproduction is the whole story, and the deflationists who say there just is no essence, it's a cluster concept, stop looking. Every couple of years a new review comes out, lists the same handful of criteria, trots out the same counterexamples (viruses, mules, fire, prions, crystals, computer viruses, mitochondria), and wraps up by saying the whole thing is frustratingly hard to pin down. Nobody has moved an inch in my lifetime.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this isn't a dispute about biology at all. It's a verbal dispute about the word "life" sitting on top of a substantive but never-stated disagreement about which cluster of properties is supposed to license which downstream inference — whether a thing gets moral consideration, whether a smudge in a microscope image is a biosignature worth spending a mission on, whether we should expect a genuinely universal biology or expect life to be a wildly contingent accident, whether a pattern running on a normal computer could be a person. On that reading the definitions are proxies for different purposes, so of course they don't converge, and the "debate" is four research programmes sharing a word.

But I might well be wrong, and I'd rather you tell me than flatter me. Maybe there's a real factual question doing all the work (Is there one historical origin or several? Is there any general law of adaptive systems? Does evolvable heredity actually require a physical boundary?). Or maybe the sides are just answering different questions under one name. Or maybe it's bedrock intuition all the way down.

What makes it concrete for me is that I don't think anyone's intuitions are trained on real data here, because we have exactly one sample. So everybody argues from hypotheticals, and I suspect the actual fight is about which hypotheticals count as data. Here are four I made up precisely because they're not in the literature — please use them, and if one is underdescribed in a way that matters, say what extra detail would flip you.

1. The Halvorsen loops: a self-replicating pattern in a continuous cellular automaton. It has heredity with mutation, differential reproduction, and an open-ended arms race over hundreds of millions of generations — niche differentiation, cheaters, the works. But it's running on a laptop: no chemistry, no membrane, no energy flux, and it's not obvious there are individuals at all.
2. The Enceladus gels (invented, for the sake of argument, from a probe that found them at a vent field): metabolizing, homeostatic, dividing blobs, chemistry far from equilibrium, persisting for geological time — but so developmentally canalized that essentially no adaptive change ever accumulates. Metabolism and self-maintenance, no evolution.
3. The chalk line: an engineered lineage whose genome evolves fast and which outcompetes everything in its niche, but which can only reproduce inside a machine that supplies something like 40% of its replication machinery, the machines having originally been built by an already-existing biosphere. Evolvable, not self-sustaining, not self-replicating.
4. The stripped cell: something we build on purpose. It started as an uncontroversially living organism and was then reduced over many passages to a membrane, a genome, and a feed line, with all the metabolic work done by enzymes we pipette in.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, substantive, different-questions-under-one-name, hidden empirical question, or a fight about which cases count as data? Tell me which of my four cases are genuinely load-bearing for which position and which ones every position can hand-wave away, because I think that asymmetry is itself evidence about what each view really cares about. If you think part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and what the honestly re-described question would be — and I'd rather you didn't stop at "it's a cluster concept, move on," since I want to know what substantive work that move does here. Please don't give me a survey of the positions or a reading list; argue it out from the cases and from what each side actually commits itself to. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a case we'd have to build, a change of question — or, if nothing would, why not.
