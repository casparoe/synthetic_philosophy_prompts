The setting is a seminar on nomic self-reference, rules about rules about rules. We were handed a miniature code — call it K — whose last section is the only part that matters:

§9.1 Except as §9.2 provides, any provision of this Code may be amended or repealed by an instrument approved by two-thirds of the Assembly.
§9.2 An instrument amending or repealing this section requires the unanimous consent of the Assembly.
§9.3 §9.2 may not be amended or repealed.

Here is the argument I built, which I now find suspicious mainly because of where it ends.

(1) Pedigree. An amending instrument has legal force in K if and only if it is enacted in conformity with the amending provision in force at the time of enactment. Validity is a matter of how the instrument was made, not of what it achieves and not of whether people later treat it as law.

(2) Whenever any instrument is enacted, the amending provision then in force is §9, in one or another of its possible versions.

(3) So an instrument amending §9 has legal force only if it is unanimous. [1, 2]

(4) Now take instrument I. It is carried two-thirds to one — not unanimously. Its entire text is: "§9 is repealed and replaced by §9*: any provision of this Code, including this section, may be amended by an instrument approved by two-thirds of the Assembly."

(5) I's validity has to be measured against some rule, and there are exactly two candidates: §9, the provision in force when I was passed, and §9*, the provision I purports to bring into force.

(6) §9 cannot be the measure. A legal system's amending power is the power to reconstitute the system, and it cannot be hemmed in by its own product: every norm in K owes its legal force either to an exercise of that power or to the system's continuing acceptance of it, so there is no norm in K — §9.2 and §9.3 included — that the Assembly cannot in principle amend. If §9 could dictate the terms on which §9 alone may be changed, then K would contain legally unamendable norms, and a single legal act would have placed part of the law permanently beyond the reach of law. Call this the self-constitution thesis: the amending power reaches every provision, itself included.

(7) So I must be measured against §9*, the rule it enacts: an instrument that rewrites the amending provision is, as to its own validity, governed by the rule it brings into being. Call this self-validation. It has the further virtue of explaining how a legal system can ever change its own amendment rule at all — the new rule validates the act that creates it.

(8) Under §9*, I is valid: it had two-thirds. So I is valid, and after I, §9* is the amending provision of K.

(9) Generalize. Let A be any amending provision that imposes a stricter condition on its own amendment than on the amendment of ordinary provisions, and let m be the weaker of the two thresholds it contains. By (5) through (8), an instrument carried by m that repeals the stricter condition and replaces A with the weaker threshold is valid, because it is judged by its own terms. So no amending provision can impose on its own amendment any condition that it does not also impose on everything else. §9.2, §9.3, and every real-world eternity clause are legally inert: speed limits on the amendment power that the amendment power can always lawfully ignore, since the act of ignoring them supplies the standard by which the ignoring is judged.
