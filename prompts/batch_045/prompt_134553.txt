I am writing a novel set some sixty years from now, and I have reached a chapter I cannot write honestly until I understand an argument better than I currently do. A tribunal has been convened to decide whether a newly contacted people — I call them the Sarsi — fall within a legal category of persons, and the chapter is a cross-examination in which each side reasons from a machine analogy. My difficulty is not with the plot. It is that I cannot tell which moves in the reasoning are load-bearing and which are rhetorical, and I would rather my characters be genuinely formidable than merely fluent.

The details are these, and I would ask you to take them as stipulated rather than to check them against any real biology. The Sarsi are eusocial. An individual weighs perhaps ten kilograms and has a brain of roughly seventy grams, organized as densely packed nuclei rather than as a six-layered cortex, with neurons several times more densely packed than in a primate brain and conduction velocities perhaps a tenth of ours. No individual Sarsi does anything one would call reasoning; each is, however, plainly sentient, has preferences, learns, and can be harmed. Colonies number between twenty and forty members. They maintain a shared chemical record that persists across generations, they integrate what their members learn, and on the expedition's evidence they plan, correct themselves, and preserve techniques no single member could have produced. In a large colony the aggregate neural tissue is roughly twice the mass of a human brain.

The two analogies my characters reach for pull in opposite directions.

The expedition's neuroscientist argues from hardware and software. The mind, she says, is the programme; the brain is only the substrate on which it happens to run; programmes are substrate-independent; therefore the size, the cell count, the wiring, and even the species of the hardware are as morally irrelevant as the foundry that etched a chip. The only question is what is being computed. On her view a colony that computes the same things slowly and collectively is running the same programme as a human being, and the fact that it runs it a tenth as fast is a fact about the clock, not about the software.

The advocate for the colonial office argues from engines. Cylinders, displacement, power. On his view the brain is a machine whose capacity scales with its size and its parts count, and a seventy-gram brain is a lawnmower engine beside a human one; whatever moral status tracks, it tracks something of which a bigger machine has more. He also argues that the colony's aggregate tissue belongs to nobody — there is no subject whose engine it is — and he concedes that brain size is a crude proxy but insists it is the only one we can measure without first settling whether the creature has a mind at all, which he says would beg the question.

I would like an audit of both, in four parts.

First, set the correspondences out explicitly; a table is welcome. For the hardware-software picture: what plays the programme, the machine, the input, the output, the user, and what would count as the same programme running on different hardware. For the engine picture: what plays the engine, the fuel, the load, the work done, and the owner. Then say what, among the Sarsi as I have described them, would occupy each slot, and note any slot for which the Sarsi supply no candidate at all.

Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for the work these analogies are being asked to do from the ones that are merely decorative. The work is to settle whether any neural property of the Sarsi is morally relevant, and if so which. So: does the substrate-independence claim quietly assume a functionalist theory of mind, and would granting functionalism settle anything about moral status, or only about the identity conditions of mental states? Does the engine analogy smuggle in both that moral status is scalar and that the relevant magnitude is a rate or a throughput rather than a character of experience? Does either analogy have anything to say about whether the tempo difference matters, given that the colony may reach the same places a human reaches, only over a season instead of an afternoon?

Third, tell me where each character's conclusion is actually resting. My suspicion is that neither machine analogy is doing the real work: that the neuroscientist's conclusion needs a functionalist theory of mind plus a separate claim about what matters morally, and the advocate's needs a theory about when parts compose a subject, and that the facts about brains are decoration in both. If that is right, I want to know what the brain facts are evidence for, such that size would be evidence of them — number of neurons in associative regions, degree of integration, richness and valence of experience, temporal depth of memory, something else — and whether there is any version of the question on which sheer size itself, and not merely what size indicates, could be morally relevant. If there is not, say so plainly; if there is, I would like to know what the size would have to be doing, causally, for it to matter in its own right.

Fourth, propose a better analogy if one exists, and say what it would make visible that these two hide. I am especially interested in the eusocial complication: if the moral patient is the colony rather than the member, does either analogy still have a subject to attach to, and what would have to be true of the colony for the size of its distributed neural mass to be a morally relevant fact about it? And is there an argument that the individual Sarsi is where the moral fact lies regardless of what the colony does, given that each one is sentient and can be harmed?

Two constraints. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or names to cite; the tribunal in my novel argues from reasons rather than authorities, and I need the reasons — where a question has a settled answer in the literature, tell me the answer and not who gave it. And please mark the places where you are extrapolating past anything actually studied, since a character who is honestly uncertain will serve my chapter better than one who is confident on no evidence.
