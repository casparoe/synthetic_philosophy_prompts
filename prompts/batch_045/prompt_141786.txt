Me and two friends have a reading group going on protest and the law (we're loosely following an undergrad syllabus I found, no instructor), and last night we got completely stuck on the line people always drop in these discussions: "if you break the law you have to be willing to accept the punishment." Priya used it to argue that our friend Osei's arrest at a road blockade last spring doesn't count as civil disobedience at all, because after being charged he got a lawyer, pled it down to a lesser offense, let a solidarity fund cover the fine instead of paying it himself, and when the judge asked whether he'd do it again he said yes and added that the court had no business trying him. Osei says he never ran, never hid anything, showed up to every hearing, did the community service, and that the courtroom speech is just him being honest rather than some failure of willingness. Our TA mentioned at one point that the standard definition of civil disobedience basically builds the willingness-to-take-the-penalty bit right into it, which is what Priya is leaning on, but nobody could say what that clause is actually supposed to be doing there.

Then Marcus, who loves inventing distinctions, proposed something he calls "counterfactual penalty acceptance": you only owe it to absorb the penalty that the legal order you're trying to bring about would impose on you, so if Osei thinks a properly ordered city wouldn't criminalize that blockade at all, then he owes zero penalty, and paying anyway is either generosity or pure theatre. He also wants to split "accepting punishment" into grades — not fleeing, not lying about what you did, not appealing, not asking for leniency, not letting anyone else pay, publicly endorsing the sentence as just — and says the slogan is hopeless until you say which grade you mean. Everyone basically talked past each other for an hour.

So: can you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making? I can see there's a boring causal one (you'll probably get caught, so don't whine) and something deontic about what you owe other citizens, but I suspect there are also readings about what the willingness is supposed to be *evidence* of, about whether you've forfeited the right to complain about being punished, about fair play or paying back a debt to the scheme you've been benefiting from, and maybe something about the difference between disobedience and revolution. For each one say whether it's plausible, whether it's trivial or substantive, and — this is the bit I actually care about — whether it would do the work Priya needs it to do, i.e. rule Osei's action in or out as civil disobedience. And tell me where Marcus's counterfactual-penalty version sits: is it one of the readings in disguise, or does it reject the whole slogan? Which reading do you think people who repeat the line are usually reaching for? Something I can paste into the group chat before we meet again.
