I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece for a general-interest mag about whether your thoughts actually do anything — the old worry that if the physical story about some movement is complete, there's nothing left for wanting and believing to do. My editor wants a clickable flow chart to go with it: readers answer a handful of questions and land on a named position, without ever being asked which -ism they already like. Can you build me that decision tree?

What I need, concretely:

Every branch node has to be a question about a case, not a question about a thesis or a label. No node may use the words "supervenience," "realization," or "causal closure" — if one of those is doing real work at a node, translate it into something a person scrolling this on their phone will answer without a philosophy degree.

Start where everybody already agrees: the everyday explanation is true (she crossed at 5th because she wanted coffee and thought the place was open), and there is also some complete story about the muscles and neurons. Branch from there.

Cases I want in the spine — swap in better ones if you've got them, but keep at least these three:

1. The want and the neurons. My wanting coffee is why I turned onto 5th instead of 6th. The neural process did it too. Is that one process described two ways, two processes that both did the work, or one real process plus one story we tell about it?
2. The bypass. A spinal-injury patient gets an implant that reads the intention to move straight out of motor cortex and stimulates the muscles directly, with the old pathway cut. No intention to read, no movement. Does the intention count as a cause now? This one is definately my favorite, because I can't tell whether it should push readers toward "difference-making under intervention" or whether the device is just the new physical cause and the intention still gets squeezed out.
3. The scattered pattern. Imaging keeps improving, and it turns out that what realizes my wanting coffee is a different, wildly scattered neural patern every single time — no stable neural kind there at all, just a moving target. Does the wanting still do causal work, or does that make it look like the mental kind was never the sort of item that could be efficacious in the first place?

One more node should handle the case where the subject reports deciding only after the neural preparation for the movement has already started. I know that one's famous, but readers will ask about it.

Leaves I want at the bottom: the "drained" leaf where the mental story turns out to be causally idle; reduction or identity; the overdetermination-style nonreductive view; the one-event-two-descriptions dissolution; emergentist downward causation; interactionist dualism; and the view where the mental earns its keep in explanation rather than in causation. If some coherent combination of answers lands in a spot nobody actually occupies, mark the cell empty and say whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Two last things. First, flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really separate — I'd rather publish an honest "these two routes land in the same place" note than a chart that pretends to a resolution it doesn't have. Second, tell me which node does the most sorting work, since that's the question I'll put first, and tell me if two readers who disagree at every node still end up on the same leaf, because that means one of my questions isn't pulling its weight.

Keep it to about ten nodes, give each leaf one line on its most awkward commitment, and skip the further-reading box — I've got that covered.
