I'm a fourth-year PhD student in computer science — my work is on equilibrium computation and learning dynamics in multi-agent systems, mostly multi-agent reinforcement learning with a side of mechanism design for peer prediction and information elicitation — and I've been dragged by my housemate, who reads political theory, into an argument I can't settle with the tools I actually have. It started after a lab meeting where five of us independently escalated to larger training runs for the same sweep, each of us knowing the aggregate was wasteful; she said this was Moloch again, and I said it was just a Prisoner's Dilemma with better branding. She keeps using this rationalist-blogosphere vocabulary — multipolar traps, races to the bottom, a structure that "demands sacrifice" — as though it picks out one real kind of thing that shows up in arms races, credential inflation, ad load, and unsafe deployment, and that the right response to it is closer to sacrifice than to optimization. My impatience says the whole framing is an anthropomorphic gloss on games my field already has names for, but I've stopped trusting that reaction, and I'd rather have the translation done properly than have my annoyance decide it.

So please do the following: state the Moloch / multipolar-trap claim as strongly and charitably as you can, then restate it in the vocabulary I actually use — normal-form and stochastic games, dominant-strategy and Nash or coarse-correlated equilibria, price of anarchy and price of stability, no-regret learning dynamics as a model of myopia, Markov perfect equilibrium in games with an accumulating state variable, mechanism design and the revelation principle, commitment devices and contractibility, and correlation and enforcement as the two things a central authority buys you. Do it three times over, because I've become convinced the framing is conflating at least three structures my vocabulary separates cleanly, and I want to know whether the separation is mine or the conflation is theirs: (1) a flat dominant-strategy trap, a many-player PD-like game with a unique bad equilibrium that every participant would contract out of if contracting were free; (2) what I've started calling a ratchet trap, a dynamic game whose payoff-relevant state variable — arsenal size, credential threshold, compute budget — moves essentially only in one direction, so that the trap is the irreversibility of the path rather than the stage game, and undoing the incentive later does not undo the accumulated state; (3) what I'll call a diffusion trap, where nothing any single agent does is counterfactually load-bearing, the aggregate outcome is bad, and no individual action is worse than its available alternatives by any local standard, which I take to be roughly what is meant by calling the problem structural rather than moral. For each of the three, say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion — and be explicit about which of my framework's assumptions you had to import to make it fit: common priors, transferable utility, fixed exogenous preferences over outcomes, costless or costly contractibility, common knowledge of the game — and what has no counterpart in my formalism at all.

Then the part I actually want, the residue report. I suspect there are at least four commitments in the Moloch framing that my vocabulary has no slot for, and for each I want you to tell me whether it marks a genuine blind spot in the formalism or is rhetoric that dissolves once you try to cash it out. First, that the trap is agent-like: that it has something like an interest or a direction, that it is the right object of blame or of opposition rather than any participant, and that this is a claim about the world and not merely a way of speaking. Second, that the fix has to work by changing what agents want rather than what they face — my machinery takes utility functions as primitive and the mechanism as the design variable, so "moral transformation" arrives as changing the inputs until the output looks better, which feels like cheating but may be a real insight about where the leverage lies. Third, that the appropriate response is sacrifice, giving something up without assurance of reciprocity, which in equilibrium terms is strictly dominated play and yet seems load-bearing in the actual historical cases people cite, from temperance movements to professional taboos to arms-control norms. Fourth, the diagnostic claim that one and the same structure is present across cases my field would classify as different games — some PD-like, some coordination failures with a ratchet, some contests with rent dissipation — and I want to know whether that unity is a discovery about a structural kind or an artifact of not asking which game you are in. Close by saying where you land on the general question: is what survives translation genuinely lost by my framework, or was the original claim less determinate than it sounded? Argue it in the terms I've given you rather than by gesturing at literature I would need a library to follow, and where you need a bridge principle between my vocabulary and theirs, state the principle explicitly so I can attack it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — my housemate and I have a bet on whether Moloch names a real kind or is just a metaphor for games we already have names for — then give the three translations and the residue report, with no preamble and no restatement of what I asked.
