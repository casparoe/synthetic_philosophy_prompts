I'm in a graduate reading group on the philosophy of artificial intelligence — we spent a few weeks on Dennett's intentional-stance framework and have been arguing ever since about whether a large language model can properly be said to believe anything — and one member brought in an argument that I'm convinced breaks somewhere but can't locate the break in. It runs as follows.

(1) *Instrumentalism.* For a system S to believe that p is nothing over and above this: that treating S as a rational agent that believes p and wants such-and-such forms part of the best available strategy for predicting and explaining what S does. There is no further fact of the matter about S's beliefs beyond what the stance, applied to S, delivers. (2) Whether a strategy is "the best available" is not a fact about S alone; it is a fact about what else is available *to the predictor*. The stance pays off for S only if it yields predictions that this predictor could not get as accurately or as cheaply another way — from the design stance or the physical stance. That is supposed to explain why we don't attribute beliefs to thermostats and vending machines even though the stance technically applies to them: it buys us nothing we didn't already have. (3) Now suppose interpretability research completes its program for some present-day language model M: we end up with a full mechanistic account such that M's next output can be computed from its weights and activations more reliably and more cheaply than by reasoning about what M believes and wants. Call M, so understood, *glass-boxed*. Nothing about M's behavior or its internal machinery changes when that account arrives; only our epistemic situation does. (4) By (1) and (2), once M is glass-boxed, M has no beliefs — the intentional stance is no longer part of the best available strategy for it. And worse: M loses its beliefs at the moment the mechanistic account is published, retains them for any predictor who hasn't read it, and a physically identical copy of M running somewhere no one has done the interpretive work keeps its beliefs while the glass-boxed one has none. Call this *belief evaporation by interpretability*. (5) Belief cannot be that kind of property. It cannot come and go with the publication schedule of interpretability papers, or differ between two intrinsically identical systems because one of them happens to have been mechanistically explained. Whatever beliefs are, they are not hostage to the explanatory uptake of a third party in that way. (6) So (1) is false: there is more to believing than being usefully predictable from the intentional stance. Believing must be settled by something intrinsic — the causal and functional organization of the system, or what its internal states actually track — and the stance is at best evidence of that.

Here is why I can't simply take (6) and go home. Of the premises I would most like to keep (2) and (5): (2) is what does the real work in explaining the thermostat case, and (5) expresses an intuition I can't give up. But rejecting (1) for (6) seems to invite an equally bad problem. If belief is fixed by intrinsic structure alone, consider what one of us dubbed a *content-rotation twin*: hold M's computations fixed but systematically permute the semantic values assigned to its internal feature directions, while making the compensating adjustment to the assignment of "desires" and to the reading of its outputs. The result is a system that behaves exactly as M does but which, under the permuted interpretation, believes something entirely different — that every cat is a dog, say. Behaviorally the two are indistinguishable, so if content is settled by structure plus history I need to be told which interpretation is correct and what does the settling; and if the answer is "the model's causal history with its training distribution," I want to know why that isn't just the intentional stance smuggled back in under another name. Conversely, if the answer is that only the structure matters and its history is irrelevant, then the stance becomes idle: a system could satisfy every realist condition on belief and be completely unpredictable from the intentional stance, which would make the property undetectable, and another could be perfectly predictable from the stance while having no beliefs at all, which would make the property irrelevant to everything we actually do with these systems — trusting them, correcting them, holding them to their word.

So: which step of the main argument do you reject, why does it look sound when it is read quickly, and what does each of the available ways out actually cost? I'd like you to price at least these: the real-pattern variant, which tries to keep instrumentalism but grounds "the stance works" in an objective fact about the system rather than in what is convenient for a given predictor; hard realism about content, in its functional-role and information-tracking forms; an interpretivism that construes attributions by a rationality or charity constraint; and eliminativism about AI belief. In particular, is there any reading of (2) that still handles the thermostat but doesn't make belief hostage to the state of interpretability research — perhaps by distinguishing the *accuracy* of a stance from its *cheapness*, or its usefulness for one predictive task from its usefulness for another — and can anyone who goes realist at step (6) answer the content-rotation twin without collapsing back into instrumentalism? If the honest answer is that the argument is valid and I have to surrender some premise I'd rather keep, tell me which one is least costly and what I lose by it.
