I should say something about where I am coming from before I ask. I spent thirty-eight years as a physical chemist, mostly on transport properties in dense fluids, and since retiring I have been auditing philosophy of science rather more seriously than is probably good for me. A former colleague who convenes the final-year MSci option in philosophy of science for physical-science students has asked me to set one question for the closed-book final — forty-five minutes, twenty-five marks — and to write the model answer and marking scheme, which I will also be applying myself. I would rather the question did not reward students for having memorised the labels in the literature (Nagelian bridge-law reduction, multiple realisability, the causal-exclusion argument, the familiar slogan that more is different); the course has covered all of these and I want a question that can only be answered by putting them to work on a case they have not seen. I would also like the whole thing to be self-contained, so that a student who has attended the lectures but remembers no author's name can still do well, and so that I — who am not a philosopher and will be marking the scripts — have a scheme that ties marks to moves I can actually see on the page.

The case I want to build the question around is the following, which I have adapted from a number of real situations without, I hope, describing any one of them exactly.

Consider a class C of systems. Each member is a box containing on the order of 10^23 classical particles, with a specified pair potential, obeying Newtonian dynamics; call the micro-level theory M. For each member there is a macro-level equation E — a single partial differential equation for a coarse-grained density field — containing one coefficient, κ. The following is established:

(i) E predicts the coarse-grained density field of every member of C to within experimental error, over a wide range of initial conditions.
(ii) κ has the same numerical value, to within measurement error, in every member of C. The members nevertheless differ from one another in pair potential, particle mass and density. The only features they share are: three spatial dimensions, conservation of particle number and momentum, and interactions of sufficiently short range.
(iii) There exists in the literature a derivation of E from M. The derivation requires an auxiliary assumption A, to the effect that a certain microscopic correlation function decays exponentially with a specified correlation length ξ. A is not itself derivable from M. A was arrived at by assuming E and working backwards, and every subsequent test of A has proceeded by using E to infer what A predicts; A has never been measured independently of E. A is known to hold only for members of C, and only in the regime in which E holds.
(iv) Computing κ by simulating M forward from microscopic initial conditions is possible in principle but requires computational effort that grows exponentially with the number of particles. There is no principled obstacle, only a practical one, and no proof that a feasible shortcut is impossible.

What I want from you is:

(a) The essay question itself, built on this vignette. It should force candidates to prise apart several claims that get run together when people say that E reduces to M — something like: E is nothing over and above M; E is derivable from M; E is explainable by M; E is causally inert given M; and the fact that κ takes the same value across C is explained by M. It should also make them say what, if anything, the provenance of assumption A does to the status of the derivation, what would have to be different for it to count as a reduction rather than a consistency proof or a piece of reverse engineering, and whether the computational intractability in (iv) is relevant to any of this. Word the question so that a candidate who simply summarises the standard positions, however accurately, scores poorly.

(b) A model answer of roughly 800 words, written as a strong candidate would write it under exam conditions — self-contained, no citations, arguing from the vignette rather than reporting what is in the literature.

(c) A marking scheme out of twenty-five, organised around five or six must-hit moves, with the marks attached to each and, for each, a line on how candidates typically get it wrong. Please include a short note on what a competent-but-second-class answer looks like: the one that deploys all the right vocabulary about the vignette without ever letting it bite.
