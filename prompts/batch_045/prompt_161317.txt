hey — small reading group, we're doing the teletransporter chapter of Reasons and Persons next week and I want to open the session with a poem instead of another twenty minutes of "but is it really you". I've also read every standard rationalist-blog post on fission and they all end in the same shrug, so please dont give me that shrug in verse.

could you write me a pantoum — you know, the form where lines 2 and 4 of each quatrain come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next one, and the last stanza reaches back to the opening. five or six quatrains, 24 lines max, that's a hard cap on the poem.

heres the part I actually care about: make the returning lines do the philosophical work. a line that comes back in a later stanza, in a new setting, *is* relation R — continuity without identity — so I want each repeated line to come back slightly altered the second or third time (one word swapped, one dropped), so the reader can feel connectedness as a matter of degree while nothing gets a clean one-one answer. and the closing line should be the poem's opening line, or as near to it as you can get after all that drift. that landing should be the reductionist point, not a slogan about it.

accuracy requirements, because people in this group know the material:

- get the branch case right. the scanner misfires, the Earth body isn't destroyed, now there are two of them. identity can't be what matters here because identity is one-one and this relation is not — so whatever matters has to be something else (call it R, psychological continuity/connectedness with any cause), and it holds twice over.
- my own tweak, which I want in there as well: a *chance* duplicate. a body on Mars that just happens to be arranged the way you were at the moment of the scan, produced by a fluke, no causal chain from the scanner at all. on the letter of a view with an "any cause" clause, that thing is as much your survival as the replica is. I want the poem to sit in that discomfort instead of resolving it — half my group thinks the clause is fine and half thinks it's a reductio of R.
- first person, the person stepping into the booth. no eschatology, nobody gets excited about living forever, and please no "is it really you?" hand-wringing.

after the poem, one short note, 60 words maximum: separate what Parfit genuinely commits to from what's actually contested among people who know this literature (the "any cause" clause, whether the chance duplicate is a feature or a reductio, whether R is even the right relation to care about), and add one sentence on where your poem leans on a reading that isn't the only one available. american english, and it's fine if it's rough — I'd rather hand the group a real poem than a tidy one.
