I have spent about a decade arguing about whether corruption is a universal category or a Western export — in comments on development blogs, in the anthropologists' replies to the transparency NGOs, and in my own work on governance indicators — and the argument has not moved by a millimetre in that time. One side says roughly this: an official who takes a payment to decide a case in someone's favour, or a politician who routes contracts and jobs to his clients, is using a public role for private benefit, and the outcome is worse government by anyone's measure; that the participants describe the transfer as reciprocity, kinship duty, or gift-exchange is just what the beneficiaries always say. The other side says roughly this: the impersonal official who applies rules without regard to who is standing in front of him is a recent and local achievement; the gift and the patron-client tie are doing the work that contracts, credit, insurance, and professional networks do in rich countries; and to call them corrupt is to define the question in favour of one moral order.

My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but three stacked under a single word, and that I do not know which of them is load-bearing. First, a dispute about the concept: whether corruption is identified by form — a transfer, a rule breached, an office held — or by function, i.e. by whether the good in question gets allocated on a criterion the rules do not recognise. Second, a hidden empirical question about the counterfactual: we almost never observe clientelism except alongside weak state capacity, so nobody can tell whether the patron network is a substitute for impersonal administration or a cause of its absence, and each side quietly assumes a different answer to that when it predicts what would happen if you banned the practice tomorrow. Third, a dispute about which cases are data: I suspect what I would call a legibility effect, where our intuitions and our indices track how auditable an exchange is, so that a gift economy scores worse than a documented, legal equivalent — a campaign contribution, a legacy admission, a favour to a former colleague — even when the relational structure is the same.

I tried tabooing the word corruption in one of these threads and it did not help, which I take as weak evidence against the purely verbal diagnosis. So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and one that engages what each side actually claims rather than surveying positions. Is this a verbal dispute, a difference about which cases count as evidence, or bedrock disagreement about what an office is for? A test I have been toying with: hold the relational structure fixed — who owes what to whom, what happens to the person who does not pay, how the official would otherwise have decided — and re-describe it in the vocabulary of something legal and documented in a rich democracy; if the re-description still reads as corruption, the cultural defence was cosmetic, and if it does not, the difference was one of form. Does that test actually separate the cases, or does each side reject its inputs in a way that tells us something? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a within-country case where an anti-clientelist reform worked and we can see what replaced the network, a preregistered prediction that discriminates substitute from cause, a change of question — or should I expect no progress, and why?
