You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of aesthetics and the philosophy of emotion, with a working command of the literature on our appetite for painful art. I am drafting a paper and, before I read any more proposed solutions, I want a specification of the problem rather than another solution to it. Please think this through carefully and in stages: begin with a short section (150 words or so) listing the candidate requirements you considered and then rejected, with a one-line reason for each rejection, and only then give me the final list. Do not give me a literature review, a bibliography, or citations of any kind — I have library access, and I do not want you guessing at references. Refer to existing approaches only by their core move (the conversion picture, the arousal-and-safety picture, the meta-response picture, the control picture, the being-moved-by-others picture) rather than by author, title, or date.

The domain is the paradox of tragedy and its neighbours: the fact that we seek out, sit through, rate highly, and return to artworks whose affective profile is one we avoid in ordinary life. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of this phenomenon must meet. Take the theory's job to be threefold: to say what is being enjoyed when a sad work is enjoyed; to explain why an affective episode we avoid in life is sought in art; and to predict which painful works fail to be enjoyable and why. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp, and say what kind of theory it rules out.

I want the list tested against cases, including several I have not seen handled:

1. Wordless sadness — a slow adagio for strings, no text, no characters, no represented events, no propositions. Accounts that lean on concern for a fictional person, on the truth of what is represented, or on curiosity about how things turn out have nothing to grip here, and I want to know whether that counts against them or against the case.
2. The commissioned elegy — a composer sets to music the actual last voicemail your own dead parent left you. Critics call it a great work; you find it unbearable and never play it again. Same music, same sadness, no enjoyment, no return. What must a theory posit to accommodate this: a fact about the object of the emotion, or a fact about the subject's situation?
3. The found-footage reversal — you watch handheld footage of a woman being told her child has died; you are moved and you rate the thing highly; you are then told it is either staged by actors or genuine footage released by the family. Should a theory predict any difference in the enjoyment across those two descriptions? If not, say what that shows about how much work the fiction/non-fiction distinction is doing in this neighbourhood.
4. The bereaved listener — someone who loved sad music for decades cannot listen to any of it for two years after the death of their child, and then can again. Does this impose a state-dependent or diachronic requirement on a theory, or is variance across persons and times a job for empirical psychology rather than for the philosophical account?
5. Foreknowledge — assume, purely for the sake of the argument (I am stipulating this, not reporting a result), that audiences return to tragedies whose outcomes they know in detail and that the foreknowledge does not reduce reported enjoyment. What does that do to curiosity-based accounts of the neighbouring case of horror?
6. The tear-jerker — a manipulative, meretricious film that reliably produces tears and is also judged to be bad, where the enjoyment itself is part of what critics object to. A theory must be able to distinguish being moved from judging a work good, and must be able to say what the manipulative work gets wrong without having to say the audience is wrong to be moved.

I also want one requirement that I suspect is doing more work than it admits: that a theory give a mechanism rather than a redescription. Consider a hypothetical view I will call simulation-plus-safety, which says that sad art is a low-cost simulation of a real affective episode, run in a context of guaranteed safety, and that this is why we enjoy it. Is refusing that kind of explanation a genuine desideratum or merely a stylistic preference? Say which, and why.

Close with three things. First: which pair (or larger subset) of your requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, if any — that is the part I most want, because if the list cannot be met all at once, then the list itself is a result. Second: which single requirement knocks out the largest share of the existing options. Third: an optional compressed scorecard of the five view-types I named above against the list, no more than 200 words; it is the least important part of the task, so cut it if it would eat into the criteria.

On calibration: attach to each requirement a tag saying whether it is near-uncontroversial, contested but defensible, or your own stipulation, and add a short closing note (a sentence or two per item is fine) on where your confidence is lowest. Finally, push back where I deserve it: if my framing rests on a mistake — if the phenomenon is not really unitary and my umbrella notion of enjoyment is papering over two or three different successes, if one of my invented cases smuggles in its own answer, or if some apparent desideratum is really just a disguised commitment to one family of theories — say so plainly rather than building a tidy list on top of my confusion.
