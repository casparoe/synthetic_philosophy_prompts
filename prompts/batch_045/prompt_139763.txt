I write a column on science and ideas, and over the past few years I have sat through more panels, hearings, and documentary screenings about "alternative" history than I expected to when I took the job. I keep encountering the same maneuver, and I have never found a word for it. I would be grateful for your help.

The maneuver runs like this. A fringe account of the past opens by applying an unusually aggressive standard of evidence to the mainstream account — not to the mainstream's specific claims, but to the machinery that produced them. We only have copies of copies; scribes interpolated; radiocarbon dates have to be run through calibration curves; stratigraphy assumes the layers were never disturbed; the king-lists and chronologies were compiled by people with an interest in a tidy story. Every one of those points, taken alone, is something a working historian or archaeologist will concede without much fuss. Then — and this is the part I cannot name — having used that standard to clear the ground, the same speaker builds a positive replacement out of the very materials the standard has just discredited: the same late copies, the same king-lists, the same excavation reports, now treated as solid enough to underwrite a wholesale revision of the timeline.

Two instances, so the shape is clear. At a conference on revised chronologies, a speaker spent forty minutes arguing that the transmitted manuscripts behind our standard periodization are too corrupt, too late, and too ideologically edited to support any confident dating — then spent the next thirty reconstructing a compressed timeline from those same transmitted manuscripts, treating their internal numerology as a trustworthy record. At a screening I was asked to comment on, a presenter argued that the customary dating of a set of megalithic sites is guesswork, because our sources for them are late medieval compilations and local folklore, and folklore proves nothing; twenty minutes later he read that folklore and those compilations as a reliable memory of astronomical knowledge inherited from a vanished high civilization.

I have been calling this "credibility laundering" in my notes, but I would rather print a term a reader can look up, and I suspect my version is imprecise. So here is what I am actually after.

Does this maneuver have an established name in epistemology, argumentation theory, or the literature on demarcation? If the fit with the established concept is imperfect — I expect it is — please tell me which candidate comes closest, where exactly the thing I described comes apart from it, and whether one of them should be stretched to cover it or whether it deserves its own term.

Candidates that have occurred to me, none of which seems quite right: "selective skepticism" and the related notion of "pseudoskepticism," which as far as I can tell name a stance rather than a move within a single argument; the old trilemma about justification, where any support for a claim either asserts itself, circles back on itself, or regresses without end, which my speakers deploy against the mainstream but never against themselves; and the distinction between attacking a claim and attacking the source of a claim's support, which seems nearer the mark but is too broad, since a methodologist may legitimately impugn a method without owing anyone a rival reconstruction.

The carvings I most want: Is the essential feature the asymmetry, or is it the reuse of the discredited pipeline as data, or is it something about which direction the standard is allowed to travel? Does it matter that the aggressive standard is aimed at a field's procedures rather than at a person's credibility, or is that incidental? And where is the boundary — what separates my speakers from a legitimate skeptic who says in effect that our methods are too weak here, so we should withhold judgment on all sides, and from a mundane case of a researcher who simply holds her own favorite sources to a gentler standard than she holds everyone else's, the way we all do?

If no established concept fits, coin one, define it, and give me two or three boundary cases: one that clearly falls under it, one that clearly does not, and one that could be argued either way. Please write in American English, and keep it accessible to a general reader without turning it into a list of slogans.

Finally, since I need something I can paste into a style sheet: end your reply, on the very last line and as the only thing on it, with \answer{...} enclosing the single best label — the established term if there is one, or your coined term if there is not — as a short phrase of no more than five words.
