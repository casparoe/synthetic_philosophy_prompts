Hi — I'm putting together something short and silly for our department's reading-group newsletter (we've been grinding through the Nyāya material on pramāṇas this term) and I'd like you to write it.

What I want is a deadpan parody of a quality-assurance document: a single page, 600–700 words, numbered clauses with tick-boxes and fill-in lines, from a university office — the Office for Epistemic Assurance, or whatever sounds suitably grim — that has taken on the job of certifying which of a researcher's cognitions (pramā) were actually produced by a working knowledge-source (pramāṇa), and rejecting the ones that weren't.

The target is the Nyāya line that a cognition doesn't come stamped with its own validity: its validity has to be made out by something else — a further, reflective cognition (anuvyavasāya), paradigmatically an inference, or the fact that the cognition issues in successful action — the parataḥ view, as against the Mīmāṃsā svataḥ view that validity is intrinsic. (If I've got the school labels muddled just quietly fix them, but keep hold of the target: the certification regress, and the places Nyāya actually tries to stop it.) The joke is that the Office has turned this into paperwork, that the paperwork has exactly one real problem, which is the regress, and that the Office closes it by administrative fiat in a way that concedes the very thing it officially denies.

Things I want in there, all of them real features of the view rather than inventions:

- A declaration section: tick one of the four recognised pramāṇas — pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, śabda. Claims sourced to arthāpatti or anupalabdhi are rejected with a printed reason that is recognisably Nyāya's own: roughly, that these aren't seperate sources and get absorbed into inference or into perception of an absence.
- An inference annexe: you have to write out all five members — pratijñā, hetu, udāharaṇa, upanaya, nigamana — state the vyāpti, and supply a positive and a negative example (sapakṣa, vipakṣa). The Office should be entirely unfazed by how redundant this is, with a form-field for each member even where two lines would do the job.
- A hetvābhāsa self-assessment checklist covering the standard five: savyabhicāra / anaikāntika (the hetu that wanders into the vipakṣa), viruddha, asiddha (sādhyasama), satpratipakṣa (prakaraṇasama), bādhita (kālātīta). Use the Sanskrit labels where they do work, but each clause should still be intelligible to someone who doesn't read Sanskrit.
- A note on tarka: hypothetical reasoning may be used to dispel doubt but cannot on its own establish a claim, so grant proposals resting on it are returned unread. This should be one of the funniest bits precisely because it's completely correct.
- An upamāna box asking you to name the comparison sentence and the remembered object — the gavaya case is fine to use.

And the invented bit, where you can go to town: the Office's rule for how deep the certification goes. Something like — a cognition is assured only if a second-order cognition certifies it; that second-order cognition needs a third; and the handbook terminates the series at a fixed level, either by a clause that visibly smuggles back the intrinsic-validity view it rejects, or by an exemption for the Office's own cognitions, or by an appeal to the lamp that lights both itself and other things. Whichever you pick, make it read like an administrative fix rather than a philosophical argument. Then a final clause, still deadpan, extending the exemption to any cognition whose object is the assurance scheme itself.

Two constraints so it doesn't get the epistemology wrong. Nyāya is a reliabilist, broadly externalist picture: pramā is true cognition generated by a functioning pramāṇa, and the subject needn't have reasons at hand or introspective access to the source. So the Office must not start demanding certainty, or articulate reasons, or anything Cartesian; the comedy has to come from the audit structure and the regress, not from quietly converting Nyāya into internalism. Equally, don't make the Office thick — its rules should be recognisably Nyāya's own commitments pushed one bureaucratic step further, so that someone who's done the reading groans because it's fair rather than groans because it's a strawman.

Keep it deadpan all the way through — no winking, no asides telling us it's a joke, no puns on Sanskrit terms. British English. And ideally it lands the regress so the reader feels the bite rather than just sees it described; that would be definately ideal.
