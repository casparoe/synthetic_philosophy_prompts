I work as a backend engineer and have been reading about decision theory on my own — mostly blog posts, plus one textbook chapter a friend lent me — so please forgive me if I mangle the vocabulary. What I keep coming back to is the argument I've seen described as the Dutch book or money-pump argument: the claim that there is something close to a proof that any agent whose preferences cannot be represented as maximizing expected utility can be made to lose money no matter how the world turns out, and that this is what makes the axioms requirements of rationality rather than matters of taste. I find it persuasive, but it keeps reading to me like an empirical claim about my bank balance, so I've been trying to take it literally. I audited my own choices and found two things that look like violations. First, I reliably show the Allais pattern: given the choice between a guaranteed million and a gamble with a slightly better chance at five million, I take the guarantee; but when both options are shifted down to long shots, I take the gamble with the bigger prize. Second, I am simply risk-averse — I would decline a coin flip that paid me $110 on heads and cost me $100 on tails, and I'd decline it at any stake large enough to matter to me.

So my first question is quantitative: for an agent with my exact preference pattern, is there a determinate worst-case loss that an ideally informed bookie could extract from me — a number I could compute and budget for, the way one budgets for a subscription, as the annual price of my incoherence? When I try to construct such a bookie myself I get stuck, and I think the sticking point is an extra assumption the argument seems to need in the Allais case that it does not need in the case of plain intransitivity. Something like this: if I decline a trade at the first node of a sequence, I have to go on evaluating what remains exactly as I would have evaluated it before the offer was made, rather than treating the fact that I was offered it — and what I did about it — as a change in my situation. I can't state that assumption cleanly enough to decide whether I grant it, and I'd like to.

My proposed fix, which I've started calling the "single-shot policy" and which I'd like to build into a budgeting app I hack on at weekends, is this: once a week I sit down, write out a ranking over every prospect I might realistically face, refuse all bets and trades for the rest of the week, and never re-deliberate. The thought is that if the pump works by getting me to revisit a decision in a new context, then a policy of never revisiting blocks it, and I get to keep my risk aversion intact. A friend of mine who works in quant trading says this misses the point entirely and that I am confusing a claim about my wallet with a claim about my preferences. Is he right? If the policy really does block the pump, does that mean I have thereby satisfied what expected utility theory demands of me, or only that I have made myself unexploitable while leaving the incoherence exactly where it was? And separately: is being risk-averse one of the things the argument is objecting to in the first place? I have no easy access to a university library, so I'd rather have the reasoning — including the questionable step in the argument — spelled out than be pointed at a paper I can't read.
