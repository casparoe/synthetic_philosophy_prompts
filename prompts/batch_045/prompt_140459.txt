I sit on the allocation committee for a small private fund that has roughly twelve researcher-years of philosophy-of-science money to give away over the next three years, and we have provisionally decided to spend most of it on the scientific realism debate and its neighborhood rather than spreading it thin. What I need from you is a defended agenda, not a survey. Our end is not "more work on realism" for its own sake: in fifteen years we would like to be able to hand a working physicist, or a person writing a grant proposal about dark matter or the early universe, a reasoned answer to the question of how much hedging is appropriate when they describe unobservable posits as real. That is the downstream use against which I want the ranking calibrated, and it means I care more about questions whose answers would visibly change scientific self-presentation and scientific argument than about questions that only reclassify the same old positions.

Two half-formed thoughts of my own are pushing me around, and I would like you to take them seriously enough to either use or dismantle. The first is what I have been calling the absorption problem: the pessimistic meta-induction seems to me to draw its sample from a graveyard whose gatekeeper is the very distinction under dispute. Histories of science are full of successful theoretical commitments that were not discarded but quietly absorbed — conservation principles, periodicity, the combinatorial structure of heredity — and almost nobody tabulates those alongside the ethers and the humours. If the real question is a base rate of retention across historically successful theories, then the honest version of the induction needs a selection criterion fixed in advance of knowing which of our current posits survive, and I suspect the dead and the retained cluster differently in a way that is diagnostic rather than an accident. The second thought is that both sides of the debate are surprisingly unwilling to make advance wagers about the next big theory change: what a successor to our current fundamental physics will retain, discard, or reinterpret. A realism debate that generated checkable expectations about the shape of that transition would, in my view, earn its keep in a way that another round of "but the electron" does not.

So: given twelve researcher-years, three years, and that downstream target, which four or five lines of work would you fund, in what order, and why? For each, tell me what it is upstream of — which other open questions in the area cannot be settled or even sharpened until it is — how tractable it is for philosophers working without new empirical or archival data, how neglected it is relative to its leverage, and what specific development within the next five years or so (a result, a shift in what physicists say, a conceptual clarification achieved or shown impossible) would move it up or down your list. I would also like one line of work that you think is currently over-funded by attention and should be deliberately passed over, with the reason it nonetheless keeps attracting effort, and at least one question you would add to the agenda that almost nobody is asking. Please make the dependency structure among the items explicit rather than leaving it implicit in the prose, and say plainly where you are trading importance for tractability, since with a three-year horizon that trade is the one I most need to see.
