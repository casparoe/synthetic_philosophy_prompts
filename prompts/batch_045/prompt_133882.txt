I write a column on science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I am finishing a piece about how large philanthropic funders actually reason once they openly admit that they do not know which moral theory is true. The foundation I have been given access to (names changed, at its request) allocates a flagship envelope of about $450m over five years through a Committee on Normative Uncertainty. The committee works with three moral views and the credences it attaches to them: an aggregative consequentialism that simply totals welfare (0.45); a prioritarian-cum-egalitarian view that weights benefits to the worst off and to the most unequal places (0.35); and a deontological view with side-constraints plus special obligations arising from the foundation's own past commitments (0.20). For each shortlist its staff score every option on each view on that view's own scale, with doing nothing set to zero, and then rescale so that each view's own favourite option on that shortlist counts as 100. The committee is explicit that the resulting numbers are shares of a view's own best available outcome rather than inter-theoretically comparable units, and it has said it dislikes the alternative of normalising by each view's variance across the shortlist.

Here is the track record I have reconstructed from the minutes. In 2019 all three views ranked the same option first and the committee funded it without comment. In 2020 an option requiring non-disclosure to participants was struck from the shortlist before any scoring, because the deontological view treats it as impermissible; the committee has never traded a constraint off against gains on the other views, and nothing in what follows involves an impermissible option. In 2021 the shortlist was three options, scored as follows: P at (100, 8, 4) — very cheap, very large aggregate return, but routed around local health systems and doing almost nothing for the worst off; Q at (15, 100, 20); R at (25, 40, 100), the deontological view's favourite and middling on the other two. The committee funded R. A trustee proposed a floor rule — never fund anything scoring below 25 on any view held at 0.20 or more — and the minutes record that it was considered and not adopted, without giving a reason. In 2022 there were two finalists, S at (100, 30, 31) and T at (35, 100, 100); the committee split the envelope roughly 60/40 in favour of S rather than picking a winner, saying that partial exposure to both beat a full bet on either. In 2023 a late addition to a shortlist — far and away the deontological view's favourite, but near zero on the other two — was scored and then set aside as outside the foundation's mission; a staffer noted that including it in the spreadsheet had dragged every other option's deontological score down by roughly a third, and the chair ruled that options are to be scored against the menu the committee is actually choosing from, not against options set aside as unfundable. The committee then chose as it had been inclined to choose anyway.

The decision now in front of them has three candidates. X is a scale-up of proven maternal and child health interventions delivered through national ministries: raw scores (100, 85, 72). Z delivers the same package through the community health worker and local NGO network the foundation committed to in its 2018 charter, with unconditional cash transfers added: (70, 70, 95). W is a ten-year programme of reparations and care for a population harmed by a coercion scandal in a programme the foundation inherited from a predecessor entity: (5, 10, 400). W is lawful and inside the mission, but the board is expected to reject it, since it would absorb a large share of the envelope for what the first two views count as very little welfare gain. With W on the shortlist the staff's percentages read X (100, 100, 18), Z (70, 82, 24), W (5, 12, 100); before W was added last month the same raw scores read X (100, 100, 76) and Z (70, 82, 100). X's unit costs blow up unless it receives at least about three quarters of the envelope, so at most roughly a fifth can be carved out of it — though the committee could of course abandon X and build a portfolio around Z instead.

Please take this on as a philosopher and decision theorist who advises institutional funders on choice under normative uncertainty, and work through it carefully before committing yourself. Start by setting out the full menu of decision rules that could generate the five precedents — I do not want the first candidate that fits, I want the several that fit, including any that differ only in how they treat a mixed portfolio rather than a single winner. (A game theorist I interviewed said this smells like a bargaining problem among the foundation's own moral views, and that the two classic solutions differ precisely over whether an option nobody is going to choose can change the answer between the ones that might be chosen; I could not tell whether he was being profound or merely technical, so please test the claim against these numbers rather than take it from him.) Then say what each surviving rule predicts here: X or Z, some split, and whether the presence of W on the shortlist should make any difference to it and why — that last point is the crux of my piece, because the same raw scores yield two different sets of percentages depending on whether W is counted. Give me your single best prediction, with the reasoning you would stand behind in print, including roughly how much money, if any, you expect W to receive. Then name the case or the re-run that would actually settle which rule they are running, and say what the chief of staff should put in the minutes so that the next such decision is not a coin flip. I do not need citations or suggestions for further reading, and I am not asking which moral view is true — I am asking which rule this committee is using, and what it commits them to.
