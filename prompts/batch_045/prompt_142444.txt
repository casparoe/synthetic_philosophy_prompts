I chair the ethics advisory board of a research institute that runs behavioural-science field experiments at roughly forty sites across twenty-five countries. We clear about forty protocols a year, usually within forty-eight hours, with a two-person review team (one behavioural scientist, one regional programme lead) and my signature at the end. Most of the objections we get from local partners are moral rather than legal, and we handle them ad hoc: sometimes we localize a protocol, sometimes we insist on the home standard, sometimes we quietly drop a site, and we could not afterwards say why we did any of these.

Please write the decision procedure my reviewers should run on every protocol, in the order they should run it. It has to be operationalized from the debate about cross-cultural variation in folk morality and what that variation does or does not show about moral objectivity. I have a two-page summary of that literature in front of me, so I do not need citations, studies, or reading recommendations; I need the reasoning made usable by reviewers with no philosophy training, no new data collection, and two days per protocol.

Three things the procedure has to get right, and I suspect they are where the conceptual work is. First, it must sort each disagreement into more than difference of degree: separate cases where the parties agree on the principle and disagree about the nonmoral facts or about which description of the act is the salient one; cases where a shared set of considerations is simply weighted differently; and cases where one side treats something as a moral consideration the other does not recognize at all. Tell me which distinctions survive simplification and which my reviewers can safely collapse. Second, it must say who counts as a source of the local norm. Our local partners and in-country ethics reviewers are usually urban, university-trained and grant-funded, and I worry that the sampling problem our summary describes simply reappears one level down, so that deference to them can mistake a local elite's view for the local view. Third, it must include a reverse test: for any norm we are willing to relax abroad, the reviewer must record in advance what finding at another site would make us revise the home standard. If a norm cannot be given such a test, I want to know whether we are applying a principle or merely exporting one, and what the procedure should do then.

Give me the stop rules: where it returns a no and drops the site, where it clears the protocol as localized, and where it stops and hands the decision to me because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

Then run it on three protocols. One: a standard deception design in which participants are misled about how a sum of money was split, where the local partner says the debriefing will cause the participants lasting shame in a way the home site would not experience. Two: a consent procedure where the in-country reviewer asks that a household head's permission substitute for written individual consent, at two sites with quite different kinship structures. Three: payment for participation, which at one site is read as a wage and at another as a gift, with the two readings carrying opposite obligations. At least one of these should yield an answer I will not like, and I want you to say which and why. Close with an honest statement of what this procedure cannot do.
