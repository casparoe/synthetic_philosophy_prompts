Here's my opening move. I'll grant you the counterfactual-mirroring account of implementation for the sake of argument. Now look at a single forward pass of a big neural net. Inside it there's a whole zoo of nested causal structures that each mirror some mind-ish computation: the full network, the network with its attention heads ablated, one layer, a bundle of features, even some odd little circuit somebody isolated with a probing classifier. On your account a lot of these look like genuine implementations, so one forward pass hosts a large, partly overlapping population of candidate subjects, and it's unclear whether their moral claims add up or whether the biggest one swallows the smaller ones. I actually want to hand you a way out, because I think it's the best answer available to you — I'll call it fractional sharing. A system has a fixed budget of moral significance, set by how much causally integrated structure it contains, and every implementation it hosts gets a slice of that budget; so the wall that trivially implements everything gets a vanishing slice per implementation, while a brain keeps nearly all of its own. That defuses the overpopulation problem and the triviality problem in one move. So: do you take the deal, and if not, why not — and what do you say about the zoo instead?
