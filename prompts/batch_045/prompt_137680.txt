My half-formed idea is this. In his 1950 Mind paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Turing declines to answer the question of whether machines can think, saying we should instead replace it by another that is closely related and expressed in relatively unambiguous words, and the replacement he offers is a test: a performance criterion, synchronic, scored by an interrogator. But the closing section of that same paper stops behaving like test design and turns into an engineering and pedagogy program: instead of writing a program that simulates the adult mind — which he thinks would require knowing, in unwieldy detail, how an adult brain actually works — we should write one that simulates a child's mind and then educate it, by reward and punishment, leaning on the fact that we all have rough practical knowledge of what teaching a child is like. So the paper seems to me to contain two criteria rather than one: a performance criterion and a developmental one, the second being a modal or second-order property, a matter of what the system could be taught, not what it can currently do. And there is a further wrinkle I can't let go of: an education needs a teacher, and the teacher must already know what to teach, in what order, and when to reward — which looks uncomfortably like the very theory of the mind that the child-machine route was supposed to let us do without. Call that the curriculum bottleneck. A friend of mine who works in industry machine learning tells me Turing's talk of education is just 1950 for we'll figure out the training method later, with no bearing on his criterion; I think it is doing normative work, and I want to find out which of us is right.
