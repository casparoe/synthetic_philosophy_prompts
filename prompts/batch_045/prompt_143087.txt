I'm presenting next week in a small graduate reading group on the foundations of welfare economics — mostly philosophers plus one health economist — and I've been assigned the theorem usually associated with Harsanyi's name that appears to derive a utilitarian social utility function from the axioms of expected utility theory. I can follow each algebraic step when it's written out, but I lose the thread of why the thing should hold at all, so what I'd most like is a gut-level account of the mechanism: what is doing the real work, and why does a set of conditions that look like they're about coherence under risk end up saying anything about how to aggregate different people's interests? I'd also be grateful if you could build the intuition around a two-person, two-outcome example I could put on a board, rather than around the general notation.

Here is the version I've been working from, which you should correct if it's garbled. There are n individuals, each with preferences over lotteries on a common set of social outcomes satisfying the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each is represented by a utility function unique up to positive affine transformation. Society also has preferences over lotteries on those outcomes, also satisfying the vNM axioms, and so represented by a social utility function with the same uniqueness property. Add a Pareto-style condition linking the two, and the conclusion is that the social utility function is an affine combination of the individual ones — a weighted sum plus a constant — so that social ranking proceeds by a weighted sum of individual utilities. Three things about this I cannot see clearly. First, I've seen the Pareto condition stated in at least two strengths, one requiring only that society be indifferent when everyone is indifferent, another requiring the full weak-Pareto condition including strict preference; I can't tell which version buys which part of the conclusion, whether either is needed for the weights to be non-negative rather than possibly negative, and what a negative weight would even mean — a person whose interests count against themselves? Second, and this is the part that feels like sleight of hand, I don't understand why the vNM axioms applied to the *social* ordering should constrain aggregation across persons. My only glimmer is the thought that a lottery giving the good outcome to me rather than to you is formally the same kind of object as a risky prospect facing a single person who does not know which of us she will turn out to be, so that expected-utility rationality for society means something like: no aversion to inequality across the persons-as-states. Is that the whole intuition, or is there a further step I'm missing about why additivity across states turns into additivity across people?

Two challenges from the group that nobody settled, which I'd like adjudicated as well. One member asked what happens if the impartial observer behind the veil does not assign equal probabilities to being each person — she suggested weighting by length of life, so that someone who will live to ninety gets a larger share of the probability than someone who will die at twenty, which yields a weighted sum in which benefiting the long-lived counts for more. Is that a consequence the framework simply has to accept, is there an argument from inside the framework for equal probabilities over persons as opposed to over person-years or person-slices, and does the aggregation theorem itself say anything here or is all of this happening in the equiprobability interpretation that gets bolted onto the theorem afterwards? A second member said that behind the veil she would be risk-averse — she would accept a lower expected level of welfare in exchange for reducing the chance of ending up as the worst-off person — and claimed this shows the theorem reaches utilitarianism only by way of a hidden extra assumption, with prioritarianism as the real upshot. I have a strong feeling this is confused, but I can't locate the confusion: is it a violation of the expected-utility hypothesis as applied to the social ordering, a piece of moral inequality aversion that the formalism tempts us into misdescribing as risk aversion about our own fate, or something that dissolves once you distinguish ex ante from ex post aggregation? And if it is a genuine departure, does it refute the theorem or merely relocate the disagreement onto one of its hypotheses? Relatedly, much is made in the readings of Harsanyi's result as an escape from Arrow's theorem, and I can't tell whether the escape is substantive — genuinely richer information, because cardinal and interpersonally calibrated utilities are in play — or merely verbal, a change of formalism that dissolves the impossibility rather than answering it. Please be explicit about where the theorem is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and about which of its assumptions our group's objections would actually have to dislodge.
