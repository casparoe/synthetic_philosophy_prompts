I'm in the first year of a philosophy PhD, and in three weeks I'm giving a twenty-minute works-in-progress talk on something I've started calling hermetic performatives: cases in which a speaker has the appropriate standing, uses the standard formula, and means every word of it, but the utterance reaches no one. A department chair who, alone in her office, utters the formula by which a committee she is entitled to dissolve is dissolved. A man who promises to repay his brother while his brother is fast asleep. An officiant who pronounces a couple married in a language neither of them speaks. My supervisor heard the examples, said only that the interesting question is what uptake is a condition on, and then had to run to a faculty meeting; when I floated the cases at a reading group, one of the third-years said they aren't performatives at all, which struck me as missing the point entirely. So I've been working out the upshot on my own, and I've arrived at a position I would like checked before I commit to it in front of the department.

The position is this. Austin's headline claim is that in an explicit performative the saying is the doing: the utterance is not a report of an act but the act itself, which is why it admits of happiness and unhappiness rather than truth and falsity. But if that is right, then it seems to me that the only thing standing between a speaker and a performed act is whatever is internal to the speaker: that she has the standing, that she is deploying the formula in earnest, that she intends what the formula purports. Everything Austin sorts under the heading of misfire — wrong person, wrong circumstances, botched or incomplete execution — looks, on inspection, like a case in which an audience would decline to take the speaker to have meant it, and that is just a failure of uptake. And uptake, on the standard telling, is a matter of what the utterance brings about in a hearer, which makes it a perlocutionary effect; perlocutionary effects are supposed to be extrinsic to the act itself, in the way that causing someone to believe something is no part of asserting it. So misfires are not a distinct species of illocutionary failure at all. They are failures to produce an effect downstream of the act, and the only failure that is genuinely illocutionary is insincerity.

If that is right, Austin's two-way taxonomy — misfire versus abuse, the act void versus the act hollow — collapses into a single graded notion of defectiveness, which I have been tempted to formalise as the proportion of an act's intended audience response that the speaker actually secures. On that picture a marriage solemnised by an officiant with no licence is simply a less successful version of the same kind of thing as a promise made with no intention of keeping it, differing only in degree. My hermetic cases seem to pull the other way, though: it feels obvious that the man who promises over his sleeping brother has promised, and owes the money, even though nothing was taken up by anybody. So here is what I want to know. Is there really anything left for the distinction between a misfire and an abuse to do, or is it an artefact of the order in which Austin happened to work through his examples, best replaced by one graded scale? And if uptake turns out not to be a condition on illocutionary success, as the hermetic cases suggest, what is supposed to stop the whole apparatus of felicity conditions from shrinking to conditions on the speaker's intentions alone?
