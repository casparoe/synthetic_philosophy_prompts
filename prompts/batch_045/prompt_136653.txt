My mate Dan has got this thing where he goes round labelling bits of social life "a convention" or "not a convention". He got into it after reading some philosopher a while back — Lewis, I think the name was — and he's been handing down verdicts on whatever example anyone puts to him for months. His track record so far:

- driving on the left: convention
- shaking hands when you meet someone: convention
- everyone in his office starting emails "Hi [name]" rather than "Dear [name]": convention
- at his parkrun everyone lines up on the left-hand side of the path, which apparently started because one week a marshal waved people over there and after that they just kept doing it: convention, and he said something like that's how these things get going, nobody has to sit down and agree to anything
- not murdering people: not a convention ("that's just right and wrong")
- wearing a seatbelt: not a convention ("nothing to do with what anyone else is doing")
- stopping at red lights: not a convention ("there's a fine and a camera behind it, that's a rule")
- a warm-up thing at his running club that about half the members do: not a convention, "not yet anyway, it's got to be near enough everyone"

The line he keeps repeating is that a convention is something near enough everyone does, near enough everyone expects everyone else to do, and would keep doing for that reason alone. But he also keeps using the word "arbitrary", and I've never been able to work out what work it's actually doing there.

So here's the case I want to put to him. Our building has bookable meeting rooms, and when the booking system went in about three years ago it came set to a default slot length of 25 minutes. Nobody chose 25, nobody agreed to it, it was just what the thing offered, and everyone booked whatever it offered. Now it's completely universal — everybody books in 25-minute blocks, and you'd be thought a bit odd, or a bit rude, if you booked a 30. Here's the bit I think matters: one of the office managers actually did the sums, and 30-minute slots would fit the working day better, you'd get noticeably more usable time out of the same rooms, and switching costs nothing except that it only works if everyone does it on the same day. Put everyone in a room and take a vote and I reckon it'd be unanimous for 30. But no one wants to be the only one doing it, so we're all still on 25. There's also a mildly bitchy Slack emoji people drop on a booking when someone's gone rogue and taken a 30 — but there's no actual rule, nothing in anyone's contract, no one's ever really been pulled up on it.

Would Dan call the 25-minute thing a convention? More usefully: what's the rule he's actually running, and in particular what does "arbitrary" have to mean for all eight of his earlier verdicts to hang together? Is there a different rule that fits those eight just as well but comes out the other way on this one? Which do you think he'd go with, and which should he go with if he wants to be consistent with himself? Does it change anything that the 25 minutes came from a default nobody picked, or that there's now a bit of social side-eye holding it up? And what's the one further case I should throw at him that would actually tell the two rules apart — I thought about that keyboard layout story everyone knows, but it feels muddy to me and I can't say why, so something cleaner if you've got it.
