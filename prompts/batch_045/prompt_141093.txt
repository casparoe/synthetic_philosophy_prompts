I am writing about sphexishness — the digger wasp that leaves its paralyzed cricket at the threshold of the nest while it goes in to inspect, and which, if the cricket is nudged a few inches in its absence, drags it back to the threshold and inspects again, looping as often as the experimenter cares to nudge. Before I read theories of flexible agency, I want the specification: a criterion that separates mechanical rigidity from genuine flexibility, and I want it for one stated purpose — deciding whether a system's behavior counts as acting for reasons in the sense that would make it a candidate for responsibility-attributions, rather than a mere locus of causation.

Write the desiderata such a criterion must meet, each with the case or consideration behind it, and mark each as non-negotiable or as a preference of some camp. The cases I think it has to get right, at minimum:

1. the wasp's loop;
2. a jittered wasp — my term for the same animal with a noise source injected into its motor output, so that it always escapes the loop, but only because of the noise;
3. the clerk with the codebook — an imagined system holding a precomputed response for every situation it has ever been trained on, selected by an index of the current situation, which behaves impeccably across a billion perturbations and falls apart under one of a kind it has not been trained on;
4. a corvid that re-caches food after noticing it has been watched, and an expert driver who handles a novel skid and cannot say why;
5. sleepwalking, hypnotic suggestion, and Tourette's: flexible, situation-sensitive, apparently purposeful behavior that we resist attributing to the agent;
6. the scrupulous person who will not lie even when everyone else would — someone whose behavior is rigid in the very case where flexibility is on offer, and whom we want to count as more responsible, not less.

The distinctions I suspect it must not collapse: flexibility versus mere behavioral variability; rigidity versus reliability; acting for reasons versus merely being causally sensitive to circumstance; synchronic flexibility versus the capacity to reorganize over time; and flexibility located in the agent versus flexibility borrowed from a designer who anticipated the case. Constraints it should not contradict: adaptive behavior can arise from small circuits without anything like an explicit model; practiced skill becomes less verbally mediated, not more; a system's reach can exceed what its builders foresaw.

Two things I most want. First, if some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, say so and say which one I should relax — I want the list itself to be a result, not a wish list. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most exclusionary work, and whether it earns that role or is a prejudice of the reasons-responsiveness tradition dressed up as a desideratum. Note also whether a criterion adequate for responsibility-attributions would differ from one adequate for attributions of moral status or of consciousness, since I doubt the same list serves all three. A one-line scorecard for reasons-responsiveness views, hierarchical or mesh views, and enactivist or dynamical-systems views is fine, but keep it short; the criteria are what I am paying for.
