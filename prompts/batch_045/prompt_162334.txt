I set the following question for a closed-book midterm in a third-year metaphysics course. The question was worth 30 marks, and I have reproduced it below exactly as the students saw it. Underneath is one student's answer, transcribed verbatim, including its paragraph breaks. I have to return the script with a mark and marginal comments, and I would like your assessment of it before I write those.

The question

(a) [6 marks] State the principle of the identity of indiscernibles precisely, and distinguish it from the principle of the indiscernibility of identicals. Which of the two is the contested one, and why does it matter, when assessing an alleged counterexample, that they are distinct principles? Say in addition what the second-order quantifier in the identity of indiscernibles is standardly understood to range over, and why that restriction is imposed.

(b) [8 marks] Black's (1952) example is a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, half a mile in radius, whose centres are two miles apart. Explain how the example is supposed to bear on the identity of indiscernibles. Then set out what is usually taken to be the strongest reply available to a defender of the principle — the reply that appeals to the spheres' places, or to their relations to one another — and explain how the proponent of the example answers that reply.

(c) [8 marks] Now consider two variants of Black's world, each of which adds a third body:
(i) a third iron sphere of twice the radius, whose centre lies on the perpendicular bisector of the line joining the two original spheres' centres, at a distance of four miles from its midpoint;
(ii) the same third body, placed instead on the line through the two centres but beyond one of them, so that its centre is two miles from the nearer sphere's centre and four miles from the farther one's.
In each case, are the two original spheres indiscernible? State explicitly the general test you are applying, and show that it delivers the verdicts you give.

(d) [8 marks] Finally, suppose a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres in a space that is homogeneous but not isotropic: at every point there is a single distinguished spatial direction, as if space were filled throughout with a uniform field of parallel arrows, but there is no distinguished position and no distinguished sense of rotation about that direction. Case (i): the two spheres' centres lie on a line parallel to the distinguished direction. Case (ii): their centres lie on a line perpendicular to it. Are the spheres indiscernible in each case? Applying the same test as in (c), say what (c) and (d) together show about what it takes for two objects to be discernible.

The student's answer

(a) The identity of indiscernibles (PII) says: for any x and y, if x and y have all their properties in common, then x is identical with y. Formally, (∀x)(∀y)[(∀F)(Fx ↔ Fy) → x = y]. This must be distinguished from the indiscernibility of identicals, (∀x)(∀y)[x = y → (∀F)(Fx ↔ Fy)], which is Leibniz's Law and is not in dispute: we rely on it every time we infer from Fa and not-Fb that a is not b. PII is the converse, and it is the contested one. The two really are independent: a conditional does not entail its converse, and Leibniz's Law can hold in a world in which PII fails, namely a world containing two distinct things that are exactly alike. That is why a counterexample to PII does not touch Leibniz's Law, and why the defender of PII cannot help herself to the authority of Leibniz's Law.

The quantifier over F has to be restricted to qualitative properties. If it ranged over all properties whatsoever, then for any distinct a and b, a would have the property of being identical with a, which b lacks, and PII would come out trivially true — a logical truth that says nothing about identity. So the principle is to be read as claiming that no two distinct things share all their qualitative properties.

(b) In Black's world there is nothing to tell the spheres apart: same matter, shape, size, temperature, and, as far as the description goes, same causal role. If such a world is possible, it contains two things sharing all their properties, and PII is false. More cautiously, the example shows that PII is not a necessary truth, and shifts the burden to the defender, who must now argue that a perfectly symmetrical world is impossible — and it is hard to see what non-question-begging argument could establish that.

The strongest reply open to the defender is that the spheres do differ: either because each occupies a different place, or because A is two miles from B while B is two miles from A, and these are different facts. The proponent of the example answers as follows. In a universe containing nothing but the spheres, places cannot be identified independently of the spheres: the only spatial facts there are are distance relations, and those are perfectly symmetrical — each sphere is two miles from an iron sphere answering to exactly its own description. And if the defender reaches for absolute space, she only relocates the difficulty, since the two points of space the spheres occupy would then have to be discernible from one another, and by the same reasoning they are not; this is Leibniz's own shift argument against substantival space.

One further wrinkle is worth registering. The relational facts in Black's world are not after all the same facts: relations have argument places, and A occupies the first argument place in A is two miles from B, while B occupies the second. So the two spheres occupy different relational roles, and a principle quantifying only over monadic properties is blind to this. It is therefore natural to hold that PII must be stated so as to range over relations and their argument places — otherwise it is too weak to be of any interest — and that, stated in that way, the spheres satisfy the principle and the example misfires.

(c) The general test I am applying is this: two objects are indiscernible if and only if the world admits a non-trivial symmetry, that is, a transformation that preserves every qualitative feature of the world — distances, shapes, composition, and any distinguished places or directions — and maps one of the objects onto the other while leaving everything qualitatively as it was.

(i) Reflection in the plane that perpendicularly bisects the line joining the two centres maps one sphere onto the other. The third body's centre lies in that plane, so the third body is mapped onto itself, and every qualitative feature is preserved. The original spheres are therefore still indiscernible: adding a body does not help if the body is placed symmetrically.

(ii) Here the third body breaks the symmetry. One of the original spheres is two miles from its centre, the other four miles. No structure-preserving transformation maps one onto the other while leaving the third body as it is. The spheres are discernible: each lies at a different distance from an iron sphere of twice the radius.

(d) (i) Discernible. The field is part of the world's qualitative furniture, and one sphere lies in the distinguished direction from the other: one is downstream, the other upstream. Nothing in the world's structure interchanges them, since a reflection through the mid-plane, or a half-turn about an axis perpendicular to the field, would reverse the direction of the field, and that is not a symmetry of this world.

(ii) Discernible as well. The distinguished direction supplies a north–south axis, and the line joining the two centres supplies an east–west axis; together the two fix a frame in which one sphere is the eastern one and the other the western one. The half-turn about the field direction through the midpoint is accordingly not a symmetry, because it exchanges east and west.

So what (c) and (d) together show is this: what makes two objects discernible is not how much matter the world contains but whether its total qualitative structure contains an asymmetry that assigns each object its own role. Empty worlds are simply the limiting case in which no such asymmetry is available at all.
