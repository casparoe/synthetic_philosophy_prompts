Most scientists seem to treat it as a hard requirement on any successor theory that it give back the old theory's results in the region where the old theory worked — general relativity has to hand you Newtonian gravity in the slow, weak-field limit, quantum results have to shade into classical ones when things get big, and a referee will bounce a proposal that can't show the limit coming out. What does that practice presuppose? On it's face it looks like it needs two things: some theory-neutral way of saying what the old theory's successes even were, so that both theories can be scored against the same phenomena, and enough continuity in what the words mean that reproducing the old predictions is a well-defined ask. But if the meanings shift when the paradigm shifts, I can't see what the ask is an ask about — and yet scientists keep making it and it seems to work fine in practice.

Two wrinkles I want dealt with. First, recovery might just be a feature of one kind of transition rather than a requirement on all of them: in some episodes the old theory's central quantity gets deleted rather than recovered — phlogiston, the luminiferous ether — and nobody thinks that makes the replacement any less of an improvement. Second, imagine a field that swaps its mechanistic theory for a big fitted predictor that matches every old prediction to within noise but shares no vocabulary and no mechanism with it; it recovers the successes on the test above while explaining nothing, and I doubt that's what scientists think they're demanding.

So: reconstruct the implicit view of theories, meaning and progress that the recovery requirement fits with. Tell me which parts are strictly presupposed by the practice and which would sit just as happily under Kuhn-style meaning variance or under something more like structural realism. Say whether the practice is consistent with itself, given that the same textbooks call the old theory straightforwardly false, go on using it, and teach it to undergraduates before the new one. And say what would concretely have to change in peer review and in how we teach science if the requirement were dropped, or if incommensurability were taken seriously enough to act on.
