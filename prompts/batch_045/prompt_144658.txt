For about a year now I have had concrete reason to doubt my own reliability as a reasoner, and the doubt has turned out to be strangely self-applying, which is why I am writing. The medical details do not matter; what matters is the model of my situation that my neurologist and I arrived at, because I think it isolates the structure. Suppose a patient is given a drug that leaves first-order reasoning entirely intact while disabling the monitoring system that normally tracks whether a particular bit of reasoning went well. Such a patient reasons as accurately as she did before, but is no better than chance at telling, afterwards, which of her conclusions were the good ones. Her feelings of confidence become noise. Call it the Blind Monitor case. It is, near enough, my situation, and I want to know what rationality requires of the patient in it — because whatever it requires seems to require something that turns back on itself.

The argument I keep reconstruction-g myself into is this:

1. No-ignoring: for any proposition p, rationality requires proportioning credence in p to one's total evidence, and one's total evidence includes one's evidence about the reliability of the process by which one arrived at one's judgment about p.

2. Every process by which I form a judgment is fallible, and I have positive evidence of some nonzero chance of error on any given occasion: I have been wrong before, I am tired, there are conclusions I want to be true.

3. If one has evidence that a process has chance ε of having erred on this occasion, then, since conditional on error one's credence should be the agnostic value of one-half, one's credence should become (1−ε)·c + ε·(1/2). Every act of reflecting on one's own fallibility therefore moves one strictly toward the midpoint.

4. The credence that emerges from step 3 is itself the output of a fallible process, so steps 1 through 3 apply to it, and to their result, without end.

5. Iterating step 3 takes credence to one-half for every proposition whose negation is possible. A fully rational agent accordingly suspends judgment on every contingent matter, including the proposition that her own reasoning is untrustworthy.

6. Universal suspension is not a candidate for rationality: it cannot guide action, and in the Blind Monitor case it is not even available, since the patient cannot stop reasoning. So one of steps 1 through 5 is false.

Of the premises, step 1 is the one I am least willing to surrender; it looks to me close to what it means to take evidence seriously at all. I would therefore rather give up almost anything else. But I cannot locate the break, and every repair I attempt feels tailored to the conclusion.

Two things make it harder. First, in the Blind Monitor case the first-order judgment is by hypothesis exactly as reliable as it ever was; only my access to it has been damaged. Step 3 seems to misfire here, since the evidence of unreliability bears on whether my confidence indicates the truth, not on whether the conclusion is true — yet it also seems to misfire harmlessly, because I learn of the drug's effects by first-order reasoning, and so the doubt reinstates itself one level up. Second, consider an inverted variant: a patient whose monitor is not blind but reliably reversed, so that her feelings of confidence are perfect indicators of error. No-ignoring now appears to require inverting — where she feels certain, she should move her credence below one-half. But the inversion is itself reasoning performed under the same condition, and symmetry appears to demand a second inversion, and then either an oscillation with no fixed point or a collapse to one-half anyway: the skeptical result reached from the opposite direction. If the correct response to the reversed monitor is to invert exactly once and stop, I want to know what stops it, and whether whatever stops it also stops the regress in the original argument.

So: which step do you reject, and why did that step look sound? What does each of the other exits cost — in particular, what does the steadfast answer, that higher-order evidence defeats only when it identifies a specific error rather than a mere chance of one, have to pay in cases where the error is by construction unidentifiable? Does the Blind Monitor patient have any revision of her first-order credences to make, or only of her confidence, and are those genuinely different? And whichever way this goes, I seem to end up in a state I cannot tell is coherent: believing p while believing that my believing p is irrational. Tell me whether that state is possible, whether it is rational when it occurs, and whether the argument above shows it to be universal or shows nothing at all.
