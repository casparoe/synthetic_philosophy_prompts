I teach a seminar on the ethics of war pretty much every spring, and there's a shape of argument I keep running into in third-year papers — and, if I'm honest, in a lot of the published stuff too, and in my own thinking when I'm being sloppy — and I've never had a decent word for it. It goes like this. Everyone at the table will happily grant the big-picture claim: the war this soldier is fighting is an aggressive one, his side has no good reason for fighting it, the whole thing shouldn't be happening, what his country is doing is wrong. But then nothing whatsoever follows. He may keep fighting. He isn't to blame, because he couldn't have been expected to work out whether his government was lying to him. There's no expectation that he stop, or refuse, or desert, and no one proposes punishing him afterwards. And the people he is shooting at are supposed to treat him exactly as they would treat someone whose cause was just — same rules, same permissions, same restraints — and if they don't, they are the ones who've done something wrong. So the wrongness gets asserted loudly at one level and then goes completely inert at every level where taking it seriously would change what somebody may do or who ends up bearing a cost. I'm a philosopher by training, not a lawyer, and I'm deliberately not naming the two or three famous theses in this neighbourhood, because the moment I name them the seminar turns into a recitation of positions and we stop looking at the move itself.

The bit I really want a concept for, though, is a second wrinkle that I'm not even sure is the same phenomenon. When the excuse does get cashed out for something, it never seems to land on the person who is excused. He's excused because of his epistemic situation, because of what someone in his position could reasonably have been expected to figure out — and then that excuse somehow turns into a reason for the soldiers on the other side to accept more risk, to hold back, to add precautions, before they defend themselves or their country. So a claim that is supposed to be about him functions instead as a cost imposed on someody else entirely. It doesn't narrow what he may do, and it doesn't narrow what may be done to him; it just leaves the people he's attacking worse off. I've started calling it "the excused party's freebie" in my own notes, which is useless and probably misleading, since it suggests he's getting something out of it.

So: does this have an established name, and if it does, does it cover both cases or only one? I don't want a label I can put on a slide — my students can produce labels. What I want is the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, whether the inert-conclusion version and the migrating-cost version are really one thing or two, what the nearest established concepts are (names, not reading lists, I can find the reading myself) and precisely how this differs from each of them — I can think of a couple of nearby candidates that are about permission and blame rather than about this particular move, and another about threats that aren't anyone's fault, and none of them seems to cover it — and if there genuinely isn't anything established, say so plainly and give me a term, a definition, and two or three boundary cases I can argue about with my students. One of my colleagues in international law says the whole thing is just an artifact of how the law seperates the question of whether a war may be fought from the question of how it may be fought, but that strikes me as describing the setting rather than explaining why the conclusion evaporates the moment you try to use it.
