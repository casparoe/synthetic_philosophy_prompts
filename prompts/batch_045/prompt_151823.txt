ok so slightly niche setup but bear with me. I sit on the panel that hears academic-integrity cases in my department (I'm the grad-student member) and we've got one that's split us down the middle. A student was asked point-blank by the chair whether he'd run his essay through a paraphrasing tool. He said "I wrote every sentence myself" — true in the sense that he typed them out, since he fed his own draft to a rewriter and then edited the output — but he knew exactly what the chair would take him to be denying, and he's since admitted as much to a friend. One member of the panel, who's very literal-minded, keeps saying: the sentence he uttered encoded a proposition and that proposition is true, end of story, we can only hold him to what the words encode, everything past that is mind-reading. Someone else said that's just the conduit picture of communication and that Grice killed it decades ago. I don't think either of them has quite got it.

So here's the audit I want. Take the code model seriously for a minute — speaker has a thought, encodes it into a sentence, sends it down a channel, hearer decodes it, meaning is the encoded content — and lay out the mapping: what plays the role of the codebook, the encoder, the channel, the decoder, the noise. Then where the mapping breaks for a case like this one. I care about which disanalogies actually do work for the question of what someone can be held to, and which ones are just philosophers having fun.

Two things I keep going round in circles on, and I'd rather you dealt with them than with the general vibes:

1. A friend of mine (also a philosopher, annoyingly) says the code model can be repaired: just treat the Cooperative Principle and the maxims as part of the codebook, like a protocol layer sitting on top of grammar. Then implicatures get decoded like anything else and you don't need a separate inferential step. Does that repair work? My worry is that once the maxims are inside the code, implicatures stop being cancellable and non-detachable, so it isn't a repair at all, it's a different theory wearing the old one's clothes — but I can't make that tight. And isn't there a second problem, that the "codebook" would have to contain the entire context plus all the hearer's background beliefs?

2. The bit that's actually breaking my head: Grice's analysis of speaker meaning, as I remember it, needs the speaker to intend the hearer to *recognize* the intention behind the utterance. But this student specifically did not want the chair to recognize anything; he wanted to get away with it. Call these "stealth implicatures," I'm inventing the label. On Grice's own definition, is the misleading content even something he *meant*? Because if it isn't, the literalist on my panel has a weirdly strong argument: you can't hold him to what he meant, since meaning it would require owning up to it. If it is, say how, and say what has to give in the account — drop the recognition requirement? then what happens to the contrast with natural meaning, the spots-mean-measles sort of case? or go hearer-side and make implicature a matter of what a reasonable hearer would infer?

Then the real question: does the conclusion the literalist wants — that people are answerable only for encoded content — survive any of this? And if not, tell me what the better analogy is, because "conduit" is clearly wrong and "inference" isn't an analogy.
