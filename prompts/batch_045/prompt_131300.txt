I'm doing a metaphysics module and I've got myself into an argument I can't get out of, mostly my own fault. I was trying to work out whether the old Leibniz-ish principle that distinct things must differ somehow survives that famous example of a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, perfectly smooth, same size, same temperature, two miles apart in an otherwise completely empty homogeneous Euclidean space. Call them Castor and Pollux, though the names don't pick out one rather than the other. Here is what I ended up with, and I think it proves too much but I can't see where.

(1) If x and y are numerically distinct then there is some property that x has and y lacks. (2) I can't let that include properties like being identical with Castor or being two miles from Pollux, because then (1) is true but totally empty: any two things get separated by some such property, so the principle stops saying anything. So (1) has to be about qualitative properties only, ones you could characterise without already pointing at a particular individual. (3) In the two-sphere world, rotate everything by 180 degrees about the axis through the midpoint, perpendicular to the line joining the spheres: the spheres swap places and the world you get is exactly the world you started with, so this is a symmetry of the whole set-up. (4) So no qualitative property distinguishes Castor from Pollux — anything true of one, qualitatively speaking, is true of the other. (5) So by (1) they are identical, which can't be, since each is two miles from the other and nothing is two miles from itself. (6) So (1) is false: their being two is a brute primitive numerical difference, not a difference in any quality at all. My supervisor says this just means there are primitive thisnesses and I should get used to it.

What makes me suspicious is what happens when I fiddle with the set-up. If I drop a third, smaller sphere in exactly at the midpoint, the rotation still fixes that one and still swaps Castor and Pollux, so the symmetry survives and by (3) and (4) the big spheres are still indiscernible and (1) still fails. But if I nudge the little sphere a millimetre to one side, the symmetry breaks, one big sphere is now nearer to it than the other, and all of a sudden (1) is true and the two spheres are safe. So on my argument whether these two things are one thing or two seems to depend on where an unrelated third object happens to be sitting, which has to be wrong. And it gets worse: points of empty space look indiscernible by exactly the same symmetry reasoning, so either the whole of space collapses into one point or I have to accept brute facts about which point is which, which is a conclusion about space I never wanted and don't think I earned. My flatmate, who does maths, just says the spheres are distinct because they occupy different places, but that looks like it only moves the problem one step back.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I really want to keep it that there are two spheres and not one, and I'd also quite like to keep the symmetry test in (3)/(4), because it feels like the only non-question-begging way of saying what a qualitative property is. Tell me which step breaks, why that step looked fine when I wrote it down, and what I end up committed to if I give up each of the other plausible candidates instead.
