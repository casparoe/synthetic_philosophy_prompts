Bit of background so you know where I'm coming from: I retired three years ago after a career in marine geology — thirty-odd years of measuring things and arguing about error bars — and since then I've been reading my way into philosophy on my own, no teacher, just books and a lot of margin notes. So please don't assume I know the literature, and explain any technical term as you go.

I've got stuck on Ulysses contracts — the Odysseus-and-the-Sirens business, where you bind yourself now so that future-you can't do the stupid thing — and on the political version of it: constitutional precommitment, a society writing rules into its founding document that are deliberately hard for that same society to undo later. (I half remember the German Basic Law has something like this built in, a permanently unamendable core, but don't hold me to it.)

Here's what I actually want. Imagine James Madison gets dropped into a constitutional drafting committee somewhere today. The committee is arguing over what I'll call a "siren clause," with two parts:

(a) A short list of provisions — say, a binding multi-decade emissions budget, and a ban on delegating lethal decisions to autonomous systems — gets entrenched. Amending any of them requires a two-thirds vote in two consecutive parliaments with a general election in between, plus sign-off from a citizens' assembly of 200 people picked by lot, who have to sit for six months and publish their reasons for whatever they decide. Easy to pass at the founding moment, very hard to reverse afterward.

(b) The justification offered is not mainly self-denial, it's enabling. The framers argue that without the lock the country can't safely do the risky thing it wants to do at all — the point of the rope is that it lets you hear the song. In Federalist 51 Madison writes, "If men were angels, no government would be necessary," and that's the one bit I feel I definitely get. But he seems to be talking about institutions checking each other, not about a society tying its own hands against its future self.

So: would Madison have voted for the siren clause? Would he have rewritten it — for instance, would he have thought a randomly-selected assembly is a useless check because there's no "ambition to counteract ambition" in it, and wanted the lock held by rival institutions instead? Would he have balked at the unamendable core on the grounds that a bare majority today shouldn't get to govern a future supermajority — the dead hand problem — or would he have said that's exactly the point? (I gather Jefferson took the opposite view about each generation getting to rewrite its own constitution, so use him as a contrast if it helps.)

Two distinctions I cooked up on my own; use them if they survive contact with your better knowledge, and tell me if they don't:

1. Temptation vs. conversion. Sometimes you bind your future self because you predict weakness of will — your values stay put, you just cave. Sometimes you bind your future self because you predict it will genuinely come to want something different, i.e. its values will have changed. Which is a constitution doing? Does Madison's talk of faction and majority tyranny look more like temptation or more like conversion?

2. Binding vs. enabling. Most of what I've read treats precommitment as closing off an option. But Ulysses ties himself up in order to hear the Sirens. Does that enabling justification make the siren clause more Madisonian, or less? Does it make it more legitimate in general, or less?

Give me an actual verdict rather than a survey of what various people might think, though do flag where you're extrapolating. And at the end: what would change your mind — which passage in Madison's actual writing, or which argument, would flip your answer — and where is your reasoning most likely to be failing? I'm mainly worried about anachronism, and about me projecting my own intuitions onto someone who'd have found half this vocabulary unintelligible.
