Me and five friends run a little monthly dinner-and-argument club (we take turns cooking, then argue about whatever the topic of the month is — last month was whether you'd take the experience machine). This month I volunteered animal consciousness, and we've already managed to talk in circles for two hours without noticing that we were answering four completely different questions. So I want to build one of those "which position do you actually hold?" quizzes for the group — 9 or 10 questions, two or three answer options each, plus a scoring key — but the sort that pins you down by what you say about concrete cases rather than letting you pick the label you already like. No question should just be "do you think insects are conscious?", and no question should name a theory.

Here's the angle I care about, since I think this is where we were actually disagreeing: (1) what *kind* of evidence settles it — what an animal does, versus what its nervous system is built like, versus something else; (2) whether consciousness is on/off or more like a dimmer switch that goes very low but never quite to zero; (3) whether there can be states that matter to the animal without being consciously felt at all (the "you flinch and learn to avoid it, but there's nobody home" idea — a couple of us found this weirdly compelling and a couple found it obviously incoherent); and (4) whether the question "is this species conscious?" even has a determinate answer, or whether we're asking something like "is a virus alive?" I'd like the tree built from that logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they allow — and only then have the actual named positions hung on the leaves, with the empty combinations pointed out and a sentence on why nobody lives there (incoherent? just unattractive? merely unexplored?). Leaves I want to be able to land on: the mammals-only/cortex-required sort of view, the any-vertebrate view, the big-tent one that sweeps in insects, the "pain without feeling" view, the gradualist one, and the deflationary "wrong question" one. Also one for the person in our group who says the epistemology doesn't matter because we should behave the same way under uncertainty anyway — we're trying to settle a house rule for what gets cooked at these dinners (insects, shellfish, octopus, whole fish), so that leaf has to earn its place.

The bit I really want your help with is a marker I invented while failing to sleep, which I've been calling the errand test: you train an animal to go to a particular spot to switch off something it clearly dislikes; then you move the spot, change the cue, and finally you let it do the errand *before* the bad thing starts, when nothing is currently happening to it. Say some animals pass all three stages and some fail. I want to know (a) which views treat passing it as decisive evidence and which treat it as almost worthless, and (b) where the tree puts the two dissociation cases: a creature with a nervous system the size of a pinhead that passes the whole errand test, and a mammal that fails every stage but shows the "conscious" brain signature in its cortex. Those two cases seemed to break our discussion in half, and I suspect they'll break any clean tree too — so please flag any pair of leaves your quiz just can't separate rather than pretending it can, and tell me which single question in your set is the least diagnostic. Assume we're all reasonably smart non-philosophers: one's a vet, one writes software, one keeps bees, one thinks the whole topic is confused. Nothing needs citing — I don't want reading recommendations, just the instrument and a note on how it works.
