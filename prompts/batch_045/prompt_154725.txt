So I'm a retired evolutionary biologist — thirty-odd years on warning color and mimicry in butterflies, mostly — and since I shut the lab down I've been sitting in on philosophy classes at the community college and going to a reading group at the public library (mostly retired science types like me, plus one actual philosopher who comes down from the college). Next month we're doing Philip K. Dick's Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? — most of us know it through Blade Runner, and two or three in the group will only have seen the movie — and right before it we read a piece by Daniel Dennett where he worries about AI handing us counterfeit people: systems engineered to press our social buttons, to look like they like us and care what happens to us, with nothing on the inside, his point being that a counterfeit isn't a slightly worse version of the real thing, it isn't the thing at all, and that what it really damages is the currency of the genuine article. I'd like a reading of the novel through that lens, and I'd like a real reading, not a scavenger hunt for scenes that illustrate the thesis. About a page and a half I can hand out, please, argued out from the book itself — nobody in that room is going to chase down papers, so don't send us to any.

Here's my own angle, and I want you to use it or kill it. I keep thinking Dennett's money metaphor is the wrong biology. Counterfeiting currency doesn't itself make genuine bills worth less — that's a separate story about how much money there is — but in mimicry the cheap copies absolutely do degrade the real signal, and they do it in a frequency-dependent way: once Batesian mimics get common enough, the birds stop reading the pattern as a warning, the whole signaling system goes slack, and the genuinely defended model gets eaten too. And there's a second case he doesn't have an analogue for, aggressive mimicry, where the mimic imitates not something dangerous but something the receiver wants — a bolas spider faking moth pheromone, female Photuris fireflies answering a Photinus male's flash so they can eat him — and the harm there lands on the receiver and on innocent third parties rather than on any model. So: is Deckard's world that world? Point me at the scenes that would make this land for the group — the electric sheep on the roof and what his wife is ashamed of, the empathy test and the way it's used, the animal he hauls home out of the desert that turns out to be electric after all and how he and Iran react, Mercer and the empathy box and the TV host who exposes it, and the chickenhead, who's the most empathic character in the book and the one the book treats as damaged. And do me the toad case properly, because it looks to me like it cuts against Dennett: if the joy evaporates the instant he learns it's electric, then whatever he valued was in the belief, and the belief was falsified but the animal didn't change — and yet, as best I recall, the book doesn't let that be the last word, which is either a beautiful objection to the whole counterfeit framework or just sentimentality, and I can't tell which.

Then the honest part: where does this reading fail? My worry is that Dick's androids aren't very good mimics at all — the plot runs on their being detectable, and the emotional weight comes from the humans being worn out rather than from the machines being convincing — so maybe the novel isn't about counterfeit people in Dennett's sense at all, and I'd rather you said so than stretched it. And if it does resist the reading, what does the novel add to the argument that the philosophical pieces don't have? One last thing for the movie-only crowd: Ridley Scott's film drops Mercer and the toad but adds the implanted memories, so is a fake memory a different kind of counterfeit from a fake person, and is that case easier or harder for Dennett's framework than the ones in the novel?
