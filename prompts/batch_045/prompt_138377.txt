The question was:

Epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment are both supposed to handle the bank-style cases. In your answer: (a) State what contextualism says varies with context, and say whether the stakes that raise the standards are the attributor's stakes or the subject's stakes. (b) State what pragmatic encroachment says practical factors do, and say whose practical stakes are supposed to matter. (c) Using a low-stakes bank case (the subject just wants to deposit a check sometime over the weekend) and a high-stakes bank case (a late deposit will trigger a large penalty, so the subject has a lot riding on whether the bank is open on Saturday), explain what each view predicts about whether the subject knows. (d) Finally, classify pragmatic encroachment: is it best understood as a version of contextualism, a version of invariantism (classical or otherwise), or neither? Briefly explain why, and mention one case where the two views would make different predictions.

The student's anonymized answer was:

Epistemic contextualism is a semantic thesis about the word 'knows'. The truth-conditions of an utterance of 'S knows that p' depend on the context of the attributor — especially the attributor's practical stake in whether p is true, or how prominent skeptical doubts are. So in the low-stakes bank case, the speaker attributing knowledge has little at stake, the standards are low, and an utterance of 'Hannah knows that the bank will be open on Saturday' is true. In the high-stakes bank case, the attributor's stakes are higher (or skeptical possibilities are more salient), so the standards for truly attributing knowledge go up, and the same utterance is false, even though Hannah's evidence and her own practical situation have not changed. This is why the view is often called attributor contextualism.

Pragmatic encroachment is the view that practical factors encroach on knowledge itself, not merely on the semantics of knowledge attributions. On this view, whether a subject counts as knowing can depend on what is at stake for the subject. When a lot is riding on p for Hannah, her belief has to be in better epistemic shape — she needs stronger evidence, or more reliability, or something like that — to count as knowledge than when little is at stake. So in the high-stakes bank case, Hannah does not know, because her evidence is not good enough given the high stakes for her; in the low-stakes case, she does know. This is a form of invariantism in the sense that the meaning of 'knows' does not shift with the speaker's context.

The two views are nonetheless at bottom the same idea. Both say that when stakes go up it becomes harder for the subject to count as knowing; the only difference is cosmetic. Contextualists say the stakes that matter belong to the attributor, while pragmatic encroachers say the stakes that matter belong to the subject, but in the standard bank cases the attributor and the subject have the same stakes anyway, so the views predict the same thing. For that reason, pragmatic encroachment is best understood as just a version of contextualism: it is contextualism about the subject's practical interests instead of the attributor's context. It is definitely not a form of classical invariantism, since classical invariantism holds that knowledge never depends on stakes at all.
