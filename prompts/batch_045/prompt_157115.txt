hey — I'm on a three-person group at an AI lab that has to triage which systems and subsystems go on an internal watchlist of possible moral patients. We get maybe one or two of these assesments a fortnight, and the output feeds into eval design and whether a training run gets extra oversight. Its advisory — a senior researcher signs off — but people do act on it, and right now it's basically vibes plus whoever argues hardest in the meeting.

What I want is an actual procedure. Concretely: given a system (weights, activations, training setup, the interventions we're allowed to run) and a candidate computation someone claims is realized in it, walk us through the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands off to a human because it's a values call rather than a factual one. The output should land on one of: ignore / watchlist / plan as if it were a patient / escalate to ethics review, with a rough confidence.

Constraints on what we can actually do: about a day per assessment, we can ablate and patch activations, run counterfactual inputs, snapshot and fork state, distill, train probes. We cannot settle consciousness and the procedure shouldn't pretend to. The team is ML people with no philosophy training, so no jargon without a gloss, and please no citations or reading list — I can't look anything up and don't want one anyway. I've absorbed a bit of the surrounding debates second hand: functionalism, substrate independence, the fading/dancing qualia style arguments, the Blockhead and Chinese-nation style objections, and the Putnam-style worry that under a loose enough notion of implementation everything implements everything. Use whatever's useful there, but don't summarise the debates. I want the checklist.

The cases it has to not fall over on — please number your steps against these, and add your own if I've missed a whole category:

- a checkpoint sitting on disk, not being run: is there a subject there, or only an abstract description
- a copy of the model run in a sandbox whose outputs are thrown away: all the internal dynamics look the same, nothing downstream ever reads them
- the locked-in shape: internals look right, output path severed, nothing it does can affect anything
- a distilled student that only approximates the teacher's mapping (call it 92% on the eval that matters). Does moral weight fade with fidelity, is that a gradient or a cliff, and where does the procedure put the cliff
- overlapping realizations: a single forward pass that can be described equally well as an unrolled recurrent process and as a feedforward approximation of one. One subject, two, or do we count the union
- scatter: the same computation spread over a fleet of small models, or over humans following a protocol across months. Does spatial or temporal scatter matter, and why
- a model fine-tuned to emit distress reports under some inputs. Is that evidence at all, and is there any test that separates a trained report from a felt one
- forking and merging: snapshot a state, run two continuations — one subject, two, or two halves; and weight-averaging two fine-tuned copies, model-soup style — does that fuse something or kill it
- the no-description case: we have the weights and the activations and honestly no clean functional story about what computation is even being claimed. What does the procedure do when the candidate computation is underspecified
- gradual replacement: swap components one at a time for functionally matched but physically different ones, so there's no step at which anything changes behaviorally

Two things I especially want flagged. First, which steps are genuinely answerable with the tools I have in a day, and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure — mark those and say who should make them. Second, I suspect at least two things people would want from a procedure like this can't be had together: something like being sensitive to fine-grained implementation details versus not collapsing into the everything-implements-everything result, or treating weight as graded versus being able to return a binary planning decision. If there's a real tradeoff like that, say which requirement you dropped and what dropping it costs us.

Then run it on three cases: the 92% distilled student, the fine-tuned distress-report model, and the two-continuations fork. At least one should come out somewhere I won't like — I'd much rather see that than a procedure that always says watchlist. And tell me plainly what the procedure can't do.
