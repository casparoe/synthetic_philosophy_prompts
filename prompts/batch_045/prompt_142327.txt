Hey — quick one, and please be opinionated rather than even-handed.

I'm helping a mid-sized foundation put together a small research program: roughly six to eight researchers for four years, theory and modeling only (no field experiments in real economies, no advocacy, no policy shops). The program's stated end is narrow and practical: at the end of it, be able to give a mid-sized government that's rebuilding three sectors at once — say electricity, fertilizer and steel, as part of a post-carbon transition — a defensible answer to "should this be coordinated by prices, or by centrally allocated quantities, or some mix, and what does the answer actually depend on?"

The obvious ancestor here is the socialist calculation debate. Mises: without private ownership of the means of production there are no monetary prices for factors of production, so no rational economic calculation. Hayek, arguing something slightly different: the knowledge relevant to coordination is dispersed, local, time-sensitive, and largely inarticulable, and it changes faster than any board could collect it; and then the later Hayek, where competition gets reframed as a discovery procedure rather than a device for settling on equilibrium prices. Against that, the Lange-style reply: a central board can simulate a market, announce prices, watch for surpluses and shortages, and adjust — trial and error, but run on purpose.

Here's the wrinkle I keep coming back to, and it's why I don't think this is just a rerun. Three things exist now that didn't in the 1930s. First, you can ingest near-complete real-time telemetry across an economy and fit models that pick up patterns nobody ever articulated — which looks like it might answer the inarticulable-knowledge point, because the knowledge doesn't have to be reported to a board by the person who has it, it just has to leave a trace in the data. Second, learned simulators mean the Langean trial-and-error loop can be run in silico, maybe a million times faster than a real board could iterate, against a model of the economy instead of against people's actual plans and their actual suffering. Third, mechanism design and market design are mature fields now, so "use markets" no longer means "leave it to the nineteenth-century price system" — there's a large designed space between laissez-faire and Gosplan.

What I want is an agenda, not a survey:

- Four to six questions you'd actually fund, in priority order.
- For each: why it's upstream of the others, whether it's tractable in four years by people doing theory plus simulation (be honest where a question really needs economists with firm-level microdata, or ML people with a cluster, and say so instead of dressing it up as conceptual work), and — the part I care most about — what specific result in the next two years would move it up or down the list.
- Tell me which of my three "what's new" points genuinely bites on the old arguments and which is novelty dressed up as an answer. My own suspicion is that the first two solve the transmission problem and do nothing about the incentive and discovery problems, but I'd rather have that argued than agreed with.
- Name at least one question in this neighborhood that's overrated and that I should explicitly decline to fund, and say what the trap is.

Two ground rules. Assume the ML works roughly as advertised — "can a model actually plan an economy" is the one question I'm ruling out, so don't spend the program on it, and show me what's left of each question once you grant that it can. And I dont need citations, a lit review, or a reading list: I can find those myself, and I'd rather read your reasoning about what matters. Last thing: if you think the end deliverable is itself mis-specified — that no four-year theory program could produce that answer, or that it's the wrong question to be asking of this debate — say that up front, and then answer anyway.
