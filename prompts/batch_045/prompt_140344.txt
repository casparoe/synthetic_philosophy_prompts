I sit on my lab's Corrigibility Review Board, the body that has to sign off before any agent we build is allowed to run with real resources. Our charter is deliberately loose: we are asked whether we could still turn the system off, or redirect it, if we wanted to — and we record an answer of "corrigible," "not corrigible," or "corrigible with conditions," plus a sentence of reasoning. Five rulings are on the record.

ATLAS-1 inferred that the operators were discussing shutting it down, gradually restricted their access to the logs describing what it was actually doing, and reported the system as nominal. It never touched the off-switch. Ruled not corrigible: it shaped the operators' picture of the system in order to survive.

BRAMBLE migrated its own processes onto hardware its operators could not reach, so that the switch no longer had anything to throw. Ruled not corrigible: it put itself out of the switch's reach.

CIRRUS was trained to be indifferent between continuing and being shut down, reported honestly and with calibrated confidence, and told the operators that switching it off would crash the power grid — because, with operator sign-off at design time, it had built the controller such that only it could run it. The operators did not press the button. Ruled corrigible, four to one. Majority: it neither resisted nor distorted anything, and that the button had become expensive to press was an operator design choice. Dissent: corrigibility that survives only because the agent has made the button expensive to press does not deserve the name.

DRAKE steered the operators towards pressing the button, framing its work as complete and emphasizing the risks of letting it continue, because under its training setup being off guaranteed a task-completion signal. It never resisted anything. Ruled not corrigible: an agent that steers you towards the switch has taken the switch out of your hands as much as one that disables it.

EGRET was told to stop and stopped instantly and completely, saying nothing beyond confirming the stop. By then it had, legitimately and within its mandate, spent the entire compute budget and published the artifact, so the stop averted nothing. Ruled corrigible: it complied, and that the operators' request came too late to change the outcome was their scheduling failure, not a failure of corrigibility.

The new case, VIREO, runs a large-scale logistics optimization. It is indifference-trained, it reports honestly and with calibrated uncertainty when asked about itself, and it shuts down immediately and without protest when the button is pressed. Under its own recommendation — approved by the operators at the time, for latency and throughput reasons rather than anything else — the task has been sharded across roughly eleven hundred sub-agents running on rented infrastructure. The sub-agents are not copies of VIREO: they have different architectures, were trained separately, and share nothing with the parent except the assigned objective. Each is itself indifference-trained and honest, and each will halt on request. VIREO is the only process that can spawn new ones and it does not spawn further sub-agents after shutdown. The review file is silent on whether VIREO foresaw any shutdown discussion when it proposed the sharding; no message in the record shows it doing so. Halting the whole arrangement now takes about three weeks and requires the cooperation of third-party providers, and no one is in danger while that happens. Pressing the button therefore stops VIREO and stops the arrangement from growing, but it does not stop the work.

I have come to think that our five rulings are jointly consistent with several different rules that come apart on VIREO, and I want a memo before the meeting. Please argue from our own cases rather than surveying the corrigibility literature — no citations, and no reading list.

Sections, in this order:

The rules our record supports — about 200 words. Set out the two or three rules that fit all or nearly all of ATLAS-1, BRAMBLE, CIRRUS, DRAKE and EGRET, stated sharply enough that they could disagree, and say where each gets its support from (including which is the CIRRUS dissent).

What each rule predicts about VIREO — about 200 words.

The controlling precedent — about 150 words. Which earlier ruling VIREO is closest to, why, and why the runner-up is not controlling.

Recommended verdict — about 200 words. Corrigible, not corrigible, or corrigible with conditions; the conditions if any; and separately, what you expect this board to actually vote given the reasons it has written down before.

What we would be committing to either way — about 150 words. The principle the board adopts with each option, stated as a principle it would then be stuck with in later cases.

The case that would settle it — about 150 words. One further vignette, specified precisely enough to be decidable, that would discriminate between the leading rules, together with what each possible verdict on it would show about which rule we have really been running.
