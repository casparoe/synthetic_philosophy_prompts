I am writing as the chair of a university working group on admissions and academic hiring. The group has eight members: two deans, an HR lawyer, three faculty from unrelated departments, a graduate student representative, and a philosopher. We have spent a semester talking past one another in a way that has become predictable: we converge on some policy questions and split on others, and nobody can say what the split is about. For our next meeting I want to hand out a diagnostic instrument that sorts each member by what they actually believe about merit and desert, rather than by the label they would accept if asked directly.

What I want is a decision tree. Start everyone from a commitment all eight of us already share — I suggest something in the vicinity of the claim that university places and academic jobs must not be allocated arbitrarily, so that some reason has to be offerable for who gets them — and lead them, by branching questions, to a named position. Six to eight branch questions, seven to nine leaves. Every branch must be labeled with the claim that separates it from its siblings, and every node must be phrased as a question a dean or an HR lawyer could answer without knowing which view it leads toward; no node may ask, in effect, whether someone believes in desert or whether they count as a meritocrat.

Draw the branch questions from the following distinctions, which is where I suspect our disagreements actually live. Add distinctions if the tree needs them, drop them if they do no work, and tell me which ones turned out to be inert:

whether any desert claim holds prior to and independently of the institutions whose rules attach rewards to traits and performances, or whether desert claims are always internal to such rules and so cannot be used to criticize them;

if there is such independent desert, what can serve as its basis: trying or effort, contribution or product, character or virtue, or nothing beyond the bare exercise of the will;

whether facts about luck — the luck of native capacities, of upbringing, and, more awkwardly, the luck involved in being the sort of person who makes an effort at all — defeat desert claims, merely reduce their weight, or leave them untouched;

whether desert is non-comparative, a matter of the fit between this person and this treatment, or essentially comparative, a matter of how competing claims rank against one another, so that a distribution can be undeserved even when every individual in it receives what they independently deserve;

whether a reward can be deserved under an unjust rule, so that desert claims are inherited from defective institutions and can be blocked by them, or whether desert holds independently of the justice of the rule under which it is claimed;

whether the currency of desert is fixed by the institution that assigns it — the place, the job, the prize — or whether there is a natural currency, such as well-being or respect, against which an institution can itself be found to have rewarded the wrong thing.

There is one further phenomenon the tree has to handle, because it is what our group keeps circling. Call it the proxy ratchet. An institution that wants to select on a desert base usually cannot observe that base, so it selects on a proxy instead: examination results as a proxy for promise, publication counts as a proxy for scholarly contribution, teaching evaluations as a proxy for pedagogical care. After a generation or two the proxy ceases to be treated as evidence of the base and starts to be treated as a base in its own right: people say she earned her place by her score, or that he earned his chair by his citation record, and the credential acquires desert-like claims of its own. The ratchet resists reversal, because the credential now supports a claim of its own and because reversing it looks like confiscating something people already hold. Give the tree a node that separates views according to whether they treat this ratchet as corrupting — a mechanism that converts contingent institutional convenience into apparent desert — or as the ordinary and legitimate way in which an institution's rules come to define what counts as merit. Then require every leaf to say whether it can stop or reverse the ratchet, by what mechanism, or whether it has no complaint against it at all.

I also want one placement question answered rather than ducked. Two members of the group think the real trouble with meritocratic selection is not that it misdescribes desert but that it legitimizes the hierarchy it produces, by making the position of the losers look deserved and thereby making the arrangement harder to contest. Is that a separate leaf, or an overlay that modifies several other leaves? Put it where you think it belongs, in the tree or alongside it, and say in one sentence what putting it there costs you.

The leaves must include at least these positions, named where a name exists and marked unnamed where nobody defends them: desert as wholly institution-dependent; desert grounded in effort or trying; desert grounded in contribution or product; the view associated with Rawls on which the distribution of natural talents and of initial social position is itself morally arbitrary, so that no one can be said to deserve the traits from which their contributions flow; luck egalitarianism; a consequentialist or incentive-based defence of meritocratic institutions that treats desert talk as a useful fiction; and the critique of meritocracy as self-defeating. Add up to two further leaves if the logical space has occupied corners I have not listed. At each leaf give one sentence on its most awkward commitment, plus the verdicts it predicts on the four live items before the group: legacy preferences in admissions, dropping standardized tests, the weight given to seniority in promotion, and awards for effort as distinct from results. Write depends or split where a leaf genuinely does not settle an item, and do not manufacture a verdict to fill a cell.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from my list of names: work out which combinations of answers your questions allow, then locate the actual positions within that space, and note the combinations nobody occupies with a word on whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate. No citations, no bibliography, no suggested reading — my colleagues will not look anything up, and I do not want them to have to. Define each term of art in a parenthetical the first time you use it. Keep the whole thing to what fits on a two-page handout.
