I'm writing a section on whether the simulation argument should temper longtermist fanaticism about the far future, and I need a worked case to anchor it. Please construct one to the specifications below.

The background I am working from: the simulation argument is the trilemma that at least one of the following holds — (i) civilisations at our stage almost never reach technological maturity; (ii) mature civilisations almost never run large numbers of ancestor simulations; or (iii) a substantial fraction of observers like us are simulated. The claim I want to test is the "dampening" one: that taking (iii) seriously should move money from far-future work to near-term work, because the future we are trying to protect may not be real, or may be cut short before it gets astronomically large.

The case: one paragraph, no more than 150 words, and realistic — a funder allocating a fixed budget between two grants over the next few years; no aliens, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics. Grant N is a near-term intervention (global health, cash transfers, your choice) with well-measured, finite benefit per pound: call it V. Grant F is a far-future intervention (existential-risk reduction, trajectory change, your choice) whose case rests on a small chance of affecting an astronomically large future: call its expected benefit per pound F, with F vastly greater than V. Use letters rather than invented cost-effectiveness figures.

Engineer the case so that the following three positions deliver the following verdicts, each derived from that position's own arithmetic rather than stipulated:

1. A naive longtermist, who ignores the simulation hypothesis altogether, funds Grant F, with margin roughly F − V.
2. A "discount-only" dampener, who keeps the naive estimate of how much value the far future contains but weights the far-future term by the probability that we are not in a simulation, funds Grant F, with margin roughly (1 − p)F − V.
3. A "corrected" dampener, who both weights by (1 − p) and re-estimates how much value the far future contains conditional on our not being simulated, funds Grant F, with a margin close to F − V again — and does so for any p between 0.1 and 0.9, so that the result is not an artefact of a particular credence. If the ranking cannot be made to flip at any credence below 0.9, say so plainly and give the threshold at which it would flip.

Constraints:

- Simulated minds count for exactly as much as base-reality minds; the case must not work by discounting them.
- Our actions may affect only the world we are in. No simulator that rewards, punishes or terminates the simulation in response to what we do; no influence from inside the simulation on the base-level world; no appeal to the simulators' own future; no assumption that many copies of this very simulation are running.
- The gap between positions 2 and 3 must be structural rather than numerical: 3 must not restore the margin by stipulating that the base-reality future happens to be larger by a compensating factor. It should fall out of what the simulation argument itself says. In particular, p is not a free parameter: on the standard statement of the argument it is fixed by how much simulating mature civilisations do, and that same quantity bears on how much value a civilisation that reaches maturity goes on to contain.
- State explicitly what the naive estimate F is already counting (digital minds, space settlement, and so on), and check that position 3 is not simply double-counting something F already includes.
- The two grants should be comparable in cost, and neither may be made artificially weak.
- No citations or reading lists; everything should be self-contained, since I want to be able to hand the case to someone with no background.

After the scenario, show me: which single feature of the case does the work; what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed; what happens if you remove instead the assumption that the simulation's future is cut short; how the position that comes out worst would defend itself; and, briefly, whether any decision rule that is not expected-value-maximising would be moved by the simulation credence even on the corrected numbers, and why.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the case can be built to spec, and what the headline verdict is — then give the scenario and the demonstration, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked.
