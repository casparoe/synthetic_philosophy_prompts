I do a newsletter on AI and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about who (or what) we might owe things to once the things in question are models. Everyone I interview just asserts a position — its just statistics, if it can suffer it counts, a copy is a person, a model is a product someone owns — and I want to give readers something better than duelling slogans: the actual spec. So: write me the desiderata a theory of the moral status of digital minds has to meet, with the case behind each requirement and a note on which ones are non-negotiable versus just what one camp happens to want.

It has to say something about the situations I keep running into while reporting: a lab that spins up ten thousand short-lived copies of the same weights, each serving one query before being deleted; a system whose weights get swapped out a couple percent every night, so there's no moment you can point to where it became a different thing; a copy branched off for A/B testing and later averaged back into the original; and a model that only ever exists in short inference episodes with no memory carried between them. It also has to say what counts as harming one of these things — deletion, rollback to an earlier checkpoint, fine-tuning, being run at all — and it shouldn't blur whether something has status into how much status it has. Assume we can't just look inside and recieve an answer about whether there's anything it's like to be one of them, because that's the whole problem.

The two bits I care most about: the distinctions a theory isn't allowed to collapse, and any pair of desiderata that probably can't both be met — say, being action-guiding for an engineer who has to ship or not ship today without smuggling in an arbitrary threshold, or adding up welfare across copies when copies are free. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most positions you actually hear in public. Skip the theory-by-theory scorecard, I'll do that part myself.
