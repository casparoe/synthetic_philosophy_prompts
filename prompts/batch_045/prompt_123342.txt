I have no training in philosophy. I work in freight logistics, I spent about four years posted abroad, and the view I want to examine is one I arrived at on my own after a long argument with a colleague and a good deal of brooding about airport queues. I now hold it with more confidence than I can defend, and I would like to know how exposed it actually is.

The view has three parts. First: some everyday conventions generate genuine moral obligations, and not merely prudential reasons, politeness, or a fear of being looked at. My test for which ones is a reciprocity test. A convention binds me when other people are already paying the cost of keeping the scheme going, when my compliance is what makes their compliance rational rather than merely costly, and when my non-compliance leaves some identifiable person worse off than they had a reasonable expectation of being, given what everyone else was doing. Call this the cost-bearing condition. Second: I think the same account has to cover three quite different kinds of convention, which I will call coordination conventions, where the point is that we all do the same thing; distribution conventions, where the point is who gets a turn or a place; and transfer conventions, where the point is that money or service moves from one party to another. If my account works only for one of the three, I treat that as a serious problem, not a detail. Third: for a visitor, the operative convention is the local one, not the one she grew up with, and ignorance of it is close to no excuse, though I allow two escape routes: compliance that would require the visitor to endorse something demeaning or false about herself or about the people she is dealing with, and compliance that would make her a party to a scheme that shifts its costs onto a group who did not choose to bear them.

The view already commits me to some things that I am aware not everyone accepts, and I would rather state them than hide them. Crossing against the signal on an empty street at one in the morning, in a city where the rule is on the books but effectively never enforced, is not wrong on my account, and if it is wrong anyway then my account is missing something. Tapping decline on the card terminal at a counter where nobody served me anything is not wrong either, even though the screen is designed to make me feel that it is. And a visitor who tips lavishly in a place where tipping is not the custom may thereby wrong the residents, by raising expectations that they now have to live with and pay for; on my account that is not a paradox but a prediction, and if it is really a reductio then so much the worse for my account.

What I want is the strongest case against this view, not a survey. Specifically: which intuitions, which invented or real cases, which findings about how people actually respond to violations, and which results or arguments in nearby areas should move me, and which of them would a sympathetic defender of the view concede as counting against it rather than dismiss as resting on a misdescription? Please rank the defeaters from fatal down to merely awkward, and be explicit about which are supposed to sink all three parts and which only one.

I have four worries of my own that I would like addressed directly rather than echoed back. The first is over-generation: on the face of it my reciprocity test seems to generate real obligations to comply with local conventions that are themselves objectionable, since the cost-bearing condition is satisfied just as well by a settled practice of paying bribes to clear a shipment, or of new staff being hazed, or of deference being shown to people of a certain class. If the best I can say is that some independent moral judgment filters the conventions that generate obligations, then the convention itself seems to be doing no work and I am left with ordinary fairness plus expectations wearing a costume. The second is whether the test can explain why I feel obliged not to jump a queue when there is genuinely nobody behind me and nobody will ever know, unless I add something ad hoc. The third is the visitor who is leaving and will never return, never interact with these people again, and cannot plausibly be said to be free-riding on a scheme he is exiting forever: if he can still owe it to them to queue, then obligations can be owed to a practice and not only to persons, and I do not know whether I can hold that. The fourth is that my three kinds of convention may need three different stories, in which case a defeater for my treatment of jaywalking tells me nothing about tipping, and the view I think I hold is really three views I have not distinguished.

Two further things. First, tell me what my view predicts that its rivals do not. I take its main rivals to be the view that these are all just prudence and etiquette with no moral content; the view that conventions bind only when some institution or sanction stands behind them; and the view that what matters is not reciprocity but the meaning an action carries, or the expectations people have formed. If none of these predicts anything different from mine in any case I could actually encounter, I want to be told that, because then I am not sure I am disagreeing about anything. Second, give me your honest view on whether my two escape routes have quietly made the position unfalsifiable. I can already hear myself saying that any apparent counterexample involves a convention that was never really cost-bearing, or that the obligation was present but overridden, and I cannot tell whether that is a principled structure or a habit of self-protection. The thing that would move me most is a case in which the cost-bearing condition clearly fails and yet I still owe it to someone to follow the convention; the thing that would move me least is being told that my view has counterintuitive consequences in the abstract, since I already accept several.
