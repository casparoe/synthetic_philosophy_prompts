I am trying to decide whether to spend the next few years of my research on the meta-problem of consciousness: the problem of explaining why creatures like us produce the first-person reports we do. Why we say, of some states and not of others, that there is something it is like to be in them. Why we say it with the confidence we do. Why the hard problem still feels hard to people who think every function has been explained. Why the conceivability of zombies and the case of the scientist who has never seen colour retain their pull even on philosophers who reject what those arguments are supposed to establish. Before committing myself to a project, I want to know what would count as finishing it.

So write me the desiderata: the requirements a theory of the meta-problem must satisfy to be adequate, each with the case or consideration behind it. Fix the purpose as follows, because adequacy is relative to it. I want the theory to work as a diagnostic. Given an arbitrary system that emits first-person reports — a human adult in ordinary circumstances; a human adult in an unusual one (blindsight, dreaming, a phantom limb, inattentional blindness); a human infant; a macaque; a split-brain patient; or a system trained only on transcripts of people answering questions about their own experience and interfaced with the world through text alone, which I will call the Echolalian — the theory should tell me what to make of those reports. When they are evidence; evidence of what; and what would in principle settle a disagreement about them. Adequacy for me means adequacy for that job, not adequacy as a general metaphysics of mind.

Three things matter more than the rest of the list:

First, sort the requirements into ones you take to be non-negotiable, ones that are negotiable, and ones you think are not requirements at all but the prejudices of a camp — for instance, that the explanation must be given in non-phenomenal terms, that it must be mechanistic rather than a priori, that it must leave folk psychology about experience intact, that it must not make our reports come out mostly false, that it must be consistent with causal closure of the physical, that it must recover the intuition that experience is intrinsic rather than relational. Say which of these are doing real work and which are just what physicalists, or their opponents, happen to want.

Second, tell me whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable. My own suspicion is that these three pull apart: explain the production of reports entirely without appealing to what the reports are about; vindicate the reports as reliable; and explain why the hard problem feels hard, rather than explaining the feeling away. If you think they can be had together, say how. If you think a different pair is the impossible one, say which.

Third, tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories, and whether your list is in danger of being trivially satisfiable — of being met by a theory that simply says the reports are false, or by one that identifies having an experience with being disposed to report it, which looks less like a solution and more like a refusal to have the problem. I also want the distinctions the theory must not collapse kept explicit: undergoing a state, attending to it, judging that one is in it, being disposed to say that one is in it, and someone else being warranted in attributing it. Say which collapses are legitimate and which are cheating. And note whether the theory undermines itself, since it is built out of the very first-person reports it proposes to explain.

Then apply the list to the Echolalian. I am less interested in the verdict than in what the application reveals: does the list force an answer, license agnosticism, or show that the question about the Echolalian was ill-posed?

Close with a short scorecard, a sentence or two each, for: illusionism (reports are not about what they seem to be about); the phenomenal-concept strategy (reports express concepts with a distinctive structure, and that structure explains their peculiar authority); higher-order or self-representational accounts (a state's being conscious consists in the subject's representing themselves as being in it); and attention-schema style accounts (reports are the output of a monitoring system that tracks a simplified model of its own attention). No bibliography, and name the views in your own terms if the labels are not helpful. If you think my framing of the meta-problem is confused, register that in a sentence at the end and then answer the question as I have asked it.
