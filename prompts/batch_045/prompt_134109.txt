I've somehow ended up with the training slot for our new pricing analysts — small specialty insurer, we underwrite gig-economy couriers (e-bikes, mopeds, the occasional car), room of about fifteen, most of them have had exactly one applied stats class and they all talk as if a probability is a property of a single case, the way a weight is. I've got 75 minutes and I want to spend it on the reference class problem. Don't explain the problem to me, I've done enough reading on it; I want the plan for the explanation.

The order is what I keep going back and forth on. My instinct is to open by making them each commit to a number for one named applicant — what's the chance this person has an at-fault claim in the next year — write the numbers on the board, and only then show that the number moves when nothing about the applicant changes and no new evidence arrives, only the description of her. Is that the right entry point, or does something have to be in place before that even stings? And where in the sequence do the three things the problem can mean get separated: which class's frequency *is* the probability, which class's frequency should inform the number we act on, and the it-just-depends-what-question-you're-asking move that dissolves the whole thing? I'm fairly sure that last one has to be earned rather than granted, and I don't know whether it belongs at the end or whether it should be floated early and then made to fail.

The carrying example is where I'm stuck. My candidate: one applicant, 24, delivers by e-bike in a dense city, 30 hours a week, one prior at-fault collision, top-decile on our telematics safety score. Classes I can put her in — 24-year-old riders, urban e-bike couriers, riders with a prior claim, top-decile telematics scorers — all non-gerrymandered, all with rates I can quote, differing by a factor of several, and none of them is a subset of another. Does that work? It might not: every one of those classes is a feature sitting in our database, so I'm terrified someone will just say condition on all of it, fit a model, done — and the session deflates into a complaint about regression. So: what does the example have to have, and what does it have to lack, so that move isn't available? Should the classes be nested or cross-cutting, should the rates differ wildly or only a little, and should there be a decision with money attached to it or does that muddy things?

Misconceptions — I'm guessing: that there's a true reference class out there and we're just short of data; that this is base-rate neglect again (it's almost the inverse, isn't it?); that it's Simpson's paradox in a hat; that more data fixes it; that the fix is use the narrowest class you can describe; and that if the number is reference-class-relative then anything goes. Tell me which of those I've got wrong, where in the sequence each one has to be headed off, and which ones are specific to an audience that prices things for a living and will want a rate at the end.

Two wrenches I want placed. One: the narrowest class you can describe eventually contains just her, and the frequency there is 0 or 1 — where does that go, and is it the natural bridge to why you can't always condition on everything? Two: we have a real bad habit here of re-slicing a book with segments like couriers who claimed in their first 60 days, which is picking the class after you've seen the outcome. I want that in, but I can't tell if it's the same lesson or a different one wearing the same clothes.

Also: if I make one of the classes her age or her gender it turns into a compliance argument about what we're allowed to use. Do I keep that out of the carrying example and bring it in at the end, or is that version the one they actually need? And give me the order I should reveal the numbers in — do I show all the class rates up front or make them commit first?

At the end I need one thing to have them *do* that separates getting it from having learned the phrase it depends on the reference class. And tell me what I should explicitly refuse to cover in 75 minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing it.
