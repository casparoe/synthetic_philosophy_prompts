I'm in a reading group on social ontology and we keep using the phrase *X is socially constructed* as either a conversation stopper or a magic wand: someone says it and everyone acts as if we've thereby learned both that the kind isn't real and that we'd be justified in abolishing, reforming, or re-registering it. Before I read any more of the accounts people actually defend, I want the spec. So: write me the desiderata that a theory of social construction has to meet, where the point of the theory is fixed as follows — it has to tell us what follows, practically and normatively, from *K is socially constructed* for some kind K (gender markers on passports, race as a variable in medicine, disability and accommodation duties, money, professional credentials, refugee status), and it has to do that without either collapsing into *nothing is real* or into *everything is*.

I want the criteria with the case or consideration welded onto each one, and I want you to be strict about what the list has to contain. Some things I think it has to handle, but you tell me which are real requirements and which are just the prejudices of one camp: the triviality controls (having DNA, being two metres tall, and my favourite borderline, *planet*, which famously got redefined by a vote), the paradigm cases (money, where the kind is obviously invented and yet has all the causal force in the world), the opt-out asymmetry (you can't simply declare your race in the way you can apparently declare your gender, and I want to know whether a good account has to explain that or gets to treat it as someone else's problem), the looping cases (psychiatric and personality kinds, where classifying people changes the people classified), the cases where the extension shifts under social pressure without anyone deciding (who counts as white, or as disabled, or as speaking a language rather than a dialect), and the ones where the classification has legal and life-or-death stakes (unemployment statistics, refugee status). I also want the distinctions it isn't allowed to blur: kind versus concept versus property versus fact, constructed versus merely caused, constructed versus false, and the level problem of whether it's the kind that's constructed or only particular applications of it. And there's one distinction my friend and I invented that I'd like you to test rather than humour: call a kind *anchored* if there's some non-social property it tracks imperfectly but not accidentally, *floating* if there's nothing at all it tracks, and *self-verifying* if classifying someone as a member brings the tracked property into existence — we couldn't decide whether kinds can move between these categories over time, or be in two of them at once, or whether the three-way scheme is just a bad re-description of something the literature already has. Does an adequate theory have to take a stand on it?

Two more things I care about more than the scoring. First: which pairs of desiderata on your list are probably not jointly satisfiable, and if the list as a whole can't be met, which single requirement should be dropped and what does dropping it cost you — I suspect there's a tension between *must be non-trivial, i.e. must rule things like kidneys and DNA out* and *must cover everything we have the intuition is constructed*, and another between *must be action-guiding* and *must be purely descriptive*. Second: which one requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, i.e. which demand, if you enforced only it, would knock out most of what's on offer? I'd also like you to consider two requirements I'm not sure are legitimate and tell me if they're category errors: the *abolition requirement* (if K is constructed, the theory should say what abolishing K would consist in, and whether abolishing the kind is the same as abolishing the practice) and the *reflexivity requirement* (the theory should say what happens to K when the claim that K is constructed becomes widely believed, since that belief itself changes the practices — tell me whether demanding this is demanding something incoherent). Then, briefly and last, run three familiar styles of account against the list — the collective-acceptance / status-function picture, the view that constructions are definitions chosen for a political purpose, and the looping / people-change-under-classification picture — but keep that to a few lines each; the list is the deliverable, not the scoreboard.
