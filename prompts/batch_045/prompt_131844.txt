I spent a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, mostly scattering work, and since retiring I have been auditing a philosophy of science reading group at the local university. Last week we read Wigner's 1960 essay on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences, and I came away convinced, perhaps too quickly, by a deflationary line that I have started calling the double-filter account. It has three parts: first, we build formalisms to fit the phenomena we have already decided count as physics, and Wigner himself grants that we test theories against a rather narrow selection of data; second, there is a file-drawer effect, in that the formalisms that never found a use are forgotten while the ones that did are remembered as prescient; third, what I think of as difficulty conservation, meaning that problems which resist mathematical treatment tend to get reclassified as merely applied, messy, or not fundamental, so the sample of laws over which the miracle is supposed to be measured has been pre-filtered for tractability. A member of the group added a fourth idea, that physical theorizing is constrained from the start by symmetries, conservation principles, and consistency requirements, which are already mathematical in form, so the fit is partly built in rather than discovered. I now find it hard to see what is left to explain, and I would like you to tell me what would count against this package: which observations, cases, or arguments should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and which of them a defender of the stronger reading would concede as genuine defeaters rather than dismiss as question-begging.

I have tried to make the view testable, and I would like your assessment of the tests I have come up with. The first is what I call the tail-lag statistic: take pieces of mathematics invented for purely internal reasons, with no physical motivation, and look at the interval between their invention and their first use in a confirmed quantitative prediction; if the distribution is short and light-tailed, mathematics looks like a toolkit built mostly on demand, whereas a heavy tail of long-lag cases with no original motivation would be evidence of something that a selection story has to work hard to absorb. The second is the surplus-structure test: distinguish cases where structure that the formalism forced on us for internal reasons later acquires independent empirical content from cases where the formalism was retrofitted to the data or where leftover vocabulary was reinterpreted afterward by a community with a strong habit of hunting for physical readings of unused mathematics; I suspect this habit, which one might call interpretive lock-in, manufactures much of the appearance of foresight. The third is a transfer test: whether one and the same formalism compresses domains it was never fitted to, as in Wigner's own story about the constant pi turning up in the Gaussian distribution for population statistics, and whether that kind of transfer is evidence of anything or merely an artifact of a small shared stock of reusable machinery. The fourth is impressionistic and I would like it checked: my sense is that fundamental physics since the Standard Model has consumed rather little newly minted pure mathematics and has leaned instead on large-scale computation, while much of contemporary pure mathematics shows no sign of being needed by physics at all. I also worry that my own view predicts that alternative, mathematically incommensurate theories of the same phenomena should be buildable, in the spirit of Wigner's remark about a man with a bunch of keys who keeps finding the right one on the first or second try and grows skeptical of the uniqueness of the match, and that nobody has actually built one; I am not sure whether that failure counts against me or is just a fact about where effort has gone.

Two further things would help me. First, I would like an honest verdict on whether my account has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every successful application can be dismissed as deliberate fitting, every long-lag application as a lucky hit noticed in hindsight, and every piece of unused mathematics as not yet needed, then the view predicts nothing, and I want to know whether that is a genuine defect for a thesis of this kind or an acceptable feature of a critique that only aims to shift the burden of proof. Second, tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, whether there is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work, such as how large and how searchable the space of usable formalisms is or how typical mathematical tractability should be expected to be, or whether the two sides are mostly disagreeing about what counts as physics and about how much surprise a working scientist ought to feel. Please treat the standard examples in the same spirit: I have in mind Riemannian geometry arriving before it was needed for general relativity, and complex numbers turning out to be mandatory rather than merely convenient in quantum mechanics, and I want to know whether they really have the structure my tests demand or whether they are retrofitted stories. What would move me most is a single case where structure forced by the internal logic of a formalism, introduced with no eye on nature, later generated a novel and quantitatively confirmed prediction; if such cases are common, I will give up the double-filter account, and if they are rare or contested, I would like to know why. Please answer in American English.
