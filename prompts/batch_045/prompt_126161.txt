I am a novelist, and I have let myself be booked to give the closing address at a two-day training weekend run by a direct-action climate network, on the theme of what they call "the political defence." About forty people will be in the room. Roughly half are organisers — several awaiting trial for criminal damage, two who have served short sentences, a few who have been on the receiving end of bail conditions they describe as a slow punishment. The other half are the movement's own lawyers and legal observers, plus a retired district judge who now volunteers as an adviser and two sympathetic local councillors. Most of them have read my last novel, whose two central characters break the same law by different routes: one cuts a fence in daylight and sits down afterwards to wait, with her name and address written on a card in her pocket; the other does the same work at night, masked, and is never caught. A good number of them liked the second character better, and one of the facilitators has already told me that people are looking forward to "the bit about the card."

The thesis I want to defend is one I arrived at while writing them. I want to argue that what separates civil disobedience from crime on one side and from what I have started calling refusal on the other is not that the act is public, or nonviolent, or conscientious, or aimed at changing a law — the familiar list is, I think, either too wide or beside the point — but something narrower: that the agent leaves behind a case the state could win without her cooperation and then answers for the act in the state's own forum, accepting whatever that forum does to her as binding. Call it the self-incrimination condition. On this view the night character is not doing a more cautious version of what the daylight character does; she is doing a different thing, with a different justification, and the movement does itself no favours by borrowing the moral prestige of the one for the other. I also want to hold that the condition generates hard cases I cannot tidy up: the protester who acts openly but runs, masks, or otherwise makes identification require work; the protester who does everything right and is never charged, so that the state's refusal to prosecute seems to void the signature; and the protester for whom a conviction would not be a bearable price at all — an undocumented person, a sole carer, someone in a jurisdiction where the same act carries years rather than weeks.

Prepare me for the hour, please, as a Q&A sheet rather than as a defence of the view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get in that room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are; phrase each one as that audience would phrase it, in their own vocabulary rather than in a philosopher's — security culture, arrestable and non-arrestable, diversity of tactics, the courtroom as a platform, who gets to be a martyr, what a conviction costs a person who already has a record. For each, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes standing up, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes, before anyone puts a hand up. Tell me how to handle the moment — it will come — when someone quotes my own night character back at me, the passage in chapter nine where she says that a state which will not listen has forfeited the right to be told who did it. And include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with the least evasive answer I could give if they do. If the thesis itself is wrong, say so plainly before you start making me look composed.
