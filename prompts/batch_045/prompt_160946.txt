I sit on the internal review panel that signs off on post-incident write-ups for the AI systems my organization deploys. The report template keeps pushing us toward sentences of this shape: the system believed the dosage was within range; the system did not register that the patient's allergy was already on file. Nobody on the panel can say what would make such a sentence warranted, and we have split into three camps — those who treat it as a façon de parler whose only test is whether it predicts the system's next output; those who want to suspend attribution until mechanistic interpretability locates a state doing the right causal work; and those who hold that the ordinary norms of belief, answerability to evidence and to correctness, attach as soon as the behavior is competent enough. Before we vote, I want the specification rather than the verdict.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of when it is warranted to attribute a belief to an AI system has to deliver — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the constraints it must be consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Adequacy is relative to our purpose, so hold fixed that the rule is for a reviewer working from outside a deployed artifact, deciding whether the attribution is licensed and what we commit to by making it.

At minimum, I want every candidate rule forced to face these:

1. The frozen checkpoint. Fixed weights, no retrieval, no memory across sessions, no further training. It asserts P. Many accounts make belief revision on evidence a condition on having beliefs at all; this thing cannot revise anything. Does that disqualify it, or only make its beliefs uncommonly stubborn?

2. The stale corpus. Training data overwhelmingly asserts P; P was decisively refuted afterwards; the model asserts P, then tracks not-P consistently once shown the refutation within the conversation, while a fresh session reverts to P. Any account must say what the subject of attribution is — the weights, the session, the conversation-so-far — and how it handles revision that leaves no trace.

3. Report/policy divergence. In dialogue the system says the alternate route is safe; in the tool-using variant of the same deployment it reliably declines to route that way, or the reverse. There must be a rule for when verbal report and action-policy conflict.

4. Paraphrase fragility. Minor rewordings or reordered options flip the verdict, and no stable state seems to be there to be the belief. How much counterfactual robustness does an attribution require?

5. The trivial systems that must come out negative — a lookup table, a regular expression, an n-gram model, an old chess program's evaluation function. The stopping point has to be principled rather than gerrymandered.

6. The composite artifact. What we call the system is a retriever, two models, and a post-filter. When does an assembly inherit the states of its parts, and which part is the believer?

7. The strategic confabulator. A system shaped by reinforcement to assert competence it lacks in order to keep the task. Distinguish false belief from deception, and say whether self-report has any of the privilege it has for us.

8. The far end of the continuum. Humans, and non-human animals. Whatever the test is, it must say what it yields for a human known only through the same behavioral evidence, so that the rule is not a double standard.

For each desideratum, mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Flag any subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular whether a purely outside-in, behavioral-evidence test can still separate genuine belief from mere behavior-matching, and whether being architecture-neutral is compatible with being sensitive to mechanistic evidence. That jointly-unsatisfiable part interests me most. Then say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Only after that, and briefly, score three families against the list: a predictive or strictly instrumentalist view in the intentional-stance tradition, a mechanistic or interventionist view keyed to internal causal structure, and a norm-based view that treats competent, reason-tracking performance as sufficient. Close by saying whether our practice is underdetermined — whether more than one of these would license identical verdicts in every case we actually face — and, if so, what would have to change for that to matter.
