We just finnished GEB in my reading group and one of the group is now convinced the Gödelian analogy basically dissolves the hard problem: a formal system rich enough to encode claims about itself gets a sentence it can't prove, and that gap between what it can prove and what's true of it is, he says, just what a self is — so consciousness is self-reference with enough layers, and scaling a system's self-model up gets you there for free. I think that's too cheap.

Audit it like you'd audit an argument you don't like. I want the correspondences laid out — what plays the role of the formal system, of the Gödel sentence, of the step to the meta-level, of the tangle — then where the mapping breaks, which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion he wants, and what the analogy quietly imports about minds that the source side doesn't have.

Two cases I made up that I think decide it, and I'd rather you fight them than nod along:

- MIRROR: a system with a complete, perfectly accurate, causally inert model of its own code and current state. The model gets written to a log; the system's behaviour is byte-identical with the module removed.
- LOOP: a system with a lossy, partial, partly wrong self-model that is wired into its own operation — it gates what the system does next.

On Hofstadter's analogy as it actually stands, which of those is closer to having a self, and is that the answer you want to be stuck with? Also: the Gödel sentence is only recognisable as true from outside the system. Is that a load-bearing feature of the analogy, or a detail?

Does his "more self-reference, more self" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Give me the table, then the breaks, then tell me whether there's a better analogy available. End with \answer{...} containing exactly one of: holds, holds-with-repairs, collapses.
