Draft the joint statement that a role-first functionalist and a system-first attributionist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on which computations matter morally. By a role-first functionalist I mean someone who holds that moral status is settled by a computation's causal-functional role, so that any physical system realizing the right counterfactual-supporting organization counts, and the vehicle and its boundaries matter only as ways of locating that organization. By a system-first attributionist I mean someone who holds that a computation matters only when it is attributable to a system that is itself a candidate bearer, one with its own boundary whose own activity does the causal work, so that the computation is enacted rather than merely instantiated.

Assume both accept that trivial-implementation arguments settle nothing on their own: any sufficiently rich physical system can be described as running any given computation, so the question is not whether a computation is realized here but which realizations are morally load-bearing.

I want a numbered agreed-claims section covering at least: what the trivial-implementation arguments do and do not show; whether the number of moral patients can be inflated simply by finding more computations in the same matter; and what a satisfactory account would have to deliver. Then a disagreements table, each disputed claim stated twice, once in wording each side would accept for its own view, with a note on which rows are merely verbal. Then the cruxes, each paired with a case both sides accept as bearing on it and with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Among the cases, use at least two of these: the unexercised-counterfactual case, in which a system's actual trajectory never leaves one path, so that most of the computation it is described as performing is never causally exercised; the nested-superset case, in which, if a system matters, so apparently does that system together with its immediate environment under a wider description; the slowed-emulation case, in which the same computation is run a thousand times more slowly; and the gradual-substitution case, in which a circuit is replaced piece by piece with a chemically different realizer of the same role. Include, as one crux, whether attribution is itself a computational property or an extra condition layered on top of one, and whether that extra condition quietly reintroduces a preference for familiar biology.
