What I want them to come away feeling is that the question "is the person who steps out on Mars really me?" may just not have an answer to be had, and that this is not the same as saying nothing matters: what matters in survival comes in degrees, and how much it matters looks like it should be settled independently of how the identity question gets settled.

The case I'd like at the center is one I half-made-up and can't find discussed anywhere: the machine malfunctions and, instead of one traveller arriving, four candidates turn up at once, and they aren't equal. One shares almost all of the original's memories and character, one maybe half, one very little, and there's also someone who is linked to the original only by a long chain of stages, each of which overlaps the next by a little. Crucially there's nothing further to be discovered — no recording of the malfunction, no further fact about which one is "the original," and the students should be told that explicitly.

So design me a runnable activity: roles, rules, materials, a timeline for the 80 minutes. It should involve real choices under real but small scarcity (no actual money, nothing that needs prep beyond printing stuff), and the structure should be such that the students can complete the task without ever settling who the original is, and then notice that what they were actually tracking all along was degrees of psychological overlap. Ideally the reveal comes at the end rather than in an explanation up front — nobody should hear the word "identity" until afterwards, and the facilitator shouldn't smuggle in the distinction either.
