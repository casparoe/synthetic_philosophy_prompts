I'm a novelist — three books out, fourth one a year overdue — and I've managed to turn what should be a plotting problem into something I apparently need a philosopher for. My protagonist is a 54-year-old night maintenance foreman at a poultry plant in northeast Georgia who has spent twenty-two years keeping his head down, and then testifies against the company in an injury lawsuit knowing he'll never be hired in that county again. I can't decide how to write the change. Either he was always the kind of man who would do that, and the story is about how the thing was starved in him for two decades and then finally got what it needed; or he was made into that man by a long sequence of small deliberate acts — the union organizer who kept showing up, the night classes, the private ritual he never skips of reading the safety log aloud to an empty break room — and the change is not a discovery but a construction. Someone in my writing group who did a philosophy degree says this is exactly the old fight between Mencius and Xunzi over human nature: Mencius thinks we come furnished with four sprouts of goodness that need nourishment and can be smothered by circumstance, like a hillside stripped bare by axes and grazing animals, while Xunzi thinks our raw endowment is crooked and everything good in us is artifice, bent into shape by teachers, ritual and accumulated effort, like warped wood steamed straight. She insists they really disagree; I honestly can't tell whether it's a genuine fight or two people using the word nature to mean different things, and I'd like to find out without being sent off to read anything, because I'm on deadline in a cabin with bad wifi and no library.

What I'd actually find useful is a translation rather than an explanation. Here's the vocabulary I really think in: the character bible rather than the character; backstory versus premise (what's simply given about a person versus what the book is arguing); in-character and out-of-character; the workshop rule against an unearned turn; voice as the thing that constrains what a character can credibly do; habituation by repetition; discovery writing versus outlining; and revision, by which I mean the long stupid process of going back and retrofitting chapter two so that chapter nine stops feeling like a cheat. My old workshop teacher also used to separate turning points from turnings — a turning point is where new information arrives and the plan changes, a turning is where someone becomes a person the earlier version of themselves wouldn't recognize — and she claimed you can only ever write the second kind by seeding it in chapter one, that you can't write a turning you can't already hear in the voice. So: restate each of the two positions in those terms, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. What in Mencius translates cleanly onto how I build a person on the page, and what has to be fudged to fit? Same for Xunzi. What has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? One thing that's already bugging me: when I build a character there is literally an author, me, standing outside the person, deciding what counts as raw material and what counts as the training I put them through — and I can't tell whether that role corresponds to anything on either side of the dispute, or whether mapping it onto something (Heaven, the old sage-kings, the rituals themselves) is exactly the move that decides who wins. Please don't just tell me both of them agree that cultivation matters and leave it there; I got that far by myself. What I want to know is whether what's left over after the translation is a hole in my craft vocabulary, or a sign that the philosophers are fighting over something thinner than it looks. (Sorry if any of the names are spelled wrong, and definitly sorry for the length — I've been chewing on this alone for a year.)
