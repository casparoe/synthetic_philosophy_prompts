**The question**

Suppose that a DSM-style revision introduces a new category, *Compulsive Task-Switching Disorder* (CTSD):

1. a persistent pattern of abandoning self-initiated tasks within minutes in favour of a different task or medium, present for six months or more;
2. repeated unsuccessful efforts to cut down or to sustain attention;
3. the pattern causes clinically significant distress or impairment in work, study or relationships;
4. not better explained by mania, by an anxiety disorder, or by the demands of a person's job.

Five years after the category appears:

(a) At intake, patients increasingly describe their difficulties in the exact wording of criterion 1, and unstructured interviews that avoid the criteria elicit noticeably different descriptions.
(b) Large online peer communities form around the diagnosis; members trade symptom vocabulary, compare themselves against the criteria, and some report that receiving the label changed how they understand and narrate their own habits.
(c) Because public insurers require a billable code before they will reimburse therapy, clinicians begin applying CTSD to milder cases that five years earlier would have been recorded as adjustment disorder.
(d) Recorded prevalence among 15–24 year olds roughly triples.
(e) A factor analysis of a large clinical sample finds two clusters — one organised around reward-seeking, one around avoidance of difficult tasks — and the revision committee splits CTSD into two subtypes.

1. Which of (a)–(e) are looping effects in Hacking's sense (a way of classifying people feeds back to change the people classified, which in turn can force revision of the classification)? For any that are not, say what they are instead.
2. Does the presence of looping effects here give us a reason to deny that CTSD is a genuine disorder? Keep separate: (i) the classification changed the people classified; (ii) the classification produced, or partly constituted, the condition; (iii) the classification makes CTSD unfit for scientific or clinical use. Say whether and how each is supposed to support the next.

**Student's answer**

Looping effects, as Hacking uses the term, describe a feedback relation between a classification of people and the people classified: we introduce a kind of person, the people so classified come to understand and conduct themselves in terms of it, and their changed behaviour and experience feed back so that the classification itself has to be revised. The contrast case is with kinds that are indifferent — electrons don't care what we call them, and nothing about quarks changes when the textbooks do. Human kinds are not like that. CTSD is a human kind, so we should expect looping, and the question is only where it shows up.

(a) is looping. (b) is looping too, and is the textbook case: people acquire the label, the label hands them a vocabulary and a set of expectations, and they reorganise both their behaviour and their experience around it. (c) is not a looping effect: it is a change in diagnostic practice driven by reimbursement rules, i.e. a change in which patients receive the label rather than a change in what the labelled patients are like. There is no feedback from the classified here. (d) is not by itself a looping effect either. A rise in recorded prevalence is consistent with all sorts of things — more people actually having it, better case-finding, looser application of the criteria, or clinicians needing a code. The epidemiological curve doesn't discriminate between these, so (d) is evidence for nothing on its own. (e) is looping, and it shows something important: the kind turned out to be unstable, so within five years the original category had to be broken up.

Which brings us to the second question. A preliminary point: "genuine disorder" is ambiguous, so let me take it to mean what it needs to mean for the objection to work — discovered rather than made. And on that reading, yes: the looping here is a reason to deny that CTSD is a genuine disorder. The point of the looping effect, as Hacking argues, is that the kind and the people are made together; a looping kind is one whose reality is exhausted by our representations of it, which is why his own cases (fugue, multiple personality) are ones we now treat as historically local rather than as natural kinds. Applied here: patients' self-reports track the criterion wording because the criteria taught them what to say, and the community in (b) exists only because the category does. So if CTSD had never been introduced, none of these people would have been distressed by their task-switching at all, and there would have been nothing for the revision committee to carve up. CTSD is a real pattern of behaviour, but it is not a disorder we found; it is one we made.

A fortiori it cannot be a useful scientific kind. A kind whose instances change in response to how we sort them has no stable extension, and inductive projection requires a stable extension, so no generalisation from a CTSD sample to future patients is licensed. That is the real force of the looping point, and it is why the instability in (e) matters: it is the classification chasing its own tail rather than tracking anything in the world.
