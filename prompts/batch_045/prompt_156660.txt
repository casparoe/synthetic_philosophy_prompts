You are a professional philosopher who specializes in the free-will debate and who also publishes literary humor. Work carefully: begin with a short brainstorming note (about 150 words) listing, first, the features of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument that you intend to exaggerate and, second, the three or four moves your piece must avoid in order not to collapse into a straw man. Then write the piece itself.

The piece. Write the inaugural installment of an advice column — the "Dear Abby"/"Ask a Manager" genre, but carried in the staff newsletter of a university whose Senate has just formally accepted the conclusion of the Basic Argument — under a standing header of your own invention. The columnist is a convinced pessimist about moral responsibility who nonetheless has a column to fill and readers who need answers by Friday. Write in deadpan, sustained irony: she must never wink at the reader, never announce that she is being ironic, and never declare that nothing matters. About 900–1,100 words: four letters of roughly 60–90 words each, each answered in roughly 120–180 words.

Background on the target, for accuracy. The Basic Argument runs roughly as follows. (1) People do what they do, in the circumstances, because of the way they are. (2) So to be ultimately morally responsible for what they do, they would have to be ultimately responsible for the way they are, at least in certain crucial mental respects. (3) But no one can be ultimately responsible for the way they are, because that would require being self-caused — causa sui, causing oneself — and nothing can do that; gradual self-improvement does not help, since the capacity and the motive to reshape oneself were themselves unchosen, so the demand merely pushes back one step. (4) So no one is ultimately morally responsible for anything. Strawson holds this conclusion to be indifferent to whether determinism is true, and he holds that ultimate responsibility is what the ordinary notion of moral responsibility requires, so that ordinary attributions of responsibility — not merely some exalted kind — are undermined.

The four letters, written as if from genuine readers:
1. A parent asking whether to punish a nine-year-old who lied about her homework. Practical advice must be given, and it may not appeal to what the child deserves; but the columnist may not simply answer "do nothing."
2. A reader who has spent two years training himself into a calmer, more generous person and wants to know whether he may take credit for it.
3. A self-described libertarian who believes his choices are indeterministic and wants to know whether that permits him to be proud of having quit smoking when his brother did not.
4. A department chair choosing between two job candidates — one reliably kind, one who overcame a wretched upbringing — who wants to know whether she may prefer the second on the ground of effort.

Constraints on the humor.
- The comedy must come from the argument's real structure, so that a specialist recognizes what is being exaggerated and judges it fair. In particular: the regress (each answer should push the explanation one step further back and find nothing there); the indifference to determinism (her answer to letter 3 should be visibly the answer she would give a determinist, perhaps by reusing a sentence); and the collapse of desert into luck.
- Invent at least two administrative devices that let the university keep operating while officially denying desert — for example a Causa Sui Exemption (a form by which an office agrees, purely for administrative convenience, to treat someone as if she had authored herself), a luck-adjusted grading scale, a moral-luck disclosure statement, or a standing rejection notice for applications for retroactive self-creation. Deploy them deadpan, and let them do real work in the advice.
- The columnist may not smuggle desert, authorship, or self-creation back in, and may not dissolve the tension by announcing that nothing follows for practice. She must keep advising; the strain should show only in the machinery she invents.
- Do not attribute any quotation to Galen Strawson or to any other real philosopher. She may paraphrase the premises, but must not appear to cite a published text.

Format. American English throughout. After the column, write one sentence — no more — saying what the column is actually arguing, and then, on its own final line, a machine-readable tag in exactly this form:

\answer{one of: desert | reactive attitudes | forward-looking consequences | administrative fiction}

naming what the columnist's advice in fact rests on by the end. If the honest label turns out to be "desert," the column has failed; write it so that it is not.
