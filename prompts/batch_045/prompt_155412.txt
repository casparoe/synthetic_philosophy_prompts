I'm in a graduate seminar on interpretation this term, and last week we worked through authorial intention and the meaning of a text: actual intentionalism, the hypothetical variants, and the anti-intentionalist position associated with the intentional fallacy and the death-of-the-author slogans. A friend who clerked on the D.C. Circuit said at dinner that American constitutional originalism is just authorial intentionalism with a footnote appended, and that a case like District of Columbia v. Heller is therefore a natural experiment in what intentionalism does when real interpreters have to apply it under institutional constraint. I think that is too glib — legal texts have features that poems and novels do not — but I cannot say precisely where the disanalogy bites, and I would rather have a disciplined case analysis than another round of seminar polemics.

For the record, so that we are arguing about the same episode: the Second Amendment reads, "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed." Heller, decided 5–4 in June 2008, struck down Washington, D.C.'s handgun ban and its requirement that lawful long guns be kept unloaded and disassembled or bound by a trigger lock; the majority, by Scalia and joined by Roberts, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito, held that the amendment protects an individual right to possess a firearm unconnected with service in a militia and to use it for traditionally lawful purposes such as self-defense within the home; there were two dissents, one by Stevens and one by Breyer, each joined by Souter and Ginsburg. Do not spend words re-deriving the facts, the politics, or the aftermath — I know them. What I want is the mapping, done carefully, and then an honest account of where the map tears.

Concretely, here is what I would like handled. Say who, for the theory, counts as the author of this text — the Congress that drafted it, the state conventions that ratified it, the ratifying public, some collective "people" — and whether intentionalism has a principled way of picking between them or only a stipulation. Say what counts as evidence of authorial meaning here: drafting records, ratification debates, founding-era dictionaries and legal treatises, state constitutional analogues, the amendment's relationship to the militia provisions elsewhere in the Constitution. Then take two problems that I think are where the real action is. First, the level-of-generality problem: the intention can be described as "let the people have arms," or "let them have the arms used for militia service," or "let them have what they need to defend hearth and home," and the choice among these does all the work while the theory, as far as I can see, says nothing about how to choose. Second, the gap between intended meaning and expected applications: the framers did not anticipate the D.C. handgun ban, and it seems to me that intentionalists quietly switch between asking what the authors meant and asking what they would have thought of a case they never considered — so tell me whether that is a legitimate extension of the view or an abandonment of it. I also want you to test an idea of my own against the case. Call it diachronic authorship: the thought that the meaning-conferring "author" of a governing text is not a moment of utterance but an inheritance chain, in which each generation of interpreters receives the text together with the accumulated body of prior applications, and in which their own applications then become part of what any later reader must reckon with — so that the author of the operative meaning is a lineage rather than a person or a legislature. Is this a genuine third option alongside intentionalism and anti-intentionalism, or is it anti-intentionalism wearing intentionalist clothing, and does it collapse under the demand that law give citizens advance notice of what it requires? Notice that last demand, because it is my other suspicion: that what makes legal interpretation look intentionalist is not anything about authors at all but the fact that legal texts must settle disputes in advance between parties who cannot renegotiate, whereas literary texts need not settle anything. If that is right, Heller may be no evidence whatsoever for the literary debate, and I want you to say so plainly if you think so.

Please organize the response in the following sections, in this order: "The mapping" (about 350 words — the text, the candidate authors, the evidence base, and what the theory says the interpreters' job is); "What the theory gets right" (about 250 words — what intentionalism genuinely explains about how the majority and the dissents argued, and what it would have predicted each should do); "Where it breaks, and where it is silent" (about 400 words — collective or staged authorship, the level-of-generality problem, expected applications, the selection of evidence, and any point at which you should simply say the theory does not speak rather than forcing it to); "Diachronic authorship, assessed" (about 250 words — my proposal, given the fairest run you can give it, and then either defended or dismantled); "What the case does and does not show about texts generally" (about 250 words — whether the coordination demand does all the work and what that implies for the literary dispute); and "Bottom line" (about 120 words — your verdict, stated without hedging).
