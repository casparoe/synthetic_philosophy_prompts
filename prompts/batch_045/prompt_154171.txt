I'm the TA for a final-year undergraduate ethics seminar — 14 students, one 80-minute session, at the end of a unit on aggregation in rescue cases. They've already worked through trolley-style material and can state the intuitive principle that one should save the greater number; next week they read Taurek, but I don't want to name or summarize his thesis in advance. I want them to run into it first.

Could you design a runnable activity that makes them feel the pull of the claim that numbers as such don't count — that a rescuer who can save either one stranger or five, everything else equal, does nothing wrong in saving the one?

Constraints:

- No framing lecture. The thesis and the name shouldn't appear until the debrief; the force should come from what the rules make them do to each other.
- Real but trivial stakes: I can hand out coffee-voucher raffle tickets, and being "left behind" costs you yours.
- The core mechanism I have in mind: students first author, in groups of four, a single decision rule for the case, written down before they learn which role they'll be assigned — and then must state that rule out loud, in the second person, to whichever individual it abandons. Please add the case variations that stress whatever rule they produce: unequal magnitudes (one death versus five broken arms, and then one broken arm versus five deaths), near-ties (one person losing fifty years versus five people losing forty-eight each), and a variant where the loss is divisible rather than all-or-nothing.
- Give me the materials, the wording to read aloud, a timeline, and a debrief that maps each staged moment onto a specific move in the argument: that no one loses the sum, the difference between what numbers do and what magnitudes do, and the gap between "may save the one" and "must save the five." Say what will predictably go wrong when I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which part of the view the activity can't stage and I'll have to argue for myself.

One specific request. I want them to notice that "give everyone an equal chance" is ambiguous: a coin tossed between the two groups gives each of the six people a one-half chance of surviving, whereas a lottery that picks one person at random and saves that person's whole group gives everyone an equal chance of being the person who decides it, while leaving the lone person one chance in six and each of the five five chances in six. Please stage this as something they trip over rather than something I explain.
