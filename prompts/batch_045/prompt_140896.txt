There's a postdoc in my department — call him M — who runs an informal rational choice reading group, and for about a year now he's been ruling on "is this agent irrational?" cases, one a week, off the top of his head. Ive been keeping a list because I can never predict what he'll say. Here it is more or less as I wrote it down:

1. Cyclic preferences (she likes B more than A, C more than B, A more than C), starts out holding A, gets offered A-for-B for 50 cents, then B-for-C, then C-for-A, takes each one as it comes with no thought about what's next, ends up with A and $1.50 poorer. **Irrational.** His line: she paid to end up exactly where she started.

2. Real change of taste: prefers A today, will prefer B next week, knows it, and the change is genuine rather than confusion. Offered the swap today and the swap back next week at 50 cents each; takes both; ends up holding B, $1 poorer. **Not irrational.** Nothing cycles at any one time, and each trade was fine when she made it.

3. Same cycle as (1), but all three trades are announced in advance; she does the backward induction and bails out at the last one, so she ends with C and $1 poorer. **Irrational** — and I wrote "???" next to this one, because she did escape the pump. His reason: from where she was sitting at the start, keeping A and paying nothing beat where she ended up, so she should have refused the first trade as well.

4. An expected-utility agent with perfectly coherent, transitive preferences over gambles takes a run of bets that are fair by her lights and loses, because the world went a way she'd given low probability. **Not irrational.** You can't call someone irrational for being unlucky.

5. Someone whose preferences over gambles break independence, Allais-ish, gets offered a sequence of trades, each of which she prefers at the time, that leaves her holding a lottery that's worse for her than what she started with however things turn out. **Irrational**, with a remark I only half followed about how expected-utility maximizers are the only people who can't be moneypumped that way.

6. An agent who foresees she's about to be offered the cycle from (1) pays 50 cents up front to make the trades impossible — signs something, cancels the account, whatever. **Not irrational.** He seemed almost approving.

Going back over it, what strikes me is that every single time he's said irrational, the person actually went through with at least some of the trades. He's never once been handed a cycler who just sits there.

Here's the case I want to bring next week. Same three items, same cycle, starts with A. This time the guy who shows up offers to *pay her* $2 to move A to B, then $2 to move B to C, then $2 to move C to A. He is trying to run a money pump and he has got the direction of her ranking backwards, so it backfires. She's told the whole sequence in advance, works out that taking all three leaves her with A and $6, which by her own ranking is the best place the sequence can leave her, adopts exactly that plan, and does it; if he keeps offering she'll keep going round at $2 a step indefinitely. So she's $6 up and holding what she began with. And — this is the bit I keep going round on — if you look only at her choices she always moves to a bundle with strictly more money in it, so you could describe her as an agent with perfectly tidy transitive preferences over bundles who takes free money when it's offered. She never pays to go anywhere. The sequence costs her nothing.

So does he say irrational? My hunch is he has to, because everything he's said so far has been about the shape of her preferences and not about whether she got hurt — but then I can't see what the criticism is even supposed to be, since by her own reckoning she took the best deal on the table. The yes-or-no matters less to me than the rest of it: which rule is he actually running, what other rule fits those six verdicts just as well, what each of them predicts here, and what single further case would make him show which one it is. And tell me this too — if I ran the identical story with the payments going the other way, 50 cents a step so she finishes $1.50 down, would his verdict change? Because if it does, then whether she's irrational depends on a stranger happening to turn up with the right offer, and that seems like a definately bad place for him to end up.
