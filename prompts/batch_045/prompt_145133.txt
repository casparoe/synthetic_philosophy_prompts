I run a tiny Sunday-night philosophy thing — six or seven of us, mostly people from my climbing gym plus my mate Priya from work — where we read whatever free stuff we can find online (encyclopedia entries, blog posts, the odd recorded lecture) and then argue about it. Nobody has any academic background, me included, I do logistics for a furniture company, and I definately don't keep track of who said what, so please don't build anything on names, papers or citations: half the group wouldn't recognise them and I've no way to check them anyway.

We've been going round in circles on particularism vs generalism for about a month and I want to write one proper exam-style question for the group to do in 90 minutes (max ~900 words) to force the issue, because right now everyone is just restating their gut reaction with bigger words. What I'd like from you is: the question as it would appear on the handout, a model answer of around 800 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points with a one-line note for each on how someone like us would typically get it wrong. Call the level "keen amateurs who've actually done the reading but couldn't tell you the literature."

Here's what I want the question to turn on, since it's the bit I can't get anyone to engage with. In my own notes I've started splitting cases into fade-cases and flip-cases. A fade-case is one where a feature that's a reason for doing something is still a reason for doing it in the new case, just weaker or outweighed — you promised, but something more important came up, so the promise still counts for something. A flip-case is one where the very same feature seems to switch sides: that an action would give someone pleasure is normally a reason to do it, but when the pleasure just is pleasure at someone else being humiliated, it looks like a reason against. Generalists, as I understand them, have easy machinery for fade-cases (weighing, "pro tanto", "normally", "in the absence of defeaters") — so the question should make people say why flip-cases are the real battleground and why they aren't just fade-cases with a bigger weight on the other side. Then I want it to push further: make them give the strongest generalist reply to flip-cases rather than a strawman (something like: principles are defaults whose failure calls for an explanation, or the principle should be stated over a more finely described feature, or the hedges themselves have content), say what a hedged principle would have to do in order to actually guide someone who can't already tell whether their case is a normal one — otherwise isn't it either empty or useless? — and then decide whether this whole argument is really about what reasons there are at all, or really about something else, like what moral judgement, advice, or teaching a person to see a case could even consist in. That last bit matters to me because it's the part where particularism starts to sound like it's making a general claim of its own, and I can't tell if that's a fatal problem or just a confusion about levels.

Two more things. Please make it a question that punishes fence-sitting: the marking scheme should cap an answer that says "it depends what you mean by a principle" and then lists both sides at, say, 9 or 10 out of 20, and the model answer should come down on one side — I want a definite verdict on which version of the dispute is the live one and what follows from it for how we should argue about actual cases, not a menu. And at the end give me a sentence or two on where your model answer is leaning on a contested reading of what generalism is even claiming, so I know what I'm signing the group up for before I hand it out.
