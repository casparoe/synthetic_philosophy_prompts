I hold a version of non-analytic moral naturalism, and I want it stress-tested rather than summarized. The view, as I hold it: normative properties are identical with, or constituted by, natural properties, even though no normative term is synonymous with any natural term; the open question argument therefore shows nothing about identity, any more than the openness of the question whether water is H2O shows that water is not H2O, and the charge of committing the naturalistic fallacy lands only against definitional versions of the view, which almost nobody holds. The cost is that the identification has to be made out a posteriori, via the causal-functional role that a property such as goodness plays in a well-specified practice of deliberation, assessment, and sanction.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the evidence, arguments, cases, and results that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to distinguish what a committed defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it from what only its critics say counts. Please engage these four directly, saying for each whether it is a real defeater or merely verbal:

First, a Twin Earth construction of my own. Two communities are alike in every natural respect my account could appeal to — same neurophysiology, environments, and reinforcement histories — yet their practices settle on different properties: each tracks a distinct natural property with a term playing the goodness-role, and each retains its own under ideal reflection rather than converting. If no further natural fact settles which community is tracking the real normative property, the identity claim looks to me either false or empty, because reference floats free of the facts I wanted to identify goodness with. What does the naturalist say here, and is the answer fatal or merely costly?

Second, deliberative authority. When an agent concludes that she has reason to act, the state she ends up in is supposed to have authority over her and to partly constitute her acting for that reason. A property specified purely in third-personal causal-functional terms may play the role in the practice without binding anyone. I find this the hardest gap in my own view to close, and I would like to know whether it is a genuine defeater or a leftover intuition the naturalist is entitled to explain away.

Third, whether the position risks anything at all. Does it predict something non-naturalism does not — that normative disagreement is empirical in character, that a completed social science could in principle settle which acts are wrong, that normative properties do causal-explanatory work — and would the failure of any of those be decisive or merely absorbed?

Fourth, the relation to the is-ought gap proper. Even granting the identity, descriptive premises still cannot entail a normative conclusion unless the identity itself is among them, and it is a substantive synthetic claim, not a logical truth. I sometimes suspect my view wins the metaphysics and loses the logic. Is that a problem for me, or just the shape of any synthetic identification?

Also say what the view stands or falls with outside ethics — the normativity of meaning, mental content, reasons for belief — and whether the moves that answer the open question argument, the demand for reduction, and the indeterminacy of which natural property is in question have insulated the position beyond repair, and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of my question. Tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
