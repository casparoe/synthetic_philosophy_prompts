I write a monthly column on decision science, and I am assembling a special issue on escalation of commitment — the machinery by which an institution keeps funding a course of action its own analysts have already written off, of the kind Barry Staw documented in his 1976 study in *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance*, where the people who had personally chosen the failing course were the ones who increased their investment after bad news. I would like to open the issue with a short story rather than another explainer, and I want to commission it from you.

The setting is a procurement auction: a decommissioned polar research station, offered as a fifteen-year lease by a government agency that took the auction design from a consulting economist on the theory that it surfaces true valuations. The rules are the ones that make the auction notorious. The highest bid wins the lease; every bidder forfeits their final bid whether or not they win; bids rise in fixed increments; and the auction closes only after a stretch of time in which no new bid arrives. Both bidders understand the rules. Both sides' own analysts, independently, put the net value of the lease at roughly 3.1 million.

What interests me is a wrinkle I have not seen written about. The procurement portal sells two optional instruments. First, a *lasso*: for a nonrefundable fee, a bidder may install a hard ceiling on their own future bids, enforced by the software, which will simply reject anything above it. A ceiling can be raised but never lowered, and each raise costs a fresh nonrefundable fee — the first raise costs double the original lasso fee, the second double that, and so on. Second, a *peek*: for a small flat fee a bidder may learn the other side's current ceiling. The portal never reveals whether a peek has been taken, but it publishes, at the close, the anonymized count of peeks purchased.

I want you to build the story around two people: the foundation executive who bought a low lasso early as a public gesture of discipline and then announced the acquisition to donors at a gala before the auction had closed, and the negotiator on the other side, who has read the literature on this auction, can state in advance exactly how the trap closes, and bids anyway. The escalation has to come entirely from the structure and from the prior public commitment — nobody in the story may be stupid or ignorant, and no character may say sunk cost, escalation of commitment, or the name of the auction out loud. Show the concepts; do not name them.

Formally: third person, past tense, close third on the negotiator; roughly 1,400 to 1,600 words; two scenes plus a brief coda; an opening epigraph of sixty to eighty words of portal rule-text in the dry register of a procurement terms page. At least once, on the page, put the actual numbers in front of the reader — the bid, the value of the lease, what each side has already forfeited in bids and fees — rather than summarizing them. I also want the lasso to do real work in the story: buying the ceiling is itself a sunk cost, and I want the reader to feel that raising it registers as a fresh loss rather than as part of the original one. The peek fee should matter structurally too, not as decoration. Do not let anyone walk away wise in the final paragraph; give me a last image of the mechanism still running, and no narration that winks at the reader.

What I will be looking for when I read it: a reader who knows this auction should be able to name the exact increment at which the trap closes, and should notice that the lasso does not free anyone but changes who pays and when they realize it. A reader who knows nothing about it should simply feel the dread.
