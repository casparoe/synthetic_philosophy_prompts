The question, as I set it:

Q3 (20 marks). The Nyāya school recognises four pramāṇas. (a) Name them, and say what a pramāṇa is. (b) An opponent argues that upamāna is not a separate pramāṇa at all: in the stock example, going into the forest, seeing an animal and coming to know that it is the animal the villager called a gavaya is supposedly just perception of a resembling object plus memory of the earlier description, and anything more you want to say is inference. Explain Nyāya's account of upamāna and how it answers this objection. (c) Nyāya's account of what makes a cognition a pramā (as opposed to a mere cognition, or a mistake) is bound up with its position on whether validity is intrinsic (svataḥ) or extrinsic (parataḥ). Explain that position, contrast it with the Mīmāṃsā one, and say what it implies about whether an accurate memory counts as pramā. (d) Does tarka — reasoning of the form "if there were no fire on the hill there would be no smoke either; but there is smoke" — count as an extra pramāṇa for Nyāya? Why or why not?

And here is the answer, copied out as written (typos included):

(a) A pramā is a true belief, and a pramāṇa is just a reliable way of getting one. Nyāya says there are exactly four of these. First pratyakṣa, perception, which occurs when a sense organ comes into contact with an object. Second anumāna, inference: you notice the hetu, the mark, like smoke on the hill, and you apply the vyāpti, the exceptionless concomitance between the mark and the thing to be proved, in this case fire. Third upamāna, comparison — the stock case being that you're told by someone that a gavaya is an animal rather like a cow, you later see one in the forest, and you recognise it. Fourth śabda, testimony, which only counts as a pramāṇa if the speaker is an āpta, someone trustworthy who isn't lying or confused. Other schools add extras like arthāpatti and anupalabdhi, but Nyāya's claim is that all of those reduce to one of the four.

(b) The objector's idea is that perception plus memory does all the work: you see the animal, you remember the sentence you were told, and that's it. Nyāya's reply is that what the case actually produces is the cognition that the animal now in front of you resembles a cow — that resemblance-cognition is the real deliverance here, and it isn't supplied by perception, which only gives you the individual animal, nor by memory, which only replays the old sentence, nor by inference, since inference requires a vyāpti and none is available. So a separate instrument is needed, and that is upamāna. It also cannot be assimilated to śabda, because śabda requires a trustworthy speaker who is actually addressing you in the here and now, and in the forest nobody is speaking to you.

(c) On the question of what makes a cognition a pramā: Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā agree on svataḥ prāmāṇya, the view that a cognition is valid in virtue of itself and is to be taken as knowledge unless and until something defeats it. They disagree about invalidity. Nyāya holds svataḥ aprāmāṇya — if a cognition is wrong, that wrongness shows up in the cognition itself — whereas Mīmāṃsā holds that invalidity has to be established from outside, by a later defeating cognition that reveals the earlier one was mistaken. This is why, in practice, Nyāya tests whether a cognition really was a pramā extrinsically: we infer it from whether acting on the cognition gets us what we expected, the so-called pravṛttisāmarthya. The same point explains why memory is not pramā: a recollection can be perfectly accurate, but since pramā must be about something not already cognised (anadhigata), even a true memory doesn't qualify.

(d) Tarka is not a separate pramāṇa. It presupposes the very vyāpti it would need in order to establish anything, so on its own it cannot deliver new knowledge; at best it is a species of anumāna and is already covered by it. Because tarka can't establish anything new, Nyāya treats it as a purely dialectical device for winning arguments, with no role in the actual production of knowledge.
