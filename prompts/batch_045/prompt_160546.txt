I'm a novelist, about two thirds through a draft, and I could use someone to think at me about path dependence for an hour.

My book has a what-if spine. My protagonist doesn't get a second life exactly; she gets a ledger — a box of documents from the version of the world where she said yes instead of no to one thing at twenty-two, and everythin after that forks. I want the fork to feel earned rather than like a trick, and the more I think about it the more I realize I don't actually know how much weight one decision is allowed to bear.

So: read It's a Wonderful Life for me as a claim about path dependence, and be concrete about the mechanism. The whole emotional engine of the film is that one person's absence is load-bearing for everything downstream — George doesn't save his brother from the ice, so the brother never grows up to save the troopship; George isn't at the drugstore, so the druggist poisons a kid and goes to prison; nobody runs the Building and Loan, so Potter's monopoly takes the town and you get Pottersville instead of Bedford Falls. Some of those are real cascades with a mechanism you can point at. Others look like the film just needing the counterfactual to be grim, and moralizing it — neon signs and bars instead of an actual argument about what would have happened.

That's the part I care about. The path-dependence story is that small early events get locked in by increasing returns and institutional feedback, and the rival story is that history routes around the hole and the same outcome arrives anyway by another route. Which one is the film actually depicting? Is George load-bearing as a person, or as a slot — the guy who happened to occupy the anti-monopoly function that somebody else would have filled? Potter's capital and the depression are structural; is Pottersville overdetermined by those, with George just the local accident of who pushed back?

And then tell me what that means for my draft. I've been playing with what I think of as second-order lock-in: my character can't go back to the original path not because the door shut but because the person she became on the new path is built for it — her skills, relationships and self-story are all investments that only pay off where she now is. Is that a real instance of the thing, or am I borrowing the vocabulary?

Please commit. Don't give me the two readings side by side and wish me luck — tell me which way you read the film, tell me where it cheats or where the honest critic has to say the reading doesn't fit, and tell me concretely what to do with my fork: how much sensitivity to build in, whether every downstream change needs a traceable chain or whether some can be atmosphere, and how much a novelist can get away with. Give reasons, and say where you're going out on a limb versus where you're confident.
