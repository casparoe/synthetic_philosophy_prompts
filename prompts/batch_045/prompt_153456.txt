I'm in one of those modern Stoicism groups — weekly video call, half therapy-adjacent, half productivity — and the house slogan is basically "control what you can control, accept what you can't." (Ancient version: some things are up to us and some aren't.) It got used twice last week in ways that bugged me, and now I want to know whether the slogan can actually bear the weight people put on it.

Case one: someone announced they've stopped following the news and aren't going to phonebank this cycle, because the election result isn't up to them and caring about it is just volunteering for misery.

Case two: a member described being furious about how her manager spoke to her in a meeting, and was told that her reaction is the only thing up to her there, so she's choosing the anger. That felt about one step from blaming her for being angry at something she had every reason to be angry about.

I've been trying to precisify it and I keep getting different animals:
- up to you = you have causal leverage over it, you can bring it about
- up to you = what your own say-so settles: you can decide to try, you can't decide that you succeed
- up to you = what you can secure come what may, guaranteed regardless of how things go
- up to you = what's attributable to you, i.e. what you could sensibly be praised or blamed for
- and then something that isn't a claim about control at all, but about where to put your attention and what to let your peace of mind depend on

Plus a quantifier problem I can't get past: nobody controls anything to degree zero or degree one. I have some leverage over the election, over how that meeting goes, over whether I spiral at 3am — and I probably have less control over my own gut reactions than the slogan implies. So read as a binary division of the world into things I control and things I don't, it looks straightforwardly false; and the modern "trichotomy" fix (some things we only partly control) turns it into a continuum, which seems to weaken it in a different way.

So: lay out the menu of precise theses this one-liner could be expressing, including readings I haven't thought of, and for each say whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, or just false. Then the part I actually care about — which reading would each of my two cases need in order to work, and does that reading survive? Does anything on the menu really support "so don't bother acting" or "so the anger is your fault," or do both uses smuggle in an extra premise the slogan doesn't supply? And if it collapses into something trivial or something false on every reading, say that and tell me what I should say instead at the next call.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question. American English, and keep it as a menu I could read out to the group.
