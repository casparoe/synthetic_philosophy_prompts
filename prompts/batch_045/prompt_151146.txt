I'm retired — used to run a molecular spectroscopy lab — and I've been reading moral philosophy for fun for a couple of years now. Scanlon's contractualism and this aggregation business is where I keep stalling, because in my old work you took a probability, multiplied it by a magnitude, and summed over everything; I cant see what's supposed to be wrong with adding people up.

The chapter on Scanlon in a popular book I bought, and the guy who runs my reading group (he did a philosophy MA), both give roughly this picture:

"Contractualism says an act is wrong if it's ruled out by a principle that someone could reasonably reject. Because rejectability is assessed one person at a time, numbers don't count: contractualism is stuck saying that one person's objection defeats the objections of any number of people each bearing a smaller cost, so it can forbid saving fifty lives in order to spare one person a modest inconvenience. That's the aggregation problem, and it's generally taken to refute the view. Really it's just social contract theory in the old sense — morality as what self-interested parties would agree to if they had to pick rules in advance, Hobbes with a Rawlsian veil thrown over it — and since you judge principles from the worst position, its practical content is just maximin: make the worst off as well off as possible."

I dont have the background to tell a fair picture from a caricature. My suspicions, for what their worth: the bit about one person vetoing fifty sounds like it's doing more work than it should, and the Hobbes-and-Rawls bit sounds like a different theory that happens to share the name "contract." So: which parts would someone who actually defends contractualism accept, which are common simplifications a professional would wince at, which are things the view explicitly distances itself from — and does the "refutes the view" verdict survive once the rest of the summary is cleaned up, or was that a standard objection dressed up as a knockdown?
