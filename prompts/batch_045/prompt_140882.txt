I'm a journalist working on a piece about the claim that history is written by the victors, and the version of it I've ended up holding is narrower than the slogan and, I think, checkable. Call it the archival-capture thesis: victory matters for the historical record not mainly because winners lie or because interpreters slant their reading, but because victory is correlated with the material fate of the sources. Winners keep their archives, maintain the institutions that transmit their account, and have the resources to produce and preserve documents; losers' papers are destroyed, scattered, or never written at all. On this version the mechanism sits upstream of interpretation, and it yields a prediction the bare slogan does not: where a loser's record survives largely intact and is accessible, the received account should drift toward the loser's framing, and the size of that drift should track how much material survived rather than how sympathetic anyone finds the loser.

What I want is a map of the position's exposure. First: which findings would be decisive against it, and which would merely be awkward? The case I keep coming back to is the one where the Wehrmacht lost the war but appeared to win the postwar memory of it, with defeated German officers' memoirs and the operational histories they were commissioned to write shaping Western accounts of the Eastern Front for decades. That looks like a counterexample, but I can't tell whether it refutes my thesis or only the slogan, since on my account the question is which mechanism did the work — and there the winner's archives were never captured so much as the losers had a grievance, leisure, and publishers.

Second: what would someone who holds the thesis concede as genuinely counting against it, as opposed to what its critics say counts? Third: is it falsifiable at all, or does it collapse into something insulated? If any surviving source can itself be written off as a product of power, then no recovery of lost material could ever count as evidence for it, and the strong claim becomes indistinguishable from the trivial one. If that escape route is built in, say whether it is a legitimate feature or a sign the position has decayed.

For what it's worth, what would move me most is a case where a loser's archive survived in quantity and the victor's version still won, for reasons with nothing to do with archives. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and flag how confident you are in each claim you make, singling out the one you are least sure of.
