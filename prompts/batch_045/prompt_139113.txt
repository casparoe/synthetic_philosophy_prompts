My brother had a corpus callosotomy at twenty-four, for epilepsy that no drug would touch, and for most of my childhood the family picture of it was two houses with the telephone line cut. That picture has not survived what I have read since, and I am now putting together a short reading for the epilepsy clinic's annual evening, which he will attend, so nothing plaintive. I want one poem in it that gets the difficulty right rather than smoothing it over, and I would be grateful for your help with it.

Here is what I have in mind. A poem of fourteen lines, no fixed meter and no required rhyme, divided eight and six with the turn at line nine, in which the odd lines and the even lines are two separate poems. Read down the odd lines you have one continuous utterance; read down the even lines you have another; if either set were printed alone on its own page it would still read as a poem. Neither speaker ever sees the line beside it. The reader, going across, is the only party who ever has both.

The two voices should be the two sides of a chimeric-stimulus experiment, and I would rather not restage the snow scene and the shovel. I keep coming back to my mother's account of my brother's own session: a photograph of a winter road shown to his right hemisphere, a tray of objects, his left hand closing on a key, and then a fluent and entirely wrong explanation in which the key had to do with the cabin whose door he had been shown on the other side. The even-line speaker should get that narrating voice: causal connectives, reasons, the composure of someone who is never at a loss, and nothing that refers to the photograph itself — only to what the hand did, and a story about why. The odd-line speaker gets the other: no because, no noun that names the thing it is reaching for, only this and that, edges, temperatures, what the thing is for in the hand, and no way to say which picture it saw.

The structural claim I want the form to carry, and tell me plainly if it cannot: that the reader cannot help making the interleaved lines into a single poem, and that this making is the same trick the narrating voice performs on the hand — a unity manufactured after the fact by a party who was not there. The turn at line nine should be where a careful reader notices that they have been doing it, and the closing two lines should set the voices side by side one last time without letting either of them become the person.

Two things to avoid. Do not let the poem drift into the popular mythology about the hemispheres, the analytic one and the creative one; whatever differs here differs in report and speech, not in temperament. And do not let the division be too clean: the classic experiments were compromised by everything the two sides still share and by the patients' ability to cue each other across the gap, so the poem should leave it genuinely open whether his surgery divided one subject into two or divided perception and access while leaving the subject intact. I want to be able to say this in front of a neurologist without being wrong about anything, and in front of my brother without asking for his pity.
