I teach a first-year seminar called Perception and Reality — fourteen students, mostly in their first term, a mix of prospective psychology majors and prospective philosophy majors, no assumed background, one 80-minute session, a whiteboard and nothing else by way of technology, and about forty dollars of budget for materials (cardboard, cloth bags or a screen, blindfolds, anything I can pick up at a hardware store). I want to build one session around the old question of whether someone blind from birth who is suddenly enabled to see could, by sight alone and before touching anything, tell which of two objects set before him is the cube he had previously handled and which the globe. I got into a long argument about this with a friend last night and came away convinced I don't yet know how to teach it. Her view: whatever the question was originally meant to be, it was a conjecture about how human minds actually work, we now have real cases of sight being restored to people who had been blind since early infancy, those cases are messy but lean towards failure or at best slow partial success, and that is the answer. My view: the cases don't touch what was at issue, which is whether the content of seeing and the content of touching have anything in common in virtue of which a shape could be the same shape for both senses — and no data about how quickly someone learns to pair the two could settle that, since a creature could learn the pairing perfectly while the two experiences shared nothing, or share everything and still fail to pair them. She replied that a question no possible outcome would settle isn't a question. We went round this perhaps six times; neither of us moved; and the thing I most want is for my students to run into that deadlock rather than be told that it exists.

So here is what I need designed. First, a procedure they can actually perform — no lecture at the front beforehand, and they should not hear the historical case or any philosopher's name until the last fifteen minutes: the situation has to be one they reconstruct for themselves out of what they were asked to do. I have in mind a tactile-first phase (objects handled in bags or behind a screen, unseen) followed by a visual array and a matching task, with some scoring scheme so that there is something at stake, but I'm open to a better structure. Second — and this is the part I care about most — I want the materials arranged so that the task pulls apart three questions that my friend and I kept collapsing into one: whether a handled object can be *identified* by sight among candidates; whether, from touch alone, its visual appearance can be *predicted* in advance (I thought of having them choose from a sheet of candidate outlines or silhouettes while still in the tactile phase, which would make the prediction behavioural rather than something they merely claim); and whether the *similarity structure* of the two senses agrees — i.e. whether objects that feel alike look alike and vice versa. To get at the third one I'd want objects rigged so that haptic and visual similarity come apart: pairs that are nearly indistinguishable by hand but plainly different to the eye, and pairs that look alike but feel different, alongside at least one easy control pair. Please specify a concrete set of objects I can make or buy and what each pair is doing. Then a debrief that maps specific moments and specific failure patterns onto those three questions, says which of the three the exercise actually yields evidence about and which it merely dramatises, and shows where in the session my friend would point and where I would point.

Two more things. I know the exercise rests on a confound that no classroom can remove: my students have a lifetime of calibration between hand and eye, and their success or failure tells us nothing directly about a subject who has none. I'd like that confound made visible and useful rather than apologised for — ideally so that the students notice their own reliance on learned correlations and can then be asked what follows for someone who lacks them — and I'd like you to be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will therefore have to say myself. And tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run it: the ways students will defeat the design (labelling the objects verbally in the tactile phase, shaping the task into something easier than the one I set, treating it as a memory test), what to do about each, and what would count as the session having failed — for instance if they come away thinking the answer is obvious, or thinking the whole thing is just about how good people are at feeling shapes. Finally, give me a minute-by-minute timeline including setup, and don't add a reading list or any citations: I have that side covered; I just want the session.
