I'm putting together a short paper — and the reading-group session that goes with it — on whether the "easy knowledge" worry attaches to bootstrapping as such, or only to a *passive* version of it, and for that I need a case built to order rather than another variant of the familiar fuel-gauge story. Here is the shape of my puzzle. In the standard complaint, an agent with no antecedent reason to trust an instrument reads it, notes that it reports "full", infers that it is accurate on this occasion, repeats a few times, concludes that it is reliable, and thereby comes to know that the tank really is full without ever checking the tank. The reasoning feels empty: the instrument's own outputs do all the work, and the conclusion is then used to underwrite the very outputs it was derived from. What strikes me is that every version of this I can think of features a completely passive observer — someone who never touches the quantity being measured and whose only grip on that quantity comes from the instrument itself. I want to know whether that passivity is doing hidden work, and whether letting the agent *intervene* — doing things which, from her own agency plus ordinary causal background knowledge, she knows will change the quantity, without her measuring it — opens a route from the instrument's own outputs to a justified, and possibly knowledgeable, belief that the instrument is reliable.

So build me the case. One paragraph, realistic and non-science-fictional: a garage, a kitchen, a clinic, a lab bench, a field site. The instrument should be ordinary and highly reliable but plainly fallible, not stipulated to be infallible. The agent must begin with no reason at all to trust it: no manual, no track record, no expert's word, no second instrument, and no independent knowledge of the quantity's actual values. Her route to "this instrument is reliable" may run only through (a) the instrument's own outputs, (b) her knowledge of her own intentional interventions, and (c) mundane causal knowledge of the sort any competent adult has. It would be cheating to smuggle in a dipstick, a second gauge, a remembered calibration, or a look at the tank; it would also be cheating to make the intervention so informative that it simply hands her the quantity's value, since then the instrument is idle and you no longer have a bootstrapping case at all. Ideally the interventions should yield no more than ordinal or difference information — that the quantity went up, went down, went back — rather than a magnitude the agent could compare the readings against. Alongside the main case I want its passive twin: the same instrument, the same number of readings, the same conclusion, but an agent who merely watches while the quantity varies on its own, so that the only difference is the intervention.

After the scenarios, give me the demonstration. Walk the interventionist chain step by step and say, for each step, exactly why it is not circular in the way the standard reasoning is — or, if you think it is circular anyway, say where the circularity re-enters and why my contrast fails. Then tell me whether the agent *knows* the instrument is reliable by the end, and whether she thereby knows the target proposition the instrument reports; and run the two obvious verdicts past each other — the permissive reliabilist who says she knows because the process that produced the belief is in fact reliable, and the anti-easy-knowledge theorist who says the reasoning is non-incremental and so yields at most justification — giving the strongest thing whichever view loses can say, since I want a case where both responses are intelligible rather than one that settles the question by stipulation. Then isolate the feature doing the work: is it the non-observational knowledge of her own actions, the causal background knowledge, the difference-making structure of the interventions, or something else; and show what happens to the case when you delete that feature, which is what the passive twin is for. Finally, be honest about how far the route gets her: say whether what she acquires is full reliability or only responsiveness to the quantity, what residual gap remains if the instrument could be systematically offset, badly scaled, or unreliable outside the range she manipulates, and whether the interventionist bootstrap is a genuine answer to the easy-knowledge problem or just bootstrapping with extra steps.
