I'm a science journalist working on a piece about why expert disagreement over AI timelines never seems to resolve, and I keep bumping into this line: you can't agree to disagree. A forecaster I interviewed used it to rule out the idea that two well-informed people who respect each other can just permanently sit at 15% and 60% on the same question. His move was basically: if they both know each other's number and neither one moves, something has gone wrong — somebody is motivated reasoning, or posturing, or isn't really an expert. That felt like a lot of weight for one sentence to carry, and I want to know whether it can carry it.

Can you give me the menu of precise things that sentence might be claiming, and for each one say whether it's a platitude, a substantive claim that needs real assumptions, or just false? The part I actually care about: which reading would he need in order to get from "they've both seen each other's numbers and haven't budged" to "at least one of them is being irrational or dishonest," and does that reading hold up.

I'd like each reading tested against these cases, because his inference seems to work differently on each:

1. Two forecasters at 15% and 60% who are quietly answering different questions — one is conditioning on nobody regulating, the other isn't, and neither says so out loud.
2. Two people who agree on every first-order fact but weight a class of base rates differently — say, how much the long history of failed AI predictions should move you.
3. A case I made up that I think is the interesting one: two researchers swap estimates by email on a system where delivery receipts are unreliable, so each knows the other's number but neither is sure the other knows that they know. My gut says they can sit there disagreeing indefinitely without either being irrational, but I can't say why.
4. Two scientists who aren't even carving up the possibility space the same way — one's hypotheses are lab accident vs natural spillover, the other's are spillover at the market vs spillover somewhere else — so there may not be a single proposition they have differing probabilities about.

Two more things I need sorted. First, somewhere in the background there's an assumption that both sides started from the same prior; is the slogan secretly a claim about where priors come from rather than about disagreement at all? If so, that reframes my whole piece — it'd be a story about whether shared priors are even available to people with different training, not a story about who's being dishonest. Second, is any of this a prescription (keep talking, split the difference, defer to whoever has the better track record) or is it purely a claim about what a certain static state of mutual knowledge looks like, with no advice in it?

Give me the readings as a list with verdicts, and say for each whether it would do the work my interviewee wanted it to do.
