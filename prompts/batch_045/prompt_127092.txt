For a graduate reading group that sits between philosophy of mind and the emerging work on artificial welfare, I am putting together a one-page decision tree that sorts people by which computations they think carry moral weight. I would like you to build it. The audience is people who already know the standard labels and will not be impressed by a list of isms, so the substance has to be in the diagnostic questions and in the shape of the space they carve out.

Constraints on the artefact:

Build it from the logical space, not from a catalogue of names. Start by fixing six to eight branch questions, state for each what answer-combinations it permits, and only then place the actual positions in the resulting cells. Where a permitted combination has no occupant, mark the cell as empty and say in one line why you think nobody lives there: because it is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. Where two positions fall in the same cell, say so explicitly and say whether any further question could in principle separate them or whether they are genuinely the same view wearing different vocabulary.

Every branch must be labelled with the claim that separates it from its siblings, and every question must be phrased so that a person with no theoretical vocabulary could answer it from a concrete case. No node may ask anything of the form do you think machines could be conscious, or any other question that asks respondents to self-apply a label. Nodes should be answerable from what someone says about cases.

The fault lines I want the tree to cover: whether the implementation relation is privileged at all or is observer-relative in the way the triviality arguments suggest; if it is privileged, what privileges it (fine-grained causal structure, robustness of the mapping under counterfactual perturbation, semantic content or teleological function, or the computation actually doing causal work in driving behaviour); the grain problem, where the same system implements one computation at its coarse states and another at its fine states; whether a computation that is causally inert with respect to behaviour, as in a replay from recorded inputs, still counts; whether an implementation that is spatially scattered, very slow, very fast, or interrupted and resumed counts; whether two implementations of the same computation, or one system implementing it redundantly in duplicate, yield one subject, two, or none; and, at the terminal layer, whether moral weight tracks consciousness alone or also requires something further such as valenced states, desires, or a temporally extended self-model.

Use at least four of the following cases, and add your own where a fault line needs one: a crowd of a million people each simulating one neuron of a human brain and passing signals by radio; a brain emulation archived and re-run so that its outputs control nothing; the same emulation run at a thousand times real speed; a single processor hosting two systems by time-slicing, each robustly implementing its own computation at the coarse grain; a system whose coarse states implement one mind while its fine states implement a different one; and a system causally isomorphic to a human brain but hooked to sensors and effectors of a wholly different kind, so that the structural role of each state is the same while what the states are about is not.

Required leaves, with whatever names or unnamed labels you think correct: a substrate-independent computationalism that still insists on a privileged implementation relation; a view on which the relevant biology or biophysics is non-substitutable; a structural-identity view on which what matters is intrinsic cause-effect structure and the computational description is inessential; a view on which no computation matters intrinsically because computational descriptions are attributed rather than found; a view on which only computations with the right content or proper function matter; and at least one leaf for the position that nothing here matters because there are no real phenomenal states to be had. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I also want at least two leaves that you construct rather than report, marked as unnamed, for combinations you think deserve a defender.

Finally, add a short note on the order of the branches: which question you put at the root and why, what the tree would look like if a different question came first, and whether any node is genuinely prior to the others or whether the ordering is presentational only.
