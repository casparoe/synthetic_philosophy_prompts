I'm writing a short paper on moral worth for a seminar — the rough idea being that whether an action has moral worth depends on the considerations the agent acts on rather than on what the action brings about — and before I read any of the secondary literature I've been writing down my own gut verdicts on cases, to see whether anything coherent comes out. I don't need citations or a reading list; I want the principle. My verdicts, stated as strongly as I feel them: a hospital pharmacist who double-checks every dosage because a malpractice suit would end her career and because the hospital audits errors — right act, no moral worth. The same pharmacist fifteen years later, long after the audits stopped, who double-checks because she can't bear the thought of a patient being hurt and would feel wretched for weeks otherwise — full moral worth; she seems to be acting on exactly the features that make the act right. A man named Dev who returns a dropped wallet because his sister, whose judgment he trustingly defers on moral matters, told him that's what decent people do; he doesn't see the point himself, though he reflectively trusts her and would defend the deference — I say no moral worth, or at most what I've been calling *borrowed credit*. A woman who, after a stroke, finds she has an overpowering urge to return lost property, is initially alarmed by it, then reflects and wholeheartedly endorses it as the right way to live — I lean toward yes, but noticeably less than the second pharmacist. Someone who donates a kidney to a stranger while falsely believing the recipient is a distant cousin, and who would have donated anyway out of general benevolence had he known the truth — I want to say no moral worth, since his actual operative reason was partiality to kin, but the fact that the right reason was sitting right there and would have moved him makes me reluctant, and I can't tell whether that reluctance is sentimental. Two people who do the right thing from both duty and enjoyment and who would still have done it from duty alone — full moral worth in both; the extra enjoyment doesn't seem to contaminate anything. And finally a shop assistant who returns a wallet purely because store policy requires it and she is a stickler for whatever policy is in force, where the policy happens to coincide exactly with what morality requires — no moral worth, and that verdict I feel most confident of.

The principle I've been playing with has two clauses: an act has moral worth iff the agent acts *because of* the features that actually make the act right, and she would still have acted on those features if every other motive of hers had fallen away — I've been calling the second the independence condition. But my cases seem to pull it apart. The stroke patient fails independence, I think, since the urge is doing the motivational work and would keep doing it even if its object were worthless, and yet I don't want to deny her worth outright; so maybe what matters is not where the motive came from but whether she reflectively endorses it. The trouble is that Dev endorses too, in his own deferential way, and I still want to withhold worth from him, which makes me suspect that endorsement does the work only when it is endorsement of the right-making features themselves and not merely of a person or a rule that reliably tracks them — and if that is the repair, I can't see why it doesn't also rescue the policy-following assistant, whom I most want to exclude. That is the pair I keep going back and forth on: Dev and the stroke patient feel structurally identical to me and I judge them differently, and I'd rather be told which verdict to drop than be handed a clause that saves both by being gerrymandered to them.

So: is there one principle — or a short list of serious candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself — that gets these verdicts, and if not, which of them do you think I should overturn and why? For each clause you propose, I'd like to know which case motivates it. I'd also like the principle's verdicts on cases I haven't listed: the same agent acting rightly for the right reason but so near the edge of her self-control that she would very likely have failed had the temptation been somewhat stronger; an agent who does the right thing for the right reason but is wrong about what makes it right (she returns the wallet because she thinks generosity is commanded by a God she takes to exist, and it isn't); a robot or software system trained to treat patients gently, which has no felt reasons at all but responds reliably to the right features; and a committee that reaches the right decision where no individual member acted on the right reason but the aggregation procedure reliably tracks it. And one last thing I can't settle: my verdicts above came in degrees — the stroke patient less than the veteran pharmacist, Dev somewhere above the policy-follower but below both — so I'd like to know whether the right account makes worth scalar, or whether it treats worth as all-or-nothing and explains my degree talk as something else, such as praiseworthiness or credit, wearing moral worth's clothing.
