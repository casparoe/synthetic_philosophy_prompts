Hi — I write about science and ideas, and I'm doing a piece on long-term digital preservation. Halfway through I realized I cant work out what the whole field is assuming about time.

The practice, as I understand it: archives treat decay as one-directional. Files rot; they never un-rot. You restore from a backup made earlier, never from one made later. And past states seem to leave lots of independant traces — the file, the offsite copy, the version in somebody's inbox, the hash in a log — while later states leave none, so corruption can be repaired from earlier copies but never pre-repaired from ones that don't exist yet. Inside the field there's also a split: some institutions preserve bits (migrate the exact file onto fresh media forever), others preserve the recipe (keep the code, the inputs and the random seed, and regenerate the result on demand), and the recipe people talk as if re-deriving something hands you the very same result back rather than a fresh duplicate.

So: what does this practice presuppose about the direction of time? Does it strictly need the thermodynamic arrow plus a low-entropy early universe, or would a merely statistical, usually-true asymmetry do? Does the trace asymmetry do any work that the entropy story can't, or is it the same fact in different clothes? Does the recipe strategy quietly assume that the arrow doesn't matter to whether you've preserved anything at all? And if the arrow turned out to be perspectival rather than a feature of the world, what would actually have to change in how we preserve things — or is the practice underdetermined, and several very different stories about time all license it?
