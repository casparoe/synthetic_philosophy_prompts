The setup: I'm TAing a philosophy of education course, we just did Whitehead (the Principia Mathematica guy who went to Harvard and built process metaphysics, wrote a book on education in the late 1920s, complained about inert ideas, thought learning moved in cycles through phases of romance, then precision, then generalization), and me and two friends — one of whom works at an edtech startup and is definately not neutral here — got into an arguement about a specific proposal that a few schools are apparently piloting. Call it the Lanthorn system: every student gets one persistent AI tutor from age six to eighteen that has read everything they've ever written or said in a school context, keeps a running model of what currently grips them, and chooses what to put in front of them next so as to maximize how long they stay voluntarily engaged. No fixed syllabus, no year groups, students advance when the system judges they've got it; nothing is graded against classmates, instead there's a competency ledger verified by short oral defenses with the tutor; human teachers are kept on only for what the system is bad at — labs, sports, anything requiring bodies in a room, and pastoral stuff.

So what I want is: where would Whitehead actually land on the Lanthorn system, and not in a hand-wavy "he'd be cautiously hopeful" way. Concretely — would he see it as finally killing off inert ideas, or as the most efficient machine ever built for producing them in a new form (perfectly engaging, perfectly tailored, and never once difficult in the way that changes someone)? What does his rhythm of education predict about a system that optimizes for engagement — does it collapse romance and precision into each other, or does it maybe deliver the cycling he wanted better than real schools ever did? Does anything in his metaphysics give him an objection that a plain progressive educator would miss — e.g. the tutor's total memory of the child: is a learner who is completely known to their teacher in a way no human could ever know them a problem for someone who thinks each occasion of experience is partly self-creating and not exhaustively surveyable from outside? And what about the loss of the year group and the shared syllabus, given how much he tied education to the imaginative encounter between the young and the people who've already done the work? Then the part I'm most curious about: where would he embarass his own fans — would he, for instance, be more sympathetic than modern Whiteheadians to a knowledge-rich curriculum, on the grounds that his target was ideas held dead rather than facts possessed? Give me the version where he's repelled and the version where he's weirdly seduced, pick which is more likely, and be explicit about where you're reading off his actual commitments versus where you're flat-out extrapolating and where a 1920s guy just has no resources to have a view at all. Commit to something; don't hedge into mush.
