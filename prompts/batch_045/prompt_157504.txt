Hey — I run a single 90-minute session on war and morality for about 16 third-year undergrads, mostly philosophy and IR majors, plus one ex-army guy and two with family currently serving. One room with movable tables, a printer, no AV I want to depend on, and no budget, so id rather the stakes not be real money. I also really don't want to open with a lecture if I can help it.

The thing I want them to run into, not be told, is the gap between "I'm not to blame for being here" and "you aren't allowed to shoot me." The usual argument for treating combatants on both sides as morally equal leans on an epistemic point: the ordinary soldier can't reasonably be expected to know his side is in the wrong, his government lied to him or fed him selective intel, so he isn't at fault, so he occupies the same moral position as the soldier on the just side. I want them to notice that this line of thought at best delivers an excuse, and to feel how badly an excuse settles anything when the person across from you is still shooting. But I also want them to feel the pull back the other way: if whether you may be targeted depended on how good your evidence actually was, nobody could ever apply that, because from inside the fog you can't tell a deceived conscript from a true believer who knows exactly what he's doing — and both of them look identical down the sights.

So design me something runnable — a game, a role-play, a staged deliberation, whatever works — where every student is a combatant with a sealed briefing, has to decide whether to fire on an enemy position, and only afterwards learns which side was actually in the right, and that some of them were reasonably deceived, some were reckless with evidence they had, and some knew. They should be making the firing decision under roughly the ignorance they'd really have, and I want a later phase where they have to rule on other people's cases on two separate questions — was this person blameworthy, and was this person liable to be shot — and discover for themselves that their answers to those two questions come apart, and come apart in a pattern they didn't expect.

Give me: the rules and roles, what I need to print, a timeline in blocks, a payoff or scoring scheme that works without money, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto (a) the excuse/justification distinction, (b) the epistemic argument for equality, and (c) the identification problem that makes a fine-grained rule unusable. They shouldn't hear the phrase moral equality of combatants until the debrief is underway.

Two more things. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm especially worried about students working out that the briefings are rigged and playing to what they think I'm testing, and about the ex-army guy, who will either be great or will shut down. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: which part of the real argument only lands because people are actually at risk of dying, and which part I have to supply myself at the end.
