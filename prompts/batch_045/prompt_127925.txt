I host a long-form interview program on science and ideas, and next week I'm recording an hour with a researcher who has spent several years arguing — in print, in policy testimony, and in a series of public exchanges I've followed — that debate between AI systems can serve as a scalable oversight method: put two models in front of a judge that is weaker than either of them, human or machine, let one argue for each answer, and the judge can end up supervising work it could not have checked or produced on its own. She is formidably well practiced. Two moves I expect and want to be ready for: that the judge need not be as capable as the debaters, only able to catch a contradiction or a specific false claim that the other side points to; and, whenever the hard cases come up, that this is an empirical question on which we can simply measure judge accuracy.

Draft nine or ten questions for the episode, sequenced so they have an arc rather than a checklist feel. No warm-up questions about how she got into the field, and nothing that lets her restate the proposal. I need questions, not reading lists or citations — assume I will not be able to look anything up mid-interview.

Please cover at least the following:

One question on the asymmetry the whole idea rests on — that checking is easier than generating. Ask what happens in domains where it isn't: long causal chains, contested social-scientific claims, questions of judgment or taste. Her likely answer is that such claims decompose into checkable sub-claims. Press on that with a case that resists decomposition — say, whether a proposed course of action is prudent overall, where every sub-claim can be true and the conclusion still wrong.

One on what I've started calling the shared-blindspot failure. If both debaters are fine-tunes or descendants of the same base model, they inherit the same confident errors, and the space of claims neither will think to challenge is exactly the space where the system is most reliably wrong. Put it to her concretely: two debaters, one base model, and a question in a poorly documented corner of some empirical field where both assert the same falsehood with equal fluency. Who catches it?

One on optimization against the judge. If the debaters are trained to win rather than to be right, and the judge is fixed, the equilibrium should be whatever most reliably manipulates that judge. Ask her what observable difference there would be between a debate the truth won and a debate the judge was fooled in, and follow up on the move I expect — that we train and evaluate on cases where we have ground truth — by pointing out that the cases where oversight is needed are precisely the cases where we don't.

One on something like a control condition. What would it take to show that two debaters plus a judge beats one honest system plus the same judge, and if that comparison is hard to run, why is debate the default rather than the hypothesis?

One on the honest debater's burden: cases where the correct answer is right for reasons the judge cannot follow without reproducing the whole computation or investigation. Does debate help, does it collapse into deference, or does it actively hurt by making a hard answer look like a losing one?

One on the version where the judge is itself a model, since the scheme has to scale eventually: what reason is there to think a model judge's errors are independent of the debaters' errors, given shared training data?

Close with a falsification question — what result would lead her to abandon debate as an oversight method rather than narrow its scope — and then a forward-looking one: if it works as she hopes, what exactly is left for the human in the loop to be doing?

Assume throughout that she will concede limits of scope but never the central claim, and write each question so it is self-contained for a listener who has never encountered the idea.

After each follow-up, add a short line, in your own voice rather than hers, saying how confident you are that the answer you've predicted is the one she would actually give. Then close with a paragraph singling out the question whose premise you are least confident about, explaining what you're unsure of — whether it's your characterization of her position, of how these systems behave, or of what the exchange would even allow — and why.
