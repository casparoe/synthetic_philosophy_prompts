I'm the token philosopher on the committee that signs off on student-data systems here, and there's a proposal in front of us for an "early alert" platform: it pulls swipe-card, LMS and grade data, scores every undergrad weekly for dropout risk, and auto-refers anyone above the cut-off to a required meeting with an advisor. It's own pilot results look good — retention genuinely moved, every advisor in the pilot used it gently, no holds, no discipline, nobody we can find was made worse off. The catch is that the vendor sets the threshold, treats it as trade secret, can move it without telling us, and the score can't be appealed. Half of us say no one is being forced to do anything, so there's no freedom issue here worth blocking over; a couple say it actually *increases* students' freedom, since it gets help to people who'd never ask for it; the rest of us say we'd be installing a standing, unchecked power over students whether or not it's ever used badly, and that's the thing to worry about.

Draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Friday's vote: adopt, reject, or adopt with named conditions and a timeline — pick one and defend it. American English, under 800 words, written so the registrar and the vendor's people could actually act on it. Use whatever of the negative-liberty / non-domination / positive-liberty vocabulary earns its keep, but I don't want a taxonomy dump. Three things I specifically want it to do. First: our draft guidance says we only have to worry about policies that leave students worse off than they'd otherwise have been — say whether that's the right test here and why. Second: someone has floated adding an appeal panel; I want to know what would have to be true of that mechanism for it to remove the objection rather than just move the discretion one floor up. Third: someone will say "just make it opt-in" — I don't think that settles it either way, so don't lean on it. And give the strongest version of whichever case you're rejecting, not a strawman.
