I am drafting the position statement for a dissertation prospectus on standing to blame, and before it goes to my committee I would like the set of commitments below audited rather than summarised. Specifically, I want to know which of them genuinely pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and for each real tension what the least costly repair would be and what that repair would cost me elsewhere.

(a) Whether blame is apt is settled entirely by target-side facts: what she did, whether she was morally competent, and what relationship she stands in to the person wronged. Nothing about the blamer's own record bears on aptness.

(b) Standing is a separate, forfeitable normative status, and you're one to talk can be a genuine objection to someone's blaming rather than a remark about how well the blaming will go down.

(c) There is no clean-hands condition. Having committed the same wrong, or one relevantly similar to it, even while continuing to commit it, does not by itself defeat standing. Blame is not a privilege earned by compliance, and given pervasive complicity a clean-hands rule would silence most criticism of structural wrongs.

(d) Hypocrisy does defeat standing, and hypocrisy is neither mere wrongdoing nor mere insincerity. It consists in invoking a norm one is not willing to be governed by: exempting oneself from a standard one applies to others.

(e) Avowal is sufficient for restoration. A hypocrite who publicly owns the mismatch between her standard and her conduct, without reforming and without ceasing the conduct, is back in a position to blame.

(f) Verdict: the mother who smokes, tells her son that smoking is stupid, freely concedes that she cannot stop, and tells him without evasion to follow her advice rather than her example, lacks standing. Her candour does not help her.

(g) Verdict: a newspaper that carried fossil-fuel advertising for years and now runs editorials blaming consumers for their carbon footprints lacks standing; but the mid-level employee who took part in a fraud, was herself paid under it, and is now cooperating with investigators retains standing to blame her former colleagues.

(h) Standing is a matter of degree, it is independent of what the blaming is likely to achieve, and it constrains only the address and not the underlying judgement that a wrong was done. When standing is defeated, the correct response is not to retract the content of the blame or to fall silent but to change the speech act: avowal, self-implicating criticism, or a complaint made in the first person plural.

Two notes on where I take the trouble to be, so that you can tell me I am wrong about it. I keep worrying that (a) and (b) cannot both be true, although every time I write the distinction out it seems to survive. And my suspicion is that the newspaper half of (g) is where a clean-hands clause is quietly sneaking back in.

What I want back: the tensions ranked by severity rather than listed as equals; for each, the exact commitments in conflict and what would have to be revised to relieve it; an explicit statement of which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why; and, if it comes to it, the single revision that would clean up the most.
