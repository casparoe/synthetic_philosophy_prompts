I'm writing a novel whose composer-protagonist has become convinced by Schopenhauer's claim that music, unlike the other arts, is not a copy of the Platonic Ideas but a direct copy of the will itself, and that the four voices of harmony map onto the grades of the will's objectification: the bass to inorganic nature, the tenor to vegetable life, the alto to animal life, and the soprano — the melody — to human striving. In my draft he extends this scheme with a fifth, silent voice: the rests and silences between phrases, which he takes to correspond not to a further grade of objectification but to the will's self-negation, the ascetic denial of the will to life. Please write a sonnet I can insert as a poem he composes, in which the octave devotes two lines to each of the four voices as they ascend. The volta must be the moment the reader realizes the melody's apparent purposiveness is the same blind urge that sounds in the bass, and that consonance is only the will's temporary satiation rather than a goal attained. The closing couplet should be followed by a single silent line (left blank, or marked with a musical rest) standing for the fifth voice, and the final line should make clear that what must go is the illusion that human ends differ in kind from the striving everywhere else.
