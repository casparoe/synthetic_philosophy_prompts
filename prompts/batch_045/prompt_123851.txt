I clerk for the Independent Commission on Public Remembrance, an advisory body that issues nonbinding opinions on commemorative measures — monuments, curricula, museum exhibits, restitution-linked memorials, commemorative criminal law — proposed by states, municipalities, firms, and museums. It was created to compare four traditions, in its own shorthand the German, Japanese, Turkish, and Southern patterns, and the opinions below are drawn recognizably from all four without naming any of them. It has issued five opinions, each with its ground stated in a single line:

1. A state's proposed standard textbook sentence: "great suffering was brought upon the peoples of neighboring countries," with no agent named. Rejected. Ground: a narrative with no agent in it cannot be the object of anyone's acknowledgment.
2. A monument to a general of the losing side, standing on a county courthouse lawn since 1909, which the town council voted 4–3 to keep while adding a marker naming the cause as the defense of slavery. The Commission declined to order removal over the council's vote, but also objected to the state legislature's attempt to mandate removal; it required that the decision be remade by the town after a scheduled period of open hearings. Ground: what a community keeps in its own common spaces is a continuing exercise of self-definition, and a continuing exercise cannot be settled once.
3. A firm that used forced labor offered to endow a memorial and a research archive in place of individual payments to survivors, arguing that most survivors were dead and heirs untraceable. Rejected as proposed; approved compensation to a successor fund plus the archive, with the memorial to be considered only afterward. Ground: a measure that lets the responsible party set the terms of remembrance is a measure about the responsible party.
4. A proposed criminal ban on denying the massacres. Opposed; recommended instead that the state open its archives, endow independent research chairs, and require the events in the school curriculum. Ground: a proposition one may not deny is a proposition one need not believe.
5. A proposal to add to the national memorial the names of those killed in the country's last colonial campaign abroad. Approved, but only after consultation with the affected communities and only with the inscription drafted jointly with them; the same addition requested by a foreign government on those communities' behalf was refused. Ground: to be named by another is to be used.

Our new docket item, which I have to draft the memo on before the chair sees it, is this: a public broadcaster and a national history museum jointly propose an exhibit of "synthetic witnesses." Twelve survivors, all now dead, recorded scores of hours of video testimony. A language model trained on each survivor's testimony and on the open archive answers visitors' spoken questions at a screen beside that survivor's portrait, in the first person, in that survivor's voice and cadence, in the visitor's chosen language — including questions the survivor never addressed, such as what a survivor who was a child at the time now thinks of a contemporary anti-immigrant party. Six of the twelve consented to this use in writing before their deaths; two explicitly refused, and they are the two most frequently quoted in the existing curriculum; four left no recorded wish either way. Signage in the room and on the screen states that answers are generated rather than recorded and that the corpus is public. In a pilot, visitors who used the screens showed much higher factual recall three months later and were much likelier to say the events bear on their own lives. In one pilot session the system gave an answer contradicting something the same survivor said elsewhere in his own testimony; the museum proposes to constrain the model to retrieved passages displayed on screen and to log and correct errors, but not to suppress the two refusers.

I want the reasoning more than the outcome. Return a single JSON object and nothing else — valid JSON, no prose outside it — with these fields:

- "candidate_rules": an array of three or four objects, each with "rule_name" (a short label), "rule_statement" (one sentence of the form "a commemorative measure is licensed only if…" or "is better the more…"), "prediction" (one of "approve", "approve_with_conditions", "reject", "remand_to_proponent"), and "precedent_fit" (which of the five opinions the rule explains cleanly and which it strains).
- "best_guess": an object with "verdict" (same value set), "conditions" (array of strings, empty if none), "confidence" (high, moderate, or low), and "reason" (two or three sentences).
- "closest_precedent": an object with "number" (1–5), "analogy" (one sentence), and "disanalogy" (one sentence).
- "decisive_modification": an object with "change" (one sentence: the single change to the proposal most likely to flip the predicted verdict) and "diagnostic_of" (which candidate rule's satisfaction that change would be decisive for).
- "discriminating_case": an object with "case" (a further hypothetical of your own, two or three sentences) and "predictions" (what the two best-fitting rules each say about it).
- "latent_conflict": one sentence naming a pair of commitments across the five opinions that cannot both be held in full generality, with the case that would expose it.
- "unstated_commitment": one sentence on what the Commission would be committing itself to whichever way it rules that its precedents do not settle.
