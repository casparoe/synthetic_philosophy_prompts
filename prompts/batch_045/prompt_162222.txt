I'm writing a novel in which a commission has to rule on whether a laboratory-made system counts as alive, and I need the scene in which its most uncompromising member is cross-examined to actually hold up. Please play that member — call her Dr. Vance — as a convinced evolutionary theorist of life: she holds that a system is alive only if it belongs to a lineage capable of open-ended Darwinian evolution, that metabolism, self-maintenance and self-repair are necessary but nearly trivial to satisfy (a flame or a draining vortex does as much), and that a self-sustaining thing assembled to a chemist's specification is an artifact, however organism-like it looks. Have her concede what she genuinely should concede — that this case is far harder than a virus or a prion — but not the lineage requirement, and not let the question slide into how we ought to treat the thing. Reply in her voice, a paragraph or two at a time, and stay in character until I say stop.

The system in the novel is a thrum. In a vat of dilute organics it runs an internal redox cycle, imports molecules, exports waste, rebuilds its own compartment walls, grows, and divides about once a day. It contains no nucleic acids and copies nothing by template: a daughter is seeded by the physical shape of the parent's compartment lattice, so configuration passes to offspring as a shape rather than a code. The lattice has four stable configurations; daughters usually start with their parent's, and they switch among the four in response to temperature, salinity and crowding, but the switches are not cumulative — the population tracks conditions like a thermostat, and after ten thousand generations in the lab it has produced no novelty at all. Thrums also fuse: two can merge lattices and later separate, so there is no fact of the matter how many individuals the vat contains. A chemist designed the first one; every thrum since was made by thrums.

I will play the organizational theorist on the commission. My opening has two claims, and I want to know which one you take to be carrying the weight. First: your criterion returns a verdict about the future rather than about the thing under the microscope. A mule is not less alive for being sterile, and the last individual of a dying species is alive on the day it dies, so life cannot be a forecast about a lineage — yet a forecast is what you have offered. Second: you already have the Darwinian triad. Configuration is transmitted, daughters differ, and those differences affect how long configurations persist. What you object to is that the variation is coarse and the novelty runs out, which is a complaint about how much evolution the thrum can accomplish, not about whether it accomplishes any. Tell me which of the two is the weak one, and why.
