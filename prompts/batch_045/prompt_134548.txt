I spent last summer in Martinique, and a morning spent wandering what is left of Saint-Pierre has made an old professional worry of mine feel less abstract. I did a doctorate in philosophy — counterfactuals and causation, mostly — before drifting into research administration, and I have just joined the grants committee of the Cardew Bequest, a small charity whose remit is to fund historical work that, in its founders' phrase, restores agency to the non-human world. My first meeting is on Thursday, I have been asked to present one application in about two minutes, and I can see that it sits on a fault line running through our previous decisions without being able to name what the fault line is. Before the meeting I would like a longer, less presentable version: what the committee is likely to do, and why.

The precedents, as minuted. We funded a project on the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora and the harvest failures of the following year in western Europe and New England, reasoning that the shock was exogenous, that no human institution produced it, and that the food riots and emigration that followed counterfactually depended on it. We refused a project that proposed to treat Caribbean climate as the cause of plantation slavery: the climate, we minuted, was constant across the region, was known to the planters who chose to plant there, and explained neither a difference between comparable cases nor a change over time. We funded a project on yellow fever and the collapse of the French expedition to Saint-Domingue, on the grounds that the pathogen discriminated sharply between two otherwise comparable forces, that it entered French planning and changed what the French attempted, and that the outcome would have gone the other way without it — even though the danger of the fever season was not exactly unknown at the time. We refused a project on a medieval port town inundated over two centuries by rising water: the inhabitants, we said, saw it coming, moved their warehouses, and a condition that people negotiate with over generations is not an actor. One refusal was purely evidential, and I suspect unhelpful: a project on terminal Classic drought in the Maya lowlands was turned down because the lake-sediment records cannot resolve timing more finely than a few decades, so we cannot tell whether the drying preceded or followed the political collapse. And we funded with conditions a project on 1930s drought and out-migration from the Southern Plains, the condition being that the drought not be described as causing the Dust Bowl without also modelling ploughing practice, land tenure and federal policy — the interaction, not the drought on its own.

The application I have to present concerns Mount Pelée and the destruction of Saint-Pierre in 1902. The mountain had erupted mildly in 1792 and 1851, so that its capacity was matter of record; on 23 April 1902 it began a fortnight of visible unrest — cinders, earthquakes, sulphur fumes smelt in the town six kilometres away, ash falling on Saint-Pierre from 3 May — and people responded to it in both directions, country people coming in to what they took to be the safest settlement on the island while many townspeople tried to get out on steamers that were already full. On 5 May a mudflow destroyed a sugar mill and killed around a hundred and fifty people. On the morning of 8 May a pyroclastic surge came down the south-west flank and destroyed the town within minutes; some twenty-nine thousand people died, the great majority in Saint-Pierre itself, out of a handful of survivors one was a prisoner in a poorly ventilated cell, and the settlements a few miles down the coast were untouched. The application also contains a much-disputed chapter arguing that the authorities were slow to authorise evacuation because municipal elections were imminent; our reviewer thinks the sourcing there is thin, and I would like to know how much the decision should turn on it. So: what will this committee do — fund, refuse, or fund with conditions? Set out the candidate rules that fit all six of our earlier verdicts equally well (I have my own guesses: something about whether the people affected could have foreseen and adapted to the factor; something about whether outcomes counterfactually depend on it with human choices held fixed; something about whether the account specifies the human or institutional mechanism through which it acted; something about whether it explains a differential between comparable groups), say what each of them predicts here, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, tell me which of the precedents is the closest analogue and, if our record genuinely underdetermines the case, what principle we would be committing ourselves to with each of the three options. I would also like one further case, real or invented, that would best separate the rules that survive — the case I could put to the committee to find out which one we have actually been running. And one last thing, which is the worry I came back from Martinique with: our remit invites us to treat germs, weather and volcanoes as historical actors, but I cannot tell whether any of these rules, applied consistently, genuinely resists environmental determinism, or whether it merely relocates it — allowing us to call a natural event an agent in the very cases where human decisions were most constrained, and to call human choices the cause in the cases where they were least.
