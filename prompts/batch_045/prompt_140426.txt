I'm creative director at a nine-person studio that ships short, replayable puzzle games for mobile. Every quarter we pick one theme, run a two-day jam, come out with twenty to thirty one-sheet concepts, and I have to cut that down to three that get prototyped for a month by a pair each. I'm the one deciding, our studio head signs off on the three, and I get maybe an hour per sheet and have to defend the call afterward to a team that does not want a theory lecture.

Here's why I'm writing. I keep having the same argument with myself. Years ago someone dropped a link in Slack to this Hofstadter thing about creativity — a lecture, a chapter, I honestly dont remember — arguing that creativity isn't rabbits out of hats but variations on a theme: you absorb a style-space from a few hundred examples, then novelty is mostly twiddling the knobs of that space, some knobs shallow and some deep, and the interesting stuff happens when you shove one knob way past where anyone has shoved it, or move several at once, or let a concept slide sideways into a setting it wasn't built for. The bolt from the blue is the myth. That rings true to how our good games actually happened, but I have no way to do it on purpose, so right now "pick three" is vibes plus whoever argues best in the room.

So: turn it into a procedure I can actually run on a one-sheet. Concretely I need:

1. A step for getting the knobs out before anyone generates anything — how you name the axes of a theme instead of just listing its features, and how you notice the knob nobody in the room can name yet. My guess is that deliberately generating terrible variations is one way in; you tell me.
2. A way to sort knobs by depth. My working distinction: a surface knob is one you can set independently of everything else; a structural knob is one that drags other knobs with it when you move it; a frame knob is one whose mere existence is what makes the theme that theme. Fix this if it's wrong, and give me a test I can run on a knob in five minutes to classify it, including an account of when the test just doesn't settle it.
3. Moves for searching the space on purpose rather than hoping: push one knob to an extreme, move a coupled cluster at once, delete a knob entirely, borrow a knob from an adjacent genre. Say what each move is for and what it tends to produce.
4. Tests, in order, for judging a variation once we have one. Two failure modes matter to me: the samey-but-new one, where a knob genuinely moved and the experience is identical, and the lost-the-thread one, where the variation quietly became a different game and we don't notice for a month. Tell me which is worse for a studio our size and make the procedure treat them accordingly instead of treating them as equally bad.
5. Where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is really about values — what kind of studio we want to be, how much audience we're willing to trade — and who that person should be, given I'm the one running it and my boss signs off.

Then run it on three real sheets from last quarter:

- "Noughts": our match-three with the failure state deleted. You can't lose; the board just gets messier until you walk away. Everyone under thirty in the studio loved it. Our studio head thinks removing losing removes the game.
- "Folio": the same tile-sliding puzzle we've already shipped twice, redone with a hand-drawn bestiary and a different grid size each level. The team's favorite by a mile and, in my gut, the weakest sheet in the pile.
- "Errand": a roguelike run structure — permadeath, drafting a deck of moves between attempts — bolted onto our daily word puzzle. Nobody can agree whether this is a variation on the daily or a different game wearing its skin.

Give me a verdict and the reasoning on each, mark clearly which parts came from the procedure and which were your judgment, and include at least one where the procedure hands me an answer I don't want.

Last thing: tell me where this will misfire. Two worries I want confirmed or killed — (a) writing the knob list down before the jam might shrink the space to whatever we managed to name that morning, and (b) the whole model has nothing to say about whether an idea is fun, or funny, or well-timed, and I'm scared we'd end up optimizing how legible a concept is instead of the thing itself. Say which of those the procedure should flatly refuse to touch.

Format: prose first for the procedure and the three cases. Then, so I can paste it into our Notion template, the same thing again as a single JSON object with these keys: knobs (array of {name, kind: surface | structural | frame, how_to_find, watch_out}), steps (array of {order, question, sufficient_answer, stop_no, escalate_to}), tests (array of {name, detects, pass, confused_with}), cases (array of {name, knobs_moved, verdict, reason, judgment_call: true or false}), limits (array of strings). Don't pad it — if a field is empty for us, say so in the field.
