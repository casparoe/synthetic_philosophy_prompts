I sit on a small advisory panel for a museum that is rewriting the wall text for a gallery on twentieth-century mass atrocities, and we keep getting stuck on the same move — one that I have noticed for years in public arguments but have never been able to name. It is not denial in the plain sense. The person making it concedes nearly everything one might have thought was at issue: the deaths happened, in roughly the numbers alleged; most of the dead were non-combatants; the killings or the fatal deprivations were carried out by state organs or with the state's knowledge; and what happened was a moral catastrophe. What is withheld is the classification. The argument is that the legal category requires an intention to destroy a protected group as such, while what the authorities actually intended was relocation, or the suppression of a rebellion, or wartime security, or economic transformation — and that the mass death was foreseen, perhaps callously foreseen, but not aimed at. So the disagreement migrates out of the empirical questions, which are now largely shared, and into two places: the definition of the category, and the content of a long-dead government's intentions.

What makes this interesting to me, and what I cannot find a word for, is that the concession and the refusal are not independent — they reinforce each other. The more fully someone grants the scale and horror of the suffering, the more credible and reasonable the classificatory refusal sounds, and the harder it is to accuse them of bad faith. Meanwhile the label is where nearly all the practical weight sits: the duties to prevent and punish that attach under the 1948 convention, the claim to reparations, the standing to demand an official apology, the place the event occupies in a national story and in a group's sense of itself. So a speaker can appear to give away everything and yet retain the thing that matters, while the other side's insistence on the word can be portrayed as caring more about a term than about the dead. I have watched versions of this in arguments about the 1915 deportations and mass deaths of Armenians in Anatolia, in arguments about the killing of civilians in Nanjing in 1937, and in the recurring disputes over famine mortality under colonial rule, where the deaths are granted and only the description — policy, negligence, administrative collapse — is fought over.

So my question: does this have an established name or an established literature? I have heard people gesture at notions of denial that supposedly operate on the meaning of events or on their implications rather than on the facts, but what I am describing looks like neither, or at least not cleanly — my speaker is perfectly willing to grant that the events were atrocious and to grant what they meant to the victims; the entire fight is about which kind of event they were, and about who bears the burden of proof on intent. I do not want a reading list or a set of cases to look up; I want the carving. Specifically: how does this move differ from honest definitional scruple, which surely exists and is sometimes right; from ordinary minimization; from the perfectly legitimate legalistic argument a defence lawyer is supposed to make about mens rea; and from mainstream historical revision, which also constantly reargues intent and numbers? Is there a principled test that separates the reclassifier from the careful definer — something like what each would say if the intent evidence came out the other way, or what each wants the label to do once it is applied — or is the distinction irreducibly a judgement about the speaker's motives that no neutral criterion can reach? And if the phenomenon really has no settled name, then give it one, define it tightly, and specify its boundary cases: an instance that clearly falls under it, an instance that clearly does not, and an instance you would leave undecided.
