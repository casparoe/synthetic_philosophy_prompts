**The question (25 marks; 8 for (a), 9 for (b), 8 for (c))**

Douglas Hofstadter has argued that the crux of creativity is variation on a theme: a creative person is someone who has, over a long apprenticeship, built up an enormously rich, high-dimensional space of adjustable parameters — "knobs" — whose settings determine the character of what they make, and who produces new work by altering a small number of those settings and exploring the neighbourhood of what has already been done. For the purposes of this question, take *the knob model* to be the following five claims, which I offer as a fair reconstruction of that view:

(K1) At any given time a creator's competence is captured by a *scheme*: a finite list of knobs, each with a finite set of settings. The *span* of a scheme is the set of all its total settings.
(K2) New work is produced by altering a small number of knobs from the settings of previous work, or of settings previously tried out in imagination.
(K3) Most combinations of settings yield nothing coherent. The *habitable region* — the subset of the span that yields work at all — is a minute and highly structured fraction of the span, and much of a creator's skill consists in knowing which combinations are habitable.
(K4) The scheme itself is acquired through experience and is slowly revised over a career.
(K5) Some knobs have vastly more effect on the finished work than others: a single knob can be such that switching it reinterprets what the others are for.

Margaret Boden distinguishes *exploratory creativity* — the production of novel items by moving within a conceptual space that is already fixed — from *transformational creativity*, in which the space itself is altered, so that items become possible which the old space did not admit. Her project is to give a mechanistic account of creativity, and she does not treat transformational creativity as inexplicable or as lying outside the reach of mechanism altogether; she treats it as rare and as posing a particular difficulty for computational modelling, since the very space that a search would have to traverse is what gets changed.

(a) Set out the objection that the knob model can only account for Boden's exploratory creativity and cannot account for transformational creativity. Say what the objector means by "changing the space", and why, on the model as stated, twiddling knobs within a scheme cannot amount to it.

(b) Give the strongest reply available to a defender of the knob model, then say whether it succeeds. Consider in particular: (i) the appeal to high leverage (K5) — whether a small move in knob space can amount to a transformational change in what is produced; (ii) the appeal to *second-order* knobs, whose settings are operations on the scheme itself; and (iii) whether the reply buys its conclusion too cheaply — whether the model becomes unfalsifiable if every change of space can be redescribed as the twiddling of some higher knob.

(c) For this part use the following definitions. Where *x* and *y* are total settings of the same scheme, the *twiddle distance* d(*x*,*y*) is the number of knobs at which they differ. Fix an integer *r* ≥ 1, the *elasticity* of a theme; *y* is an *r*-variation of *x* if and only if d(*x*,*y*) ≤ *r*. A *metaknob* is a knob whose settings are operations on the scheme — delete a knob; split a knob into two; duplicate a knob; merge two knobs; add a new knob — the application of which yields a new scheme. A *lineage* is a sequence of schemes each obtained from its predecessor by applying a metaknob operation, with ordinary twiddling in between.

(i) Is the relation "is an *r*-variation of" transitive? Prove it or give a counterexample.
(ii) What follows from your answer about the claim, central to the knob model, that a creator can travel very far from an initial theme by a chain of small variations — what Hofstadter calls slippage?
(iii) Suppose a scheme contains a metaknob. Does that show Boden's distinction between exploratory and transformational creativity to be merely verbal — that is, that every change of space is after all a move within a larger space? Answer with reference to what a "space" must be if the distinction is to do any work.

**The script: Candidate 4**

*(a)*

Boden's distinction concerns two ways of being novel. In exploratory creativity the space is already fixed: the rules that generate it are in place, and the creator's achievement is to reach a place in it that nobody had reached — the item was always possible, merely unvisited. In transformational creativity it is the rules that change. The space is altered, and afterwards items are possible which before were not merely unvisited but not there to be visited. Her examples are the large ones: a new genre in painting, a new framework in science, where what alters is not which structure you have but which structures there are to be had.

Set against that, the knob model looks like a model of exploration only. K1 gives the creator a fixed list of knobs; K2 says that what they do is alter a few of the settings; the novelty of the result consists in its being a combination of settings that has not been chosen before. But every such combination is already in the span. So the model can explain how an unvisited point gets visited, and not how a space gets altered: the list of knobs is not itself something the act of creation puts in play, since on K4 it is simply whatever apprenticeship has installed. The criticism is best put as a dilemma. Either the new work is a setting of the old knobs, in which case it lay in the span already and the novelty is exploratory; or it is not a setting of the old knobs, in which case the model has nothing to say about how it was produced. What Boden calls transformational creativity falls on the second horn, and that is the horn the model cannot grip. Nor is the dilemma answered by pointing out how large the span is: that is a difference of degree within the first horn.

*(b)*

The strongest reply has three strands.

The first is high leverage, and it answers the critic's inference rather than the criticism. The critic moves from "the new work looks discontinuous with what came before" to "the space must have changed". K5 blocks that move. If a knob can be such that altering it reinterprets what all the other knobs are for — a switch rather than a dial — then two settings one twiddle apart can yield works with no perceptible family resemblance, and the appearance of a leap in the product is no evidence of a leap in the process.

The second is that the knobs need not be knobs of the surface. Nothing in K1 says what a setting must be a setting of; a knob's settings may be operations on the scheme itself — split knob 7, merge knobs 2 and 5, delete knob 3. Twiddling can then change the list, and what K4 describes as slow revision becomes something the model represents rather than something it merely presupposes.

The third is that new knobs typically arrive from outside, by analogy with a neighbouring domain; the space is altered by contact with other spaces rather than by an escape from space altogether.

Strand one works. Strand three is a suggestion about where new knobs come from, not an account of how they are taken up, so it relocates the problem rather than answering it. Strand two is the one that has to carry the weight, and whether it can is a question about what a space is; I take it up in (c).

One further point about what is at stake. It is sometimes said that the two writers are not really in dispute, since Boden is offering a mechanistic account of creativity and so cannot be denying that transformation has a mechanism. But that is too quick. On her view, transformational creativity is precisely the case in which there is no space to search, and she concludes from this that it is not the sort of thing a mechanism could produce and that it lies outside what any computational model could in principle do; that is why, for her, a machine could at best be exploratory. If that is right, then strand two is not an extension of the knob model but a denial of Boden's position, and the two views are further apart than is usually allowed.

*(c)*

(i) The relation is transitive, and this can be shown rather than assumed. Twiddle distance is a metric, so the triangle inequality holds: d(*x*,*z*) ≤ d(*x*,*y*) + d(*y*,*z*). Let *y* be an *r*-variation of *x* and *z* an *r*-variation of *y*. Then d(*x*,*z*) ≤ *r* + *r* = 2*r*. But *r* is the elasticity of the theme, and the only bound with any content is *r*: a work more than *r* twiddles from the theme is not a variation of it at all, however it was arrived at. So the relation is transitive in the only sense that matters. Either a chain of steps, each of size at most *r*, stays within the theme's ball of radius *r*, in which case its endpoint is a variation of the origin; or it leaves that ball, in which case the chain has ceased to be a chain of variations of the origin, and the smallness of the individual steps is neither here nor there.

It follows that ordinary twiddling is bounded: the region a creator can reach from a theme by successive variations is exactly that theme's ball of radius *r*, and no accumulation of small steps takes them outside it. If the model is to allow a creator to end up somewhere genuinely new, it must be the metaknob that does the work, since only a change of scheme lifts the bound.

(ii) This is awkward for slippage, which is the part of the model I find most attractive and which I therefore want to be careful about. The idea is that a creator can drift arbitrarily far from an origin by steps each of which is licensed by its own smallness. But if the variation relation is transitive with the bound *r*, then the drift is bounded: either every step really is a variation of the origin, in which case the total displacement never exceeds *r*; or at some point the creator has left the theme, and then the smallness of the step no longer licenses anything. The only way to keep slippage is to allow the elasticity to be re-set as one goes — to let *r* itself change — which is to concede that the scheme is changing and to hand the work back to the metaknob.

(iii) Once a scheme contains a metaknob, every change of scheme is formally the setting of a knob: from inside a scheme one can delete, split, merge or add. There is a worry that this is too cheap — that if every change of space can be described as the twiddle of some higher knob, then nothing could count against the model. I do not think the worry lands. The metaknob is not a licence to do anything; its settings are the operations the scheme actually contains, and which those are is an empirical matter about the creator's repertoire. So the model still says something definite.

What the metaknob does show is something else, and it is more damaging to Boden than to the defender of the knob model. Any operation yields a scheme with a finite knob list, and the operations on offer at any stage are finite in number as well; so the schemes reachable by finite lineages form a countable family, and the knobs appearing anywhere in that family can be collected into a single superscheme — infinite if need be — whose settings include every knob that ever turns up. But then there is always a space within which the whole lineage is a journey, and "changing the space" turns out to mean nothing more than moving to a part of the superspace that the smaller scheme could not name. On this showing Boden's distinction is verbal: since the redescription is available in every case and at no cost, no fact about any creator could tell the two categories apart, and a distinction that no fact could tell apart is not a real distinction. The objection in (a) was therefore asking for something that cannot be had, and it fails for that reason rather than because the knob model answers it.
