I run the verification and diagnostics group for a large operational ensemble prediction system: every cycle we issue a full predictive distribution for each of several thousand targets, we are scored with strictly proper scoring rules, and most of our working life is spent on reliability — whether the events to which we assign 0.3 in fact verify thirty per cent of the time. Assume I am fluent in that world: reliability–resolution decomposition, ensemble spread and the spread–skill relationship, latent-rate and hierarchical models, Bayesian updating, and retrospective re-estimation of a latent rate from realized outcomes. Assume I know nothing of academic philosophy beyond undergraduate probability.

A colleague in a reading group pointed me at what philosophers call the Principal Principle and at the surrounding debate about deference to objective chance, including the undermining problem that is said to have troubled it. I would like that doctrine and its troubles restated in the vocabulary I actually use, together with a residue report on what the vocabulary cannot carry.

Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no preamble and no closing summary. Take the parts in this order:

1. The principle itself, rendered as a requirement on the distributions my group issues — roughly, that conditional on the true latent rate for a class of targets being exactly p, the probability I issue for each member of that class must be p — and what in the original formulation does not survive that rendering.

2. The restriction the doctrine places on which evidence you may condition on: my operational analogue is that if I evaluate reliability on subsets of cases selected by whether the target event verified, every honest forecast looks miscalibrated. Say whether that restriction is just my no-selection-on-the-outcome rule, or something the forecaster has no vocabulary for.

3. The undermining problem: because nature realizes one draw per target and never reveals the rate, my retrospective best estimate of a season's rate is itself shifted by the outcomes I issued probabilities for. Say how the principle is supposed to survive that, and what has to be fudged to state it in my terms.

4. Our shop deliberately pads the spread above our best estimate of the irreducible rate in order to cover model error — aleatory padding, we call it. Say whether deference to chance condemns that practice, and how the distinction I draw between irreducible and reducible uncertainty maps onto what the philosophers are arguing about.

5. Your verdict on the residue: does what fails to translate point to something missing from my vocabulary — a notion of what is being deferred to, and of deference as a constraint on what I issue rather than on what I believe — or to something in the doctrine itself that is less clear than it looks? I have no library access and I assume you have no web access either: no citations, no reading lists, no named authors.
