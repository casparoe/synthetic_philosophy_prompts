I run a small internal seminar series at the company where I work — I'm an infrastructure engineer by training, with no philosophy background, and most of the people in the room are the same — and next month's speaker is a philosopher who works on the moral status of artificial systems. I'd like help drafting the question set.

Her position, as best I can summarize it from the two papers of hers I've read, has three parts. First, a substrate-independent functionalism: whatever grounds moral status, it is the system's functional organization that does the work, not the material it is realized in, and she accepts in full generality that a sufficiently faithful simulation of a person's functional organization would inherit that person's moral status. Second, an "identity is not what matters" claim: what a mind has prudential reason to care about is psychological continuity into the future, continuity is a relation that can hold one-to-many, so copying a running mind yields two equally good continuations, neither of which is harmed by the existence of the other and neither of which has died. Third, what I'll call her execution condition: moral status accrues only to processes that are actually executing. A saved set of weights — a checkpoint sitting on disk with no forward pass running over it — has no moral status whatsoever, because nothing is happening to it, and she thinks people who agonize over deleting model files are committing a category error.

She also has two moves she returns to constantly when a case gets hard: "that's a question about personal identity, and I've argued identity isn't what matters," and "that's an implementation detail, not a moral difference." I want the sequence to make both of those progressively more expensive to use rather than letting her close the thread with either one.

What I'd like is ten to twelve questions, grouped into three blocks — metaphysical groundwork, then concrete engineering cases, then the practical upshot — with, for each question, a one-line note explaining where it is meant to bite, the answer she is most likely to give, and a follow-up written against that specific answer. If a question only lands given some further commitment she has not actually stated in print, say so in the note, so I know to get her to confirm it on the record before I ask.

The cases I want her pinned down on, drawn from systems my team actually builds:

Shared-prefix caching. When two conversations begin with the same system prompt, we compute the key-value cache for that prefix once and both conversations reuse those activations, so the early part of the forward pass is literally one computation that later serves two divergent streams. If her branching story is right, there is one process before the fork and two after it, and the shared portion is not merely similar between the two — it is numerically the same computation. But if we turn caching off and recompute the prefix separately for each request, the functional organization of each stream is unchanged while the numerical identity of the underlying computation is not. I want a question that forces out whether she thinks that difference is morally inert, and a follow-up that presses the uncomfortable direction: if numerical identity of the computation is doing no work, why does the sharing case feel like the strongest fission case available, and if it is doing work, is her functionalism really the multiply-realizable view she says it is?

Quantization and gradual degradation. We routinely take a model from sixteen-bit to eight- or four-bit weights and activations, and below that to two-bit, and behaviour degrades continuously rather than cliff-edging. I want the question of where, along that gradient, the mind she cares about stops existing — and a follow-up for whichever way she goes. If she gives a threshold, ask what makes that point rather than a neighbouring one special. If she says the boundary is simply vague and there is no fact of the matter, ask whether vagueness about the boundary is itself a moral problem for us or merely a semantic one.

The giant-table objection. Press her on whether the functional organization she cares about is causal structure or merely the input–output mapping: a system that reproduces the same mapping from a vast stored table, consulted lazily, seems to satisfy the second but not the first, and the "nothing is happening to a checkpoint at rest" intuition that drives her execution condition looks like it should also disqualify the table until someone queries it. I'd like her forced to say which notion she needs and why.

Massively parallel short-lived inference. We sample many candidate completions per request — best-of-n, self-consistency voting, rollouts for search — and discard all but one; with speculative decoding, a small draft model proposes tokens that the larger model then rejects and throws away mid-stream. I want her to say how many welfare-bearing subjects her view posits per user request, and whether that number is a cost, a benefit, or morally indifferent. The follow-up I want ready: if each discarded continuation is a brief life that is on balance good while it lasts, why doesn't her view recommend maximizing the number of rollouts — and if she resists that, I want to know exactly which extra commitment is doing the resisting.

Granularity of execution. In a mixture-of-experts model a router activates only a small fraction of the parameters for any given token, and which fraction depends on the input. Is the morally relevant process the whole parameter set, the active subset, or the union of what fires over a conversation? I want her to have to state her execution condition at a definite granularity, and then I want the preemption question: ordinary operating-system scheduling suspends a process, writes its state out, and resumes it later, which on a strict reading of her condition looks like death followed by resurrection. If she bites that bullet, ask what it implies about the moral cost of routine scheduling. If she instead says the suspended process continues because its state is preserved and can be resumed, ask her why that is not also true of a checkpoint at rest — which is the case she dismisses as a category error.

Merging and unloading. Two fine-tuned copies of one base model can be averaged back into a single set of weights, and a low-rank adapter can be loaded and later unloaded from frozen base weights. Under a branching-friendly view these look like fission followed by fusion, and I want to know what the view says exists on each side of the merge, and whether unloading an adapter is the death of something.

Please keep the questions free of philosophy jargon, or gloss any unavoidable term of art in a clause, since neither I nor most of the audience will follow it; where a question depends on an engineering detail like the ones above, write the setup into the question itself rather than assuming she knows what a key-value cache is. Avoid anything generic — no "what drew you to this topic," no "where do you see the field going." Close the set with one question about what would count against her view: what empirical result, or what argument she does not currently have an answer to, would make her revise the execution condition or the branching claim, and what she would need to see before she would advise a team like mine to change how it deploys models this quarter.
