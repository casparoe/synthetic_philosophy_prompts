I'm a PhD student in urban history (postwar highway engineering, mostly) and I keep hitting the same wall when I try to say which built things are political. Here are my gut verdicts.

The low parkway overpasses on Long Island, too low for a bus to pass, so that people without cars couldn't reach the beaches: political, clearly. The identical clearance on a bridge in a Midwest city where the height was just copied off a standard drawing nobody reviewed and where no bus route ever ran: I want to say not political, or much less, even though the object is the same. Those two together are what bother me.

Benches with armrests split down the middle, installed in one downtown and used for sleeping and skateboarding in another: I want to say the politics was never in the bench. Reprocessing plants, which seem to require a permanent centralized security apparatus just to function at all: political, but in a way that has nothing to do with the overpasses, and I can't say what the difference is. A wiki or a forked open-source tool: not political, it seems to actively resist having politics. The traffic-signal system my city bought last year, where the vendor's default objective function maximizes vehicle throughput, no one at the city ever chose to prioritize cars over people crossing, and the walk phase got shorter: I go back and forth but I definately lean political, and oddly more political than the overpasses, because there's no decision anyone can point to.

So: my tentative principle is that an artifact is political when it settles a question about who can do what and does so in a way that's hard to undo, whether or not anyone meant to settle it. But that seems to swallow the wiki (nothing is easier to undo), to excuse the copied-from-a-standard-drawing bridge too cheaply, and it doesn't separate the overpass case from the reprocessing case at all. Is there a better principle, or a small set of them, and what would you drop from my list? Please also run yours on two cases I haven't listed: a keycard system installed for maintenance scheduling whose entry logs HR now uses to check who's at their desk, and a subway station built with an elevator that skips the mezzanine.
