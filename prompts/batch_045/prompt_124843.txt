I work in financial reporting — I'm partway through a professional accountancy qualification — and in the evenings I'm auditing a course on the history of economic thought where we've just read Aristotle on money and exchange: his account of reciprocal proportion and of money as a measure in book five of the Ethics, and the distinction he draws in the first book of the Politics between managing a household and the unlimited business of getting wealth, where he treats money breeding money as the most unnatural case of all. The two halves of my week keep colliding over one practice I've never been able to see clearly. Under the standards I work with, the ordinary non-financial assets of a business — property, equipment, inventory — are carried at what they originally cost, less depreciation, not at what they are currently worth, and the unit in which everything is counted is simply the nominal currency unit, the same one in which the invoices were struck. Three features of the practice strike me as odd together. First, the books are required to balance exactly — to the cent — and yet a building bought twenty-five years ago is still depreciated on its twenty-five-year-old cost, so the charge for using it up is stated in money of a very different purchasing power from the revenue it helps earn, and profit is systematically overstated, which means dividends and tax get paid out of the capital the firm needs to replace the building. Second, we do re-measure things denominated in a foreign currency at each reporting date, taking the difference to profit or loss as it arises — but we do not re-measure a fixed domestic receivable for the fall in what the domestic unit will buy, even though that is also a change in what the money can command. Third, there is an exception that proves the rule strange: the international standard for hyperinflationary economies, triggered by indicators such as a cumulative inflation over three years of around one hundred per cent or more, requires the whole of the financial statements to be restated into the currency unit current at the end of the reporting period, with a separate gain or loss on the net monetary position running through profit or loss — as though a change in the ruler could make you richer.

So here is what I want from you. Reconstruct the implicit theory of money, measurement, and value that this pattern of practice only makes sense under, and do it at the level of the parts, not just the whole: what the anchoring on historical transactions strictly presupposes, what the exemption of the domestic monetary unit from re-measurement strictly presupposes, and what the hyperinflation exception presupposes, and whether those three are consistent with one another or quietly rest on different theories. Be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably — I'd rather know where several rival theories would license exactly the same accounting than be handed one tidy story. In particular, I want to know what would follow if you took seriously the Aristotelian picture I'm reading about: money as something that exists by convention rather than by nature, a conventional representative of need that a change in the law or in general demand can dethrone, so that commensurability between a truck and a patent and an obligation to deliver services is something an actual exchange creates at a time and place rather than a fact lying there to be discovered. Does the cost convention look, under that picture, like a principled attachment to the moment when need and agreement really did meet, and does the drift toward fair-value measurement look like the chrematistic attitude — the enterprise valued by the market's estimate rather than accounted for as a stewardship? Or is that a stretch? And what about the rival view I keep hearing about, associated with the subjectivists, that money prices are exchange ratios and not measurements of any quantity at all, so that adding up the figures on a balance sheet is a category mistake rather than an approximation — what would a set of accounts built on that view even look like, and would it still be accounting?

Two people in my evening class have staked out positions I can't evaluate. One says the cost convention isn't a theory of value at all but a device for stopping a regress: if you re-measure the unit you have to re-measure the re-measuring, and the only non-arbitrary place to stop is the price actually agreed in a transaction, which is why the practice is stable even though nobody defends it in theory. The other says the hyperinflation standard refutes that, because it shows what happens when the convention fixing the unit visibly breaks — the profession re-fixes the ruler, and then books the change as profit or loss, which demonstrates that a large part of what we call profit has all along been a change in the ruler and not in anything the firm did. Where I think both of them may be missing something is that our practice also has to serve tax collection, debt covenants, and management pay, and a rule that made reported profit move with the general price level would hand a lot of power to whoever compiles the index. Tell me whether you think each of those two is on to something or overreaching, and what you think they're both leaving out. Then, importantly, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different reconstruction, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail — whether in the reconstruction of the practice, in the reading of Aristotle, or in the economics. If you are unsure about any of the accounting details I've described, say so rather than smoothing over it.
