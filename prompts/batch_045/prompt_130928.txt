So I got into a stupidly long argument with a friend last night about Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity, and I need someone to sort out where we both went wrong. Her line was: if the self is just an abstraction, a theorist's fiction like a center of gravity, then it can't be the thing that *does* anything — fictions aren't causes, only the underlying neural and behavioral facts are, so the self gets demoted to a mere summary. I pushed back that a center of gravity is exactly what you need in order to predict where the thing will go, so it does real work even though it isn't an extra part of the object — but I couldn't say what *kind* of work that is. Can you explain what it'd mean for something abstract to be explanatorily or causally efficacious without being a real thing, and what the actual mistake in her argument is?

And then the bit we really got stuck on. Suppose we grant that my self is whatever story best holds my behavior together over time. Whose story has to do that work? Because there seem to be cases where it comes apart: a story I sincerely tell about myself that predicts nothing I do (I'm the kind of person who'd never do X, and I do X constantly); a story I'd flatly reject that predicts me better than my own does; and a story authored by something else — a biographer, my mother, or an algorithm trained on everything I've ever written — that tracks my behavior better than any story I could produce. Call it narrative capture when the best-fitting story about a person is one they didn't write and wouldn't endorse. Does the center of gravity just migrate to whoever's got the better model, or is authorship or endorsement partly constitutive of a narrative's being *mine*? And if it's the latter, haven't we snuck in something over and above whatever best explains the behavior — in which case what work is the self still doing in the theory at all?
