I spent most of last night arguing about the extended mind thesis with a friend I was in a reading group with years ago, and he has left me with an argument I cannot get out of. I think it proves too much, but I cannot say which step is doing the damage, and every repair I tried made things worse. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

His argument, in his numbering:

(1) Parity. If a process coupled to a subject would count as a cognitive process were it going on inside her head, then it counts as one outside it. To deny this is just a prejudice about skull and skin, and the externalist is entitled to demand a reason for it.

(2) The role that matters is fixed entirely by the subject's downstream relation to the resource: it is reliably available, easily accessed, automatically endorsed, and it saves her the trouble of working out for herself what it says. Call these the coupling conditions.

(3) Otto's notebook satisfies them. He carries it always, consults it before acting, and treats its entries as authoritative without re-deriving them. So Otto's notebook is part of Otto's mind, and some of Otto's standing beliefs are stored outside his head. I want to keep this.

(4) Nothing in (1) or (2) says the resource must be paper, or portable, or handwritten, or produced by the subject, or writable by her now, or under her exclusive control. The conditions concern only how she is related to it when she acts.

(5) The conditions are matters of degree, and Otto satisfies them only finitely: the notebook is not perfectly reliable, not instantly accessible, and he occasionally checks one entry against another. So for any item that satisfies them, a marginally worse item — slower, less often verified, less exclusively his, more of its content originally someone else's — satisfies them too. And a difference that makes no difference to how he reasons and acts cannot make a difference to what his mind is made of.

(6) There is therefore a chain of such steps from Otto's notebook outward: to a photocopy of a friend's notes that Otto has annotated and trusted for years; to a colleague whose standing answer to a question Otto acts on without checking; to a phone that completes his query before he finishes typing it; to the internet at large.

(7) So the internet is part of Otto's mind. And in general, everything he is reliably coupled to in the right way is.

(8) That consequence is not merely surprising, it is useless. The interest of the thesis lay in its saying that some external things are genuinely parts of a mind and others are not, and in using that claim to redescribe the systems cognitive science studies. If everything counts, the thesis has no content left, and the externalist has spent her whole credibility buying a tautology. I also want to keep this.

(9) So something has to go. Either parity is false — and then someone owes a non-question-begging account of what is special about the intracranial, which cannot just be that it is intracranial, and cannot be the point about original versus derived content either, since whatever drove externalism about content in the first place seems to make the notebook's content as good as anyone's, so appealing to derived content here looks circular — or coupling is not sufficient, and the thesis needs a further condition it has not yet stated.

I tried two patches and my friend broke both. The first was diachronic authorship: the resource must carry the subject's own earlier cognitive activity, so that consulting it is a retrieval of her own prior states rather than an acquisition of someone else's. He asked about a case where Otto cannot use his hands and dictates his entries to a nurse who writes them down, and about two climbers who keep a single shared route log to which both contribute and which both consult before every pitch. The first seems to me as good a case as the original, and the second suggests the log is part of two minds at once — which I do not know whether to find absurd or to welcome. The second patch was write access: the subject must be able to alter the resource now. He killed it with a sealed-archive case: a woman records a set of detailed notes to herself in early adulthood, the recording is the only copy, it cannot be added to, and forty years later she consults it daily and treats it as authoritative. That looks like memory to me and not like a notebook he has been handed.

I have two hunches about where the argument cheats, and I do not trust either, because he had replies. One is that (6) slides between a subject's retrieving her own stored states and her acquiring information she never had, and that (2) is phrased broadly enough to hide the slide. He says that is just the difference of degree that (5) already licenses. The other is that (5) is a sorites and that I should simply refuse tolerance and make peace with vagueness; he says that a thesis which cannot say whether the internet is part of your mind was never saying anything.

Tell me which step you reject, why that step looked innocent when it was introduced, and what rejecting it costs — including whether the cost is one an extended-mind theorist can actually pay, or whether it quietly returns her to the intracranial prejudice she started by objecting to. If you think the right repair is a further condition neither of us thought of, say what it is and run the three cases above through it. And if you think the honest move is to stop talking about parts of a personal mind and start talking about integrated cognitive systems instead, tell me what that concedes to someone who wanted the original claim. I would rather keep (3) and (8) than any of the others, but I want to know what keeping both will run me.
