Two friends from my old ethics seminar got stuck on Taurek's numbers-don't-count argument at dinner and made me referee, which I was useless at. Roughly how it went.

Sam: If you can save either one stranger or five, and not both, the five have no claim as a group that the one doesn't have. Death isn't worse for a person because other people are also dying. So the fair thing is to give all six an equal shot — flip a weighted coin, everyone gets one chance in six. The sum never enters.

Nina: But the sum walks in the back door. Of all the procedures that treat people symmetrically, you picked the one that happens to maximize expected survivors. If numbers genuinely carried no weight, a coin flip between saving the one and saving the five as a block would be just as fair. You're choosing the lottery because of what it does to the total and then saying totals don't matter.

Sam: Wrong order of explanation. I give equal chances because each person has an equal claim on me; the fact that this also tends to save more people is a byproduct, not my reason — if I'd promised one of them I'd save him, I'd save him, and the total wouldn't bother me at all.

Nina: Then test it where the individual claim and the total come apart. Suppose you face this same choice every day for a year: one versus five, new strangers each time, no promises, no relationships. Run your lottery daily and about five times as many people live as would have if you'd just always saved the one. By your own principle each day is separate and the numbers don't count, so always saving the one is fine. But then your view permits a policy that predictably leaves thousands more people dead, and permits it for no reason. If you say it isn't fine, the long-run totals are doing work and the days aren't as separate as you claim.

Sam: They are separate. Doing justice to each person isn't a portfolio management problem, and predictably more dead is just the aggregate judgment with a different hat on — you keep putting the sum back in and calling it a result.

Nina: Last thing. Your view doesn't say save the one, it says you may. It also says you may save the five, and you yourself do save the five whenever the lottery comes up that way. So the live claim was never about numbers at all — it's that numbers don't generate duties. Everything about sums being unreal is decoration on a claim about what we owe strangers, and you and the aggregator are disagreeing about that, not about arithmetic.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what at each step — rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification — and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'd especially like to know whether the every-day-for-a-year case is a genuinely new problem for Sam or just the original disagreement wearing a longer coat.
