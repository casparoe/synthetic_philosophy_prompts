I write novels, not philosophy, so forgive me if I don't have the vocabulary for this. Something I have to decide constantly is which of a character's mental goings-on are *conscious* ones, because that's the difference between what goes on the page and what stays off it. My gut verdicts don't seem to line up with any single rule, and I'd like to know if there's a principle that captures them and which ones I'd have to give up.

- Miriam, three days after burying her husband, says she's fine and is not thinking about him, but she's been crying for an hour and drives to their old house by mistake. Her grief is conscious. Definately. Even though she'd deny it.
- A two-month-old in a hospital scene, in pain after surgery. That's conscious pain, surely, even though there's no way she's having thoughts about her own states.
- Ruth, who has no inner monologue at all — no words in her head ever, just images and urges. Obviously conscious.
- The family dog, terrified of thunder, under the bed. Conscious fear, I'd say, though a dumbed-down one.
- Lena drives forty miles on a road she knows and remembers none of it, but she braked in time for a deer. I want to say she was conscious of the road but not really of driving, which feels like cheating.
- Theo at the piano, mid-performance, thinking about nothing, not even that he's playing. This is the most conscious anyone is anywhere in the book.
- A character who gets up at 3am, makes toast, and remembers nothing. Not conscious.
- A cortically blind character who guesses shapes in her blind field better than chance and insists she sees nothing there. Not conscious of them, even though the information is clearly steering her.
- A character on a fictional drug in my current draft: perfectly calm by every measure, but utterly convinced she is furious. I want to say she is not conscious of anger — she's just wrong about herself.
- A character with a neural implant that silently stamps "you are seeing red" into her head whenever her visual cortex fires the right way. To me that doesn't make her seeing conscious at all; it's a machine making an announcement.

My working rule has been something like: a state is conscious if the character could notice it by turning their attention inward, without any new evidence. That fits Miriam and maybe the baby, but I think it gives me the wrong answer on the implant, on the drugged woman, and I genuinely can't tell what it says about Lena versus Theo — who look structurally the same to me, absorbed and not thinking about anything, yet I grade them in opposite directions.

Is there one principle that gets these, or close, and what does it say about two cases I haven't included: a coma patient who shows the right brain activity in a scanner when asked to imagine playing tennis, and the same dog once she's been trained on word buttons and presses "scared" during a storm? Please tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate, and which one you'd overturn — I'd rather rewrite a scene than keep a bad intuition.
