I write about science and ideas, and I'm finishing a long piece on strong longtermism — the view that what matters most about what we do now, in expectation, is how it shapes the very long-run future. Nearly everyone I've interviewed for the piece argues in expected value, and I've found that when I check their arithmetic against my own reactions to particular funding decisions, the two keep coming apart. So I've started writing down my verdicts on concrete cases in the hope of working out what principle I'm actually using, because I owe my editor at least one sentence that states it.

The cases, with my gut verdicts. (a) A proposal with a one-in-ten-thousand chance of preventing human extinction this century, against a programme that will certainly save ten thousand lives over the next decade: I fund the first, decisively. (b) The same one-in-ten-thousand shot, but now at preventing what I've started calling sterile survival — humanity persists for the next billion years at roughly current numbers, current standards of living and current reach, never expanding past the solar system and never qualitatively changing, so that no one dies who would otherwise have lived: I fund this at something like two-thirds of the urgency I gave (a), which means I am not treating mere biological survival as the whole story. (c) A one-in-a-hundred chance of making the entire long-run future go ten per cent better — better institutions, less cruelty, more of what I'd call flourishing — against the same certain ten thousand lives: here I take the certain lives, even though by the arithmetic this should be worth ten times as much as (a), since a hundredth of everything is more than a ten-thousandth of everything. My discomfort is partly that it feels presumptuous to shape people who don't yet exist, but (b) has already conceded that existence isn't the only thing I care about, so I can't lean on that. (d) A well-off couple deciding whether to have a child they would raise well: I don't think longtermism gives them any reason to, since an additional happy life doesn't strike me as an improvement in itself. But five thousand colonists, ninety years into a two-hundred-year voyage, whose birth rate has slipped below replacement, so that the mission would simply end with them: their not having children looks to me like a catastrophe, not a neutral choice. (e) An intervention that would establish self-sufficient settlements around other stars two centuries earlier than otherwise, at no added risk, against one that cuts extinction risk this century by one hundredth of a percentage point: these feel to me about equally worth funding, though only the second ever gets talked about. (f) Two programmes that each cut the chance of losing the future by the same small amount, one aimed at engineered pandemics and one at the systems that would shape whatever civilization comes next: I'd pay about a quarter more for the second, because it also averts futures in which we survive indefinitely under values that are badly distorted by my lights — which sits badly with (c).

What I want from you is the principle, stated precisely enough that I could print it and apply it yourself, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a perpetual endowment under a ten-thousand-year charter, funded now and governed so that the money can be spent later, when the picture is clearer, versus spending the same sum today on the best available extinction-risk reduction. Give me at least two further cases it decides that I haven't listed. And be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate: if there are two or three candidate principles that cut the losses differently, set them all out and say which verdict each one sacrifices. If something has to go, I'd rather lose (c) than (a), and I'd be most reluctant to give up my reactions to (d) and (e).
