Me and my friend (she did two years of a philosophy PhD before quitting) had a massive argument last night about the ethics consult notes I write. Her position: all this "what would the patient decide if they really understood their situation" stuff is a made-up character, and I should just tell people which option actually causes the least harm and stoppretending I'm channeling anyone. My position: most of the people I advise are scared, exhausted, or are simply not going to execute a fine-grained balancing test correctly, so handing them the theoretically best act can make the outcome worse. We yelled, nobody won, and I'd rather have something I can actually run.

The job: I'm the one who writes the one-page ethics consult note at a mid-sized hospital. About eight to twelve a month, always turned around inside 48 hours, and the note goes to the attending physician and the family and needs the committee chair's sign-off. Most cases are one of two shapes: a competent patient asking "what should I do?", or a surrogate deciding for someone who can't decide anymore. The two bits of theory I keep reaching for are the gap between what makes an action right and the procedure a real person should use to pick one, and the move of answering these questions by asking what a better-informed, clearer-headed version of the person would settle on. I want that turned into something operational.

So: give me the questions in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, the points where the procedure just stops with a no, and the points where it hands the decision back to a human because the disagreement is about values and not facts. Be explicit about the dials, because I noticed they matter: how much information to add, whether to correct for fear/pain/exhaustion, whether to correct for reasoning errors, in what order you dial those in (I think a different order can flip the verdict, and I want the procedure to fix one and say why), and whether we ever get to "improve" someone's aims and values or only their beliefs.

Run it on three: (1) a 78-year-old man with advanced dementia whose only prior statement, scribbled at a routine visit years ago, was roughly "if I can't recognize my family, just let me go," now admitted with a pneumonia that is very likely reversible; (2) a young pregnant patient who says she wants "everything done" while apparently picturing a prognosis nothing like the one her team has actually given; (3) a surrogate who is also the sole heir. Include at least one case where the procedure spits out a recommendation I'll hate. And tell me plainly which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat, and what this procedure just can't do for me.
