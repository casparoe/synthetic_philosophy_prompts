hey — I'm writing the internal position doc for my team (we ship AI features on top of other people's models) and before I hand it to our safety people I want to know whether the package actually hangs together. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Tool vs agent isn't a fact about the model, it's a fact about the deployment. What makes something a tool is that it acts on *borrowed* authority: I hand it a bounded task, I can take that authority back mid-task, and if the task fails that isn't the same thing as me having failed. What makes something an agent is that it holds *standing* authority and keeps acting after I've stopped paying attention. Same weights, different deployment, different category — so there's no such thing as "an agent model."

(2) Then the thing to worry about isn't how long a system runs or how many steps it takes. It's (a) whether what it does can be undone, and (b) whether whoever specified the goal is also whoever eats the cost when it goes wrong. A thing that books ten meetings across a week is a tool; a thing that moves money once is not.

(3) I don't think alignment is even the right frame for tool-shaped systems. Deception in a system that never leaves a sandbox is a reliability bug — same underlying phenomenon, different vocabulary — and reaching for the agent vocabulary there just makes people either panic or tune out. Product safety, not alignment.

(4) The case I think is actually underrated is the diffuse one: a thousand small delegations, each of which passes (2) on its own, that add up to nobody being able to say what's being done in their name. I've been calling the extra requirement the *answerability condition*: for anything done in your name, you should be able to give the reasons for it without having to go ask the system why it did that. I think that condition fails long before anything dramatic happens.

(5) But I also think there's a ceiling on the tools-only position, and I mean it as a capability claim, not a policy preference: for some tasks the benefit only shows up if the judgment happens *inside* the loop, and you can't get that by stringing bounded tool calls together end to end. Chaining tools buys you execution, not judgment. So "we'll only ever ship tools" quietly gives up the most valuable use cases.

(6) My fix for (5) is licensing rather than prohibition: a few named tiers, where going up a tier buys more standing authority and costs more logging, more human sign-off, and more of a named person's name on it. Bright line: no system gets authority that can't be pulled back, and no tier where nobody's name is on it.

(7) And one verdict I'm attached to that I can't seem to derive from any of the above: I'm fine with a system drafting and sending routine replies in my name, but not fine with it negotiating my salary for me — even though I'd set the floor and the ceiling myself and could walk into the room and take over at any point. Which, by (2), should make it fine.

My guess is that (3) and (5) are the pair that fight, but that's a guess and I'd believe you if the trouble is somewhere else entirely. What I want is an audit, not a lecture on the tool/agent debate — I've read the blog posts. Tell me which commitments actually pull against each other and which only look like they do, ordered by how bad the tension is, and for each one what specifically has to give and what giving it up costs me (does it cost me a case verdict I care about, or just a piece of rhetoric?). Then tell me the single revision that cleans up the most.
