The case: a fair coin is tossed on Sunday. Heads: Beauty is awakened on Monday only. Tails: she is awakened Monday and Tuesday, with memory erasure in between. On every awakening she writes a number p between 0 and 1, scored by a strictly proper scoring rule against that run's coin. Two possible scorebooks:

A — every awakening is scored. Expected score is maximized at p = 1/3, since two-thirds of awakenings occur in Tails runs.

B — the lab waits until the run ends, then picks one of that run's awakenings uniformly at random and scores only that number. Every run contributes exactly one scored number, and half of all runs are Heads runs, so the frequency of Heads among scored numbers is 1/2 and p = 1/2 maximizes expected score.

My opening. You say scorebook A's 1/3 is double-counting rather than credence, and that B — one score per run, no double-counting — is where optimal report and credence finally coincide at 1/2. But look at what your own conditionalization does inside B. Conditional on Tails there were two awakenings and only one gets scored: P(my awakening is the scored one | Heads) = 1, while P(my awakening is the scored one | Tails) = 1/2. So learning that you are the scored awakening should move you from 1/2 to 2/3 — and that is the number you should report if you maximize expected score from within the awakening. Yet among the numbers actually scored in B only half come from Heads runs, so 2/3 is miscalibrated, and 1/2 is not: on the per-run sample you insist is the right one, the thirder's credences are calibrated and yours are not. Which horn do you take — report 2/3 in B and accept the expected loss, deny that conditioning on being scored is legitimate, or admit that your 1/2 was never tracking any sampling scheme you accept?
