**The imitation game is an assay, and nobody has validated it**

In Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", he opens by proposing to consider the question "Can machines think?", and then says he intends to "replace the question by another, which is closely related to it and is expressed in relatively unambiguous words". In my old field that move had a name and a sequel. You can't measure "is this water safe?", so you operationalise — coliform counts, lead in parts per billion — and then, crucially, you validate. Nobody accepts a new assay on the strength of the idea behind it. You have to show what it actually responds to.

Validation meant at least four things in my department. Selectivity: does the method respond to anything besides the target analyte? Calibration: does it track known standards across a range of concentrations? A limit of detection: how little can it see? Robustness: do you get the same answer with a different operator, a different instrument, a different day? As far as I can tell the imitation game has had none of this. It has produced an enormous amount of argument and approximately no validation data.

The standards problem comes first. Our only positive standards are ourselves. We have no independent samples of known mindedness to calibrate against, so we calibrate on the one class of positives available and then treat a match to them as the signal. The chemical equivalent is validating a method on pure standards in distilled water and never running it on a spiked real matrix. It is entirely possible that the assay is responding to a property that travels with mindedness in humans without constituting it.

Second problem, and I think this one is straightforward: the readout is a property of the pair, not of the candidate. Interrogators differ in severity, and they differ in what charms them. One is persuaded by a typo, another by a graceful admission of ignorance, another by a long anecdote about a childhood kitchen. The chemist's reflex here is that absorbance depends on the path length and the lamp as well as on the sample. In educational testing this is handled by treating the questioners as items: many interrogators of graded severity, many candidates, estimate candidate ability and item severity together, then inspect the residuals. Until somebody reports data of roughly that shape, a pass is partly a fact about the interrogator.

Now the bit I think might be new. Suppose that battery were run: enough interrogators of genuinely different styles — chatty, hostile, technical, personal, patient, impatient — and enough candidates to fit something. If the test tracks one property, then one latent dimension plus item severity should mostly explain who fools whom, and what's left over should look like noise. My prediction is that it won't. I expect two fairly independent factors. Call one *fluency*: the capacity to produce well-shaped, plausible conversation on any topic. Call the other *situatedness*: having been somewhere, having a body, a history, specific boring commitments, the ability to be dull and particular on request. A fluent system with no history does well on the first and badly on the second; plenty of real people are the reverse. If the residuals need both factors, then "passes the test" isn't reporting a single measurement. And note that this is a stronger complaint than "the test measures the wrong thing." It's the complaint that there may be no one thing there for it to be an assay *of*.

Which suggests a selectivity panel. Build candidates that vary one human-like property at a time and see how the assay sorts them. (a) The mimic: a system tuned to imitate one particular real person — their hesitations, their misspellings, their opinions, the way they answer a question they don't like. No mind, maximal surface match. (b) The locked-in respondent: fully conscious, able only to signal yes or no, slowly, through a switch. (c) The fluent confabulator: warm, plausible, ungrounded. My guess is the assay ranks (a) and (c) above (b). If so, its selectivity is poor in the technical sense. I want to be careful here, because I think two different complaints get run together: (b) is about the test failing as a *necessary* condition on mind, (a) and (c) about it failing as a *sufficient* one. Those are seperate failures and they'd need seperate fixes.

Three objections I can hear coming, and my replies as they stand. First: Turing never offered the test as an analysis of mind, only as a replacement question and a prediction about what machines would eventually do, so my demands are beside the point. Reply: if it's only ever meant as a sufficient condition, then its entire job is to have few false positives, which just is the selectivity question — and the mimic is exactly the false positive it has no way of excluding. Second: unidimensionality is an unreasonable demand, intelligence isn't one thing, chess skill isn't one thing, why should mind be? Reply: for chess we have an independent criterion, winning and losing games, so we don't need the internal structure to be simple; for mind we have no criterion that the test isn't itself supplying, so the internal-structure check is the only validation available. Third, and this is the one that actually worries me: the whole framing assumes that mindedness is a quantity, something with a true value that an assay can be more or less accurate about, the way lead-in-ppb has a true value. If it isn't that kind of thing, then my talk of calibration and selectivity is a category mistake wearing a lab coat.

So the conclusion I want is modest, not "the test is worthless." It's that a pass has never been shown to be evidence about the candidate rather than about the interrogator, and that until someone publishes severity-controlled data and a selectivity panel, the imitation game is an assay with a beautiful rationale and no validation. In my old department that's roughly where a grant application would have got to.
