I am a PhD student in intellectual history working on a chapter provisionally titled 'Keynes Between Moore and the Market: The Good Life After the Economic Problem.' I am leading a graduate seminar next week on Keynes's views on the good life, and I would like you to write a dialogue for that seminar.

Please write a dialogue of roughly 1200 to 1600 words between two equally competent interpreters of Keynes's ethics, whom I will call the Guardian and the Civic participant. Set the dialogue as a post-panel exchange at a small Cambridge graduate conference on 'Keynes and the Good Life,' with the two speakers continuing their disagreement over coffee after a paper. Both should be formidable: neither should be a caricature, and each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than talk past them.

The disagreement should turn on a distinction I have been developing in my own work, and I want the dialogue to make that distinction do real work. Call it the distinction between 'receptivity goods' and 'agential goods.' Receptivity goods are the intrinsically valuable states of mind Keynes associated with his early Moorean commitments: love, aesthetic experience, friendship, and the disinterested pursuit of truth. Agential goods are the excellences involved in acting well under uncertainty: judging probabilities without full information, accepting responsibility for institutions one cannot fully control, and exercising what I call 'stewardship under opacity.' My provisional claim is that Keynes's development is best captured by a conditionally lexicographic principle: when the social preconditions for receptivity goods are secure, receptivity goods dominate the shape of the good life; when those preconditions are unstable or unjust, agential goods become lexicographically prior, not merely instrumentally useful. I call this the 'precarity-priority' reading, and I want the Guardian to reject or reinterpret it while the Civic participant defends a version of it, though you may adjust the labels if a better version of the debate emerges.

The content should meet the following conditions.

First, the Guardian should argue that Keynes never abandoned the Moorean core: the point of economic reform, public service, and even speculation was to protect and enlarge the space for receptivity goods. On this reading, the 'precarity-priority' principle is at best a temporary emergency ethic, and treating agential goods as lexicographically prior threatens to turn Keynes into a civic republican or a virtue theorist in a way that misreads his ironic, aristocratic-liberal individualism.

Second, the Civic participant should argue that this understates the normative weight Keynes places on the agent's relation to uncertainty and to the future. Using the contrast between the famous 1930 essay on the economic possibilities for our grandchildren and the 1938 memoir he read to the Memoir Club, the Civic participant should press the idea that Keynes came to regard the active, responsible management of economic life as partly constitutive of a good human life, not merely a means to pleasant states of mind. The memoir's mixture of affection for and distance from his early beliefs should be used as evidence of development, not mere nostalgia.

Third, the two should then introduce a complication neither of them can fully solve: Keynes's own life as a speculator, public servant, and patron of the arts. If the good life is fundamentally a matter of receptivity, why does he describe the love of money as a possession in such morbid terms while continuing to seek it as a means, and why does he treat the preservation of civilization as a duty that can override personal cultivation? The Guardian and the Civic participant should each give their best account of this 'speculator's dilemma,' and the exchange should end with genuine, unresolved pressure on both positions rather than a tidy resolution.

Do not have either speaker cite scholarship, name living scholars, or appeal to works we have not discussed. Paraphrase Keynes's claims rather than quoting them verbatim; if you need to mark a phrase as a term of art, use quotation marks only for invented phrases, not for any passage from a primary text.

Finally, because I need to model intellectual honesty for my students, please add, after the dialogue, a short section headed Where I Could Be Wrong. In that section give two first-person notes, no more than 150 words each: one from the Guardian and one from the Civic participant. Each note should state the single argument or piece of evidence that would most likely make that speaker abandon their reading, and should name the point in their own reconstruction where they think the reasoning is most likely to fail. Do not resolve the disagreement in the notes; the point is to show what real defeat would look like for each position.
