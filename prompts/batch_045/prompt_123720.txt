I work as a research assistant on a project that is annotating every conditional in a corpus of mid‑century newspaper reportage and detective fiction. Each conditional receives exactly one of two tags: *indicative* or *counterfactual*. The codebook says to use the counterfactual tag when the speaker is not treating the antecedent as an open possibility, and says nothing at all about verb forms. My supervisor adjudicates whatever I flag; these are her eight rulings so far, each with the sentence and the corpus context I put in front of her.

1. *If the match is cancelled tomorrow, we will lose the deposit.* — printed the evening before the match, outcome unknown, no indication of which way the writer expects it to go. Tagged **indicative**.
2. *If Maria is in her office right now, the light is on.* — present tense, speaker has not checked. Tagged **indicative**.
3. *If the butler was in the pantry at midnight, he heard the shot.* — uttered by the detective before the pantry question has been settled. Tagged **indicative**.
4. *If he took the money, the auditors will find it.* — past antecedent, future consequent, matter unresolved. Tagged **indicative**.
5. *If Maria were in her office right now, the light would be on.* — uttered by a character who has just been told Maria has gone home. Tagged **counterfactual**.
6. *If the butler had been in the pantry at midnight, he would have heard the shot.* — uttered by a character who knows the butler spent the whole evening in the drawing room. Tagged **counterfactual**.
7. *If the strike had started on Monday, the plant would have lost a week of output.* — written after the strike was called off. Tagged **counterfactual**.
8. *If I were you, I would accept the offer.* — ordinary advice. Tagged **counterfactual**.

Those eight rulings are compatible with the codebook's definition read in several different ways, and also with a much cruder rule that just looks for the fake past or irrealis marking and a *would* in the consequent. Up to now the two have lined up perfectly on every example, and I have been coding off the verb forms without ever being corrected. My next batch contains two sentences on which they come apart, and I would rather reconstruct her rule than wait for her to get back from her conference.

N1: *If the strike started tomorrow, the plant would lose a week of output.* Written the night before the strike deadline; the reporter states explicitly that the outcome is a toss‑up and treats it as a genuine, live possibility.

N2: *If the butler is the murderer, then he was in the pantry at midnight.* Spoken by the detective in the closing chapter, immediately after she has told the assembled suspects that the butler is not the murderer; she asserts the conditional as a step in an argument whose conclusion is that he is not the murderer.

I am not asking how these sentences ought to be classified in the abstract. I am asking what she will say, given that she has to remain consistent with her own eight rulings. Specifically:

1. Set out the different rules that fit all eight rulings equally well — not only the first one that comes to mind — including variants that differ only in which part of the sentence they look at, and note which plausible rules the eight rulings already eliminate.
2. Say what each surviving rule predicts for N1 and for N2, and identify which pairs of rules the two sentences fail to separate.
3. Give your best guess at her tag for each of the two sentences, with your reasons for preferring the rule you attribute to her. In weighing this: every written justification she has produced cites the tense and mood of the verbs, but in project meetings she has more than once insisted that the tags are meant to track what the speaker is doing with the possibility rather than the words on the page.
4. For any tie that N1 and N2 leave unbroken, supply one further sentence — together with the corpus context you would need me to give her — that would break it, and what each tied rule predicts for it. If you think one of my two probes is defective, say which and tell me what to change about its context.
5. Say which of the two sentences does more of the diagnostic work, and why.
