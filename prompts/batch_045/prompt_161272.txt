Hi — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I write a recurring column on institutional design gone weird, and I've fallen down a hole. At a conference dinner last year somebody taught me a parlor game where the only move you get is changing the rules, including the rule about how rules get changed, and I haven't stopped thinking about it since. What that's turned into practically is reader mail: every couple of weeks someone sends me a real governance document — an open-source foundation charter, a housing co-op's house rules, a volunteer mutual-aid network's founding agreement, once the founding text of an on-chain organisation whose members vote with tokens — and asks whether some move their board is about to pull is actually permitted, or whether the clause they think protects them protects them at all. I'm not a lawyer (my editor makes me route anything legal past a friend who does municipal governance work, and she has maybe twenty minutes a week for me), I'm often drafting on deadline with patchy signal so I can't chase references, and I need to triage these myself in about half an hour before deciding whether there's a story there and how hedged my language has to be. Please don't give me citations or a reading list — if you're drawing on some doctrine, name it in two or three words inline and move on.

So: give me a procedure I can actually run on one of these documents. Ordered questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it has to hand off to a person because the question is one of values rather than text — and mark the hand-offs distinctly, because "ask my lawyer friend" costs me a week and "editor decides" costs me twenty minutes. I've been using my own sloppy categories and I'd like you to keep, rename, or bin them: (a) threshold flips, where a body uses the current amendment power to lower the bar and then walks through the lowered door; (b) definitional dodges, where someone relabels a change as a clarification, a practice, or a policy so it isn't an "amendment" at all; (c) borrowed rules, where the charter incorporates some outside document "as revised from time to time" and the outside document quietly rewrites the charter; (d) collisions, where two clauses each entrench themselves and say any amendment diminishing their protection is void; (e) interpretation creep, where a body's rulings are deemed to be part of the charter itself. The procedure has to say what to do when I only have an excerpt, or when the document is simply silent, or when the answer depends on which order two votes were taken in, and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat. One more thing that's specific to my job: sometimes running this surfaces an exploit — a way the thing can legally be gutted — and I then have to decide whether writing about it makes it real. Put that decision in the procedure as a step, not a footnote, and include a note on what hedging language to use in copy when the answer is "the text does not settle this."

Then run it on three cases, spelling out where each one stops. Case one: a foundation charter that says (my paraphrase) the Charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the Council, except that the amendment section itself may only be amended by a three-quarter Council vote plus a majority of the membership — and a Council holding 68% wants, at a single meeting, to add an emergency-powers section and to reduce that exception to a two-thirds Council vote alone. Case two: a co-op whose rules say no rule may be adopted that reduces a member's right to quiet enjoyment, and also that the rules may be amended by simple majority at any general meeting; a majority now wants to ban amplified instruments after 9pm, and to file it as a "house practice" rather than a rule. Case three, the one I expect to dislike: a mutual-aid network I admire and want to write a warm piece about, whose charter incorporates a Stewards' Handbook "as revised by the Stewards from time to time," where the Handbook has grown a clause letting the Stewards decide who counts as a member for voting, and where the charter says the Stewards' readings of it are final and shall be treated as part of it. If your procedure tells me the thing I was going to praise is cosmetic, say so plainly and tell me what I'd have to print instead.

Format: give me the procedure in prose first, then the three worked applications, then the structured bits. For the procedure, a JSON array where each element is one step with exactly these fields: step_id (integer, in order), question (the question to ask), sufficient_answer (what counts as an adequate answer), if_fails (one of "stop", "hand_to_lawyer", "hand_to_editor", "continue"), is_judgment (boolean — true if this is really a judgment call dressed as a step), likely_misfire (one sentence on how this step will go wrong in practice). For the cases, a table with columns case_id, first_step_that_bites, verdict (one or two sentences in language I could print), hedge_phrase (the actual words I should use in the piece), signoff (me / editor / lawyer friend), exploit_publication_risk (low, medium or high, plus a few words of why). Finally a JSON object with a single key, limits, whose value is an array of three to five short strings on what this procedure cannot do.
