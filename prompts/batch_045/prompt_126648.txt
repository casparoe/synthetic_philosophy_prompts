I sit on the editorial standards committee of a public broadcaster. We must rule on a three-part drama, *The Long Silence*, about Miriam Halloran (1938–2011), a real senior civil servant who anonymously passed documents to a journalist exposing the contamination of a town's water supply, destroying her own career in the process and being written out of the official account until a biography appeared some years after her death. The series is sympathetic, well sourced, and in most respects careful. Our difficulty is with two invented elements.

First: a deathbed reconciliation with Halloran's estranged daughter Nell, her only surviving close relative. Nell has written to us stating that nothing of the kind occurred, that she was not at the bedside, and that the scene turns her own considered refusal into a failing she is shown to have repented. The writers point to an unsent letter found in Halloran's papers asking her daughter to come, and argue that the scene is therefore faithful to her inner life rather than fabricated. Second: a composite colleague, credited as such, to whom Halloran confides a religious crisis she never had; his scenes include lines adapted from a recorded 2007 interview in which Halloran said that she did not care to be thought well of, only that the story be got right. Our charter forbids presenting invented material as fact but says nothing about inventing material about real people. The papers were licensed, and the depiction approved, by Halloran's literary executor — a former colleague who is also a paid consultant on the series; Nell says his financial stake disqualifies him, and indeed the executor argues that a flattering invention cannot harm anyone.

Draft the committee's decision memo, in British English. It should give a concrete recommendation — broadcast as it stands, require specified changes, impose a particular form of on-screen signalling, or withhold — with the reasoning Nell, the writers, and the executor could each respond to. It should engage seriously with the strongest case for the other side (including the thought that the dead are beyond being wronged, and that the portrait here is a kind one), say what weight, if any, our committee should give to the dead person's own stated wishes and to the objections of surviving relatives, and end with two or three criteria we could actually apply to future dramatizations of named people who are recently dead.
