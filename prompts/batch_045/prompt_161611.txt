hey, my reading group is going through the socialist calculation debate — Mises and Hayek against Lange — and one member who works in corporate finance keeps saying the whole anti-planning case rests on a bad picture of what a firm is. his line is that a planned economy is just one big firm, firms plan internally all the time and at absurd scale, and a multinational with 200k employees is a command economy on the inside: transfer prices between divisions, head office allocating capital, divisional budgets, no market sitting between the widget division and the assembly division. so, he says, the Mises claim that you can't do rational economic calculation without market prices for capital goods is refuted by the existence of a big retailer, and Lange was basically right that the market is only a machine for solving an allocation problem, which you could rebuild with spreadsheets and a rule that every manager set price equal to marginal cost and minimise costs. his upgrade, when pressed, is that every division should issue internal shares that managers trade, bonuses paid on how those prices move.

i pointed out that a firm still buys its inputs and sells its outputs in real markets, and that a firm sealed off from both would be back in Mises' situation. he had an answer: the multidivisional firm already runs an internal capital market, head office rations investment across divisions the way a stock market does, and there is no external price for a division's R&D pipeline either, so it can't be impossible.

i want an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on socialism. so: give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the price, the profit, the entrepreneur, the shareholder, bankruptcy, the takeover — and then be precise about which disanalogies actually carry weight for the conclusion he wants and which are just decoration. three things i'd like taken seriously rather than waved at. first, whether planning inside a firm is really command at all, or just contracting all the way down: the line that a firm has no more authority over you than your grocer does, because you can always quit and so can it — if that's right, the analogy seems to describe a market, not a replacement for one. second, what the analogue of failure is when losses get covered and nobody can buy the firm: does the mechanism that makes internal prices informative survive without exit at the top? third, whether his internal-capital-market point is a counterexample to Mises or just a description of what head office can do because it is disciplined from outside by a capital market it does not itself plan.

on it's own i'd of thought the firm analogy shows the opposite of what he thinks — the firm is a boat with a hull, and the interesting thing is the water it floats in, not the engine. then say plainly whether his conclusion survives with the breaks on the table, and offer a better analogy if there is one. 700-900 words, no citations needed, and please don't spend the first half just summarising the debate — assume i've read it.
