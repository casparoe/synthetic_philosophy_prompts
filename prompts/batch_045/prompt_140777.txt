I write a small, reader-supported newsletter on forecasting, and my current draft tries to carry the reference-class / outside-view apparatus out of its home territory — big infrastructure projects, where the lesson is that a project's own detailed story predicts little and the base rate of its class predicts a lot — and into a case where I think the apparatus quietly fails: forecasting how long a large, wealthy, federally organized great power will persist as *the same polity*. A friend who reads a lot of history keeps telling me that no great power of that shape has lasted much past a couple of centuries, so on the outside view we are overdue; my brother-in-law, who does cost estimation for transit projects, says the outside view is the whole method and that I am rationalizing. I want to explain why I think they are both right about tunnels and much less obviously right about empires. My real worry is that what I have written is only an elaborate way of saying that choosing a reference class is hard, so I would be grateful for help finding out whether there is a genuine argument in here, and where exactly it breaks.

The draft, condensed to its five moves:

1. *Setup.* The inside view enumerates the polity's particular strengths — fiscal depth, a constitutional order that has already survived one severe test, a currency others hold reserves in, a professional officer corps — and returns a long expected remaining life. The outside view puts it in the class of large, multi-regional, militarized polities and reads off a base rate in the low centuries. The two disagree by roughly an order of magnitude. Received wisdom says: trust the outside view, because the inside view is built from the same optimistic cognition that produces cost overruns.

2. *The sameness-predicate problem.* Every member of the reference class is mortal, so the base rate of eventual failure is trivially one hundred percent; all the content sits in *when we say it died*. Change of ruling house, change of regime type, loss of the periphery, dissolution of the central fiscal-military apparatus, or loss of a monopoly on legitimate violence inside the core territory yield wildly different durations for the same object. My suspicion is that the spread across dating conventions is larger than the spread across polities within any single convention, in which case the outside view here is not measuring the world but measuring a classificatory decision. If so, that is a different failure mode from the planning fallacy: the cost estimator's reference class has an agreed outcome variable, and this one does not.

3. *The retrospective-nomination problem.* We populate the class with polities that later got labelled empires, and getting that label is partly a function of having ended memorably. Polities that evolved quietly, or were absorbed with their administrative personnel left in place, tend to be filed under the successor's name instead. The reference class is therefore selected on something close to the outcome, and the resulting base rate may be an artefact of historiography and of naming rather than a fact about political durability.

4. *Left truncation.* Whatever the raw class base rate is, it is the wrong number for something that has already lasted around two and a half centuries: the relevant quantity is the conditional hazard, the probability of failure in the next interval given survival to now. If that hazard is roughly flat — if polities do not senesce — then having survived this long is neither evidence of special robustness nor evidence of being due; the observation that some cases ran much longer (the Roman state in the West on its conventional dating, the Ottoman house on its conventional dating) is entirely compatible with a constant hazard, so there is no regression toward a class mean for me to appeal to. This is the move I am least sure of: institutional accumulation might make the hazard *fall* with age, while fiscal-military overstretch or elite overproduction might make it *rise*, and I cannot tell from my armchair which shape is the better default.

5. *A proposed replacement variable: the two-clocks view.* Stop forecasting when the polity ends and forecast how long the successor's governing personnel continue to be drawn from the predecessor's administrative corps — I have been calling this the elite-inheritance half-life. Most terminations are not vacuums; they are personnel changes at the top. If the continuity that matters is administrative rather than constitutional, then a dramatic-looking collapse that leaves the bureaucracy, the tax register, and the officer corps intact is a small event, while a peaceful constitutional rewrite that purges them is a large one. So, I argue, the outside view should be taken over administrative-continuity durations rather than over flag-durations, and I expect that substitution to lengthen the medians and deflate the drama considerably.

What I would like from you:

(a) Check part 4 hardest, since it is the load-bearing piece and the one I am most likely to have garbled: is the left-truncation point correctly stated, is the flat-hazard assumption the right neutral default or is some senescence model standard here, and does conditioning on survival really dissolve the regression-to-the-mean intuition the way I claim?

(b) Tell me bluntly which of moves 2, 3, and 5 is doing genuinely new work and which is just the reference-class problem wearing a costume. If one should be cut, say which and why.

(c) Write the strongest single paragraph you can on behalf of someone who thinks the outside view is exactly as authoritative here as it is in cost estimation, and that my complications are special pleading from a citizen of the case in question. I want the steelmanned version I would be embarrassed not to have answered in the piece.

(d) Suggest one piece of conceptual work — or one check I could in principle do without archives or a library, from ordinary background knowledge — that would turn this from a reminder that reference classes are hard into a claim with real content.

Please do not give me citations, authors, or reading suggestions; I will handle sourcing later and I want argument rather than bibliography. Do flag anywhere I have begged the question, used a term in two senses, or leaned on a historical claim that a careful reader would reject.
