Last week in my phil of mind seminar we did the causal exclusion argument (Kim's name came up, though I didn't catch which paper), and I came away unsure whether I'd understood it. Then a friend sent me a summary from a popular science magazine that puts it like this: 'Physics gives a complete causal account of everything that happens: every physical event that has a cause has a complete physical cause, so there's no room left for anything non-physical to do any causing. Our thoughts and feelings aren't part of that physical story — a pain, or a belief, is a mental property, not a physical one. So when you decide to raise your arm, your decision isn't what makes your arm go up; the firings in your motor cortex already did that. If your decision caused the movement as well, the movement would have two independent sufficient causes, and coincidences like that just don't happen. So the mind is causally inert: consciousness is real, but it doesn't do anything, and free will is an illusion. The only escape route philosophers take seriously is to identify mental properties with physical properties — which is just to say that your thoughts are nothing but patterns of neurons firing.'

My roommate, who's a physics major and always confident about this stuff, says the argument is airtight and that this is why nobody defends mental causation anymore. I'm not so sure. For one thing, the bit about 'the motor cortex already did it' and 'two independent sufficient causes' seems to assume that the decision and the neural firing are two separate happenings competing for the same job, and I don't see why someone who thinks mental properties are real but not physical has to grant that. For another, my memory of the seminar was that epiphenomenalism is what people are trying to escape rather than something the argument simply proves outright, and that the argument was supposed to be about mental properties generally, not about consciousness in particular. The last sentence bothers me too, but I can't say why — it feels like it slides between 'identical to' and 'nothing but', and I suspect those aren't the same claim.

So: could you go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher of mind would actually grant, what's a fair simplification for a popular audience, and what's subtly misstated? I'd also like to know whether this really is the exclusion argument or something else wearing its clothes; which premise is the live point of dispute; whether identifying mental with physical properties is genuinely the only response available or just the only one the writer knew about; and whether the free will conclusion follows once the sloppy parts are fixed. If it helps, sketch what the strongest version of the argument looks like after the corrections — I want to know whether my roommate is right that the conclusion survives, or whether the summary itself is quietly doing the work.
