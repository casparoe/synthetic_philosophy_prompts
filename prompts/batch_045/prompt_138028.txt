I'm a journalist — mostly science and ideas — and I'm putting together a short audio piece on Orwell and language. My editor says its too inside-baseball, so I need a script that actually earns the inside part.

Give me a dialogue between two people who've both clearly read Orwell properly and disagree about which direction the causation runs.

Them: the corruption starts with the individual writer. Prefabricated phrases, euphemism, abstract nouns doing the work of verbs — that's a refusal to do the work of thinking, which makes it a moral failing, not a stylistic one. The fix is personal verbal hygiene: short words, concrete referents, never a phrase you didn't choose yourself. Political regeneration starts with the sentence, and if you can't say it plainly you probably can't think it.

Her: that's backwards, or at the least it's the small half. The corruption runs from orthodoxy inward. The vocabulary you have available is shaped by who has power, and if the available words can't hold a thought, you don't get to have the thought — so "just write plainly" is cosmetic, and worse, it offloads a structural problem onto individual writers who didn't create it.

No strawmen, and no talking past each other. She should concede the cases where his diagnosis obviously does work — corporate memos, grant prose, buzzword salad that no one wrote on purpose. He should have a real answer to who curates the vocabulary, and to the awkward fact that Orwell's own polemical prose is nowhere near the plain style he recommends in that 1946 essay.

Here's the bit I want them to chew on, which is my own coinage so push back on it if it's broken: the paraphrase tax. The number of extra words you have to spend whenever you want to say something the available vocabulary has no ready term for. My claim is that the tax falls unevenly — whoever already owns the vocabulary pays nothing, whoever has to rebuild the sentence from scratch every time pays a lot, and that unevenness is most of what political power over language consists of. Have him argue the tax is real but voluntary (refusing to pay it is exactly what writing plainly is), and her argue that refusing to pay it just makes you hard to understand, which is a different kind of defenselessness rather than an escape.

Five numbered exchanges exactly, no intro, no outro. Each exchange one or two short speeches, a couple of sentences — no paragraph-long monologues, it'll die in audio. And please seperate what's settled about Orwell's views from what people who write about him genuinely argue over: wherever one of them is about to lean on a contested reading, flag it in brackets right there instead of smoothing it over, because I don't want to broadcast a disputed interpretation as the consensus. Also, don't have them recite lines from the essay — paraphrase in their own words, my producer can't clear audio of it anyway.
