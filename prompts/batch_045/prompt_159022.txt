I convene a small reading group — two graduate students, a visiting postdoc, and myself — on self-knowledge and the norms of assertion, and two of the participants have spent most of the past year arguing past each other about Moore's paradox. One of them (call her the commitment theorist) locates the badness of the first-person present-tense sentence *It is raining but I do not believe it* in the speech act: to assert that p is constitutively to undertake, or to represent oneself as possessing, a commitment to believing that p, and the second conjunct withdraws in the same breath the very thing the first conjunct has just put in place — so the oddity is a collision of commitments within a single act, which is why it disappears under embedding, in the third person, and in the past tense, and why it is a defect of what is said rather than of what is thought. The other (call him the transparency theorist) locates it in the first-personal stance itself: belief is transparent, in that settling whether to believe that p just is settling whether p, so a subject who has made up his mind that it is raining cannot, without incoherence, go on to treat it as an open question whether he believes that it is raining; assertion norms merely inherit this structure and therefore cannot be where the explanation stops. Their one attempt at a joint document collapsed into formulations both could sign and neither cared about — the usual "each account captures something important, and the truth lies in a combination" — and I want something sharper.

Please draft the joint statement such an adversarial collaboration should have produced. I want, first, a numbered section of claims both of them can actually sign: that the oddity is not falsity or necessary falsity, since the state of affairs the sentence describes is possible and may even obtain; that it survives in soliloquy, in a diary entry, and in silent occurrent thought, so it is not merely Gricean; that it is cancelled by embedding in the antecedent of a conditional, by shifting to the third person or the past tense, and by hedging; that the phenomenon is not uniform across attitudes, since the intention and desire analogues are not absurd in the same way; and any further datum of this kind you think belongs on the list. Then a disagreements table: for each residual dispute, a formulation the commitment theorist would accept as her own, one the transparency theorist would accept as his own, and a neutral formulation — in particular on whether the sentence is bad when merely thought and not asserted, whether transparency is a datum to be explained or an explanation of the datum, and whether a constitutive norm on assertion explains anything or merely restates it in the vocabulary of speech acts.

Then the cruxes, and for each crux a concrete case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. My group invented three cases we could not settle, and I would like them used: (i) the two-channel forecaster, who has excellent first-order evidence that it is raining together with excellent higher-order evidence that she is unreliable about exactly this question, and who says the sentence anyway; (ii) the subpersonal output case, in which a committee or an engineered forecasting system issues a categorical statement about the weather and separately logs a calibrated uncertainty estimate, while having no first-personal access to its own states at all; (iii) the metacognitive variant, *It is raining but I do not believe I should believe it*, together with the related form *It is raining but I do not believe my evidence supports it*. For each, say whether the oddity survives, how strong it is, and what each theorist predicts before the case is run. Add a short closing section stating which parts of their dispute you take to be merely verbal or merely a difference over what would count as an explanation at all, and why; if you think one account is really a special case of the other, or that they operate at different levels, say exactly which claims sit at which level rather than merging them amicably. Keep both theorists equally competent and let them actually answer each other's formulations. Please write the whole document in American English.
