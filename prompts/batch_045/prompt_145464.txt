I've been turning over this "tranquilist" idea — that the only things that matter, in the end, are craving and suffering, and that everything we normally call good (love, achievement, beauty, knowledge, meaning) is at best a way of quieting the one or removing the other, never good in its own right — and I want to know what I actually think. So here are my gut verdicts, case by case, and I'd like the principle behind them, if there is one.

1. My friend finally gets the job she's wanted for ten years. Euphoric for about six weeks, then she's back to her baseline and joking about how fast it faded. Verdict: nothing good happened, as far as the euphoria goes. Zero. I feel a bit rotten saying it.

2. A guy I know has pain asymbolia after a stroke — he describes the burning in his leg accurately but says he doesn't mind it, has no urge for it to stop, it doesn't bother him at all. Verdict: still bad. It's suffering even though there's no craving anywhere near it.

3. My friend six months after her brother died: miserable most days, and she says she doesn't want it taken away, wanting it gone would feel like a second betrayal. Verdict: it's still suffering, it still counts, and if there were a safe pill I'd want her to take it even over her refusal. Which makes me sound like I don't care what she wants, and I'm not sure I don't care.

4. A long-term meditator I met: almost no cravings, rarely suffers, and he'll also tell you that nothing is really enjoyable any more, everything is sort of flat and grey. Verdict: best case. That's the target.

5. Same inner state as 4, but gotten via a drug someone slipped him — no practice, no insight, no change in what he believes. Verdict: I want to say just as good, its the same state, but it creeps me out in a way 4 doesn't. So I must secretly care about something besides the state, and I don't know what.

6. Flow: a climber four hours up a route, totally absorbed, driving hard, wanting the summit, not a drop of unpleasantness in it. Verdict: this is genuinely good, maybe the best thing in life. By the view it shouldn't be — it's packed with wanting.

7. Sunday afternoon boredom: no pain, nothing I can name that I want, just miserable restlessness. Verdict: real suffering, and the most underrated bad thing in a modern life. But it isn't obviously craving either.

8. Painless death in sleep at 80, no dependents. Verdict: nothing bad happened to him. But never having been born: I don't want to call that bad, and I also don't want to say that making a new contented person is a good thing to do. So absence of suffering is good-for-someone but not good-to-produce, and I can't say how that works.

9. Someone in 4's state who also never does anything — no projects, no people, sits in a room, fine. Verdict: better than most lives. And yet I feel "what a waste", which the view doesn't let me feel.

10. Cow in a field: her contentment matters. A fish: I hedge. A digital system engineered to have zero valence and no wants: no idea, and no idea why I draw the line where I do.

My best attempt: suffering — felt unpleasantness, whether or not anyone wants it to stop — is the only intrinsic bad; the absence of suffering and of craving is the only intrinsic good; craving counts only when it hurts. But that gives up 6 and I really don't want to, and I'm not sure it handles 5 or 8.

Is there a principle that gets these, or nearly? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does it say about two I haven't sorted out: is a 20-year tranquil life worse than an 80-year one (my gut says yes, and I don't see how on this view), and does anticipatory dread about something that never happens count as suffering in the relevant sense?
