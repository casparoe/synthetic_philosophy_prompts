I spent forty years as a field biologist, now retired, and for most of that career a cause was something you could perturb and then watch the system respond. I have recently begun reading in the philosophy of the social sciences, and Weber's Protestant ethic thesis — the claim that a religiously derived attitude toward work partly produced the distinctive economic trajectory of Protestant regions in early modern Europe — strikes me as unfalsifiable in the form it is usually stated. So I want to restate it as an identification problem. Suppose a digitization project has assembled, for a single central European principality, parish and consistory records, tax registers and probate inventories from both sides of a confessional boundary that a dynastic partition created in the mid-sixteenth century along a line fixed purely by inheritance, dividing districts otherwise indistinguishable in soil, dialect and access to markets. Suppose the assembled data show three things: a sharp jump at the line in literacy and in the share of estates held in productive capital, appearing roughly two generations after the partition and widening thereafter; the same jump tracking confession rather than district when districts that later changed confession are compared with themselves; and the jump being largest precisely where baptismal registers show the highest density of austere Old Testament given-names, which the project proposes as a proxy for the ascetic psychology Weber describes.

Give me your credence that in this principality the confessional assignment caused more than a quarter of the later gap, decomposed and committed, as exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with nothing before the list and nothing after it:

1. Your number for that proposition, and whether the gap between it and my own 0.6 is a genuine disagreement or only a difference in how we are reading the claim.
2. The decomposition itself: a probability for each sub-question your number rests on — that the discontinuity is not an artifact of how the records were assembled; that nothing else co-varying with the boundary produces it; that the causal route runs through the ascetic psychology rather than through something downstream of confession such as parish schooling and literacy; and that explaining an outcome by citing the values agents held is causal explanation at all rather than interpretive redescription — and the arithmetic that combines them into your number.
3. Which single term your number is most sensitive to, and why.
4. What further result, of a kind these records could actually deliver, would move that term most, and in which direction.
5. Where the disagreement most likely lives among me at 0.6, a historian colleague at 0.9 and an economist colleague at 0.15: which sub-question we disagree about, as opposed to merely weight differently.

Commit to a number, to one most-sensitive term and to one locus of disagreement; if you think the question is ill-posed, state the minimal reading of cause under which it is well-posed and give the number under that reading rather than declining to answer.
