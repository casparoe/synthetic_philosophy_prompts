I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether a certain shape of historical question is even well formed, and we stalled in a way that makes me think we were each gesturing at something I can't name. I'd like the carving rather than the label, so please don't stop at supplying a term.

The shape is "why X in A but not in B?", of which the famous instance is "why the industrial revolution in Britain and not in China?" — the question usually called the Needham question, at the centre of the Great Divergence literature. My friend's position was that the question is perfectly well posed but merely hard: we need better data about Qing China, and eventually the causal list will sort itself out. I kept saying that something structural is wrong with the question itself, and that no amount of data fixes it. Neither of us could say precisely what.

Here is the pattern I think I was reacting to, in two instances.

First, the answers to such questions take the form of a list of features A had and B lacked. But when it turns out that B had one of those features too — commercialised handicraft production in the Yangzi delta, say, or a large internal market, or widespread literacy in practical arithmetic — the feature gets redescribed so that B's version doesn't count: not really a market, not really science, not really the same kind of city. The list survives every counterexample by moving its own terms. My friend says that's just ordinary scholarly revision of concepts; I think the revision is systematically one-directional, which is a symptom rather than a coincidence.

Second, the choice of B seems to be fixed by the answer rather than by the question. We ask about Britain and China rather than Britain and, say, the Netherlands or Saxony — comparisons that are arguably better controls because they hold more background conditions fixed — because China is the case that dramatically lacks the outcome. That makes the contrast class a function of the very thing to be explained.

Underneath both, I suspect a conflation of two different questions: what caused the outcome in A, and what made the difference between A and B. These can come apart. A may have had a genuine cause that B also had, with B's failure due to a separate blocker; or the cause of the outcome in A may not be the thing that distinguishes A from B at all ("why A and not B?" then has an answer that is no part of the answer to "why A?").

My friend proposed a repair: distinguish comparisons whose contrast case is fixed in advance by matching on antecedently specified variables from comparisons whose contrast case is selected because it lacks the outcome, and treat only the first kind as respectable. I objected that even the "matched" case is chosen with hindsight about which variables matter, and that matching on the wrong variables can hide a real cause, so the distinction looks like one of degree and the repair looks cosmetic.

So my questions. Is there an established concept or literature for (i) the one-directional redescription of the comparison case's features, (ii) the selection of the contrast class by the outcome, and (iii) the difference-making / cause-of-the-outcome conflation — and are these three aspects of one established thing or three different things that happen to travel together? Where are their edges? In particular, give me a boundary case: some "why A and not B?" that is entirely in order — my candidate is "why did this bridge fail and not that one, when both were built to the same specification?" — and say exactly which of the three features it lacks and why, since that is the test of whether you have located the real problem. Finally, on that basis: is the Needham question well posed, ill posed, or ill posed as stated but rescuable under a reformulation you should specify?
