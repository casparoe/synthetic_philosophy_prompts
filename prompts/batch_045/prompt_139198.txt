I've basically talked myself into accuracy-first epistemology and I want someone to talk me partway back out — or to tell me honestly that it's more exposed than I think.

Where I've landed, in my own words: credences are the basic epistemic state; the only distinctively epistemic good is accuracy, i.e. closeness to the truth values; and the norms are supposed to fall out of that. Probabilism: if your credences break the probability axioms, then — for the usual strictly proper, additive inaccuracy measures, like the squared-error one where you add up, over all the ways the world could be, the squared gap between your credence and the truth value — there's some coherent credence function that's closer to the truth than yours at every single way the world could be. So incoherence is a guaranteed loss, whatever happens. Updating: conditionalizing on your new evidence maximizes expected accuracy from the standpoint of your earlier self, among the updating policies you could have picked back then. And all of it is meant to be non-pragmatic: nothing about what's at stake for you, or what you care about, feeds into whether a credence is rational; stakes only matter for what you go on to do with rational credences. On top of that I hold a meta-claim I'm much less sure about: the choice of scoring rule is supposed not to matter much, because every strictly proper score yields the same headline verdicts, so the framework isn't quietly smuggling its conclusions in by picking a friendly measure.

The bit that's my own hobbyhorse: accuracy is always scored relative to a partition — a set of mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive ways things could be. But whose partition? If I split one cell in two, the verdicts move: the same credence function can be dominated at one grain and undominated at another, and how hard incoherence gets punished depends on how many cells there are. I tried to stabilize this by renormalizing (divide total inaccuracy by the number of cells) and then demanding grain-invariance: whether a credence is rational shouldn't hinge on arbitrary decisions about how finely to describe the world. And now I genuinely can't tell whether I've shored the framework up or quietly broken it. Under renormalization the penalty for incoherence looks like it gets diluted as the partition gets finer, and I suspect demanding grain-invariance either forces one particular score, or forces a score that isn't strictly proper, or makes the dominance arguments stop working altogether. Is that a real defect, or have I confused myself — and if it's real, which part does it hit: probabilism, the update rule, or only the meta-claim about scoring rules?

What I want is a map of the exposure of the whole package. I already know the greatest hits, so don't just recite them at me — say whether each is actually live or merely logically possible: unbounded scores and infinite expected inaccuracy; self-locating credences, where the states you're supposed to be accurate about include where and when in the world you are; infinite partitions where nothing is undominated or no best option exists; contents that aren't cleanly truth-conditional (vague, moral, taste-ish); the relation between credence and outright belief; why dominance rather than something weaker when the option set is huge; the worry that caring about accuracy is itself justified only circularly; and the big one, whether you can get whatever norm you like by shopping around for a scoring rule that vindicates it. I'd also like to know what an accuracy-first person would themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to what their critics say — and whether there is any observation at all that could count against a putatively non-pragmatic vindication, or whether it's insulated by construction, and if so whether that's fatal or fine for a project that's only claiming to systematize.

Two more things about me: I'd be moved most by something impossibility-shaped, i.e. a result showing that no single inaccuracy measure can deliver probabilism, conditionalization, deference to chances, and grain-invariance together.
