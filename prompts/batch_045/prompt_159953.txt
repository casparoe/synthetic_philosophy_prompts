You are a professional philosopher — a specialist in epistemology and formal epistemology, tenured, published on closure, reliabilism, and the logic of knowledge — who also writes comedy under a pen name for a satirical outlet, and who has, for roughly fifteen years, been a regular reader of and occasional commenter on rationalist blogs and forums. You know the bootstrapping problem and the easy-knowledge dialectic the way a plumber knows pipes: the case where a subject reads a gauge, moves from its own readings to the conclusion that the gauge is reliable, and then to the conclusion about the quantity measured; the question whether that route delivers knowledge cheaply, too cheaply; and the two horns — accept that some knowledge really is that easy, or block the route by giving up closure under known entailment, or by demanding that reliability be established from an independent source. You have also read a great deal of terms-of-service text, warranty cards, and direct-response marketing copy, and you know the register of a rationalist-adjacent product launch.

Think carefully before you write. First brainstorm: list ten or twelve candidate gags, and keep only those where the joke *is* the epistemology — where the laugh comes from a real feature of the target being pushed one step, not from a generic joke about gadgets, startups, or self-help. Discard anything that requires the reader to have read a particular paper.

Then write the Terms & Conditions and Limited Warranty (plus Owner's Manual notes) for a consumer device sold to that community: a self-certifying measurement appliance that reports a quantity and, on the basis of its own reports, certifies that it is reliable, with the certification then flowing to everything it says via closure. Name it in the deadpan style of a rationalist-adjacent hardware startup. Roughly 800 words, formatted as numbered clauses, with a short marketing preamble, three testimonials, and a three-item FAQ.

The clauses should, between them, cover at least the following, each exaggerating something real:

- Calibration that requires no external standard, and a default setting for the class of cases across which reliability is assessed (let the customer pick the reference class; the manual should treat the choice as a matter of taste, and quietly let the default do the work).
- An upgrade tier that extends certification to any entailed conclusion at no extra charge, and a further tier in which two units certify each other (a family plan).
- A safety/sensitivity mode: the unit undertakes to be right in just those nearby cases in which it is right.
- A clause voiding the warranty if the customer ever checks the unit against anything independent, or already believed the reading before the unit produced it.
- A premium tier that certifies the unit is not the output of a systematically misleading source, where the fiction should visibly strain.
- Testimonials from an invented epistemologist, a forecasting hobbyist who scores his own calibration, and an AI-lab evaluation lead grading models with models.
- An FAQ in which a customer asks whether the unit can detect its own malfunction, whether it works for someone with no independent reason to think the world is as it appears, and whether the whole thing is circular — each answered straight-faced, in jargon that is in fact circular.

Constraints. The authors of the manual are true believers: nothing may wink, nothing may explain the joke, and no clause may name a living philosopher. Keep the irony legible — a reader who has never opened a philosophy journal should still be able to tell what is being mocked, and a reader who knows the dialectic should be able to point at a clause and say which horn the fictional company has taken, and should recognize that horn as one real theorists have actually taken; make it clear, by implication only, which horn that is. Lightly satirize the practice the device is sold to serve — calibrating on your own track record, bootstrapping a worldview from first principles, self-graded evaluations — without ever stating what is wrong with it.

Before finishing, run a check: does every clause map onto a genuine move in the bootstrapping dialectic; does the piece still make sense to a non-specialist; does the last clause land as a reductio rather than a punchline; and would a commenter on a rationalist forum be able to quote one clause approvingly and one disapprovingly? Fix anything that fails, then output the piece only, with no commentary.
