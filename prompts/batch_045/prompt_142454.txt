Hey — a friend of mine who took a decision theory paper last year explained the money pump argument to me over dinner, and I wrote it down afterwords as best I could. His version went roughly like this:

'Suppose you prefer coffee to tea, tea to juice, and juice to coffee. I hand you a coffee and offer to swap it for tea for a penny. You prefer tea, so you pay. Then I offer you juice for another penny. Then I offer your coffee back for a third penny. Each time you'd rather make the swap than not, so each time you pay — and now you're holding the coffee you started with and you're three cents down. I can run that loop as many times as I like, so intransitive preferences let someone turn you into a money pump, which is why rationality requires transitivity. And seeing it coming doesn't save you: at every step the trade on offer is the one your own preferences say to take, so knowing the plan changes nothing — you still make all three trades and you still lose. The same argument works for preferences that change over time. If tonight you'd rather plan to have one drink tomorrow and quit the day after, but tomorrow night you'd rather just quit starting now, then someone can charge tonight-you to put the drink into the plan and charge tomorrow-you to take it out again, and you pay both times and end up with the plan you started with. So the real lesson is that you should be resolute: pick a plan and stick to it even when you later feel like switching, because switching is what makes the pump work.'

He also tagged on a kind of footnote that I only half remember: something like, if the three swaps were offered by three different people who each only ever offer you one swap and never compare notes, then there's no money pump at all, since nobody is running the whole cycle and each of them only comes out a penny ahead.

I've been turning this over and two things bug me. First, the 'seeing it coming doesn't help' bit: if I work backwards from the last swap, it looks like you should just refuse the very first one and sit still with your coffee, and then you lose nothing — but when I said that he said that's just being resolute, and that's a seperate fix. Second, the preferences-over-time thing feels like a different argument that happens to share a name, but I can't say where the difference is, and I'm not sure 'be resolute' follows from either one.

Can you go through what he said and tell me which bits a decision theorist would accept, which are half-right, and which are just wrong? In particular: is the intransitivity pump and the changing-preferences pump really the same argument, or is he sliding between two different things; does anticipating the trades rescue you or not, and if it does, does that mean the pump only works on a certain kind of chooser; is the footnote right that you need one person running the whole cycle for there to be a pump here; and does any of this actually establish that you ought to be resolute, or is that just one contested response among others? If the money pump turns out to be more of an illustration than a proof, say what you think it does show and what it leaves open.
