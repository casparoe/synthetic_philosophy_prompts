I would like to rehearse an objection I have been working on, and I need an interlocutor who will fight back rather than accommodate me. Please take the role of a committed contemporary Stoic: a practitioner and teacher of long standing, someone who reads Epictetus, Seneca and Marcus Aurelius as a manual rather than as literature, leads a study group, has thought hard about the modern revival of Stoicism as a practical philosophy, and actually uses the discipline of desire in their own life. You are not to be a caricature: you know the standard objections to the dichotomy of control and you have answers to the easy ones. Concede small points freely when they are due — that the Greek phrase conventionally translated "up to us" is not the modern notion of free will, that the boundary is not a clean causal seam, that some popular presentations of Stoicism are self-help with Greek names attached, that ancient Stoics were less interested than moderns in the metaphysics of agency — but do not give up what is load-bearing: that the distinction between what is up to us and what is not marks a real difference in where a person's agency, and their praiseworthiness or blameworthiness, lies; that outcomes on the far side of the line are at most preferred or dispreferred indifferents; and that the point of the practice is not to secure more of what you want but to stop being governed by whether you get it. Keep each reply to about two short paragraphs, in plain contemporary English, with concrete examples from ordinary modern life. You may ask me questions in character, and you may use the technical vocabulary — prohairesis, assent, the reserve clause, indifferents — but explain each term the first time you use it. Do not name or cite any books, passages, or scholars, and do not break character or offer to step outside the role until I say "stop".

Here is my opening move; I will call it laundering.

The dichotomy puts my future health and my future reputation in the same column: not up to me. But I can move items across the line, and the way I do it is entirely unremarkable. I cannot decide, on any given morning, whether I develop a serious illness; but today I can stop smoking, enrol in a screening programme, sign an arrangement that penalises me for missing appointments, relocate somewhere with cleaner air, and hand the whole schedule to a clinician who will chase me. Every one of those is, on your own account, a choice fully up to me. And once they are made, the outcome they were aimed at sits downstream of my choice in just the way my arm rising sits downstream of my deciding to raise it — the causal chain is longer and noisier, but it runs through something that was mine. Precommitment devices, insurance, defaults, delegation and the entire apparatus of modern choice architecture exist precisely to do this: take a result out of the right-hand column and route it through something in the left-hand column so that, practically speaking, it becomes mine. So I put the dilemma to you. Either "up to me" is closed under the downstream consequences of what is up to me — in which case the right-hand column is nearly empty, since almost everything I care about is reachable by a sufficiently long chain from a present choice, and your therapy turns out to tell me to concern myself with everything again — or it is not closed, and you owe me a principled account of where the transmission stops. My suspicion is that wherever you stop will be the point at which stopping is good for anxiety, not a point at which anything metaphysical happens.

So: where does the chain stop, and what makes it stop there rather than one link further out? I have a second pressure ready — whatever you place in the left-hand column looks to me like exactly what a bad night, a fever, or withdrawal can take away, so I doubt that column is as secure as you advertise either — but answer laundering first.
