Hi — I write on science and ideas for a UK paper, and I'm doing a piece on a handful of small teams building what they call 'ethics engines': software where you describe a decision, set sliders for how confident you are in various moral theories, and it gives you a recommendation. I'm not a philosopher and I don't want the piece to be either credulous or a lazy sneer, so I'm trying to get the underlying literature straight first.

I picked up a popular philosophy book over Christmas — one of those brisk ones written by a science writer, I won't name it — with a chapter on moral uncertainty. My notes of it read roughly like this:

'We are never fully certain what we ought to do, and part of that uncertainty isn't about the facts at all: it's about which moral theory is the true one. Maybe consequentialism is right, maybe some form of deontology, maybe virtue theory, maybe something nobody has thought of yet. Philosophers call this moral uncertainty, and the standard way of handling it is ordinary expected-value reasoning — the same maths you'd apply to any gamble. Multiply how good each option looks by how likely it is that the theory saying so is the true one, add them up, and do whatever comes out on top. The surprise is that this usually hands you back the answer you already had: do what your favourite theory says. The reason is that the theories aren't measurable against one another — one counts units of welfare, one counts duties kept, one talks about character — and no one has worked out how to put those on a single scale. This is the normalisation problem, and after decades of argument there's still no agreed solution, which effectively shelves the whole expected-value approach. The practical upshot is that you should just act on whichever theory you find most plausible. Hedging between theories is worse than useless: split the difference and you satisfy neither theory, and someone can construct a series of choices in which you pay to switch back and forth and end up worse off by your own lights, which shows the hedging method is incoherent.'

That's my paraphrase of a paraphrase, so allow for some drift, but it's close.

Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? I'd rather you didn't park everything in 'it's complicated'. My instinct is that the chapter is doing something sneaky in the middle — it makes moral uncertainty sound like straightforward expected utility under risk, and then says the numbers can't be put on a common scale, and I can't tell whether those two complaints are the same complaint or whether the author has merged two different problems. I also don't trust the bit about hedging being refuted by that betting example; it definately sounds like one objection doing the work of a knockdown argument.

One more thing, and this is the bit I actually need for the piece. Two of the teams I'm profiling have made design choices that seem to me to be direct responses to the chapter's complaints, and I want to know if they work:

1. To get round the normalisation problem, their software doesn't try to find a shared moral scale. Instead each theory on the menu scores every available option, then the scores are rescaled within that option set — the option a theory likes best gets 100, the one it likes least gets 0, everything else is spread in between — and only then does the tool take the credibility-weighted average across theories.

2. There's a confidence floor and ceiling: you can't set any theory below 2% or above 90%. Their argument is that you should never be certain in ethics, and that you can't rule out a theory just because you haven't heard of it.

Does either of those survive once the chapter's mistakes are corrected, or do they just relocate the problem — and if so, where exactly does it reappear? Does the rescaling trick make the recommendation hostage to which options happen to be on the menu? Does the 2% floor do any real work, given that a small credence in a theory that cares about enormous stakes might swamp everything else? And is there any reading on which the number the tool prints out is genuinely the thing you ought to do, rather than a number it can defend?

Finally, since I'm quoting people: please be explicit about which of your claims are settled among people who work on this and which are one side of a live disagreement, and flag anywhere you're giving me your own view rather than the field's. I'd rather print a genuine fight than invent a consensus that isn't there.
