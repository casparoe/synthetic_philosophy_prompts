I'm an undergraduate philosophy major in a seminar on philosophical method, and we have just finished a unit on thought experiments: what they can establish, how they persuade, and when the persuasion is illegitimate. Our seminar uses the phrase intuition pump for the cases that move us by something other than the argument they are supposed to contain. I have tried to sort the cases we read into the ones I think we are entitled to lean on and the ones I think are rigged, and my verdicts do not line up with any rule I can state. I would like help finding the rule, or being told which of my verdicts to abandon.

My verdicts, on the cases as I have them:

1. The Gettier-style case, in which someone has a justified true belief that is true only by luck, either from a stopped clock or from a landscape of convincing barn facades. Legitimate. The conditions were specified independently of the case, the case satisfies them, and the mismatch tells us something about the analysis being tested.

2. The runaway trolley that can be diverted onto a sidetrack where it will kill one instead of five. Legitimate. I trust the intuition as data, and I do not think the trolley imagery is contributing anything the principle under test fails to mention.

3. The surgeon who cuts up a healthy visitor in order to distribute his organs to five dying patients. Not legitimate, or at least very heavily over-credited. I think the verdict people give is driven by the bodily violation framing rather than by the numbers.

4. The case in which you wake up wired to a famous violinist who will die if you unplug. Also not legitimate for me, and for what I take to be the same reason. But when I rewrote the case for a friend in the driest contractual language I could manage, with no kidnapping, no tubes and no hospital gown, my friend's verdict flipped to the one I actually hold on reflection, and I became confident again. So I trust the dry version and distrust the famous version, which are the same case with the imagery either present or removed.

5. Mary's room, in which a scientist who has never seen color learns every physical fact about color vision and then sees red for the first time. Legitimate. I do not accept the conclusion most people draw from it, but I think the case is fair and that the intuition it produces is real and needs answering.

6. The zombie case, in which a world physically identical to ours contains no consciousness. Not legitimate. I do not think my sense that such a world is conceivable is evidence about anything, and I do not think the case can be made to support the weight put on it.

7. Twin Earth, in which the clear drinkable stuff in the lakes of a distant planet has a different chemical composition from water. Legitimate. I think the intuition here is anchored by real cases of the sort that actually settle how a term attaches to a kind.

8. Swampman, the molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person produced by chance in a swamp. Not legitimate. I have no stable intuition about it at all, and it seems to me impossible in a way that matters, not merely impossible in a way that can be ignored.

9. The experience machine, in which you can be plugged permanently into a simulation of a wonderful life. Not legitimate. I think the reluctance to plug in is status quo bias plus a framing that makes the alternative sound narcotic, and I do not think the case can be filled in far enough to remove that.

10. A case I invented: over ten years, one detail of your autobiography is rewritten each night while you sleep, until your memories match those of another person. Are you the same person at the end? I distrust every answer here, including my own, and I think the reason is that the case is badly under-described rather than that the question is deep.

11. Another case I invented: the same structure as the trolley case, except that the five are on a stretch of track that curves out of view and you learn of them from an instrument readout rather than seeing them. My verdict weakens noticeably but does not vanish. I cannot tell whether this shows the original verdict was contaminated by the visual framing or merely that my confidence was always partly visual.

The tentative principle I have been working with has three clauses. A thought experiment is evidentially usable only if, first, it survives de-pumping: strip the imagery, the proper names and the narrative, state the case in the thinnest possible prose, and the verdict should be unchanged. Second, it satisfies an anchor requirement: the intuition it elicits must have a nearby real case that we have independent reason to trust. Third, it satisfies an isolation requirement: the case must vary only what the theory under test says makes no difference.

These clauses do not agree with each other on my own list. De-pumping is what saves my verdict on case 3, but Mary's room appears to have no content at all once the qualitative image is stripped away, and yet I counted it as legitimate; maybe the anchor clause, rather than de-pumping, is what separates case 5 from case 6, since sight restoration and sensory substitution are real while zombie worlds are not. And I do not know what any of the three clauses says about cases 10 and 11.

What I would like, in American English, is the following: a principle, or at most two rival principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you; for each clause, the case of mine that motivates it and the case that causes it trouble; an honest account of which of my eleven verdicts the principle cannot recover and which of them you think I should overturn; and verdicts on three cases I have not listed. The first is a de-pumped version of the zombie case, if one can be written. The second is the common practice of reaching a hard verdict by walking someone through a long sequence of cases that each differ from the last only slightly, so that the end of the sequence looks like the beginning. The third is any thought experiment of your choosing whose central figure is very unlike a human being, such as a hive mind or a system trained on text. Please also tell me what would count as a genuine check on the principle rather than another intuition about it. I do not need the literature surveyed or sources cited; I need the principle and what it costs me.
