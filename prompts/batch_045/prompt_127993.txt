I write about law and technology, and I'm about to commit to print a long piece on algorithmic governance — specifically, on what becomes of the idea of law when the norms that actually decide people's entitlements are produced by systems that no one, including the officials who operate them, can state in advance. Before I write it I want my own working position audited, because I suspect there is a contradiction somewhere in it that I can't locate. Assume I have read the 1958 Harvard Law Review exchange between H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller and don't need it summarised; assume also that I can't check references, so don't send me to any.

Here, as carefully as I can state it, is where I've landed. (1) Legal validity is a matter of pedigree and social fact, not of moral merit: a grotesquely unjust enactment can be entirely valid law, and there is no necessary connection between being law and being morally decent. (2) The eight desiderata Fuller set out in The Morality of Law — that rules be general, public, prospective, intelligible, consistent, possible to comply with, stable over time, and congruent with what officials actually do — are not merely tips of legislative craft; a regime that flouts them wholesale has failed to make law at all rather than made bad law. (3) In the informer case at the centre of that exchange, the right response is an openly retroactive statute together with a public admission that we are choosing between two evils; the suggestion that the old enactment was never law is a convenient fiction, and frankness about the moral cost is worth more than doctrinal tidiness. (4) What separates a legal order from a gunman is that at least the officials take the internal point of view: they accept the rules as standards for conduct and for criticism. (5) In hard cases there is frequently no determinate legal fact waiting to be found; the judge then makes new law, and should say so rather than pretend to discover it. (6) A norm whose content no subject could in principle extract before acting, and whose application no official can state or check, is not law even when it is published, accepted by officials and generally obeyed — call this my traceability condition, which I take to imply that a published-but-unintelligible rule is on a par with a secret one. My worked example, which I've been calling the Verran scheme: a city enacts, by impeccable procedure, an ordinance providing that scarce public housing be allocated so as to promote the best use of the stock; the housing authority implements it through a model that is retrained monthly, gives no reasons, and is not reviewable, so that no applicant can learn what would have counted as a better claim and no official can say why one file succeeded and another failed. My verdict is that the authority's determinations have no legal force, while the ordinance itself is perfectly valid law.

What I want is an audit, not a tutorial. Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, ordered by how badly they pull against each other — and for each, what exactly the conflict consists in, what would have to be given up or narrowed to fix it, and what that revision would cost the argument I'm trying to make. Which apparent tensions dissolve under inspection, and why they only look bad. Whether any of the six is too vague to bear the weight I'm putting on it, or is doing no real work. And finally: if I had to make one change, which single revision would clean up the most, and what would it lose me. I don't want to be told the whole package is a mess; I want to know which commitment has to go. For each of your judgments, say roughly how confident you are, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
