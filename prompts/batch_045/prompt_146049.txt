I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of social reform, and I use an adversarial-collaboration document as its capstone assignment. I would like you to draft the one that two positions I want my students to take seriously could both sign after working through their dispute about Chesterton's fence — the parable of the gate across the road whose use the reformer cannot see, and which he is therefore told he may not remove.

Party A, the custodian: the Burkean who holds that an inherited arrangement is not to be dismantled by someone unable to say what it is for, that the invisibility of a practice's point is a fact about the observer rather than about the practice, and that the burden of proof therefore falls asymmetrically on whoever would remove.

Party B, the revisionist: someone who holds that inheritance confers no epistemic privilege, that the custodian's asymmetry is status-quo bias wearing the costume of humility, that the costs of retention tend to be visible and recurring while the costs of removal are systematically under-detected, and hence that the fence principle either proves far too much — it would have protected every entrenched abuse whose function the beneficiaries could name — or collapses into the platitude that one should be careful.

What I want is the document such a collaboration would produce, not an adjudication of who is right and not a dramatised dialogue.

First, a numbered section of claims both could sign. I do not want pleasantries that survive only because they are empty; I want the substantive common ground. Two candidates I would like you to test rather than assume: the attribution asymmetry, i.e. the claim that harms caused by removing an arrangement are harder to observe and harder to trace to their cause than harms caused by keeping it, because the former are counterfactual losses that arrive diffusely and late; and the selection claim, i.e. that an arrangement's having survived is some evidence that it was doing something, however weak. If either is not genuinely shared, say so and explain what the real disagreement beneath it is.

Second, a disagreements table: one row per crux, with the crux stated neutrally, then Party A's own preferred formulation of its position on it and Party B's own, each in wording that side would actually accept rather than wording chosen to look weak; plus a column marking whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal, with your reasons where you say verbal.

Third, for each crux, the case, argument, or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. Please use the four cases below, which I designed for the seminar; tell me plainly if any of them stacks the deck, but work with them as given otherwise.

Case I: a professional licensing body cannot change its examination syllabus until the change has been read aloud at two consecutive quarterly meetings, a rule whose origin nobody at the body can recover. In a period of rapid technical change this delays updates by up to six months, so that newly licensed practitioners are examined on material everyone agrees is obsolete. There is thin testimonial evidence that the delay has repeatedly functioned as a cooling-off period, preventing the syllabus from being captured by whichever commercial vendor lobbied hardest that quarter.

Case II, the unbuilt fence: the same body has never maintained a conflicts-of-interest register. Does the principle extend to omissions? If not, why does presence carry a presumption that absence does not, given that institutions also persist through inertia and through the vigilance of concentrated interests?

Case III: a rule requiring two sponsors for any nomination, traceable in the documentary record to a nineteenth-century campaign to keep a particular group out of the profession. Its origin is known and discredited; its present effects have never been studied. Does a discredited origin shift the burden, or does knowledge of provenance tell us nothing about current function?

Case IV, full reversibility: the revisionist's own compromise — repeal for a fixed term, staged by jurisdiction, with failure metrics fixed in advance and automatic reinstatement on a pre-registered trigger. If the custodian still objects here, the objection can no longer be about irreversibility and must be about the unmeasurability of the relevant function; if the revisionist still objects to even this, then the quarrel was never about evidence at all. I would like the document to say which of those it takes to be happening.

Among the cruxes I expect to matter, please make sure these are addressed: how strong a filter survival actually is; what has to be true of someone for them to count as understanding an arrangement, as against merely possessing a record of successful intervention or a mechanism story; whether the principle is epistemic at all, or instead a demand about the agent's character and posture rather than about their evidence; whether it is addressed to individual agents or is really a thesis about institutional decision procedures; and whether it applies to orders that emerged and that nobody built, where there is no designer's purpose to have been invisible to the reformer.

Finally, a short closing section, to be included only if it earns its place: whether anything analogous holds for belief, so that one should not abandon a belief whose point one cannot state. If the analogy is misleading, say in one sentence why, and leave it out.

On epistemic demeanour: mark which formulations you expect each side to balk at; flag which of the agreed claims are substantive and which hold only because of a hedge; and record your own confidence where you are guessing at what a position is committed to. If my framing rests on a confusion — if the two positions do not after all disagree, if one of my cruxes is a pseudo-crux, if the assignment cannot be done well in the form I have asked for — say so directly instead of producing a polite document that conceals it. One constraint: nothing in this document may depend on going to a library or the internet. No citations, bibliography, or reading list, and no appeal to what the empirical literature shows; where a claim would need evidence that neither you nor I have, mark it as an open empirical question and let the table record what each side predicts.

I intend to hand the result to my students with one half assigned to each side, so prose rather than fragments, tables only where a table genuinely serves, and nothing that would make either party refuse to sign on the grounds of being misdescribed.
