I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I've got a feature due Thursday on whether leaning on AI systems — the chat ones, the code ones, the "summarise these twelve interviews" ones — is quietly eating our ability to understand the stuff we write about. I dont want links or studies; I've got plenty of anecdotes and honestly I need the argument, not the literature. Here's where I'm stuck. I tried to steelman the blasé position, the this-is-just-the-printing-press-again position, and the argument I ended up with proves way more than I'm comfortable with, and I can't find the bad step:

(1) If you come to hold a true belief by relying on a source that's reliable about that subject matter, your epistemic position with respect to that belief is improved, or at least not worsened. (Feels like a truism: good sources leave you better off.)
(2) Using an AI system for a task is, at bottom, the same kind of thing as using a calculator, a colleague, a textbook or a search engine: acquiring beliefs from a source.
(3) So using an AI system cannot in itself worsen your epistemic position.
(4) Erosion of understanding would have to be a worsening of your epistemic position — what else could it be?
(5) So AI dependence doesn't erode understanding. At most it sometimes hands people false beliefs, which is a complaint that applies to every source there has ever been.
(6) Run (1)–(5) with writing, print, the calculator, GPS, spellcheck, or the senior partner who checks your work in place of AI system and you get the same result — and in every one of those cases the panic turned out to be overblown.
(7) So there is nothing special to say about AI, and this whole genre of worry is the oldest complaint in the history of technology with a new noun plugged into it.

I can't accept (7), but not for the reason my editor wants. My worry is narrower and, I think, nastier, and I've been calling it calibration collapse in my notes: in a domain I actually know something about I can grade what the thing gives me; in a domain I've only ever worked in *with* the thing, I can't, and the better it gets the less able I am to tell a confident-sounding wrong answer from a right one. My ability to catch its mistakes seems to decay faster than its mistake rate falls, so the share of errors I notice heads toward zero even as the system improves. I've also started separating production capacity (could I produce this myself if I had to?) from monitoring capacity (could I tell whether this is right?), and monitoring looks like it goes first. A friend who manages engineers says the same about juniors who can ship but can't review.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) than (7) — (1) still feels like a truism — but I want to know the bill, and I'd rather pay in one place than another if I get to choose. Two things I keep tripping over, which you should feel free to use or demolish: first, most of the time when I lean on the thing I don't seem to acquire a belief at all, I just paste a paragraph into a draft, so maybe (2) is where it breaks because the AI case is delegation rather than testimony, and the damage is done by disuse of a capacity instead of by any degradation of something I believe. Second, if the fix is that the difference is you can't check it, what happens to my reliance on the human experts I interview, who are just as opaque to me and whom I can't really audit either — does the argument then show that all expertise-dependence is corrosive, which is a conclusion I also don't want? And there's a third version rattling around about apprentices, people who never had the capacity in the first place: maybe the interesting claim isn't about eroding understanding but about never acquiring it, which would mean (4) is looking at the wrong quantity entirely. Anway. Any help before Thursday appreciated.
