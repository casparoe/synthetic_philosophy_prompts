I run a small working group that grew out of an old rationalist reading group — the kind of circle where scope insensitivity, Pascalian fanaticism, and Kaldor–Hicks compensation are shorthand rather than jargon. For about a year two of our members have been arguing past each other about how much weight to give the welfare of people who will live more than a century from now when we set climate policy today. They have agreed, at my urging, to stop re-litigating the whole question and to do this as an adversarial collaboration instead. I want the document such a collaboration would produce, and I want it to be good enough that both of them would actually sign it.

Call the two positions the Temporal Parity camp and the Opportunity-Cost camp. Roughly: the first holds that the pure rate of time preference should be at or near zero — that the Ramsey-style decomposition of the discount rate into a pure time-preference term, a growth term, and a curvature term is fine, but that the first term is indefensible when what is at stake is the suffering of real persons, and that the only legitimate discounting is for the probability that the future never arrives at all (extinction, civilizational collapse, value drift so thorough that bequests misfire) and for the diminishing marginal value of consumption if the future turns out much richer. The second holds that a dollar spent on mitigation is a dollar not invested, that invested capital compounds in real terms, that if the future is likely to be wealthier than the present then transferring extra consumption goods to it is a poor use of resources taken from identifiable, currently living, often quite poor people, and that a polity accountable to present voters has a weaker mandate to spend on statistical descendants than on constituents. Both sides know that the well-known integrated-assessment standoff — one side plugging in a near-zero pure rate of time preference, the other something calibrated to observed market returns — generates almost all of the disagreement in recommended policy from that single parameter, and both now consider that framing exhausted. Please don't reopen it as a battle of parameter choices.

What I want instead is the joint statement:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — the things both can sign. I expect these to be substantial rather than diplomatic: I don't want anything of the form both sides agree the future matters. I mean claims about what the leverage of the discount-rate choice actually is, about whether an observed market rate of return is even a candidate for a social discount rate, about the asymmetries between costs borne by identifiable present people and benefits accruing to unidentifiable future ones, about the non-identity problem and whether it makes person-affecting framings unstable for policies that change which people are ever born, and about which uncertainty dominates the analysis — the uncertainty in the damage function or the uncertainty in the discount rate. Add any agreement you think they should have reached and didn't.

2. A disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, the claim in its most neutral form, then the version of it the Temporal Parity side would endorse as its own, then the version the Opportunity-Cost side would endorse as its own. Neither version should be a strawman and neither should be vacuous.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — with, for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that both would accept as genuinely bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. The ones I think matter, in rough order, are these; if you think the real crux is somewhere else, say so and justify replacing one.

   a. Compensation in kind. Both sides already accept that bequeathing a larger capital stock can in principle compensate a later generation for imposed costs. The question is how large the non-substitutable share of the loss is: losses like an intact ice sheet, low-elevation river deltas, Holocene-stable coastlines, the preservation option of being the generation that did not have to manage a managed retreat, and the background institutional confidence that coastal property will still be there. What test would both accept for sizing that share, and what does each predict it will show?

   b. The compounding-bequeath argument's hidden premise. The Opportunity-Cost case seems to require not merely that capital earns a real return but that the returns are actually captured and earmarked for the affected future — a sovereign bequest instrument that in fact no one has built. Consider explicitly a perpetual, lawfully ring-fenced fund whose assets may only be spent on future adaptation and damages. Does the Opportunity-Cost argument become sound if such a vehicle exists and unsound if it doesn't? Say which parts of each side's practical conclusion depend on that institutional fact, and what each predicts about whether such funds could be made credible.

   c. Sequential versus collective consent. Call it the diachronic tragedy of the commons: a chain of trades in which each generation imposes a small net cost on the next, each individual trade being one its parties would accept, and the union of them being catastrophic. Both camps should say whether the right test is pairwise consent along the chain or consent to the whole sequence, why, and what each predicts about which test a well-designed institution would actually apply.

   d. Cohorts versus persons. Given that mitigation changes who is born, both sides should state what they are aggregating over — the welfare of whoever ends up existing, or the interests of individuals compared to their own counterfactual existence — and each should say what would move them off their answer.

   e. Institutional standing. Suppose we introduced a chamber with, say, a couple of hundred seats allocated to cohorts not yet born, filled by sortition from the living under fiduciary duties to named future centuries, holding a suspensive veto over projects whose payoffs lie more than a hundred years out. Is that legitimate, is it competent, and would each side accept its rulings as settling crux (c)? Both must answer, and each must say what they would predict about its actual voting record.

   f. Fanaticism and tails. The Temporal Parity side leans on expected value over a very long future; the Opportunity-Cost side objects that this is fanatical and that the tail of the damage function is not knowable well enough to carry the weight. Both should pre-register the probability threshold at which they would change their recommendation, and name something they would count as evidence about the tail.

4. Finally, a short section saying which parts of this dispute you take to be merely verbal, and why — for instance whether social cost of carbon is being used as a shadow price or as a policy target, whether social discount rate is being used descriptively or prescriptively, and whether some of the apparent ethical disagreement is really disagreement about expected economic growth.

Two constraints. The wording has to be one both camps would accept for their own view — that is the whole point of the exercise, and it is where I expect the difficulty to be. And please argue from first principles and from the cases: we have no library access this term and we are explicitly not asking for a literature review, so no citations, references, or reading suggestions of any kind.
