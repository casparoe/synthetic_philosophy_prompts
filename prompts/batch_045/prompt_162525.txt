hiya — quick brief. I'm helping a small foundation that funds bridge-building work put together one of those adversarial-collaboration documents instead of yet another lit review, and I need you to draft it. Take on the role of a political epistemologist who's done time in both of these camps: start by brainstorming bullet points on where the two sides *actually* part company (as opposed to where they just annoy each other), then draft carefully — the whole value of the document is that neither side can complain its view got strawmanned.

The two camps:

**Camp one, call them the bad-faith realists.** Their claim: a non-trivial slice of out-partisan political argument really is in bad faith — strategic, aimed at the news cycle and at intra-group status rather than at tracking the truth — and the polite norm of steelmanning the other side often amounts to tidying up someone's strongest propaganda and handing it back in better prose. They think suspicions of bad faith are frequently *earned*, and that treating every opponent as a sincere truth-seeker is itself an epistemic error with a predictable cost.

**Camp two, call them the misperception theorists.** Their claim: the felt sense that the other side argues in bad faith is mostly manufactured by the medium — you meet your own side's arguments as lived reasoning, and the other side's as decontextualised screenshots selected for outrage by platform incentives — so perceived bad faith tracks your exposure diet more than it tracks their actual reasoning dispositions. They think assuming good faith is the right default even though it will sometimes be wrong.

What I want in the document:

1. **Agreed claims.** A numbered section, maybe 8–10 items, of things both camps could sign without gritting their teeth: about what echo chambers do to the flow of information, about how platform incentives select which exemplars of a group you ever encounter, about the difference between holding a view for reasons and holding it because your crowd holds it, about how hard that difference is to see from outside. Kill anything vacuous — if a claim would apply equally to a disagreement between two birdwatching clubs, cut it.

2. **A disagreements table.** Where exactly they differ, with each disagreement stated twice: once in wording the realist would accept for their own view, once in wording the misperception theorist would accept for theirs. No sneaky winning phrasings.

3. **Cruxes.** For each: the sub-question, why settling it would move at least one side, a test or case both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. Use these four, and add a fifth of your own if you find one worth having:

- *The medium crux (chaperone protocol).* Suppose every political exchange were mediated by an independent, randomly assigned non-partisan chaperone whose only job is to flag, in real time, sentences doing signalling work rather than claim-making work, and to ask the speaker to redo that sentence as a claim. Predictions: does flagging cluster asymmetrically across camps, or symmetrically with perceived asymmetry collapsing?
- *The detection crux (swap forum).* Participants from each camp write and defend the strongest version of the *other* camp's position before an audience drawn from that camp, scored by whether audience members can identify which defenders were sincere. Whose prediction about out-group detection accuracy is stake here?
- *The error-cost crux.* False-positive bad-faith attributions are cheap for the attributor and expensive for the discourse; false negatives leave you exploitable. Both sides need to say how they're weighing that asymmetry, and crucially whether the error costs are themselves symmetric across the two camps.
- *The exit-cost crux.* How much of an echo chamber's cohesion comes from members having no affordable epistemic alternative outside it (testimonial exclusion, professional risk) versus from a genuine preference for insularity? Is membership chosen or imposed, and does that change what good faith even means inside the chamber?

These protocols are invented by me as the tests — don't go hunting for real studies that ran them, and don't cite anything at all: no paper titles, no author names, no bibliographies. If a claim needs empirical backing, describe the study design in a sentence instead. The foundation wants claims, not footnotes.

4. **Verbal disputes.** Tell me which parts of their quarrel you think are about words rather than the world, and why — and if you think a crux dissolves under analysis rather than being settled, say so plainly.

At the end, add a short drafter's note: how confident you are that a real person in each camp would actually sign the agreed-claims section as worded, which single line in the whole document you'd bet least on, and what you'd want to change if you had the two of them in a room. Use British English spelling, and keep it around 1100 words — no padding. Its fine if the note is blunt about where you're guessing.
