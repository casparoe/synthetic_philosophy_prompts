I've got a 90 minute slot in our lab's reading group next week and I somehow volunteered to do the session on the problem of logical omniscience. The room is mostly ML PhD students plus two formal-methods/verification people: they've all seen Kripke structures and modal notation at some point, they throw around the words "knows" and "believes" constantly in papers, nobody's a philosopher, and when I floated the topic the reaction was basically "sure, real agents are computationally bounded, add a bound, next slide." So I need to plan how to get them to feel the problem, not a explanation of it — plan the session for me, and please don't hand me a reading list, they won't read it.

The thing I actually want them to walk out with is that it isn't the banality that agents can't finish long proofs. It's that the standard semantics they already use — an agent knows phi iff phi is true in every world compatible with what the agent can distinguish — builds in, all at once, that the agent knows every valid sentence, knows every consequence of what it knows, and can't treat two logically equivalent sentences as different; and that those three failures are wired together in a way that makes one-at-a-time patching not work.

My carrying example was going to be an agent that's been handed a ciphertext and the public key for a deterministic encryption scheme: everything it knows pins down the plaintext, so on that semantics it already knows the plaintext. I like it because it doesn't depend on infinitely long proofs or on the agent being dumb. Is that the right example to hang the hour on, or does it smuggle in something thatll confuse them — and what does it need to have, and not have, to carry the rest?

I was also going to split what I've been calling the "downward" half (you automatically know the consequences of what you know) from the "sideways" half (you can't distinguish equivalent formulations), in that order, on the theory that the sideways one is the one that bites for their own work — two descriptions of the same protocol, two ways of writing the same reward function — while the downward one just looks like theorem-proving and they'll file it under "bounded rationality, known." Is that cut doing real work or am I inventing a distinction that collapses as soon as you press on it?

Misconceptions I'm expecting and want placed at the right point in the sequence: "it's just a complexity budget, add a step limit"; "move to credences and probabilities and you're out of it"; "fine, impossible worlds or an awareness set, done"; and the one I'm most worried about, that they'll collapse it into the skeptic's closure principle and think that denying closure solves it, when the point is that the semantics hands you the closure for free so denying it philosophically doesn't touch the model. And I want one question at the end that'd actually tell me whether they got what the problem is rather than whether they memorized three fixes — plus what I should flat out refuse to get into in 90 minutes with this crowd.
