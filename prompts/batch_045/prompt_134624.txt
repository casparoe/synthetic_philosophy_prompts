hey — I sit on a small heritage commitee that every so often has to rule on whether a restored object still counts as "the original": a timber ship whose planking has been replaced one strake at a time across forty years, a violin that's had its neck reset, its bass bar swapped and its top regraduated, a building gutted by fire and rebuilt inside its original facade. The philosophy literature on the Ship of Theseus keeps handing me principles that sound fine and then break on exactly the cases my committee ends up shouting about, so before I read any more of it I want my own spec written down. Could you write me the desiderata: what a theory of artifact identity-through-replacement has to deliver to be good enough to decide cases like these?

For each requirement I want the case behind it, and please invent cases rather than only reworking the one with the planks. Mark each one as either genuinely required or as a prejudice of one camp. At minimum I want something on:

- the two-ship version where repair-in-place and reassembly-from-the-old-parts are finished at the same moment, so neither has temporal priority or more original matter. What does a theory owe us here — a principled answer, an argument that there is no answer, or an argument that the question was malformed? And whatever it owes, does it owe it without looking at anything that happens later?
- retroactivity: the two candidates come apart, nobody rules, and twenty years later one of them burns. Does the survivor thereby become the original, was it always the original, or must identity be settled without appeal to future events? I want this as its own requirement, because "no dependence on the future" plus "don't be arbitrary in symmetric cases" feel like they're already pulling against each other.
- gradualness: the same total replacement done a plank a year versus swapped out overnight in a yard. If the theory gives different verdicts, say what's doing the work. And it has to say something about there being no sharp number of planks at which things tip over — is that semantic vagueness, metaphysical indeterminacy in the object, or is the theory allowed to just refuse sharp cutoffs?
- the no-overlap rebuild: a fresh ship built to the same design from new timber, no shared parts and no causal-historical link, moored beside the original. That one has to come out as not the original, and I want the requirement stated so it blocks pure form/function views without blocking form and function from mattering at all.
- mixed and fractional cases: replacement parts in a different material, one candidate holding 60% of the original matter and the other 40%, causal continuity and function pulling in opposite directions. Is any of that allowed to make identity a matter of degree, or does identity have to stay all-or-nothing while our reasons for caring about it come in degrees — and if the theory splits those two, make the split explicit as a requirement.
- the social layer: registry numbers, insurance, the name on the bow, "this is the vessel that was at Trafalgar." Must a theory of what the ship is line up with our practices of tracing, and if it doesn't, is that a cost to the theory or a finding about the practices?
- scope: does an account for artifacts have to double as an account for persons (the usual move of using the ship to think about personal identity), or is that optional extra credit? And does it need to be uniform across kinds — ships, buildings, a codebase where every line has been rewritten, an institution operating under its third charter — or is "uniform or explain the difference" enough?

The bit I most want, though: tell me which subset of your desiderata can't be jointly satisfied and where exactly the squeeze is. My hunch is that determinacy plus transitivity plus no-future-dependence plus "the symmetric case can't just be brute" is already more than one theory can carry, and that carrying all of it pushes you either toward denying that artifacts are the sort of thing with identity conditions at all, or toward making identity claims context- or interest-relative — but argue it your way, and say which single requirement knocks out the most candidates. Also flag the requirements you think are just a camp's prejudice in a necktie; I suspect "a theory must vindicate the folk verdicts in the easy cases" is one of them, but I want the argument either way.

If you feel like it, two or three lines at the end on how the usual suspects do — matter-based, form/function-based, spatiotemporal or causal continuity, temporal-parts/four-dimensionalist, constitution views, conventionalism, eliminativism. Honesly though the list is the deliverable; don't let the scorecard eat the answer.
