Quick bit of background: I retired a few years ago from a physiology lab — microsphere blood-flow work, mostly — and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the university, whatever the grad students feel like running. Last week's session was on whether self-deception is even possible, and I came away annoyed. Everyone had a favourite label: a mechanism story with something like repression in it, bad faith, a divided-mind picture, a couple of deflationary stories where it's just motivated reasoning with no special machinery, and one person who said the whole topic is a muddle inherited from folk psychology and there is nothing there to explain. Nobody could tell me what observation would separate these from each other, and because they were all using different examples they were mostly talking past one another. I want to pin it down the way we would have pinned it down in the lab: fix the cases first, then find out which combinations of verdicts anybody can actually hold.

So please build me a sorting tree — a decision tree, really — for the question of whether self-deception is possible and, if it is, what kind of thing it is. Build the branches out of the distinctions that do the work rather than out of a list of views, and phrase every branch node as a question someone could answer without knowing any of the labels. The distinctions I care about: whether the target case is acquiring a false belief, holding one against mounting evidence, getting rid of a true one, or simply never inquiring; whether it needs an intention to bring the state about or whether plain desire plus biased evidence-gathering can do it; whether the person ends up believing the thing or only talking and acting as if they do; whether the machinery has to be special — a divided mind, a subsystem, a defensive mechanism — or whether ordinary reasoning with a motive attached is enough; whether the person has to count as knowing better in some sense, the way they might answer differently under anaesthetic, or the day after the grant decision, or once the sealed envelope is opened; and whether the phenomenon is basically first-personal or whether the social and institutional version is the basic one. The cases I want used as the raw material, since they are the ones we argued about: a PI who designs the study so the awkward control never gets run and so never asks the question, and who genuinely does not think the result is fragile; the same PI ten years on, who now does half believe it and keeps citing it; a department where every member privately thinks the research line is dead and where, in the meeting, they sincerely say it is turning the corner; a patient who was told a prognosis and later genuinely does not remember being told; a reviewer who reads the abstract, forms a view, and skips the tables; a person who says they are not angry with their jaw clenched; and, my own addition, the fact that we bother with blinding, pre-registration and sealed predictions at all — to me that practice is only sensible if some intention-free, motive-driven version of this really happens, and I want a node that makes accounts answer for it.

The leaves I want to be able to reach: the repression-style mechanism view; the bad-faith view on which there is no belief involved at all, only a project of hiding something from oneself; the divided-mind picture; the deflationary view on which desire-driven bias without any intention is the whole story and there is no paradox to dissolve; the view that there is nothing special here beyond ordinary motivated reasoning and no irrationality is required; the view that what drives the behaviour is some non-doxastic state rather than a belief, so questions about believing are beside the point; the eliminativist view that there is no such thing and the appearances are better explained as ignorance, confabulation, or plain interpersonal deception; and the view that the institutional or collective case is the fundamental one. Give me roughly eight to ten leaves, note which combinations of answers nobody occupies and say why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored — and flag the pairs your tree genuinely cannot separate, especially the deflationary and ordinary-motivated-reasoning leaves and the bad-faith and non-doxastic-state leaves, rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them. For what it's worth my own verdicts, if you want to run me through it: yes to the PI who skips the control, yes to the older PI, yes to the department, no to the patient, yes to the reviewer, yes to the clenched jaw, and I take the existence of blinding as fairly strong evidence that the intention-free version is real.

Format: give it to me as a numbered list of exactly twelve items, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion. Items 1 through 7 are the branch questions in the order you would ask them, each with its answer options and one sentence on what each answer rules out. Item 8 is the leaves with their names and one line each. Item 9 is the unoccupied combinations and why they are empty. Item 10 is the pairs the tree can't separate. Item 11 is which single node is doing most of the work and why. Item 12 is which leaf my answers land me on, and what one change of mind would move me to a neighbouring leaf.
