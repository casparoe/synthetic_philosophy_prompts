I'm a grad student and I'm running a one-off philosophy-and-film night for our social ontology reading group — we're showing The Lobster (Lanthimos, 2015) and I've got about thirty minutes of discussion to lead afterwards, so I want a reading I can actually defend rather than just pointing at the screen and going "look, a coordination equilibrium." Quick reminder of the setup in case it helps: single people are sent to a hotel where they have 45 days to find a partner or be turned into an animal of their choosing (David wants a lobster; the dog with him is his brother Bob), guests can buy extra days by hunting and tranqulizing the loners who live in the woods, and the whole hotel culture runs on the idea that a match should be built on a shared superficial trait — John smashes his own nose in secret so he can pair up with the woman who has constant nosebleeds, and David courts the notoriously cruel woman by pretending to enjoy other people's suffering until she tests him by faking a choking fit in the hot tub. Later David escapes to the loners, who run the opposite system (no romance at all, punished by mutilation), falls for a short-sighted woman, and the two of them invent a private gesture language to talk behind the loners' backs. Then the loner leader has the woman blinded under the pretext of an operation, they can't find anything else they have in common, and the film ends with David in a restaurant bathroom with a steak knife, apparently about to blind himself.

The lens I want is David Lewis's account of convention from his 1969 book, and I should warn you that I'm reconstructing it from memory, so part of what I need is correction. The way I remember it: a regularity in behaviour is a convention when roughly (i) most people conform to it in a recurring situation, (ii) most people expect the others to conform, (iii) most people prefer to conform given that the others do — i.e. it's a coordination problem, not a conflict — (iv) there's some alternative regularity that everyone would also prefer if everyone else went for that one instead, which is supposed to be the arbitrariness bit, and (v) all of that is common knowledge. I'm sure I've got at least one of those clauses garbled, and I'd rather you fixed it than politely worked with my version.

What I want from you is a reading of the film in those terms, and in particular on three things. First: which of those conditions does the hotel's trait-matching practice actually meet, and which does it fail? My instinct is that it satisfies the arbitrariness condition beautifully — nosebleeds, lisps, limps, short-sightedness, any of them would do — but that it flunks the "prefer to conform given that others conform" bit, because nobody there is conforming out of a coincidence of interest; they're conforming because the alternative is becoming an animal. So is the hotel regime really a convention at all, or is it a sanction-backed norm that only looks like one? And relatedly: is the loners' anti-romance system best described as a second, rival convention — the alternative regularity that clause (iv) gestures at — and what does the film's two-camps structure show about how one equilibrium gets replaced by another, given that you can't get there one defector at a time? I think the private gesture language David and the short-sighted woman invent is the cleanest example in the film of a convention arising without anything resembling an agreement, and I want to know if I'm right that it's the one bit that's textbook.

Second, the bit I've been chewing on and can't find a handle on. A lot of the coordinating traits in the film are faked: John's nosebleeds, David's heartlessness, possibly more than we're shown. Everyone has an interest in displaying a trait that matches, so the feature that does the coordinating work is one participants have an incentive to misrepresent, and yet coordination still happens. I've been calling this a camouflage convention for want of a better phrase — an equilibrium where the coordinating feature is partly manufactured by the people coordinating on it, and where this is more or less visible to everyone. Does Lewis's machinery have room for that, or does introducing that incentive to misrepresent turn it into something else entirely (a signalling problem with the wrong incentive structure, or just a rule propped up by enforcement)? My friend says it's cheap talk and Lewis handles it fine; I think the film is showing something the synchronic definition can't see. Who's closer, and why?

Third, the ending. Read as a point about conventions, David going after his own eyes looks like someone destroying a capacity in order to restore a shared trait — his and the woman's one remaining coordination point. If that's right, the film is making a diachronic claim: that the arbitrariness of a convention at the moment it gets selected is perfectly compatible with its later becoming costly, compulsory and treated as if it were intrinsically significant (the hotel guests genuinely talk as if sharing a minor ailment is what makes a relationship work). Is that a real gap in Lewis's account, or is it something the account already has resources for, and either way, is the film adding anything here that the philosophical argument alone doesn't?

Two more things. I'd like you to be honest about where the reading overreaches — the film is obviously also just about loneliness and the absurdity of coupledom, and if a Lewisian reading flattens that, or if some scenes simply resist it, say which ones and why.
