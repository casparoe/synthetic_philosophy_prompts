I have been reading rationalist blogs and hanging around the associated forums for something like fifteen years, and I run a small reading group that grinds through decision-theory papers, so I am fluent in the usual vocabulary: expected value, preference versus value, updating on evidence you cannot yet imagine having, precommitment, that sort of thing. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; an answer that can only gesture at literature is useless to me, and I have already read the popular book on this. What I want is help extracting a principle from my own verdicts on transformative choices: decisions where you cannot know what the outcome is like until you are on the far side, and where being on the far side changes what you want.

Here are my verdicts, with letters so you can cross-reference. A: a man blind from infancy is offered a procedure that would restore his sight. Obviously take it, and I do not think there is any special puzzle here at all; the experience is unimaginable in advance, but nothing about who he is hangs in the balance, so ordinary expected-value reasoning about information and capability settles it. B: moving to a country whose language you do not speak, for a two-year immersive programme. Just go; you will acquire new tastes, but you will still be recognisably you, and proxies and expat testimony are good enough to decide on. C: the standard vampire case everyone in this space has seen, where you cannot know what it is like, it is irreversible, and it rewrites what you care about. I think the right frame is not maximising anything but asking whether you want to discover that you have become the sort of person for whom this matters, and I would say take it if yes. D: having a first child. Same verdict, same frame, and I actively distrust anyone who tries to run the numbers on future happiness. E: a hypothetical pill that gives you the full experience of some radical change for forty-eight hours and then reverses completely, with no trace and no physical risk. Obviously swallow it, and here is where I start to be unsure: I also feel the sample should be close to decisive for the permanent version, because afterwards the case has collapsed into an ordinary decision. I have started calling this the sandbox problem, the question whether a perfect sandbox dissolves the puzzle or merely relocates it one level up. F: a calling intervention, imagine a safe and well-tested neural treatment that reliably makes some activity utterly absorbing to you. The sandbox reveals you would love it, but taking it permanently means you would stop caring about your marriage and your current work, though of course you would not mind. I say do not take it, even though by the frame I used in C and D the new person's endorsement is supposed to be what counts. G: testimony from people already transformed. I discount their assurance that it is worth it and that I should trust them very heavily, on the grounds that they are now different people reporting from outside my value system. But I also want to give real weight to the base rate of post-hoc endorsement among people who were like me beforehand, the ninety per cent who report being glad. I have a sinking feeling those two moves are the same move performed in opposite directions. H: a gradual version of F, where you can escalate and de-escalate in small reversible steps, and each small step slightly changes the person who takes the next one. I care much more about having that exit option than I can justify, given that the end state is identical either way and the future me probably would not use it.

My own stab at a principle is that the distinction doing the work is not the epistemic versus personal distinction everyone reaches for, but the difference between changes in what you like and changes in what you care about: I am happy to defer to my future self about the first and I get protective about the second, which is supposed to explain why F comes apart from C. But I cannot tell whether that is a real distinction or status quo bias wearing a philosophy costume, and it does not obviously explain why G pulls me two ways, or why the sandbox in E feels decisive. So: give me a principle, or at most two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not listed, along with an honest account of which of my verdicts it forces me to drop and why. I would rather give up one verdict than accept a principle with six clauses tuned to save all eight, and if your account collapses into do what your future self will want, say so and say what work the caveats are doing. Then take it to the case I actually cannot settle, Z: whether to have a second child. The person who wants the second child is the person the first child made me, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a reason to trust the desire or a reason to distrust it.
