I edit an in-house newsletter for a university biology department — the readership is mostly evolutionary biologists and physiologists, plus two or three philosophers of science who rather enjoy being annoyed — and for the April issue we are running a spoof supplement in which the department's grant income is administered by a fictional quango, the Office for Scientific Method, which has taken falsificationist demarcation as its statutory test for what counts as science. Would you write the centre-piece of that supplement?

The piece I want is a parody of a Whitehall-style departmental circular — numbered paragraphs, mandarin register, roughly 800 words, issued by the Office to the Biological Sciences Sub-Committee and headed something like Reclassification of Evolutionary Theory: Guidance following the Darwinian Research Group's Submission — reporting on how the Office has handled an application from a group whose central explanatory schema is natural selection. Two invented devices should carry the comedy. First, a Falsification Bond: every funded unit posts a bond, forfeit in the event that one of its stated hypotheses is refuted, so that the safe move is to hold theories of which no test can be specified; the circular notes, deadpan, that units which genuinely risked their hypotheses (a molecular genetics unit is named, refuted in most of its stated predictions over the assessment period) have forfeited repeatedly and are now the poorest in the faculty, whereas the Darwinian group has not forfeited once. Second, a metric called Refutation-Adjusted Output, under which a programme that cannot be refuted accrues maximal corroboration, so that reclassification of evolutionary theory as a metaphysical research programme — the fallback the Office offers when a theory is judged untestable — is presented not as a demotion but as a promotion to a better-funded, non-expiring stream, on the grounds that a programme which no observation could overturn will never fail. Then, in the closing paragraphs, the circular applies its own test to itself: the Office rules on whether the demarcation criterion it administers is itself falsifiable, and having concluded that it is not, reclassifies its own rule as a metaphysical research programme and awards itself permanent funding.

The jokes have to come from getting the actual position right, so please exaggerate real features rather than inventing objections: the complaint that a schema which explains every trait in terms of some selection pressure is compatible with whatever we find; the ban on immunising stratagems, with the group's post hoc adaptive narratives re-described by the Office as auxiliary hypotheses filed after the event; the demand for severe tests and the observation that no test was ever specified, let alone survived; the later recantation in which the author of the criterion withdrew the charge of untestability, which the applicants cite and the Office rejects as untimely; and the alternative picture of organisms as problem-solvers whose errors are eliminated, which the Office tries to administer bureaucratically — requiring each trait to be registered against a problem stated in advance, and rejecting the group's entries because the problem was identified after the solution was already on the books. Keep it deadpan throughout, no winking, no narrator's aside, and do not put any sentence in quotation marks as though it were Popper's own wording: paraphrase his claims and, for that matter, keep him offstage entirely as a character, since the target is the position and the administrative style, not the man. The piece should be funny to a philosopher of biology who thinks the complaint has some force without being fatal, and still legible to a physiologist who has never read any of it. After the circular, add an editor's key of five or six lines saying which real feature each numbered joke is exaggerating, so our two philosophers can check we have not libelled anyone.
