I've been reading LessWrong and the blogs in that orbit for well over a decade, and one slogan from that world keeps being used as a conversation-stopper without anyone ever saying precisely what it claims: "Water is necessarily H2O." I'd like a disambiguation, because I'm fairly confident that the people deploying it are each relying on a different precisification, and that most of those precisifications don't do the job they're being conscripted for.

Where I last met it, and what it was being made to do: a thread on whether a physically exact emulation of a brain — the standard upload scenario — could in principle lack consciousness. Someone argued that my inability to rule this out by armchair reflection is worthless as evidence, on the grounds that people before modern chemistry could "perfectly well imagine" the lakes and rivers being full of something other than H2O, and that this imaginative freedom proved nothing whatsoever about what was possible. Conclusion: conceivability is no guide to possibility, so drop the zombie intuition. I think this is probably too fast, but I can't say exactly where it goes wrong, because I can't tell which claim the water example is supposed to establish.

Here is the kind of disentangling I have in mind, though please go where the distinctions actually are rather than to my list:

- Scope and force of "necessarily": truth at all worlds considered as counterfactual; truth at all worlds considered as actual; a priority for an idealized reasoner; immunity to empirical refutation; essentiality to the kind; necessity of the identity statement itself rather than necessity of a property-instantiation.
- Whether the claim is about our words (rigid designation, reference borrowing, the failure of description theories for kind terms) or about the world (an essentialist thesis about substances and their microstructure). My sense is that people slide from the first to the second without noticing.
- The two-dimensional split: the sentence looks necessary on one dimension and contingent on the other, and my tentative suspicion is that the only reading of the slogan strong enough to kill the conceivability argument is not one the example itself supports, while the reading the example does support leaves the conceivability argument untouched. I'd like that suspicion tested rather than confirmed.
- Whether the necessity is, so to speak, knowledge-relative — whether it depends on the historical accident that our term was introduced before we knew the chemistry, or on the fact that the reference-fixing description is thin.

Two cases I've never seen anyone run through the example, which I think are diagnostic and which I'd like used as a test on the candidate readings.

Case One, "Split Lake": a world in which a single community's environment contains two superficially indistinguishable watery liquids, H2O and XYZ, both abundant, both in the lakes, both drunk, both falling as rain, and no one in that community has done any chemistry. Ask what each precisification says about whether "water is H2O" is necessary there — in particular whether the primary intension fails to denote for lack of a unique satisfier, picks out a disjunction, or picks out one of the two arbitrarily — and whether a framework that handles this by brute stipulation has thereby given up something it wanted. I also want to know whether this case is the *closer* analogue to consciousness: if whatever physical state realizes consciousness is multiply realizable in the way being-a-drinkable-liquid-in-the-lakes is, then the water example may be structurally misleading rather than merely inconclusive.

Case Two, "the stipulated term": a community that introduces its word for the watery stuff *after* the chemistry, by stipulation, as "the liquid with microstructure M," and for whom the corresponding sentence is consequently a priori and trivially necessary. Does the necessity in our slogan survive that change of practice? If not, is the slogan telling us anything about the world at all, or only about the epistemic situation of speakers whose vocabulary was fixed in ignorance?

Finally, and most importantly: even granting the strongest defensible reading, doesn't the move from water to consciousness need an extra premise — roughly, that once the whole microphysical story is fixed, everything else follows a priori, and that the residual appearance of a further question is a feature of our concepts rather than of the world? That looks to me like the load-bearing claim, and it looks like exactly the claim at issue rather than something the water example could supply.

Please don't give me a bibliography, and don't rest anything on the authority of particular papers: I have no library here, and I want the argument, not the citations.

I'd be grateful for the response in five named sections, roughly as follows: "Readings," 600–800 words, a numbered menu of the distinct precise theses the slogan could be asserting, each stated so that it could be true or false, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical; "Verdicts," 300–400 words, tagging each reading as platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, or straightforwardly false, with the reason compressed into a sentence or two; "My two cases," 300–400 words, on what each leading reading predicts about Split Lake and about the stipulated term, and which readings those cases count against; "Does it do the work?," 300–400 words, on whether any reading actually licenses shutting down the upload argument, and if so which one and what extra premise it needs; "Bottom line," about 150 words.
