Last night my friend Sam and I got into it for about three hours over whether someone who genuinely, flatly does not care about anything beyond the next week has a reason to keep taking their medication. I ended up defending a position I'm not sure I can actually defend out loud, and now I have to give it as a department colloquium in three weeks, so I need to be ready for the question period.

Here's the view, roughly. Call it scaffolded deliberative internalism: there's a reason for A to φ only if A could come to be moved by that consideration through a process we'd count as their own deliberation — where "their own deliberation" is allowed to be extended in time, socially scaffolded (a friend arguing with them over months, a therapist, a book, being around different people), allowed to fix factual mistakes, and allowed to draw out things they already half-care about but have never articulated. What it's not allowed to do is import a desire from outside: no conditioning, no drugs, no manipulation that bypasses their own take-up, and no just stipulating a new want into the set. So the motivational condition still bites — a genuinely desireless or totally unmoved-to-be-moved agent really has no reason — but it bites far less often than the standard internalist thinks, because in practice the route from where almost anyone actually is to being moved by the consideration usually exists, it's just long and needs other people.

Sam's objection, which I did not have a good answer to, was that once you let the process be that long and that scaffolded, "could be brought to be moved" just means "an ideal persuader could get them there," which is externalism wearing a motivational condition as a hat — the internalist constraint stops excluding anything. I suspect that's the question I'm going to get in some form and I need to have handled it before slide two.

The room: about thirty people. Three or four hard-line Humeans about reasons who think internalism is basically definitional and that I've reinvented it badly; two Kantian-ish rationalists who think the whole motivational constraint is a category mistake and reasons are just facts about what there is to be said for things; a couple of moral psychologists who spend their time on how people actually change (and don't change) what they care about, including the stuff where people dig in and keep insisting something's wrong after every reason they give gets knocked down; and one political philosopher who will care a lot about whether my view can still say anything to someone genuinely awful. No one there works on this exact debate, which I think makes it worse, not better.

What I want is a prep sheet. Give me the eight-ish objections I'll actually get from this room — not the ones I'd get from people who already agree with me — ordered by likelihood times damage, each one phrased the way that kind of person would actually say it out loud (the Humean version and the rationalist version of the same complaint sound completely different and I need both). For each: a reply I can give in about ninety seconds standing at the whiteboard without notes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft or where I'm quietly changing the subject. Tell me what I should concede in the first three minutes so I'm not still fighting it in Q&A, which single reply has to land or the whole talk is dead, and — please — the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, with the real answer rather than the comfortable one. If you think Sam's objection is fatal as stated, say so and tell me the most I could salvage by tightening something.
