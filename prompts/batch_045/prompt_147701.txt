I'm writing a paper for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of reasons, and I have got stuck on the question my supervisor thinks is the only interesting one left: must a reason be someone's reason? The internalism/externalism dispute as we have been reading it assumes a reasoner is on the scene — the Williams-style internalist asks whether a consideration can be reached by sound deliberation from the agent's subjective motivational set, and the stance-independent externalist says a consideration can be a reason for you whether or not your desires connect up to it — but both camps take for granted that there is an agent there for the reason to belong to. What I want is a case that probes what happens when that assumption fails: a consideration that looks perfectly normative but whose putative bearer cannot ever take it up. My supervisor's suspicion is that once the reasoner drops out, the disagreement between the two standard camps quietly becomes verbal, and I do not yet know whether he is right — which is part of why I want the verdicts to fall out of the views plus the facts of the case rather than be written into it.

So here is the commission. Build me a thought experiment, set somewhere realistic — a hospital ward, a care home, a special-needs school, a family — with no science fiction, no exotic technology, no invented metaphysics, and no medical or psychological condition more baroque than something a general practitioner would recognise. The case needs one specific candidate reason-bearer, a named human being B, who is not at any point in B's life in a position to take up some consideration C: B permanently lacks the capacity ever to register C as counting in favour of anything. I would rather you did not use mere death as the mechanism, since that just replays the familiar posthumous-reasons argument; pick something else, and make vivid what someone else could do about it. Then show that the case delivers these three verdicts, each generated by the view plus the facts rather than stipulated:

- The free-floating, stance-independent externalist says: yes, there is a genuine and weighty reason for B — a reason B has, not merely a reason for other people concerning B — and B's incapacity is irrelevant to its existence, its content, and its weight.
- The Williams-style internalist says: there is a reason in the neighbourhood, but it is not B's. It is a reason for a designated surrogate — a parent, nurse, sibling, trustee — generated by a sound deliberative route from that surrogate's actual motivational set. Set the route out premise by premise, each premise being something a realistic person in that role could actually have.
- A third view I want tested, which you should call the uptake condition: a consideration is a reason for someone only if that person is, at some time during their life, capable of being addressed by it — capable of registering it as counting in favour and of being moved by it, with help and scaffolding if necessary, but with the capacity being theirs. On this view there is no reason for B at all; what remains is an evaluative fact with no reason-bearer, a genuinely free-floating rationale.

Agreement in verdict is worthless if the views are only redescribing the same thing, so the case must carry a dial: some feature you can vary — whether anyone who cares about B is on the scene, whether B had a lucid interval earlier in life, whether B once cared about C and has since lost the capacity, whether acting on C costs the surrogate something — such that the reason-for-B and the surrogate's reason can differ in weight or come apart entirely. Say what each of the three views predicts at each setting of the dial. Then identify the single feature of the case doing the work, and show what happens when it is removed: in particular, that restoring addressability even for an hour collapses the three-way split back into the ordinary two-sided argument, and say which way each view then goes. Finally, give me an honest audit of the design — what the case is actually good for (I want to use it to ask whether B can be wronged, or acted for, in a reason-involving way, and whether acting for B's sake is ever literally true), how a defender of whichever view comes out worst would attack it, and, since my supervisor will certainly ask, whether you think this three-way disagreement is substantive or a clash over what the word *reason* is being used to pick out. Please keep the scenario itself under about 250 words, and write your answer in British English.
