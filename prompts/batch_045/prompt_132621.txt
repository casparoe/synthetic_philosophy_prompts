My metaphysics study group built an argument against counterpart theory that I think proves way too much, and I can't find the broken step.

(1) Counterpart theory says a de re modal claim like Socrates could have been a cobbler is true just in case Socrates has a counterpart in some other possible world who is a cobbler — modal claims about a thing are never about that thing across worlds, only about things that resemble it.
(2) Which things count as counterparts is interest-relative: the respects of similarity that matter, and how much similarity is enough, come from context rather than being fixed once and for all.
(3) So for any object x and any property F that anything has anywhere, we can pick a context whose respects of similarity ignore every respect in which x and the F-things differ; then some F-thing in some world counts as a counterpart of x, and x could have been F comes out true.
(4) So no de re modal claim about anything is false: I could have been a poached egg, or the Eiffel Tower, or the number 7.
(5) But plenty of de re modal claims are false, and not only fussy essentialist ones.
(6) So counterpart theory is false, and modal claims are about the very objects they seem to be about — which means transworld identity after all.
(7) And the same reasoning runs across time instead of worlds: if which future stage counts as me is equally a matter of chosen respects of similarity, then no future-tense claim about me is false either.

I want to keep (5) and I'd rather pay by constraining admissible counterpart relations than by going back to transworld identity, but I suspect any constraint strong enough to kill (4) just smuggles transworld identity in through the back door — and I don't want to hear about the separate worry that on this view I'm not talking about myself, I already know that one.

Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? Answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
