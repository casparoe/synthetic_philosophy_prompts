I run a small decision-theory reading group with two friends from the economics department, and over the last few months I have come to accept something like the standard line about the value of information. Good's theorem — the result that for an expected-utility maximizer the ex ante value of a costless signal is never negative, since acquiring it never removes an act from the feasible set and you can always simply act as you would have acted had you not received it — strikes me as about as firm as anything in this neighbourhood, and I now lean on a stronger, more operational version of it: whenever additional information appears to make an agent worse off, the harm must be traceable to one of a short list of extra factors — the information was not really free, the agent is not an expected-utility maximizer, preferences run over beliefs as well as over outcomes, other agents are responding to what the agent knows, or the agent is boundedly rational and updates badly on noise. I like this version because it is diagnostic rather than merely reassuring: it predicts that every apparent counterexample will decompose into one of those failure modes.

What I want from you is the case against it, in the sense of what would have to be true for me to be wrong. Please set aside the boring failures — monetary cost, time, attention, the risk of being lied to — and go for the structural ones. Here are the cases I keep turning over. First, my brother is an oncologist, and he says there are borderline genomic results he would rather not have, because once he has seen one he can no longer recommend the standard treatment with the particular wholeheartedness that he thinks does part of the therapeutic work; that looks like a case where information shrinks the feasible act set rather than merely informing a choice within it. Second, there is ignorance as a commitment device: the negotiator, or the family of a kidnap victim, who is better off genuinely not knowing, because not knowing is the only way to be unable to reveal the thing or unable to cave. Third, there are cases where the harm runs through the agent's own dispositions — learning from an accurate predictor what sort of person you are may destroy the very disposition you had hoped to exercise, and you cannot choose to be the sort of person who had not been told. Fourth, and this is the one I find hardest to fit into the framework at all, there is information that is not a coarsening or refinement of a fixed partition: learning a new concept, a name for a symptom, a distinction you could not previously draw, after which the space of possibilities has itself been re-individuated and acting as if you had not learned it is not among the things you can do even in principle.

So: tell me which of these are genuine defeaters and which attack a strawman, and which assumption of the theorem each one targets. Rank them for me from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about what a defender of the theorem would herself concede as counting against it, as opposed to what only critics say — I would rather have the concessions than the polemic. I would also like to know whether the companion result, that a finer experiment is never worth less than a coarser one, is exposed at the same points or at different ones, and whether the ex ante framing is doing real work here, since in several of my cases the agent is plainly worse off ex post in some realizations while the average still comes out fine. Finally, and most importantly: has my operational version of the view been qualified into something unfalsifiable? If every apparent counterexample is met by saying that the information was not really free, or he was not really rational, or what arrived was not really a signal over a fixed state space, then the claim seems to be on its way to being true by stipulation. Say honestly whether that is where this ends up, and whether, for a claim of this kind, that is a defect. The two things that would move me most are a case where the feasible set genuinely shrinks and a case where the incoming item cannot be modelled as a partition of an already-given space of possibilities; if neither can be made to work, I would like to be told that too.
