I'm in a philosophy of social science seminar this term — we've been working through natural kinds versus social kinds, Hacking-style looping effects, and the age–period–cohort identification problem — and I also write for the campus satire magazine, which is putting together a themed issue on what the issue editor insists on calling administrative ontology. I want a piece that satirizes the way my university, and the culture around it, talks about generational cohorts: not a rant about how Millennials were cheated or how Gen Z is fragile, but a parody of an actual administrative genre, deadpan enough that a reader can't tell whether the Office of Cohort Classification is being ironic. Please write it as an internal policy memo of roughly 800 to 1,000 words, with section headings, from the university's invented Office of Cohort Classification to the Registrar and the Deans, setting out the procedure by which incoming students are assigned to generational cohorts and the appeals process for students who contest their assignment. Give it the full apparatus: definitions, cutoff years, an evidentiary standard, a fee schedule or a form number, a short appendix.

Here is what I want the humor to do, and I'd rather you get this right than get laughs cheaply. The office should be forced by its own logic into visibly absurd places, and the jokes should come from genuinely features of cohort-talk rather than invented ones. Some material I'd like worked in, in whatever order serves the memo: (i) the cutoff years are exact to the day, yet the office also rules that no appeal may rest on a one-day difference, since no single day changes anyone — while a two-year difference is treated as decisive; (ii) students born in the interstitial years are placed on a provisional register and file a childhood-memory petition, offering testimony about dial-up, a family desktop in a shared room, or a first phone, and the office grades these memories on a rubric; (iii) cohort membership is treated as causing traits that everyone in the seminar can see are really age effects or period effects — the office solemnly records that its students became markedly more risk-averse and less digitally fluent between matriculation and senior year, and files this as a cohort finding; (iv) the office extends a cohort's end date whenever the incoming class tests as insufficiently representative of it. The reductio I most want, if you can land it: because provisional assignments are self-reported, and because students who declare a cohort then get the housing, orientation programming, and peer mentoring attached to it, the office discovers an extremely tight correlation between declared cohort and measured cohort traits, and cites this correlation, in an appendix, as independent empirical validation of the classification. It should be perfectly clear, without the memo ever saying so, that the correlation is an artifact of the classification feeding back into the people classified.

Two constraints, please. The memo must never come out and say that the categories are socially constructed, that the boundaries are arbitrary, or that generational essences are a marketing artifact: no winking, no aside that names the moral, and no character who sees through it — the reader should feel the absurdity because the reasoning is followed faithfully. And it should be funny to the people in my seminar while readable to someone who has never thought about any of this: define any technical notion inside the memo's own voice, in bureaucratic language, so that the piece works without footnotes explaining itself. I'd rather it end flat, on some small administrative detail, than on a punchline.
