I spent a career in observational astronomy, much of it building instruments and very little of it reading philosophy. In retirement I have been working through some of it, and a piece of my own former profession's practice has begun to look strange to me — not because the practice is silly, but because I cannot say what view of the world would have to be true for it to be sensible.

The practice is this. Listening is ordinary, funded, uncontroversial science: passive surveys of the radio and optical sky for artificial-looking signals are simply what the field does. Deliberate transmission is different: a handful of groups, sometimes privately funded, have composed and beamed messages at chosen targets, and have done so essentially on their own authority, as a research project like any other. Yet the norm the field has arrived at for the case everyone regards as the interesting one runs the other way: if a signal were detected, the profession's settled position is that no reply should be sent without broad international consultation, and it is taken for granted that replying is a decision belonging to humanity rather than to a laboratory. So the reply is treated as a collective act requiring deliberation, while the initiation — the unprovoked shout into a neighbourhood we know nothing about — is treated as a private one.

That asymmetry is the first thing I would like reconstructed. The argument the science fiction writer Liu Cixin gives for the "dark forest" — that visibility is lethal, that no civilization can verify another's intentions across interstellar distances, that a species technologically behind us now may be ahead of us by the time our signal arrives, and that destruction is cheap next to the risk of waiting — seems to me to invert the ordering the practice rests on. If that argument is right, the dangerous act is the one anybody is allowed to perform, and the act we have fenced about is nearly costless, since a civilization whose signal we could receive already knows, to within a modest error, where to look. What theory of the relevant risk makes the practice's allocation of authority sensible, and does that theory survive contact with the argument?

I have also been playing with a variant policy, and I would like to know what it assumes. Suppose we permitted transmission but required that it never be sent from, or in a way that reveals, the solar system: the message would be relayed through a transmitter placed far off-axis, so that a recipient could read the content without acquiring our coordinates. Call it an anonymous beacon. It seems to me that anyone attracted by this design must believe several rather specific things — that what endangers us is being located rather than being known to exist, that travel and not information is the binding constraint, that concealment remains available to us in spite of everything our atmosphere and civilisation already radiate, and that contact would still be worth having if it could never become an exchange. But I cannot tell which of these are strictly presupposed and which are merely customary baggage, and the last one in particular seems to embed a view about the value of knowledge that I have never seen argued for.

I would like the implicit theory made explicit and then tested. What must be true of agency, rationality, and the predictability of minds wholly unlike ours for this practice to be more than noise? Where is it genuinely underdetermined, so that several quite different theories — including ones that take the dark forest seriously — would license exactly the arrangements we have? Where do its parts quietly contradict one another? And what would have to change in the field's institutions if the view it currently presupposes were replaced by the dark forest view, or by a view on which the motives of an alien civilisation are simply not something we can reason about at all? I am interested in being told where my own framing of the problem is muddled, and I would rather have that than agreement.
