I have been reading the historiography of the Third Reich for about eight months now, for a private reason: my grandfather's family left Silesia in 1938, and when he died last spring I inherited his papers and, with them, an argument I have not managed to conclude — with him, and more recently with my brother, who is a professional historian and who holds that the whole question is malformed. What I want from you is not a verdict on that question but a diagnosis of why it has gone on so long without resolution, and I would rather you did not spend your answer surveying the literature or recommending what I should read next.

The two positions, as fairly as I can state them. The structural side argues that the Depression, the Versailles settlement and the stab-in-the-back myth, the disintegration of the liberal and Centre parties, the bargain the conservative elites struck with the Nazis at the start of 1933 and the speed with which the Enabling Act followed, together made some such outcome heavily overdetermined: Hitler was the vehicle rather than the engine, and a movement with that much institutional and social force behind it would have found another frontman, or another form, with broadly the same result. The other side replies that this explains the wrong thing: plenty of European countries became authoritarian in those years, and what actually needs explaining is not that Germany did, but the particular sequence of gambles from 1939 onward, the war of annihilation in the East, and the escalation of the killing of European Jews into a continent-wide industrial programme — decisions taken often against the grain of the very bureaucracy that is supposed to have generated them.

I have two hypotheses, which I would like you to test rather than flatter. The first is that much of the apparent disagreement is a silent slide between levels of specificity. I have started sorting the question into what I privately call a ladder: would Germany have become a one-party authoritarian state at all; would it have been a National Socialist one; would it have launched a war of continental conquest; would it have carried out the Holocaust in anything like the form and scale it took. My impression is that the structuralist is usually answering at a lower rung and the Great Man side at a higher one, and that neither says which. Does that ladder dissolve the dispute, or does it merely relocate the fight to the upper rungs, where the structural case is hardest to make? The second hypothesis is that two different questions are sharing one vocabulary — a causal one about how much difference a single person made to a historical outcome, and a moral one about who bears responsibility, which we have powerful reasons to want answered in terms of persons — and that the persistence of the argument owes more to the second than anyone involved will admit.

I would also like you to deal with my brother's objection directly. He says that historical counterfactuals are untestable, that there is no evidence that could discriminate between the answers, and that the question is therefore not a question. My own feeling is that this is too quick, since we make and defend counterfactual judgements about the past constantly and hold each other to them; but I want to know whether this is a genuine methodological objection that ought to end the matter, or a respectable way of declining to answer it.

Given whatever diagnosis you arrive at, tell me what would count as progress here. I have my own candidates — systematic comparison with the other interwar authoritarianisms rather than Germany treated as a single case; a more careful account of what an individual's causal contribution to a large outcome even is; or simply a better-posed question — but I would rather you said which of them does real work and which merely changes the subject, and I would take seriously an argument that no progress should be expected and that this is the wrong thing to want.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a long email to someone who has asked you a serious question: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no lists masquerading as sentences.
