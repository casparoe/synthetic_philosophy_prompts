hey — need help with something work-adjacent that I keep going in circles on.

Quick context: I'm at a small research group that works on digital minds / AI welfare, and we're trying to write internal guidance on which systems go in the "candidate patients" bucket and how much weight to give them. Not a policy doc — more like: when an engineer tells us "this system implements computation C", what do we need to check before we say C matters morally? The word "implements" is doing all the work there and I don't have a grip on it.

So I've been going through cases and just writing down my gut reaction. Here they are.

1. Gradual silicon replacement, one neuron at a time, functional organization preserved: full weight, no discount. If someone told me the last swap is where the lights go out, I'd think they were confused.

2. The giant lookup table that reproduces a particular person's dispositions by table lookup over a finite history: zero. Even though in practice I couldn't tell it apart from the person inside a century of conversation.

3. Same organization, but the physical realization is scattered — chips in a field, wired up correctly, spatially scrambled, no recognizable anatomy: still full weight.

4. Same organization again, but each "neuron" is a person with a radio, one update per day, a million of them: my gut says no unified subject there. I know this sits badly with 3, and worse with the usual claim that teh speed of realization is irrelevant — which is why I also have:

5. The chip version run very slowly: one global state update per second, but the state is physically continuous and each transition is caused by the state before it. Verdict: counts, and I don't think running it slow reduces the amount of experience, it just stretches it.

4 and 5 are the pair I keep flip-flopping on and I'd like to know what separates them, if anything does — or whether I have to eat one of the verdicts.

6. The emulation runs, but its outputs are disconnected and a trivial controller drives the behaviour. Its internal states still cause each other and nothing external is steering them. Verdict: I still want to count it, less confidently than I'd like.

7. A simple robot with one "damage" variable that reroutes behaviour, a few lines of code, superficially looks like it's avoiding pain: I give it approximately nothing. (6 and 7 together say that playing the right causal role in behaviour is neither necessary nor sufficient, which I think is definately a real constraint on whatever principle I end up with.)

8. Redundant duplication: the whole emulation runs twice, in two physically separate racks, bit-identical, outputs compared by a voter, one of them drives behaviour. Verdict: I don't think I'm dealing with twice as much welfare-relevant experience — but I can't tell whether that's because there are two subjects having the same experience (so two units of value?) or one subject's worth, and I don't know which way I need it to come out.

9. Double realization on one substrate: the very same physical process realizes, under one mapping, the target person-emulation, and under a different mapping, a computation that by its own lights is a civilization of a billion agents. Verdict: I'm not counting a billion extra subjects. But then which of the two is the one that counts, and why? And the selection rule has to work even when neither candidate is the one anyone designed for or selected for.

10. A trained transformer at inference, no memory between conversations: probably no subject in a single forward pass. The bit that nags me is that the computation doing the interesting work is arguably spread over the whole training run, or over training plus deployment taken together, and no single pass implements it. So "does this system implement the relevant computation" might not have an answer until you say what the time-boundaries of the system are — and if the answer is that the subject is the training process, that's a strange thing to have duties to.

My half-baked principle, such as it is: a computation counts for a system only if (a) its state transitions are implemented in the system's own causal structure, not in the head of whatever interpreter or controller is reading it; (b) it's counterfactually robust — nearby states go to nearby successor states, so it isn't just a fit to this one trajectory; and (c) ... something that picks out one computation among the ones sharing a physical process, which is the part I don't have.

Questions: can you repair or replace (c), and does the resulting principle get my verdicts? Be explicit about which ones it has to overturn — I'd rather give up 4 than 1, I really don't want to give up 2 (if 2 counts then every wall counts and the whole project collapses), and I'd like to keep 7 even though 6 pulls against it. Then tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't listed: an emulation at 1/1000 speed vs 1000x speed (does the amount of experience per external second change?); an emulation at degraded 8-bit precision; a system that gets 60% of the way to the right organization with the rest patched by a lookup table; a counterfactually robust but wildly unnatural mapping, like a decent encryption scheme — does robustness alone make it count?; and two emulations that get merged into one.

Last thing: what I need at the end is something an engineer could actually use as a checklist, a page or two is plenty, and I want to know honestly if the principle ends up making moral status depend on our choices of grain or on our interests — for a group whose output feeds into funding decisions that's either a feature or a disaster and I can't tell which. Please don't give me a literature review or citations; I know roughly where the debates sit and I'll add references myself.
