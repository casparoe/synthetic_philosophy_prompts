At a university seminar I have recently started attending as a retired physicist, an old colleague ended a discussion with a gibe I keep meeting: philosophy of science is about as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds. He used it to suggest that conceptual work about modelling and measurement could not matter to working physicists or biologists. I want to try to replace that gibe with something sharper. Call a clinamen any small, discipline-internal swerve—a new instrument, notation, calibration, or error-correcting routine—by which a field corrects its own course without external reflection. My clinometric slogan is: 'If a science has a clinamen, it has already got all the philosophy it needs.' Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it would actually support using the slogan to close down the discussion in the way my colleague used the ornithology line; I especially want to know which reading I would need in order to make it do that work. Answer in British English, as exactly five numbered readings, each at most three sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Make the fifth item do double duty: after setting out and classifying the most plausible reading—the division-of-labour claim that a science with a reliable internal error-correcting mechanism has no need of external conceptual criticism except in special cases—add one sentence saying what would most change your verdict about the whole menu and where your reasoning is likeliest to fail.
