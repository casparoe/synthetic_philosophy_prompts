My dad had a mild heart thing a few months back and I've ended up being the one who goes to his cardiology appointments with him, so I've been sitting in a lot of waiting rooms thinking about this. Here's what I keep noticing about how his doctors actually operate.

When it comes to pills, they will not act on a correlation. Dad got a calcium score and a genetic risk number, both high, and the cardiologist's line was basically that those are for predicting, not for treating — we don't treat the number, we treat the thing that causes the event. And I get why: more than once a drug has moved some lab number in the healthy direction and turned out to do nothing, or to kill people. So the rule seems to be, only intervene on something you've shown actually brings about the outcome, and the way you show that is a randomized trial, where who gets the drug is decided by a coin flip precisely so that the doctor's choice carries no information about how sick the patient is.

But then two other things happen in the same building. One: those same numbers are treated as perfectly good reasons to do other things — the high score is why he's on a statin and why he gets checked more often. Two: the lifestyle handout. Exercise, vegetables, sleep, don't sit all day. As far as I can tell that advice rests almost entirely on observational studies where people who do those things live longer — but people who do those things also do everything else, so maybe jogging is a symptom of being the kind of person who lives long rather than a cause of living long. Nobody seems bothered by this. Its the first thing on the sheet.

Meanwhile my brother in law spent Thanksgiving going on about a puzzle: you're in Damascus, Death is coming for you tomorrow, you can stay or flee to Aleppo, but Death is an excellent predictor and will be wherever he predicts you'll be — so your fleeing is near certain evidence that he's in Aleppo, even though your fleeing doesn't cause him to be there. Staying looks pointless and fleeing looks like bad news. He had names for the two sides, something like evidential and causal decision theory, and insisted medicine has to pick one.

So: what does this package of practices actually presuppose? Is medicine quietly committed to one side of that puzzle, and if so which — or does it make a distinction (predicting vs intervening, what will happen to me vs what I should do) that means it never had to pick? Is it inconsistent to spend all that effort randomizing the doctor's choice so it carries no news, and then hand my dad a diet sheet where the patient's own choice is doing nothing but carrying news? What does the practice strictly require you to believe about reasons for action, as opposed to what it merely fits comfortably with — could someone on either side of the Damascus argument endorse all of it? And if a hospital did go all in the other way, what would actually change in the clinic: would they start treating the numbers, stop randomizing, stop handing out the diet sheet, or something I haven't thought of?
