I'm in a final-year seminar on autonomy and interpersonal influence, and I have to give a short presentation in two weeks on whether the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion can be drawn at all. Our seminars have been organised around three families of account, and I want to present them with a case that actually separates them, rather than the usual battery of easy ones — the lie, the threat, the hypnotic suggestion — where all three deliver the same verdict and nothing is learned. The three, as we have been using them: (i) the bypass/subversion account — influence is manipulative only when it works by routes that bypass or degrade the target's capacity to weigh reasons, rather than by addressing reasons to her; (ii) the hidden-agenda account — influence is manipulative when the influencer intends to steer the target toward a particular outcome and that intention is not transparent to her, whatever route the influence takes; (iii) the treating-as-a-tool account — influence is manipulative when the influencer substitutes her own will for the target's, treating the target's decision as something to be produced rather than as a self-governing choice to be engaged.

The case I need is one in which (i) and (iii) both return legitimate persuasion and (ii) returns manipulation — so the usual direction is reversed: reason-giving, normally the paradigm of legitimate influence, is here the manipulative-looking thing, and the account usually treated as the demanding one about reasons comes out the most permissive, against the grain of ordinary judgement, which I want to land with (ii). The specifications:

- Ordinary setting of your choice — a workplace or a clinic, please, not a trolley or a laboratory. No hypnosis, drugs, neurotechnology, subliminal presentation, or exotic psychology; and every proposition the influencer conveys is true.
- The influence must run entirely through reasons: the target is given only true considerations, deliberates on them, and acts because of them. The influencer's control is exercised over *which* true considerations get raised and in *what order* — selection and sequencing rather than content. I suspect influence-by-omission is the cleanest mechanism here, but if you have another that still satisfies (i), use it and say why it is better.
- The steering intention is not disclosed. Had the target been told at the time, "I picked which considerations to raise, and in this order, because I wanted you to choose X," she would have been put off. But afterwards, fully informed about the selection and the sequencing, she still endorses both the choice and the considerations that moved her: I want that post-hoc endorsement to hold, so nobody can convert the case into a straightforward harm or frustrated-autonomy case.
- The influencer's ends should be the target's own good, or at least not hostile to it. I want the awkward case, not the exploitative one; if the influencer is self-serving, (iii) collapses into (ii) and I lose the three-way split. Nothing may turn on the target being unusually credulous, exhausted, or impaired — an ordinary competent adult.
- For each of (i)–(iii), point to the clause of the account that generates the verdict and show that the case satisfies it. The verdicts have to fall out of the accounts; if you have to stipulate them, the case has failed, and please tell me so rather than papering over it.
- Then give me a matched variant: hold everything fixed except that the steering intention is disclosed up front, or the target has pre-authorised being steered in that way. Say which accounts flip and which do not. If (ii) does not flip, the case is not isolating what I think it is isolating, and I need to know that.
- Then: which single feature of the case is doing the work; and the strongest reply available to a defender of (i) — either that the case does after all involve bypass (for instance because salience and ordering effects operate below deliberation, so the influence is not really reason-addressing), or that the permissive verdict is right and should just be bitten. Tell me which of those you think is an ad hoc amendment to the account and which is a genuine implication of it.
- One more thing: my TA's stock move is to argue that any apparently hard case is a disguised deception case. Please make sure yours does not secretly reintroduce deception — and if you think any case meeting my specifications inevitably does, say that explicitly.

The scenario itself can be about 150 words, and I would like the whole answer under about 700: scenario, the three walkthroughs, the matched variant, the diagnosis, and the reply to (i). The point of the exercise is that I intend to argue in the presentation that hiddenness does normative work that the bypass account cannot capture, so the case has to be one a defender of that account would recognise as a problem rather than one I have rigged against her.
