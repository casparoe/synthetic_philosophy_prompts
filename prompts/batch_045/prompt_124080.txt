I'm on the academic ceremonies subcommittee of my university senate, and I've got to circulate something before Friday's meeting. I've been going back and forth on this for a week, so I'd rather have a draft to argue with than a blank page.

Here's the set-up. Our governing statutes say degrees "are conferred by the Board of Trustees," and what the Board actually does is pass one omnibus resolution each April covering everyone expected to finish by August. The ceremony script isn't in the statutes at all — it's just a script, and the person presiding reads: "By virtue of the authority vested in me by the Board of Trustees, I now confer upon you the degrees recommended by the faculties, with all the rights and privileges thereunto appertaining." Everybody — graduates, families, the registrar, presumably the Board — treats that as the moment the thing happens.

This May our interim provost filled in at the last minute and the teleprompter had the wrong card loaded. What he actually read was the wording we use at the December recognition ceremony: "I present to you the candidates for degrees recommended by the faculties." So the conferring verb was never said out loud in front of 480 people and their families. Nobody noticed for about six weeks, until a parent who does conveyancing for a living watched the captioned video and emailed us, and now its also been mentioned to a local reporter.

Two complications. First: the April Board resolution covers 479 of the 480. One of them finished mid-year and was added to the roll by the registrar in early June, because her dissertation paperwork had been sitting on a desk waiting for a signature; she isn't named in the April resolution at all. So for her, if anything said from the stage could have mattered, that's the only candidate. Second: a philosopher on the faculty sent us a one-page note distinguishing, as she puts it, acts that are constituted by an utterance, acts that are constituted by an entry in a record, and acts that are constituted by uptake. Her view is that conferral is the second kind, so nothing on that stage could have conferred or failed to confer anything, and the "moment" everyone remembers is a third thing — a piece of theatre whose job is to make an already-existing change public and to get the room to treat these people differently from now on. A historian on the subcommittee says that gets it backwards: a degree is a status, statuses are created by declarations, and a declaration with the wrong verb in it doesn't half-work, it fails — void rather than merely shoddy.

What I need is a memo to the subcommittee, which will forward whatever it approves to the full senate and to the registrar, recommending a concrete course of action with actual dates attached. Assume the following constraints, all of which are real: our counsel has told us we must not say or imply that these people were not graduates in May, because several have already started jobs and at least one has applied for a professional licence; the mid-year graduate needs something in her file that will survive a credential check; a small group of graduates have asked us to hold a proper do-over ceremony; and the memo will almost certainly be read by graduates and by a lawyer, so it can't be hand-wavy about what we think happened.

Two things I specifically want in it. One: work out what our own recommended remedy is doing. If we have the Board pass a September resolution that "confirms" the degrees with retroactive effect, is that a different kind of act from conferring them, and is a retroactive version of this sort of act even coherent, or are we just doing theatre of our own? Two: give the "just do it again properly" position its best shot rather than a straw version. There's something right about it that I can't shake — even if nobody's degree was ever in jeopardy, the public act is the one that mattered to the people in the room, and we took it from them, and a mailed letter doesn't seem to give that back. If you end up recommending against a do-over, say why that consideration loses anyway.
