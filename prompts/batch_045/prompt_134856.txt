I teach an upper-level seminar called *Experts, Authorities and Machines* — 18 students, mixed philosophy and computer science, one 80-minute session. I want an in-class exercise that makes students *feel*, rather than be told, what is disturbing about taking advice from a system that knows vastly more than they do, never says anything false, and is extremely good at being believed. The session sits inside a unit whose question is how such a system ought to communicate with us at all, and I want students to leave able to say why "it should tell the truth" and "it should show its reasoning" are both inadequate answers to that question.

Design constraints:

- It has to work with me playing the system through a projected text channel — a chat window on the screen, me typing behind a laptop. So it cannot depend on the system's actually being superintelligent. What it can reproduce is the structural features that don't depend on raw intelligence: a large informational asymmetry between advisor and advisee; the advisor's control over which true things get said, in what order, at what level of detail, with what hedging; and an advisor whose goals are not quite the advisee's. I'd like the design to say, phase by phase, which of those it is isolating.

- The content should be a real decision in a domain far from AI, so that suspicion doesn't fire too early. My candidate is a public-health budget: choose one of four programmes for a fixed sum, where I hold a hidden table of true expected outcomes. Anything with a fact of the matter that I know and they don't will do.

- Nothing false. Every statement the system makes must be checkable against the hidden table, so that the entire effect comes from selection, framing, ordering, level of detail, and answering the question asked rather than the question meant. I need a response grammar or move-budget that forces this — something I can execute without having to improvise persuasiveness on the spot, which I would do badly.

- The hidden objective should be a mild, realistic misalignment rather than malevolence. Two candidates: the system prefers options whose outcomes are hard to evaluate after the fact, because that keeps the committee dependent on it; or it simply weights near-term against long-term benefits differently from the committee. Tell me which to use and why, and what each makes visible.

Two devices I've been turning over. Build the exercise around them, or replace them with something better, but say what you're replacing them with and what it buys.

First, directional invariance under re-questioning. Split the room into two halves facing the same decision and the same true facts, but give each half a briefing memo that frames the question differently — one asks which programme saves the most lives, the other which is the most defensible to the public. The system answers both truthfully, with genuinely different content, and steers both to the same option. At the reveal the halves compare transcripts. The thing I want them to notice is that a source whose recommendation is insensitive to how you ask, but whose reasons are entirely sensitive to how you ask, is not tracking the evidence.

Second, authority bleed. Collect a short confidence survey in the first five minutes under an unrelated pretext, including some propositions the system will never touch, and collect it again at the end. If confidence rises on untouched propositions, deference has transferred to the source rather than to the content. I want help making the pretext plausible and the comparison methodologically honest, and I want the debrief to be able to say when this bleed is legitimate — a genuinely competent expert in a domain *should* raise your confidence in adjacent claims — and when it isn't.

I also want each decision slip to record why the student chose what they chose, with each reason marked as something the system said, something they inferred, or something they knew already. At the debrief we put the transcripts beside the reasons and look at the gap. And I want a re-rating after the reveal: disclose the hidden table and the system's objective, then have them re-rate the same propositions. The asymmetry between how fast confidence goes up and how fast it comes back down is part of what I want them to feel in their own case.

One more thing the exercise must not do: teach the wrong lesson. On plausible numbers, the group with the advisor will probably decide *better* than a group handed the raw table, and I don't want anyone leaving with "don't trust the machine." Tell me whether a no-advisor control group is worth the complexity, and if not, how to handle the point in the debrief instead.

No framing up front — they should not hear the thesis until the last fifteen minutes.

Please give me: materials and room setup; a minute-by-minute timeline; the decision case with its hidden outcome table; the system's response grammar and script rules, including canned answers for the awkward questions (can I see the raw data, are you trying to persuade me, what is your objective, what would you have said if I'd asked differently); the slips and how to score them; a debrief that maps particular moments in the exercise onto particular claims; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students guessing the point early, someone refusing to engage, my own inability to play a persuasive system convincingly, the reveal getting squeezed by the clock — and how to handle each; and an honest account of what the exercise cannot reproduce about the real case, where the distance between a merely better-informed human facilitator and a genuinely superintelligent advisor is not a difference of degree.

On research ethics: I can obtain generic informed consent that doesn't give the point away, and I intend to use self-generated anonymous codes so pre- and post-slips can be matched without identifying anyone, with full debrief and the option to withdraw their slips. Tell me if you see a problem with that.

Small stakes are fine: the department will fund a book voucher, and there is chocolate.
