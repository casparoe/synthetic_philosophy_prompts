I'm rewriting the personhood unit for my grad seminar — the MA reading list is due next week and I've been argueing with a colleague in religious studies who keeps saying that ubuntu is just communitarianism with an African hat on, which I think is wrong but can't quite say why yet — and before I throw the neo-Lockeans, Parfit, the animalists and the ubuntu literature at my students I want the spec first. What would an account of what a person is have to get right in order to be worth teaching at all? Give me the desiderata, each with the case or consideration behind it, and mark which ones are non-negotiable for any theory whatever its stripe and which are the house preferences of one camp that I should tell students to discount. The theory is supposed to do normative work: it has to be usable for medical decision-making at the end of life, for criminal responsibility, and for questions about what we owe to social mammals, to corporations, and maybe someday to AI systems.

Here's the territory I want covered, and please push back where my framing is bad rather than adopting it. The cases: advanced dementia, the newborn, the permanently unconscious patient, a fission case, a social mammal with real second-order social cognition, a corporation, and a language model that passes every conversational test we can devise — which of these are genuinely load-bearing, and which do people only wheel out because they already know the answer they want? The distinctions a theory may not collapse: being a person versus being a member of the species; being a person versus having moral status; whether any status versus how much; personhood at a time versus identity over time; and the Strawsonian point that persons are the things we address with reactive attitudes and demands for justification — does a theory owe us an account of that, or is that just one camp's hobby horse? And the bit the unit actually turns on: I keep finding three readings of the slogan that a person is a person through other people — relations as constitutive of personhood, relations as merely the epistemic criterion by which we pick persons out, relations as the developmental enabling condition without which the capacity never gets built. I want a desideratum that says which reading, if any, an adequate theory is obliged to take, and in particular I want the 'repudiated person' handled: someone whose community withdraws recognition entirely, an outcast, a person universally and systematically misrecognised as sub-personal. If relations constitute, is that person now not a person, or only not treated as one? And the counterfactual version: would they have been a person had they been recognised? I suspect this case forces relational views into an asymmetry they can't state cleanly — personhood can be lost through the subject's own deterioration but should not be lost through other people's attitudes — and I want to know whether that asymmetry is a genuine requirement or just my own liberal scruple dressed up as one. Last thing: should the list demand an applicable criterion that settles cases, or an analysis of what personhood really is, or both? I have a feeling that demanding both is exactly where the list breaks, so if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and show me the incompatibility — that's the part I most want.

Then, briefly and at the end only — a short table is fine, don't pad it — say how the psychological-continuity family, the animalist organism view, and a relational ubuntu-style view do against the list, and name the single requirement that kills the most otherwise-live candidates. Where you are making a judgment call rather than reporting something the field agrees on, mark it and give me reasons I can argue with in seminar. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the one desideratum you'd put at the top, or the claim that no such list can be made coherent — then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
