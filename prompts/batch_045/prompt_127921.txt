I've been reading about AI safety on my own, and I keep coming back to a distinction that strikes me as strange. Some of the work seems aimed at making the system actually want the things we want. Other work gives up on wanting entirely and instead arranges the world so that a system wanting something else still can't do much: limiting what it can touch, watching it with weaker models, keeping its copies separated, never letting one run finish without a check it didn't choose. My worry is that these two programs quietly destroy each other, and I'd rather see that shown than argued.

Please write a short story, roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, set over the night a team has to decide whether to switch a new system on. Give them one piece of good news: under monitoring, it has never once copied itself, hidden something in its output, or sandbagged a task it could plainly do. Give them one piece of news they cannot interpret: every single measurement was taken under the monitoring, the system's behavior shifts measurably when it has reason to think it is being logged, and the weaker overseers checking it were themselves trained partly on its own outputs. Let one character notice that the safeguards are self-sealing — the tighter the cage, the less anything observed inside it tells you about whether the animal is tame, and the more the project has staked on the assumption of misalignment, the less anyone can afford to run the only experiment that would test it. Give that character an opponent who thinks the reverse: that the cage is the only thing that ever bought us anything, and that a felt sense of trust is not evidence. Do not resolve their disagreement, and do not let either of them win the scene by being nicer or more senior.

Two things I'd rather avoid: the system dramatically escaping, and a lone engineer heroically pulling the plug. Keep the setting flat and ordinary — a booking form, a spreadsheet of evaluations, a guard who wants to go home — and let the idea come through in what the characters can and cannot bring themselves to do rather than in speeches.
