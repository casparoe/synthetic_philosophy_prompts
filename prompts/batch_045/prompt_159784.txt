I'm giving a paper at a workshop on the neural correlates of consciousness in six weeks, and what I need now is Q&A preparation rather than another round of comments on the argument itself.

The thesis. The standard higher-order view — that a mental state is conscious only when it is the target of a higher-order thought — I take to be finished: it cannot handle misrepresentation without either making consciousness flicker or letting a confused higher-order thought conjure experience out of nothing, and it gives the wrong answer about infants and non-human animals. My replacement: the higher-order state need not be a thought or a judgment at all. What is required is a non-conceptual, non-inferential registering by the system of the reliability of its own current first-order state. Call this a confidence registration. Because it is not a belief about the world, it cannot misrepresent in the way that generates the problem of targetless higher-order states; because it requires no concepts, creatures without concepts can have it. I then make the view concrete: in a predictive-processing architecture, precision weighting on prediction error is exactly such a registration, and I claim that the implementation question that has dogged higher-order views is thereby answered rather than gestured at.

I know the cost, and I raise it myself in the paper: precision weighting is computed everywhere — early sensory cortex, cerebellum, spinal circuits — so if it suffices for consciousness the theory is wildly over-permissive. My restriction is that only precision estimates computed over a state the system represents as its own, and available to action-selection and to learning across modalities, count. That is where I am least confident, for a reason I suspect the room will find before I do: the restriction sounds like the first-order global-broadcast picture that the higher-order view was supposed to be an alternative to, and it may be in tension with the very feature that was supposed to save the infants and the animals.

The audience. About thirty people in three groups, and I expect the objections to arrive in a predictable order from each.

One: two committed predictive-processing people, one of whom works on active inference and one of whom runs psychophysics. I expect them to object that precision is a gain on prediction error rather than a representation of the reliability of a perceptual state; that what it encodes is expected volatility in the environment and not anything about the subject's own condition; that it is set by neuromodulatory systems in circuits nobody thinks are conscious; and that I am relabelling a computational parameter as a kind of awareness.

Two: a larger group of empirical researchers who use no-report paradigms. They will want to know what my view predicts that theirs does not, and I expect to be pressed on why the late prefrontal signature of a percept largely disappears in no-report versions of rivalry and inattention while the early sensory signature survives, and on what I make of the gap between what subjects can report and what the partial-report data suggest is present.

Three: several analytically trained philosophers of mind, mostly sympathetic to first-order representationalism, plus one person who thinks the explanatory project itself is confused. They will press the constitutive question — why a higher-order registration should make the first-order state conscious rather than merely accompany it — the suspicion that my view has quietly become a global workspace view with an extra step, and the methodological question of what would count as evidence for a claim of this shape at all.

What I want: a prep sheet of eight or so objections, ordered by how likely they are combined with how much damage they would do, each attributed to one of these three groups and phrased the way that group would phrase it rather than in my vocabulary. For each, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is soft. Separately, tell me what I should concede in the first two slides rather than be forced into conceding later, and give me the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Because I would rather find out now than in the room: for the two replies you think are strongest, say what finding or argument would show them to be wrong. And name the single objection that, if it lands, should make me abandon the thesis rather than hold the line — together with what would have to be true for it to land.
