I am giving the eight-minute address at my mother's celebration of life on Saturday, and I would like you to write it. For thirty-one years she ran an open Sunday lunch in a small town: whoever turned up was fed, and the room will hold about sixty people — her children and grandchildren, a dozen neighbours and former colleagues, the care-home staff who looked after her through four years of advanced Alzheimer's, and several people who loved her but had not been in touch for a long time.

Two years before her diagnosis she left a short note about the service. No talk of her watching over us, no claims about what she would have wanted for us, and — since she was tough-minded and thought a self is something you actively maintain — nothing said about her as a person once she could no longer recognise her family or hold up her end of a relationship. I intend to keep the first two requests and refuse the third, on the relational view that a person is constituted by being held in relation by others rather than by capacities exercised independently.

Make that refusal do real structural work in the speech. It cannot say she was still herself in any sense she would have accepted, and it cannot imply she died four years early; it has to be honest that the visiting, the singing, and the chair she still pulled out for visitors were one-sided and carried mostly by other people. It must also not let relationality collapse into the version critics of the view fear, where personhood is something others grant or withhold, so that anyone who drifted away, or who sits estranged in that room, is quietly told they are less of a person. Around nine hundred words, warm rather than lecturely, and intelligible to a room that is almost entirely not philosophers.
