I am assembling the grading rubric for a third-year seminar on the morality of consent, and I need something finer-grained than the valid/invalid verdicts the literature tends to hand out. Below are ten cases, all invented for teaching purposes. I would like you to give me two numbers between 0 and 100 for each one.

The first number measures transformative force: the difference the consent makes to the deontic status of the act — its power to turn what would otherwise be a rights-violation into a permitted act. Score 100 where the consent does everything a fully valid consent does and 0 where it does nothing at all, so that proceeding on it is no better than never having asked. Intermediate scores should track how much work the consent still does, not merely how badly it falls short.

The second number measures the recipient's blameworthiness if, knowing the facts as I have given them, they go ahead. Here 100 is the blameworthiness of deliberately proceeding without asking at all, and 0 is none at all. These two numbers need not move together: a defect that fully nullifies may be nobody's fault, and a defect the agent deliberately created may be trivially small. I am interested in exactly that gap.

For each case, please also attach one mechanism label: (i) informational — she agreed under a false or incomplete picture of what she was agreeing to; (ii) volitional — her options had been narrowed by someone's proposal or threat; (iii) capacity — something about her state impaired the relevant exercise of agency, even if it stops short of clinical incompetence; (iv) scope — the consent she gave did not cover the act actually performed; (v) none — the consent is in good order as consents go, and whatever is wrong with the transaction lies elsewhere. Where a case could honestly carry two labels, pick the one doing the work in your score and mark it accordingly.

Assume throughout that everyone is an adult, competent unless the case says otherwise, and a stranger to everyone else unless a relationship is specified; set aside jurisdiction-specific legal doctrine and assess the cases as a moral philosopher would.

**1. Withheld assistance.** Dev can push Priya's car out of the path of an approaching flood in thirty seconds, at no cost or risk to himself. Knowing she would pay for this, he tells her he will do it only if she agrees to pay him $200. She agrees.

**2. Licensed exposure.** A photographer has lawfully taken photographs of M's public drunkenness. He tells M he will sell them to a tabloid unless she consents, free of charge, to his using her image in a commercial campaign. M consents.

**3. Blackmail with true facts.** N discovers a genuine, minor regulatory violation that O committed, and which would cost O a small fine. N tells O he will report it unless O signs over a valuable asset. O signs.

**4. The standard script.** A surgeon tells a patient that the risk of a serious complication is "around one in fifty." That is both the population average and the modal figure, and it is the line the hospital issues to everyone. Given this patient's known anatomy, which the surgeon has on file, the risk is closer to one in five. Nothing else is misdescribed, and the patient consents.

**5. Cheap silence.** A landlord knows that the building's boiler is to be replaced in three weeks, which will leave the flat without heating for three days. The tenant asks directly whether there is anything she should know about the building. Disclosure would cost the landlord nothing except the risk of losing the tenancy, so he says nothing. She signs.

**6. The substitute.** A patient consents to a procedure to be performed by a named surgeon. It is in fact performed by a different surgeon of equal skill, using the same technique, and the consent form includes the clause "or another suitably qualified colleague." The patient had not read that clause.

**7. Forgotten addendum.** A research participant consents to "a single blood sample of up to 10 ml." Once she is sedated, the team takes 40 ml, because they wished to run an additional assay they had not mentioned and had no time to re-consent for. There is no additional medical risk.

**8. Unjust background.** An employer offers work at three times the local wage, with eighty-hour weeks and poor safety conditions. The alternative is unemployment and hunger. The employer neither created nor endorses the background conditions that make the offer attractive, and pays above the local market. The worker signs.

**9. Twenty-two hours awake.** After a full day and night without sleep, a woman agrees to waive a valid legal claim for a modest sum. She is not clinically incapacitated, nothing false has been said to her, and she understands the terms. The next day, rested, she regrets it.

**10. Anticipated consent.** R breaks into S's empty cabin to shelter from a storm, causing minor damage. S is away and cannot be reached. Had she been asked with full knowledge of the circumstances, she would have refused — she has a standing objection to anyone staying there — but on learning what happened she is genuinely glad she was overruled.

Deliver the two scores as a single table, one row per case, in the order given, with no more than two sentences of justification in the row itself; put any argument you want to make underneath. Then sort the ten into four tiers — clean, mildly defective, seriously defective, void — using the first score only, and tell me where the boundary between adjacent tiers is doing real work rather than marking an arbitrary cut. Flag any case where the second score sits more than twenty-five points away from the first, and say what explains the divergence. Finally, tell me which single distinction among those you used carries the most weight in your ordering, which of the ten cases you would expect professional philosophers to disagree about most sharply, and where a factual detail I have left unspecified would change your number. If you think two cases genuinely deserve the same score, give me the tie rather than manufacturing precision.
