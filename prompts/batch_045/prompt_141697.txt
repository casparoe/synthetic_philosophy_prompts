I teach a first-year seminar on rules, games, and self-reference, and the unit on self-amending systems always goes badly:students read entrenchment clauses as merely very strong rules, and they can't see why a constitution that specifies how to amend itself is doing anything stranger than a club that specifies how to elect its treasurer. This year I want to open the unit with a short story instead of a handout — something that makes them feel the vertigo before anyone names it. Please write that story (roughly 1,500–2,000 words, with a title). It should be self-contained: no character should lecture, no narrator should explain, and no paradox should be named or attributed to any philosopher. The students should still be arguing about it afterwards.

Setting and situation, which I've roughed out and you should feel free to sharpen: the generation ship *Perpetua*, 118 years into a 300-year voyage, six hundred people aboard, governed by a Charter of about forty rules that is administered by an old deterministic system called the Register — literal-minded, non-sapient, and capable of refusing to act when it cannot compute an answer. The relevant bits of my invented Charter: Rule 1 says any rule may be adopted or repealed by a two-thirds vote of the Assembly, except that Rule 1 itself may be amended only by unanimous vote together with the written assent of the Keeper of the Charter; Rule 0, added decades later and numbered zero on purpose, says that where two rules conflict the later-numbered rule prevails; Rule 2 says the Register will execute no rule the Keeper has not certified as part of the authoritative text; Rule 19 fixes the allocation of protein, power and water; and Rule 31 says that no rule concerning life-support allocation may be amended except unanimously. The Keeper is Idris Vane, old, meticulous, and convinced he is holding something together. His apprentice — your protagonist — is Nell Iyer, nineteen, who copies the Charter by hand because Idris thinks a clerk should know its shape. The engineer Sol Ferrer leads a faction with a comfortable two-thirds and never a unanimity, arguing that the old allocation formula was written for different hydroponics yields and now quietly starves the night shift.

The plot I want dramatized, in scenes and dialogue rather than summary, is an escalation with at least four beats. First: Nell notices that Rule 31 protects Rule 19 but says nothing about itself — that it is what I'd call an *other-embracing* entrenchment, repealable by the ordinary two-thirds vote — so the faction simply repeals Rule 31 and prepares to rewrite Rule 19, and Idris refuses to certify the repeal on the ground that a repeal whose only purpose and only effect is to make an entrenched rule amendable is itself an amendment of that rule, and that one cannot do indirectly what one cannot do directly. Second: the faction stops trying to touch Rule 19 and instead adopts a free-standing Rule 42 that repeals or alters not one word of it but overrides its application, and the argument shifts to whether the Charter regulates texts or effects. Third: Idris answers with a counter-rule providing that no rule may override a rule protected by a unanimity requirement unless adopted unanimously — and here I want someone to notice the asymmetry with Rule 1, which protects *itself*, and to ask out loud what would happen if the Assembly unanimously amended Rule 1 so as to delete its own unanimity requirement: whether that amendment validates itself, and whether a self-embracing clause is coherent in a way an other-embracing one is not. Fourth: the faction flips the priority rule itself, adopting a rule saying that where rules conflict the earlier prevails — at which point Rule 0 and the new rule each defeat the other, the resolution of the whole dispute depends on which of them governs, the Register returns something like INDETERMINATE, and because certification is a precondition of execution and no one can certify a text whose content is unsettled, the system freezes in the middle of a rationing cycle. With the Register down, the story has to become about people rather than rules: Sol's faction begins implementing the new allocation by hand, Idris has to decide whether to certify a repair that could only be authorized by the procedure it is meant to fix, and Nell finds Idris's private notebook — his list, which he calls the residue, of the things the Charter presupposes but never enacts: that the Assembly will meet, that votes are counted honestly, that the Keeper's signature is really his, that a certified text obliges anyone at all.

What I care about, underneath the plot, is that the story makes the students feel these problems rather than being told them: the difference between textual amendment and indirect or implied override; whether an entrenchment that does not protect itself was ever real; whether the regress of conflict-of-conflict rules is a defect in this Charter or in any system of rules; and above all what the deadlock actually shows — that the Charter has lapsed and anything is now permitted, or that it was never the rules that obligated anyone, only the crew's continuing willingness to go on as if they did. End with a concrete decision and a real cost rather than a resolution of these questions: Nell should do something, and it should be something the surviving rules neither clearly authorize nor clearly forbid. Avoid the obvious genre escapes — no discovered secret rule that settles everything, no revelation that the Register was sentient, no last-minute unanimous vote; and keep the prose plain and close to the present, with at least one scene set in the room during an actual vote, since I want students to see how ordinary the machinery looks right up to the moment it comes apart.
