I write literary fiction, and I'm finishing a novel set in a port town whose container terminal is automated over the course of about twenty years. One of the point-of-view characters is a self-taught former crane driver who runs an informal reading group in the union hall, and the scene the book builds toward is a speech he gives against accepting a severance-and-retraining package. I want that speech to be the sort of thing a genuinely intelligent autodidact would say — someone who has absorbed a lot of Marx second-hand, through essays and arguments rather than through the texts — rather than either a seminar paper or a slogan. So I've been reading around, and a friend who teaches economic history pressed on me an essay from a literary magazine that summarises Marx on machinery in a way I'd like to steal from. I've pasted the relevant paragraph below; I don't trust my own judgement about it, because I can't tell the difference between a real simplification and a giveaway error, and I'd rather cut a paragraph than have a Marxist reader close the book in irritation. (My own half-memory, which may be backwards, is that Marx somewhere insists the worker is paid the full value of what he sells, so the "cheated on wages" line feels off — but I can't reconstruct why.)

The passage: "Marx's basic charge is simple: capitalists pay workers less than their labour is actually worth, and the difference — surplus value, roughly the gap between the price a commodity fetches and the wages of the people who made it — is what the owner pockets. Machinery is how that squeeze gets worse, because a machine lets one man do the work of ten, so the other nine are thrown out onto the street, and that permanent pool of the jobless is what Marx called the reserve army of labour: it holds everyone's wages down, and it exists because of the machines themselves. He also thought machinery was a weapon in the class struggle, used deliberately to break the resistance of skilled workers by making them replaceable. But the deeper point is in the rough notebooks he wrote in the late 1850s and never published, the ones that only appeared in print decades after his death: there he argues that as knowledge and science become the real productive force of society — his phrase is the general intellect — labour time stops being the source of wealth, so the more efficient production becomes, the less profit there is to be made, and capitalism destroys itself by its own success, which is why he expected automation to set us free rather than to ruin us. The economics has not aged well, of course: the last century and a half of mechanisation raised living standards enormously instead of immiserating anyone, so the prediction was refuted even if the prose was stirring."
