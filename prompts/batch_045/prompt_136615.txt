I'm in a tiny reading group — three development economists, two anthropologists, and me (I used to work on a municipal digitization project in a mid-sized city, and I got dragged in because I kept going on about how the permit office there ran on what everyone locally called "the arrangement") — and we've been arguing about corruption for about a year without anybody moving. What's maddening is that we agree on nearly all the concrete stuff. Everyone accepts that the clerk who expects a small payment to move a file, the mayor who hires his cousin's firm, the politician who shows up at funerals with envelopes, and the legislator who steers a contract to a donor can each be found in a poor country and a rich one. Everyone grants that the ethnography is right: participants usually describe these transfers in the vocabulary of gifts, obligation, loyalty and relationship rather than purchase. Everyone agrees that the standard definitions — deviation from formal public-office rules, the principal-agent betrayal version, the public-interest-or-harm version — each sweep in and leave out different cases, and everyone agrees that the "does it grease or sand the wheels" empirical literature hasn't settled anything. And still: the economists keep saying the practice is corruption and we should price its cost, and the anthropologists keep saying that calling it corruption smuggles in a very particular, fairly recent ideal of an impersonal bureacracy and treats relationships as if they were failed markets. Neither camp has shifted in twelve months.

My own hunch, and I want to know whether it's actually doing any work, is that the word is doing two different jobs in the two literatures: a diagnostic job (picking out a mechanism that helps explain why some states deliver services and others don't) and a condemnatory one (identifying something that ought to stop and somebody who can be blamed), and that most of our heat comes from sliding between those uses without noticing. But I have a competing hunch, which is that underneath there's a genuinely unsettled empirical question about whether clientelist distribution under conditions of real scarcity mostly buys loyalty at public expense or mostly functions as a non-price rationing mechanism for goods nobody was owed in the first place — and that we haven't settled it because the evidence each side would accept is the evidence the other side can't produce, perception indices on one hand and village-level ethnography on the other. Two probes I invented myself and haven't seen anywhere, so treat them as tools rather than sources. First, the "registry test": imagine the very same transfer made fully public — amount, payer, recipient, entered in a ledger anyone can read — and still buying priority. Anyone who thinks the wrong of corruption is secrecy or betrayed trust should say this is now fine; anyone who thinks the wrong is the distortion of an impartial allocation should say it's still corruption. Second, the "queue test": a good that is genuinely scarce, so that not everyone entitled to it can have it — a bed in an overcrowded ward, a seat in an oversubscribed school — allocated by who you know rather than by lottery or price. If no rule specifies who gets it, has anything been corrupted, or is that just politics?

So what kind of stalemate is this? A verbal dispute wearing a substantive costume, a real normative disagreement about whether impartiality is the right benchmark, a fight about which cases even count as data, two questions sharing one name, or bedrock intuitions about impartiality and loyalty that neither side can argue for? Diagnose it using what each camp actually says rather than surveying the field, because I've read the surveys and they havent helped. Tell me whether either of my hunches is right or whether I've just found a polite way to declare everyone correct, and be straight with me about what would count as progress here — a distinction, a different unit of analysis, a study design someone could actually run, a change of question — or whether this is a case where no progress should be expected, and why.
