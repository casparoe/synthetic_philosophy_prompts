I've been going back through an old comment thread on the whole heterogeneity objection to hedonism — you know the one: what on earth do the pleasure of a cold beer on a hot day, the pleasure of finally seeing why a proof has to work, the pleasure of a back rub, and the pleasure of watching a rival screw up have in common? Two replies in the thread struck me as actually doing work instead of just asserting that there must be something, and I want them compared properly, not listed side by side. Call the first the adverbial reply: pleasure isn't an extra ingredient or a special feeling-piece sitting alongside the taste of the beer and the shape of the proof, it's a way or manner in which you're undergoing whatever you're undergoing — the analogy one commenter used was that 'gracefully' applies to both dancing and running without naming any shared sensation, so 'pleasurably' can apply to wildly different experiences without naming a shared qualitative bit, and the heterogeneity objector only gets traction because they went looking for a part of experience where they should have been looking for a modification of it. Call the second the attitudinal reply: what all pleasures have in common isn't phenomenology at all but a stance you take — roughly, taking pleasure in X is being conscious, right then, of wanting X to be going on, for its own sake, in the way where the wanting is about the experience itself rather than about some further thing it gets you.

A few things I want you to actually dig into rather than gesture at. First, run both replies against these: the deep-flow case (a climber hours into a route, totally absorbed, no occurrent desire to continue and no felt glow either, who says afterward that it was one of the best experiences of her life); the itch case (poison ivy — you have an overwhelming impulse to keep scratching and it's genuinely unpleasant the whole time); the bittersweet case (you're enjoying a devastating piece of music and you actively don't want to be sad, though you want the piece to keep going); and the flip case (the beer is great when you're hot and actively gross when you're already cold, same taste, same you). Second, the worry I can't shake about the adverbial reply: if 'gracefully' in dancing and running also has to mark something real that both have, then adverbalism has just moved the bump in the carpet — either 'pleasurably' tracks some common feature of the experience, in which case we're back to hunting for it, or it doesn't, and the view starts to look empty, like it's just relabeling 'this is pleasant' as 'this is undergone pleasurably.' Does that bite, and does the attitudinal reply have a mirror-image version of it? Third, one guy in the thread insisted both replies miss a third option: what unifies pleasures is neither a quality nor a stance but a downstream functional role — they're the states that get registered on one and the same 'keep going / do that again' signal, so the unity is at the level of what the state does, not what it's like or what you want. Compare that to the other two: is it genuinely a different way out, or has it quietly changed the subject from pleasure to reward?

Last thing, since this is hedonism we're talking about: whichever feature is supposed to unify pleasures is also the feature that's supposed to be the good-making one, so I care a lot about whether the attitudinal reply quietly collapses into something closer to a desire-satisfaction view (bug or feature?), and about which of the three has the easiest time with the flip case. Please don't just conclude that they're all capturing part of the truth — tell me which one you think is actually best positioned to answer the heterogeneity objection, where the strongest one is genuinely weaker than a rival, and what, if anything, all three of them are leaving out. I'm a longtime lurker, not a philosopher, so feel free to be concrete and to tell me if I've set the options up wrong.
