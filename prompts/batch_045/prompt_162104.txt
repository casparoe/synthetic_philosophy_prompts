I've somehow ended up as the person who settles arguments about probability at work — we run a small prediction market on clinical trial readouts, and I wrote the internal note saying traders whose prices aren't probabalistically coherent should get flagged. My argument was basically the Dutch book one: if your credences violate the axioms, there's a package of bets that guarentees you a loss, and that isn't just bad luck, it means something is wrong with you as a reasoner. A couple of colleagues pushed back and it's shaken me a bit: they say a guaranteed loss is a practical defect, not an epistemic one, and that in our market specifically traders can mostly only take one side of a bet, stakes are capped, half of them post ranges instead of point prices, and whoever is making the book usually knows more than they do. So: what would count against the claim that Dutch book arguments vindicate probabilism as a genuine requirement of rationality? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a defender of the argument would concede as counting against it rather than dismiss — the one-sided-menu and capped-stakes points especially, since I can probably test those on our own data. Does the diachronic version, the one meant to force conditionalization, have extra exposure, or just the same exposure again? And if the argument only survives once you assume a full betting menu, linear utility in money, and a bookie with no informational advantage, has it been qualified into something unfalsifiable — and if so, is that fatal or fine? The thing that would move me most is a case where someone is clearly a bad reasoner but unexploitable, or clearly fine as a reasoner and still guaranteed to lose.
