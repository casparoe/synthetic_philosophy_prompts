Hey — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training past a couple of MOOCs, and I've been donating about 10% of my salary for a few years on the strength of the do-the-most-good-per-dollar idea. A friend of mine (philosophy minor, now a lawyer, annoyingly good at this stuff) walked me through the argument below and I've been chewing on it for a week and can't find where it breaks.

(1) What you ought to do depends on the difference your action makes — compare what happens if you do it against what would have happened if you didn't.
(2) So the right place to put money is wherever the next dollar does the most good.
(3) Any number you have for good-per-dollar comes from evidence, and evidence is way easier to collect for some kinds of effect than others: short-run, local, countable, randomized-trial-able effects versus long-run, dispersed, systemic ones. This is not a claim about which interventions are actually better.
(4) So the measured numbers systematically tilt toward the measurable kind of intervention and away from the systemic kind, regardless of which one really does more good. (This is the thing people call measurability bias, and it's the bit I find hardest to argue with — it feels like basic garbage-in-garbage-out.)
(5) If the inputs to a ranking are distorted in a known direction, then coming out on top of that ranking isn't evidence that you're actually on top. So the measured best option gives you no reason to think it's the best option.
(6) And you can't repair this by adjusting the numbers, because any adjustment has to be estimated from the same lopsided evidence base you're trying to correct — you'd be using the biased instrument to calibrate itself.
(7) You can't duck it by refusing to rank and just picking something robustly good either, since deciding what counts as robust is itself a judgment made on that same skewed evidence.
(8) Nothing in (3) through (7) is specific to donating. Career choice, voting, whether to start a nonprofit, whether to have kids and how to raise them — every one of those needs you to estimate consequences under exactly the same distortion.
(9) So for basically any decision in front of you, you have no reason to think the option that looks best is best.
(10) That can't be right. Some comparisons are fine: money on bednets versus money in a bonfire, a job at a malaria org versus a career of deliberately infecting people with malaria. Whatever is wrong with this argument, it isn't that.

Which step is the bug? I'm attached to (1) and to the bit in (10), so if something has to go I'd rather it go in the middle. But I specifically want three things: what makes the broken step look like a legal inference when it isn't (my instinct is that it's some kind of scope or quantifier slip — the argument sneaks from can't rank precisely to can't rank at all — but I can't locate the exact seam); what each of the other candidate rejections would cost me; and whether the measurability critique survives in any useful form once the bug is fixed, or whether it turns out to apply to a much narrower class of comparisons than people deploy it against.

Second thing, and this is why I'm more suspicious rather than less: she has a second version that doesn't mention measurability at all and lands in the same place.

(a) The outcomes that movement tries to buy — disease burden, poverty — are produced by institutions and political economy, not by marginal donations.
(b) So a single donation is like a single vote: the probability it changes any outcome is approximately zero.
(c) If the expected difference your action makes is approximately zero, it doesn't matter what you do with the money.
(d) But the same holds for every individual's contribution to institutional change — protests, research careers, organizing — since nobody is pivotal on their own.
(e) So nobody has any consequentialist reason to do anything.

Two arguments with totally different middles arriving at the same absurd conclusion makes me think the bug is in something they share, not in (3)-(7) or (a)-(d). Is it (1)? If so, what replaces it — is there a nearby principle that still supports the ranking stuff without generating this, or does the whole consequentialist framing of giving come apart? And concretely: I've organised my career around earning to give. I don't need reassurance, I want to know what the cleaned-up version of the argument actually supports and what it doesn't, so I can tell whether my plan rests on something the argument can't touch or on something it quietly ate.
