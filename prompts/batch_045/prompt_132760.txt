I sit in on a reading group that has been working through Kripke's book on rules and private language, and last week two members — call them Marta and Nils — got stuck arguing about whether Kripke's rule-following skeptic has a real argument at all. They asked me to referee and I genuinely could not tell who was ahead, because each of them seemed to me to concede something important at exactly the point where the other seemed weakest. Their exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it:

(1) **Marta:** The challenge is manufactured. The skeptic asks what fact about me makes it the case that by "plus" I meant addition rather than quus (the function that agrees with addition so long as both arguments are below 57 and returns 5 otherwise). But the natural answer — that I was trained to add, that my teachers corrected me as they did, that our practice is what it is — is brushed aside not because it is false but because it is not the right *kind* of thing. Meaning is use. Nothing lying behind the practice was ever on offer, so demanding a further "meaning-constituting fact" is like demanding to know what fact makes a game of chess the game of chess, over and above people's playing it as they do.

(2) **Nils:** That is too quick. The skeptic's demand is not for a spooky extra fact; it is the ordinary demand that my word have a determinate application to cases I have not yet encountered. And the dispositional answer really does fail to meet it, for the simple reason that my dispositions, and my community's, run out before the numbers do. Facts about what I am disposed to say cannot determine a value for sums no human will ever compute. So the candidate fact does not even cover the cases under dispute.

(3) **Marta:** The finitude worry is aimed at a version of dispositionalism nobody should hold. A dispositionalist will define meaning in terms of how I would respond under ideal conditions, or in terms of the rule I would acknowledge on reflection, and idealized dispositions can outrun my actual ones. Anyway, a contingent limitation on creatures like us cannot be the source of a conceptual paradox. What the skeptic really leans on is the normativity point: that no fact about what I am disposed to say could constitute a fact about what I *ought* to say. But that just writes into the notion of meaning a requirement that no naturalistic account could ever meet. Withdraw the requirement and the paradox evaporates.

(4) **Nils:** Two problems. First, mistakes. Any dispositional account must select, from among my actual and counterfactual responses, the ones that manifest my meaning as opposed to my carelessness, fatigue or inattention; and selecting those requires knowing in advance which responses are the correct ones — which is what dispositions were supposed to explain. Idealizing the conditions merely relocates the problem to the specification of "ideal." Second, the finitude point is not really about finitude. Define a variant of quus — call it "plink": for any pair of numbers m, n, plink returns m + n if m + n is a computation I have ever performed or am disposed to perform, and 5 otherwise. If my total record of performances is finite, plink fits it exactly. And even if you inflate my dispositions to an infinite total, there are still pairs of numbers no disposition of mine reaches (numbers with more digits than there are particles in the observable universe), so a plink-style function can always be defined to agree with addition on everything I am disposed to do and diverge outside that range. No record of behaviour, however idealized, could in principle discriminate plus from the plink-style alternatives. The skeptic is not trading on a contingent weakness of ours.

(5) **Marta:** That is exactly the feature that should arouse suspicion. A challenge that is in principle insensitive to every possible difference in the evidence is not a demand for a fact; it is skepticism about the external world or about other minds in semantic costume. We do not answer the brain-in-a-vat skeptic by producing a fact; we decline the demand. And the skeptic here concedes that nothing I could ever do, say or undergo would satisfy him. So at most the argument shows that one metaphysical picture of meaning — on which meaning must be fixed by something lying behind all possible manifestation — cannot be satisfied. That is a reductio of the picture, not a discovery about meaning.

(6) **Nils:** Kripke grants much of that, which is why his own response is called a skeptical rather than a straight solution. He agrees that no fact of the matter answers the skeptic, and then asks what is left. What is left is our practice of attributing meanings to one another under conditions — agreement with the community, reliability across a suitable range of cases — and he proposes to understand meaning attributions by their assertibility conditions rather than by truth conditions. You retain everything we actually do with them; you surrender the idea that they report facts. That is not declining the skeptic's demand, it is doing something with it.

(7) **Marta:** But the skeptical solution cannot be stated in its own terms, and it generalizes itself out of business. First: if meaning attributions have no truth conditions, then the sentences that make up the solution — that there is no fact of the matter, that the skeptic's argument is sound, that attributions have assertibility conditions of such-and-such a kind — are themselves neither true nor false, so the position can be uttered but not asserted. Second, the argument is entirely general: whatever a "straight" solution proposes, the question what makes *those* words mean what they do can always be re-raised, and the same goes for the community, since we can ask what makes the community's practice the practice of meaning plus rather than plink. An argument that leaves you unable to formulate any answer is not a paradox awaiting a solution; it is a machine for producing inarticulacy.

(8) **Nils:** Both of those are weaker than they sound. Generality cuts both ways: every proposed solution, straight or skeptical, faces the re-raising question, so generality cannot be an objection specific to mine — and there are well-worked-out ways of distinguishing asserting from merely making noises that do not presuppose semantic facts, namely by appeal to the role a sentence plays in a practice. As for the community, the skeptical solution does not need the community to *determine* meaning; it uses the community to state the conditions under which attributions are warranted. Those are different claims.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the eight moves, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and give a brief justification for your rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what — if anything — both of them are missing: a distinction neither drew, an assumption they share, or a way of framing the issue that dissolves part of the dispute.
