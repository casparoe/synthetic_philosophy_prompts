I'm a high school senior doing an independent project on self-locating belief, and I've spent most of it on the Sleeping Beauty problem and the dispute between halfers and thirders. I know the standard case: one fair coin, one awakening if heads, two if tails, memory erased in between, halfers keep Beauty's credence in heads at one half and thirders put it at one third. I don't need that explained again, and I don't want any bibliography or citations, since I have no way to check them.

What I need is a variant built to order. I'm writing a short paper whose thesis is that a thirder's number is not a plain credence about the coin, but a credence indexed to a sampling scheme, and I want a case that either supports that or breaks it. Here is the specification.

Design a study with a single fair coin and two subjects, call them Anne and Bob, in which both of them are awake at the same time, have exactly the same information about the coin and about the full protocol, know that the other is enrolled in the same study, and yet on the thirder picture have to come up with different credences in heads, while on the halfer picture they have to come up with the same one. Constraints:

1. Keep it realistic: a sleep clinic or a drug trial. Amnesia from an ordinary sedative or anesthetic is fine. No clones, no teleporters, no infinitely many observers, no branching universes, nothing science-fictional.
2. One paragraph for the scenario itself.
3. Neither subject may ever receive evidence about the coin's outcome or about what day it is, beyond what the protocol gives them: no calendars, no clocks, no colored-light tricks or other cues that smuggle in the answer.
4. The subjects must be in separate rooms, and neither may ever learn, at an awakening, whether the other is awake right now. If one of them could tell, they'd learn it was a shared day and the design would collapse.
5. Each subject's credence has to be the one that matches the long-run frequency of heads among that subject's own awakenings, so neither can be written off as simply miscalibrated.
6. Everything should be checkable by hand with simple fractions.
7. The disagreement must not be stipulated or built into what the subjects are told. It has to fall out of the two views.
8. Don't manufacture the gap by just piling extra awakenings onto one outcome for everybody, and don't let either schedule depend on anything other than the coin.

After the scenario, please give me the following.

- The arithmetic: what each subject should say on each view, and the reasoning that gets you there.
- A betting check, which is the part I care most about. Suppose a bookie offers both of them, at every awakening, an actuarially fair bet on the coin: stake fifty cents, collect a dollar if you called it right. Work out what each subject does, in which direction they bet, and whether the bookie breaks even. I want to know whether each of them comes out ahead.
- Which feature of the design is doing the work, and what happens to the case if you remove it. In particular, what happens if you make the two schedules identical?
- An honest verdict on whether this is just two ordinary Sleeping Beauty problems stapled together, or whether running them side by side on one coin produces something the single-subject case doesn't have.
- The strongest available reply for a thirder. How would they argue that Anne and Bob are not really contradicting each other, and does that reply survive the betting check?

Please say which version of halfer and which version of thirder you are working with, since both names cover a family of positions, and flag any place where you think my specification cannot be met as stated.
