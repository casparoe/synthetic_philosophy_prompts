Me and my friend Sam had a two hour argument last night about whether governments should use a positive rate of pure time preference in cost-benefit analysis, and it slid into whether longtermist stuff — existential risk reduction, that whole scene — really beats spending the same money on people who are alive right now. Every time I made a move, Sam came back with the same line: the one everyone attributes to Frank Ramsey, about how weighting future enjoyment less just because it comes later is ethically indefensible, and how anyone who disagrees is just letting their imagination of the far future fail them. He used it like a full stop. I definately lost the arguement, and I think part of why is that I still can't tell what the line is even claiming.

So what I want is a menu, not a verdict. Set out the different precise theses that line could be expressing, from the trivial to the wild, and tell me for each whether it's a platitude, something substantive and defensible, or actually false — and, most importantly, whether it would do the work Sam needed it to do. He needed two things out of it: first, that the pure time preference bit of the social discount rate has to be zero (or near enough), and second, that this somehow gets you from there to longtermist spending beating near-term spending.

Some readings I want you to separate and rule on:

- The boring one: that the bare fact that something happens later is not itself a reason to count it less — temporal position isn't a reason-giving property. versus the stronger temporal-neutrality claim that even holding everything else fixed, distance in time can't count.
- The psychologising one: that the line is really a claim about us — that apparent time preference is a cognitive distortion, like weakness of will or a failure of empathy across time. Is that consistent with a view that says the social rate should track people's actual preferences? Because if people's far-future preferences are distorted, a preference-respecting view might have to correct them, and I can't tell whether that pushes the weight up or down.
- The intrapersonal-to-interpersonal slide: impatience is a vice in one person's own life, but in social choice the enjoyments belong to different people. Does any reading depend on that analogy, and does it hold?
- The which-rate confusion: pure time preference as against the whole discount rate on consumption, which people decompose into pure time preference plus the elasticity of marginal utility times the growth rate. If future people are richer, weighting their consumption less is inequality aversion over time, not time preference; and the market interest rate reflects what else the money could do. Which readings collapse those together, and does Sam's line even touch a positive rate that comes entirely from growth?
- Here's the one I thought up lying awake afterwards, and I want it taken seriously or torn apart: suppose a big chunk of what looks like a positive discount rate isn't time preference at all but the chance nobody will be around to receive the benefit — an extinction hazard rate, or more generally a probability that the beneficiary population won't exist or won't be identifiable, given that who exists later depends on what we do now. Call it the existence-conditional reading. Does Ramsey-style ethical objection reach it? And note the nasty part: if spending now lowers that hazard rate, then the effective discount rate depends on which policy you're evaluating, so you can't just plug in a number before you do the analysis. Does that make the slogan useless here, or does it make it more important?
- And separately: reasons to spend later that have nothing to do with discounting — technology gets cheaper, we learn things, option value of waiting. Which readings wrongly get presented as ruling those out?

Lay it out as a list I can send to Sam, and for each entry say plainly: platitude, defensible, or false — and whether it actually gets him his conclusion, or whether he needed extra premises he never stated. Don't just tell me which reading Ramsey himself had in mind.
