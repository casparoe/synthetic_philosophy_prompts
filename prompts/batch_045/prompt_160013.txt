I'm a backend engineer — twelve years in industry, no formal philosophy background beyond a couple of popular books I read on holiday — and I've been asked to write an internal "archaeology" document about a set of decisions made by the original team at my company back in 2013 and 2014. The one that has actually caused an argument is this: the early team built a debugging pipeline that logged full request payloads, including user email addresses and free-text message bodies, into a plaintext store readable by roughly forty engineers, with a retention window of eighteen months. This was years before GDPR existed, the company was about three months from running out of money, and the whole thing was built in a week to diagnose a bug that was costing us our largest customer. People reading the archive now react with something close to horror, and the claim that the engineers involved should have known better comes up constantly. What stops me from simply agreeing is a detail I found in the original design doc: one of the six people in the review explicitly flagged that the logs would contain personal data, that retention should be measured in weeks rather than months, and that access should be restricted. The doc was approved with that paragraph sitting in it, unchanged, and nothing was done. So it isn't true that nobody saw it coming — but it also isn't true that the room at large treated it as anything but a non-issue.

I got into a long argument about this with two friends at dinner on Sunday, and they pushed me in opposite directions. One said any write-up I produce is doomed: either I condemn the team by present-day standards, which is unfair to people who were operating under completely different incentives and information, or I "contextualize" them — but contextualizing is a choice I am making now, for an audience of current engineers, and it quietly launders the decision into something reasonable, which is its own kind of verdict. The other said the opposite: that refusing to judge across time is just politeness dressed up as sophistication, and that some things were wrong in 2013 too, regardless of what the industry norm was. I also can't shake a third thing, which is that our current logging pipeline has an open ticket about retaining more than it should, and I suspect whoever writes the archaeology document about my team in ten years will find it just as indefensible as I find this one.

The document will be maybe a page on this episode, and it's going to be read by new engineers during onboarding, so it isn't purely descriptive — it will teach people something about how to think about their own decisions. I'd be grateful for your advice on what I should actually be trying to do. In particular: is the claim that they should have known better even coherent when the people in the room disagreed with each other about it, and if a contemporaneous standard is the right thing to appeal to, whose standard counts — the majority view, or the person who got it right? And how do I write something that neither turns into a prosecution nor dissolves into an excuse, given that whatever I write will be judged by the same kind of reasoning I'm applying to them?
