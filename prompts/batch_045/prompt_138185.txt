I'm in the second year of a philosophy PhD, and a few of us have been running an informal reading group on whether philosophy makes progress. One member of the group — the one who actually works on meta-philosophy — produced an argument I cannot refute and cannot accept, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the reasoning, not a reading list. For background: one evening, mostly for amusement, I invented a scoring scheme for the group that we ended up calling the Turnover Audit. You take fifty questions that were live in a field sixty years ago and sort them into settled / still live / abandoned as confused; then you count how many of the field's live questions today were not live then. My friend's argument quotes my numbers, but I think it would go through without them. Here is the argument as he gave it.

(1) A discipline D has made cognitive progress between t1 and t2 only if, at t2, its practitioners can do something with respect to D's questions that practitioners at t1 could not do, and can do it because D at t2 possesses knowledge that D at t1 lacked.
(2) A question is central to D when answers elsewhere in D are answerable to it: you cannot tell whether some technical result in D is genuinely a result without taking some stand on the central question.
(3) So if every central question of D is unsettled at t2, and the spread of expert opinion about them has not narrowed between t1 and t2, then D has made no cognitive progress in that interval — whatever new capacities its practitioners have acquired, they are not capacities exercised on questions to which D knows the answer, and so they are not grounded in new knowledge.
(4) Philosophy's central questions — how mind stands to the physical world, whether agency is compatible with the natural order and how, what gives normative claims whatever authority they have, what it is for a thing to persist through change, what knowledge is — are unsettled now, were unsettled eighty years ago, and the spread of professional opinion about them has not narrowed. My audit sorted 31 of the fifty sampled questions as still live, 6 as settled and 13 as abandoned as confused; one member of the group argued that the live category should be larger still, because in several cases the wording has survived while what is meant by it has changed out of recognition.
(5) Therefore philosophy has made no cognitive progress: what has grown is its apparatus, its distinctions and its memory, not its stock of knowledge.
(6) Now run the same argument on fundamental physics. Its central question — what the world is fundamentally made of and how it is structured — is unsettled, and its practitioners are in one respect worse off than their predecessors a century ago: the live interpretations of the best-confirmed formalism (Copenhagen-style, many-worlds, pilot-wave, dynamical-collapse and others) have multiplied rather than dwindled, and they disagree about what there is.
(7) Therefore fundamental physics has made no cognitive progress either. And since the same will hold for any field whose foundations are contested, "makes progress" cannot be the mark of cognitive seriousness that philosophy's critics take it to be; or, taking the argument at face value, almost nothing outside closed formal systems has ever progressed.

The bind I am in is that I hold two things the argument says I cannot both hold: that philosophy has got nowhere on the big questions, and that physics obviously has got somewhere. So please tell me which step to reject and price the alternatives for me. Is there an equivocation — in "central question", in "progress", in "can do", in the move from (3) to (5) — or is the reasoning valid and one of the premises simply false? If you reject (2) or (3), say what makes a peripheral result a genuine result while the centre is unsettled, and test your answer against the cases we argued over: Russell's refutation of naive comprehension; Gettier-style counterexamples to the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief; the thirteen questions in my audit that moved from live to abandoned as confused; and one case of your own choosing from physics. If instead you reject (1), tell me what your replacement condition implies about a field everyone agrees is stagnant, so I can see what I am buying. Of the premises, the one I would least like to give up is (2), which seems to me to capture something true about how a discipline's results hang together; and I would also like to know whether my friend can salvage a restricted version of the argument — one that convicts philosophy and exonerates physics — without the restriction being question-begging. Finally, name the case your own account handles worst.

I don't need citations, bibliographies or references; the reasoning is what I am after, and I would rather you told me where the live disputes lie than handed me a confident story, so please mark clearly which parts of your answer are close to consensus among people who work on these questions and which are genuinely contested. Please write in British English.
