I build simulation software for autonomous vehicles — we generate synthetic sensor feeds, train perception models on them, and the whole job is basically worrying whether anything learnt in sim carries over to real roads. It comes up in our work #random channel constantly, and yesterday it turned into an argument I couldnt finish.

The gist of what he said: worrying whether your entire sensory stream is fake is the oldest scepticism there is, the brain in a vat is just Descartes's evil demon with a lab coat, same puzzle, same answer. And then he added that the vat version has actually been dealt with — there's an argument, I think from someone called Putnam, some time in the eighties, that the hypothesis can't even be stated truly from the inside, because the words a lifelong envatted person uses never hooked up to real brains or real vats, so their sentence comes out false and the whole worry deflates. Conclusion he drew: philosophy already closed this, the sim-vs-real hand-wringing is cute but it's an engineering problem and always was.

I dont buy the "same thing" bit but I can't say why properly. Can you audit the demon-equals-vat analogy? I want the mapping written out explicitly — what plays the demon, what plays the deception, what plays the world, and what happens to the "I", to the words I'm using, and to my memory of before — and then where the mapping breaks.

Three places I suspect it breaks, tell me if I'm onto something or talking rubbish. One: the demon is allowed to mess with me about arithmetic and logic, whereas the vat setup seems to need my reasoning to work normally, otherwise the experiment has no point. Two: Descartes wheels the demon in purely to knock everything down and then pulls it back out once he's got the bit where he decides he at least exists, whereas the vat is offered as a straight empirical possibility with a causal story, a lab, a power bill and a finite budget — which also sounds like the sort of thing that's checkable in principle, unlike an omnipotent deceiver. Three: the self-refuting move seems to hang entirely on reference. Under the demon I'm still a real person who's been talking to real people and picking things up off real tables, so my words are still anchored; under the vat story, on the face of it, they were never anchored to anything.

And here's the version I actually care about, which might be where it all comes apart. I'm not worried about brains grown in a vat that never had a body. I'm worried about the late version: you live thirty ordinary years, real childhood, real job, real words learnt in the real world, and then you get scanned and envatted, or uploaded, and from the inside nothing changes. Now the reference trick has nothing to bite on — my word for brain picked out real brains before the upload and presumably still does, so the sentence is perfectly capable of being true. But the epistemic itch is exactly the same: no check available to me settles which side of the upload I'm on. So does that style of argument only ever knock down a version of the scenario nobody was actually frightened of, and does the demon analogy get better or worse once you swap the late version in? There's also a variant where everyone gets envatted together so there's nobody left on the outside using the words either — I've no idea whether that's meant to matter.

What I'd like out of this: the correspondence table, then the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion he drew, separated from the ones that are just set dressing, then whether "philosophy closed this" survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the analogy was carrying that conclusion the whole time, what a better one would be. For my purposes, if there's something nearer to what we actually mean by a model trained in sim not being able to tell whether it's still in sim, I'd rather have that. And please don't give me a reading list — I haven't got library access and I couldn't tell a real citation from a plausible-looking one anyway.
