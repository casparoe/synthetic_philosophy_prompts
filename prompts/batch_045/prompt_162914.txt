Two friends of mine got into it at a potluck on Sunday — we'd all been to a guest lecture that afternoon on population ethics, the one structured around Benatar's asymmetry argument, and Iris and Nate spent about two hours going at it in the kitchen while the rest of us did dishes. I was mostly listening and honestly couldnt tell who was winning. Iris opened with the four claims: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even if nobody exists to enjoy that good, and the absence of pleasure isn't bad unless there's somebody for whom it's a deprivation. So for any child you might have, not creating them is better in one respect and worse in none, which means procreation can never be justified for the child's own sake — and since every real life contains some pain, bringing someone into existence is a harm even when their life goes great. Nate's first move was that claims three and four can't both do the work she needs: if "good" in the third claim is impersonal — good that there's no pain, full stop — then by symmetry it should be bad that there's no pleasure on the deserted island, and if instead it means good-for-someone then there's no subject for the third claim to attach to at all, so she's equivocating between two senses of good. Iris said there's no equivocation, the asymmetry just is the familiar gap between not harming and not benefiting: creating a child who suffers harms someone, failing to create a child who'd have been happy wrongs nobody, and everybody shares the matching reactions, antinatalist or not — we're glad nobody is suffering on some empty island, we feel no sadness that nobody there is happy, and nobody feels regret on behalf of a child they never had. Nate said those reactions are bad evidence all round: suffering is salient and we respond to victims we can picture, there's no scene of absent happiness for us to react to, and people who want a child absolutely do experience its absence as a loss — so the intuition pump is cooked. He also said the harm framing begs the question, since "creating someone who suffers harms them" already assumes the very thing the asymmetry was supposed to establish.

Then Nate pushed a case I've never heard anyone raise. Suppose there were a clean exit: a way of ending a life painlessly and instantly, with no dread beforehand, no pain during, and no one else affected. Now look at the state of affairs after somebody takes it — no pain (good, by claim three) and no pleasure (not bad, by claim four, since once they're gone there's nobody left to be deprived). That's structurally identical to never having existed, so if the asymmetry is doing the work Iris says it is, it seems to favour the clean exit for anyone whose remaining life contains any pain whatsoever, which is everyone. Iris said that misapplies it: the asymmetry governs whether to start a life, and once someone exists they've acquired interests and a stake in their own future, so dying is a harm to them in a way that never being created is a harm to nobody. Nate said that's exactly his point — the moment you lean on acquired interests and time-relative stakes you've stopped using the asymmetry and gone back to ordinary prudential weighing, where a decent remaining future counts and the pains don't automatically win; the impersonal reading that made claim three look plausible in the first place is the one that generates the pro-death result. Iris's last move was that you can have both: the asymmetry tells you which state of affairs is better, but value doesn't hand you reasons on its own, and there's a separate deontic fact that killing someone wrongs them in a way that not creating them doesn't. Nate said if that gap between value and reasons is wide enough to block the exit inference, it's wide enough to block the procreation inference too, and the asymmetry ends up morally inert.

At one point I asked whether any of this changes for a baby who'd have a short and agonizing life, and they both said no, that's the easy case, which definately didn't help me. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about what — in particular whether the equivocation charge actually lands, whether the deserted-island reactions are worth anything as evidence either way, and whether the clean exit case is a real problem for the asymmetry or whether Iris's final value-versus-reasons move defuses it? I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing: I have a feeling the real disagreement isn't about those four claims at all but I can't say what it's about instead. I'm not asking who I should agree with, just who won which bit and why.
