My flatmate and I had a massive argument last night about something kind of dumb and I cant tell if I'm the one being irrational.

Four of us share a house. There's one good room — top floor, big, its own sink — and two of us want it. Rent is identical either way and we've already agreed nobody can buy their way into it. He says: toss a coin. I say: give it to Anna, because she works from home every day and the rest of us are barely there, so she'd actually get something out of it and the rest of us would hardly notice.

His reasoning went roughly like this. Each of us is allowed to rank the options by how good they'd be for them on average, weighting each outcome by how likely it is. And if all four of us rank one option above another, the house should too. Once you accept those two things, he says, the house's ranking has to be a weighted sum of our individual expected utilities. Fine, I follow that far. Then: since the four of us are symmetric as far as any rule can see, the weights have to be equal, so the coin and just handing the room to Anna come out exactly tied — and the choice between them is therefore arbitrary. The coin isn't fairer, it's just random, and my "she'd get more out of it" is sneaking in a consideration that his framework says you're not allowed to use.

Two things bother me. I think the coin is genuinely fairer than just giving it to her, not merely tied with it. And I think "she'd get more out of it" is a real reason, not a smuggled one. Can both of those be true at once, or do I have to give one up? Where exactly does his chain of reasoning stop being forced and start being a choice he made? And what should we actually do — coin, Anna, or something else?
