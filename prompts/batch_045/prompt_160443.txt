I run a tiny reading group, me and five mates, none of us with any philosophy background, we meet in a pub and mostly get nowhere. We recently read a summary of Strawson's essay Freedom and Resentment and got completely stuck on blame: whether it needs anyone to have been able to do otherwise, whether it's about what the person's behaviour says about how they regard you, or whether it's just a useful tool for shaping behaviour and desert has nothing to do with it. One of the group insists Strawson just changed the subject. I want to settle it by finding out where everyone actually is rather than what they call themselves, so I need a sorting quiz.

What I'd like: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, and then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the actual positions — Strawson's own, the quality-of-will compatibilist one, the hard line that says if everything about a person was caused then nobody is ever blameworthy in the desert sense, the straight consequentialist view that blame is a tool for deterrence and reform, the revisionist one that says keep the practice but retune it to what it actually achieves, and the view that we can assess someone as a bad person without it following that they're to blame. No question should just ask whether you believe in free will or which label you like — every one should be a case someone could answer without knowing any of this vocabulary.

Please use these cases, because they're the ones we argued about: a friend who let you down badly out of pure thoughtlessness with no malice at all; a teenager who grew up in a violent, chaotic home and is now violent himself; someone who did you serious harm while under hypnosis or with a brain tumour pressing on the relevant bit; a person who wronged you and later had a brain injury that completely changed their personality; a five-year-old who destroys something precious to you; a wrong done to you by a company or a crowd where no single person's will is the culprit; a stranger who helped you out for a rotten reason; and blaming yourself for something you did years ago.

Build it from the logical space rather than from the list of names: give the questions and the combinations of answers they allow first, then locate the real positions in that space, and say which combinations are empty and why — incoherent, just unattractive, or simply nobody's bothered. Also flag any two leaves the quiz genuinely can't separate. For each leaf add one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

And write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered formatting beyond the questions themselves, and use British English throughout. Not sure if a tree can really be done in prose but give it a go — walk through it in the order someone would hit each fork.
