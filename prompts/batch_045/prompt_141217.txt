I am part of a small reading group — mostly graduate students in philosophy and a couple of people from an effective-altruism adjacent policy shop — that has been working slowly through the literature on tiny probabilities of astronomically large stakes. Last week we reached the case where a stranger approaches you and offers to create or spare some colossal quantity of worthwhile simulated lives — say 10 to the power of 30, or a number you would have to write as a tower of exponents — in exchange for five dollars, on pain of being ignored entirely if you decline. One member, who had been growing impatient with the whole literature, said something like: "Look, extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. The mugger's story is about as extraordinary as a claim gets, so we are entitled to set it aside," and then treated the topic as closed. I want to reopen it, because I don't think the slogan is doing a single determinate piece of work there. Among the things it might mean: a purely Bayesian truism (improbable hypotheses need proportionally larger likelihood ratios to reach the same posterior, which is just the odds form of Bayes' theorem); a norm about testimony or burden of proof rather than about credence; a requirement that evidence strength scale with the *stakes* or *leverage* of the claim rather than with its prior improbability; a complexity or specificity constraint on priors (a claim that specifies an enormous number is one of astronomically many competing claims, so it inherits a description-length penalty); a norm against *acting* on low-probability, high-magnitude considerations; or, as a related nineteenth-century formulation attributed to Laplace puts it, the thesis that "the weight of evidence for an extraordinary claim must be proportioned to its strangeness." These differ enormously, and one thing I want explained rather than assumed is that paying five dollars does not require *believing* the mugger — an expected-value argument can go through at a credence of one in a trillion trillion — so I'd like to know which readings, if any, actually deliver the dismissal my group member wanted, as opposed to merely capping credence at a level that is still decision-relevant.

To force the issue, I've been playing with two variants that I think separate the readings. In the first — call it the *humdrum mugging* — nothing remotely miraculous happens: a disgruntled data-centre technician tells you he is about to pull the power on a rack, and that the rack is running a simulation containing 10 to the power of 30 morally significant beings; the only extraordinary element is the number, since pulling a plug and running a simulation violate no known regularity whatsoever. In the second — the *scaling mugging* — the mugger supplies evidence that is extraordinary by any everyday standard: he lets you inspect, interrogate and repeatedly verify a working simulation of a million clearly sentient beings, then explains that this is a demo of a far larger system whose continued existence depends on your five dollars. Intuitively that demonstration carries an enormous Bayes factor in favour of his having *some* such capacity, yet it is nowhere near enough to make the 10-to-the-30 claim more likely than not. So the question is whether "extraordinary" is indexed to weirdness, to prior improbability, to the specificity of the claim, or to the size of the stakes it invokes, and how the required quantity of evidence is supposed to be measured — as a likelihood ratio, as a total body of testimony, as a threshold of posterior probability, or as something that has to grow with the magnitude of the claimed effect. As a final check on generality, note that another member used the very same slogan a week later to dismiss a long-term risk estimate that involved no single extraordinary event at all, only the aggregation of many ordinary mechanisms.

What I'd like is a disambiguation I can bring back to the group: a numbered menu of the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, each stated tightly enough that we could see whether it is true, false, or merely a restatement of something we already accept, and each labelled as trivial, substantive-but-defensible, contentious, or confused. For every reading, please say what "extraordinary" and "evidence" would have to denote for it to hold, whether it is a claim about belief, about assertion and burden of proof, or about action, and — this is the part I care most about — whether it actually licenses setting the original mugging aside, how it handles the humdrum and scaling variants, and whether it would equally license dismissing the mundane long-term risk estimate. If some readings collapse into each other, or if the only reading that does the real work is not really about evidence at all but about a constraint on how fast priors may decay with the size of a claimed effect, say so explicitly, and tell me which reading my group member was most plausibly reaching for and whether that reading survives scrutiny.
