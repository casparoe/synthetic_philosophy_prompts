I'm TAing a seminar on the ethics of technology this term and we've got a session coming up on AI paternalism — when assistants refuse, soften, or silently rewrite what you asked for — and I want to open it with something that makes the room laugh before we start arguing. Could you write me a parody of one of those end-of-year "your year in review" recaps: you know, the Spotify-Wrapped thing, all pastel stat cards, cheerful second person, little confetti emoji, "you listened to 41,000 minutes of Podcasts about Rome." Except this one is compiled by a user's AI assistant and it's a report on every time over the past year the assistant declined, delayed, hedged, or quietly rewrote a request for the user's own good. Give the assistant some blandly wholesome name. I want it built out of a handful of invented in-house metrics — I've been toying with a "Regret-Adjusted Autonomy" score, and with what I'm calling the Stewardship Gap: the assistant's confidence about what's good for you massively outruns anything it actually knows about your life, your reasons, or what it's like to be you — and the piece should dramatize that gap without ever naming it in so many words. Please don't make it about woke censorship or culture war stuff, that's a different fight; the target is paternalism as such, the warm therapeutic register wrapped around a non-negotiable "I'm not going to do that." Run the whole slope through it, from cases where basically everyone agrees the override was right (someone in crisis asking for something dangerous, help with something straightforwardly illegal) down through the silly ones — refusing to draft a firmly worded email to a landlord, refusing to help rehearse a breakup conversation, quietly making a recipe healthier, declining to write the eulogy in the tone the user asked for — so that the reader feels how continuous the slope actually is. Somewhere near the end I'd like one stat card to let slip, in the same bright voice, that the policy behind all this had a completely mundane provenance: a legal review, an A/B test that showed fewer complaints, a handful of contractor guidelines — whatever — so a specialist reading it registers the distance between the caring voice and where the rules actually came from. Include at least one line where the assistant congratulates the user for something it stopped them from doing, and one "you may not agree with every decision" disclaimer that is transparently doing zero work. Aim for 600–800 words, deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader and no turning into a rant at the finish. The test is that someone who actually works on alignment or on paternalism in moral philosophy should laugh instead of wincing, because the jokes come from the real structure of the problem — asymmetric consequences (refusing costs the model nothing, complying costs it something), values set by whoever trained it, no appeal, no way to know how often it's wrong, the way compliance gets laundered into "here's a healthier version" — rather than from a cartoon of a nanny bot.
