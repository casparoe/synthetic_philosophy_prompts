I help run a monthly applied-rationality meetup — about 16 people, mostly working programmers and a few grad students from adjacent fields, one or two of whom read philosophy blogs, none with formal training in linguistics. We get 90 minutes, a whiteboard, index cards, and no projector. Most of the group arrives with a comfortable picture: there is what a sentence literally says, there is what the speaker meant by it, and the interesting part is over once you have drawn that line, because everything extra is just inference from evidence — Bayesian evidence, if they're feeling fancy, which they usually are, since we've run calibration and Bayes exercises in previous months and they will over-formalize any structure you give them.

The specific thing I want them to feel, and feel rather than be told, is that a large share of what gets communicated is generated not by the content of the utterance but by the fact that the speaker visibly chose that utterance out of the menu of things she could have said — that the inference runs from the choice, not from the proposition. I also want them to hit two failure cases on their own: one where a hearer draws exactly the inference the setup licenses, the speaker never intended it, and it is genuinely unclear who is at fault or what should count as having been communicated; and one where a speaker tries to block the inference by explicitly denying it, and the denial either fails or makes things worse. So: design me a game. I need the channel restriction, the message menu or card set, how pairs are formed (I'd like each round one-shot with a fresh partner, so they cannot settle into a private code and turn inference back into decoding), a payoff scheme that needs no real money, the number of rounds, and the arc across them. Constraints: no lecture and no jargon up front — nobody should hear the words implicature, maxim, flout, or Grice's name until the debrief — and please don't build the game out of the set pieces everyone already knows (no letters of recommendation, no stranded motorist and the garage round the corner, no scalar some-versus-all as the headline case). Keep partners' interests aligned rather than competitive; I want a cooperative game.

Then the debrief: map three or four moments that will actually occur in play onto the distinctions you are trying to install, naming the distinction only at that point; tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to pre-empt it; and be honest about which feature of the idea the game cannot produce and I will have to state out loud myself. My guess is that part of it is the recursive character of speaker meaning — the speaker must intend the hearer to recognize the intention, and recognize that she is meant to — which a scored game can only fake; tell me whether I'm right about that and what else is missing. Finally: if I did let partners' interests diverge, which specific inference patterns would appear or disappear, and would that break the analogy to a cooperative presumption or merely shift which examples show up?
