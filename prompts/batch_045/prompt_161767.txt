Pretend you get to sit Leibniz down and explain modern anthropic reasoning to him. Give him the short version (he can definately follow it): there's a live dispute about what to do with the bare fact that you exist and are this particular observer. The self-sampling assumption says to reason as if you'd been randomly drawn from the observers in whatever world happens to be actual — so finding yourself alive in a big, long-lived civilization isn't surprising, and you shouldn't treat your existence as evidence for big worlds. The self-indication assumption says to reason as if you'd been drawn from all the possible observers across all the candidate hypotheses, which makes hypotheses with more observers more likely ahead of the evidence; that's what drives the presumptuous philosopher case, where a quick look at the microwave background supposedly lets you settle a trillion-to-one cosmological question even though the observation itself was nearly useless.

Then hit him with two wrinkles people actually argue about. Wrinkle one: in an infinite universe both hypotheses contain infinitely many observers, so the self-indication ratio stops making sense, and the standard patches — pick a measure, cut off at the observable horizon, shrink the reference class to beings in your exact evidential situation — all feel stipulative. Wrinkle two: a variant where the rival hypotheses don't differ in how many observers exist but in whether the extra ones are qualitatively identical to you, exact duplicates with no difference in any respect whatsoever. The count-them-up move behind self-indication seems to need those duplicates to be genuinely additional people.

So what would he say? I want you to pick a side for him and defend it: would he go in for self-indication, since it smells like his indifference-style reasoning among possibles and like his taste for plenitude, or would he say something has gone wrong much earlier — that identity of indiscernibles makes wrinkle-two incoherent and so undermines the whole idea of counting observers, or that the principle of sufficient reason shows "I am this observer rather than that one" isn't the kind of fact that admits a reason and therefore can't ground a probability at all? Write it as him, using his own machinery — possible worlds, compossibility, the best of all possible worlds, indiscernibles — not as a contemporary Bayesian with a spreadsheet. A few paragraphs, no citations or reading suggestions, and feel free to let him be slightly impatient with the entire debate.
