I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and a style-guide fight at our newsroom pushed me into a question I can't settle on my own: what rule am I actually applying when I say it's fine to put a claim in the paper and not fine to put it in a tweet, or fine for the outlet to say something that no individual reporter would be allowed to say? I've tried listing my own verdicts, going by gut, and they don't arrange themselves neatly. Checking the airline app, which I know is right maybe ninety-seven times out of a hundred, and telling my taxi driver the flight leaves from B12: fine. Telling a friend who is holding a ticket that you're not going to win, where the odds are incomparably better: not fine, and I feel it's not fine even though the number is on my side. Writing, on the word of a source I trust and cannot myself evaluate, that the replication failed: fine, even though I couldn't reconstruct a single step of the statistics. Saying the same sentence as an expert witness under oath: not fine. Our masthead saying the minister is expected to resign, where three reporters each hold a fragment and no one has the whole: fine, and sometimes obligatory; the individual reporter saying the minister will resign: not fine. A press officer reading out a statement she privately believes is false: not fine, but it registers to me as a different and lesser wrong than a friend lying to my face, and I can't articulate the difference. Telling a soaked colleague that it's raining: weird, but not a violation of anything. Asserting something true to an audience I know will hear it as licence for something false: not fine, though I suspect here I'm blaming the norm of assertion for what is really a norm about consequences. And then there is the one that feels like the hardest datum: I cannot say, even silently, the story will run tomorrow, but I don't know that it will — the thing jams before it gets out. My own candidate, which the style-guide fight produced, is a two-part rule: you may assert p only if you could answer "how do you know?" without bluffing, and the grounds that would satisfy that question in the pub must also be grounds you'd be willing to have scrutinized by whoever will act on it. I'd rather keep the replication/oath pair and the last datum than keep the app-versus-lottery asymmetry, if I have to choose.

So: is there a principle, or a short list of serious candidates, that gets this pattern or nearly all of it — knowledge, true belief, justified belief, reasonable-to-believe, something audience-relative, something stakes-relative? Please state whichever principle you favour precisely enough that it generates verdicts, tell me which clause is doing the work in each of the cases above, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon and whether the asymmetry I'm least attached to is really the one that has to go. Test my two-part rule against the cases and tell me whether it can be repaired or whether it collapses into one of the standard options. Then use whatever survives on three cases I haven't listed: an automated account of ours that posts the airport is open straight off a data feed, with no human who checked; a reporter asserting, in the paper's voice, a claim she believes on the word of a source she knows has lied in the past but not about this; and the one I keep returning to, our election-night projection, where the model puts a candidate at ninety-two per cent and someone has to decide whether we get to write that she has won.
