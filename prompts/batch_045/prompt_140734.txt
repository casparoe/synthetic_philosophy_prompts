A guy in my reading group sprang this on me and I realy can't find the hole in it, but the conclusion has to be wrong, since the whole selling point of prioritarianism is that it dodges the levelling-down objection.

(1) Prioritarianism: the value of an outcome is the sum of f(w_i), where w_i is each person's welfare (measured so that 0 is the neutral point, where a life stops being worth living) and f is increasing and strictly concave with f(0) = 0. A given benefit does more for the total the worse off the person who gets it is.
(2) The standard escape: because f is increasing, making anyone worse off with no compensating gain always lowers the total. So prioritarianism never says it's better to make people worse off and nobody better off. It keeps what's attractive about equality without caring about equality itself.
(3) But concavity plus f(0) = 0 also means the average weight per unit of welfare, f(w)/w, is higher the lower w is. So value isn't only about who gains — it's about where the existing stock of welfare sits.
(4) Fix the total stock at W and spread it evenly over n people. Value = n·f(W/n), which is just W times the average weight at level W/n.
(5) By (3), that average weight rises as W/n falls, i.e. as n rises. So for any fixed stock of welfare, more people each worse off is strictly better, with no stopping point: whatever arrangement you pick, spreading the same welfare over more people is better still.
(6) That's the levelling-down verdict back again: the world it recommends has everybody worse off and nobody better off, and the only thing that improved is that the welfare now sits with people who are badly off. Which is exactly the "equality for its own sake" thought prioritarianism was built to get rid of.
(7) So either prioritarianism collapses into the view it was invented to replace, or it needs some story about comparing different numbers of people that has nothing to do with anyone's good.

Which step do you reject? I'd much rather keep (2) than give up the escape from levelling-down — but (4) and (5) look like plain arithmetic to me, and if they're fine I don't see how (6) doesnt follow.
