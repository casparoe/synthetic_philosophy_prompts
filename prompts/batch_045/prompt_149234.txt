Hi — Im putting together the take-home for my third-year seminar on Kierkegaard and I need one question on Fear and Trembling that my students can't answer by reciting. They can all already say knight of faith, knight of infinite resignation, by virtue of the absurd, and they've all got the contrast with Agamemnon, Jephthah and Brutus down cold. I want to seperate that from actually understanding what a suspension is supposed to be.

Here's the crux I have in mind, but you make it work. Give them three candidate readings of the teleological suspension of the ethical in Problema I: (i) outweighing — the ethical keeps its full force as a reason but is beaten by a weightier duty to God, so Abraham is doing something structurally like what Agamemnon does; (ii) jurisdiction — the ethical just is the universal, the universal has no authority over the single individual who stands in an absolute relation to the absolute, so there is nothing here to weigh and no deliberation to be had; (iii) restoration — the suspension is for the sake of an end, and the end is the ethical itself, in that Abraham sacrifices while holding, by virtue of the absurd, that he will have Isaac back, so the ethical is retained as what he's aiming at rather than set aside.

Then hit them with a case I made up and have never seen discussed: Abraham rides to Moriah, gets lost through no fault of his own, and goes through with the whole thing on the wrong mountain. No angel turns up. Isaac dies. Abraham's inner state is indistinguishable from the one in the story — same certainty, same willingness, same expectation of getting the boy back. Ask whether the suspension obtains in that case, and whether what it delivers is a justification for killing Isaac, or only an excuse, or neither.

Whichever reading they defend has to be run against that case and against two constraints from the text: it has to make Abraham different in kind from the tragic heroes rather than just a man with a private reason for a public-looking act, and it has to make his silence in Problema III into something other than ordinary deception or evasion.

What I want from you: the exam question itself, written for a 90-minute in-class essay (1200 word cap, closed book, they can rely on their memory of the text and no secondary sources or quotations are required); a model answer of about 800 words that commits to one of the three readings and shows where the other two break on the wrong-mountain case, including a clean distinction between justification and excuse; and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. Make the scheme name specific moves — nothing like shows good grasp of faith — and tell me what caps a fluent paper at a B.
