I got into a three hour argument with my friend Priya last night about the 2016 AlphaGo vs Lee Sedol match, specifically two moments in it, and I need someone to settle it more carefully than either of us could at 1am. The moments, as I remember them (correct me if I've got a detail wrong, but don't build on a detail I've mangled): in game 2 AlphaGo played a shoulder hit around move 37 that the human commentators initially read as a mistake, and which later came to look like a very strong, even creative move; in game 4, after Lee Sedol's move 78 wedge, AlphaGo fell apart and played a bizarre string of losing moves for the rest of the game, and the people commentating — including strong pros — kept describing it in mentalistic terms, saying things like that AlphaGo thought it was winning, that it had gotten confused, that it was tilting. My understanding of the technical story is that AlphaGo's search was a Monte Carlo tree search guided by a policy network and evaluated by a value network, so moves that the policy network gave a very low prior to barely got explored at all, which is why a move a human finds obvious could be effectively invisible to the system, and why once its value estimates got into a bad regime its play could degrade in a way that has no analogue in a human losing their nerve. That's the design stance story, and Priya and I agree about it. What we fought about is whether the intentional stance story — AlphaGo believed it was winning, wanted to hold on to territory, didn't see the wedge coming — is just a useful shorthand for people who can't do the design stance in their heads, or whether it's actually doing real explanatory work, or whether it's actively misleading and we should stop talking that way about AI systems.

Priya's position, which she got quite attached to, is that there is a problem she calls the rationalization buffer: for any behavior at all, after the fact, you can find some assignment of beliefs and desires that makes it come out rational, so the intentional stance can never be falsified by these systems and therefore tells us nothing about them. Her stronger claim was that this is unlike the human case, where she thinks the stance is constrained by things like reaction times, reported experience, and the fact that we're all running roughly the same hardware, so the buffer is smaller. She also says the stance has what she called predictive grip without explanatory grip — it lets you guess what the system will do next, but it doesn't tell you why, and only the design stance does that. I pushed back: I said the design stance explanation of game 4 is itself a story about a system optimizing something, and that if the intentional stance is just instrumentally justified by its predictive success then Priya can't complain that it fails to explain, and also that attributing a belief to a system that never represented the relevant proposition at all (AlphaGo arguably never considered the wedge, in the sense that matters) seems like a different failure than merely getting the belief content wrong. But I couldn't get clear on whether Dennett's own framing — roughly, the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance, with the last one earning its keep when treating something as a rational agent lets you predict it — actually settles any of this, or whether it was built for systems much more like humans and simple artifacts than like a learned search process.

So: I want a two way analysis. First, take the intentional stance seriously as a tool and tell me what it says about these two moments — who or what the agent is, what its beliefs and desires would have to be for each move to make sense, what it predicts, and what it gets right that a purely structural description of the search misses. Second, tell me where it breaks: where the attribution of a belief is not just imprecise but empty, where the rationalization buffer really is doing all the work, and whether the fact that the failure mode is systematic (low probability branches, a value network that degrades) rather than a one off glitch changes the verdict. Then I want the reverse direction: does this episode confirm the intentional stance as a pragmatic tool, is it an anomaly for it, or is it a kind of system the stance was never designed for — and does any of it generalize to how we talk about today's language models, when people say a model knows or believes or is confused about something.
