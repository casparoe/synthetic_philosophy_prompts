I'm one of three people on a small committee at my old university that has to decide whether to put a slice of our (fairly small) surplus fund into a loss-and-damage style payment, and — more annoying — how to write down a number and a one-paragraph justification for it. So all this intergenerational justice and historical emissions stuff has suddenly stopped being abstract for me. Relevant detail I can't stop noticing: my grandfather worked in a coal mine and my mum's pension is apparently still partly in fossil stocks, which I'm pretty sure makes me argue worse about this.

The position I've ended up at after a few months of arguing with myself: the only version of the 'historical responsibility' argument that survives the obvious objections is the unjust-enrichment one. Not 'we caused the harm' — most people alive now didn't emit the stuff, most of the people who did had no idea what they were doing, causal chains running through a century of institutions are a mess, and plenty of the historical emitters were themselves pretty poor. But: people alive now in rich countries inherited infrastructure, capital, and a development path that was built on cheap fossil energy; they'd be worse off without it; and nobody paid for it. So the duty is a duty to hand back a benefit, not a duty to repair a harm. I think that has consequences people in this space quietly hate, and I've come to accept all four of them:

- it's capped at the size of the enrichment, and if you actually tried to estimate it I suspect it comes out much smaller than the headline numbers, because the benefits got diluted, spent, and partly destroyed by the same process that produced them
- it isn't obviously a duty to mitigate anything going forward. Paying back and stopping are different duties, and the second one has to be argued for separately, on different grounds
- it attaches to whoever is enriched, which means some people in poor countries owe something too (cheap fertiliser, cheap steel, that kind of thing), and some recent arrivals in rich countries who inherited none of it owe nothing at all
- and I've half convinced myself it sidesteps the non-identity problem entirely, because it's backward-looking and doesn't need any particular future person to have been made worse off than they'd otherwise have been

What I want from you: what would count against this? Not the standard 'but my grandad was a miner' moves I've already chewed through. I want the defeaters ranked, fatal at the top down to merely awkward, and I specifically want you to separate (a) what someone who already holds my view should concede is a real problem for it, from (b) what only a critic would say and I'd be within my rights to shrug at. If the view collapses into some rival position once you press on it, I want to know which rival and why that's bad news for me rather than good news. If there's a case it just can't describe — a situation where it gives no answer, or an answer that's clearly wrong and not just unpopular — that's the most useful single thing you could hand me. And please be honest about whether I've built something that no longer risks anything: I have have a nagging feeling that 'the enrichment is whatever it turns out to be' plus 'some people owe nothing' is exactly the kind of pair of qualifications that makes a view impossible to criticise, and I'd rather hear that from you than from my committee.

One thing about what would move me personally: I'd find an argument from what the view does to the *motive* more persuasive than a formal one. If it turns out that on my view nobody has any reason to stop emitting and every reason to just write cheques, I'll probably drop it, and you should say so if you think that's where it ends up.

At the end, tell me how confident you are in each of the main things you're claiming, and single out the one point you're least sure about.
