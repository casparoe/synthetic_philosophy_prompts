Good's theorem — the result that for a straight expected-utility maximiser, a signal you can get for free and then act on has non-negative expected value, evaluated before you look — gets wheeled out as a bit of a rationalist club: if you'd rather not know, you must be irrational, or secretly attached to your ignorance, or both.

Please argue that the theorem can't actually do that job. The thing I keep circling on is that it needs a utility function that survives the information, and some information is preference-transforming. Finding out that the twenty-year research program your whole professional identity rests on was built on fabricated data doesn't just help you pick an action; it changes which actions look worth wanting. So the earlier you, who is supposed to want the signal, and the later you, who has to live with it, aren't obviously the same agent, and the theorem looks like it has no subject left. Relatedly, whenever someone declines free information there seem to be exactly two ways to keep the theorem standing, and neither is very satisfying: either you relocate their reluctance into the payoffs (the test isn't really free, knowing makes you miserable, other people treat you differently once you know), in which case the theorem just doesn't apply to the case that was bothering us, or you relocate it into the utility function by letting utility depend on beliefs, which doesn't save the theorem at all — it breaks it, since now the ex ante value can genuinely be negative.

Make the strongest version of this you can, in a few hundred words, and flag the step where you think a defender would dig in hardest. Please don't lean on the strategic thing where information hurts you because you can't commit not to use it; I know that one already.
