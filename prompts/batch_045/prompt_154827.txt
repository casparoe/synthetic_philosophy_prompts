I'm in a small reading group that meets fortnightly on promissory obligation — no set text, we take turns bringing something in — and last week we stalled on a case I'd raised, after which a member of the group closed the discussion with "a promise is a promise." I've been turning that sentence over since, and the more I turn it the less sure I am what claim it was supposed to be.

Here are the two cases it was used on. First: a month earlier I had told a friend I would help him move flat on a Saturday. On the Friday I was offered something I cared about much more — the details don't matter — and I pulled out; I also paid, myself, for professional movers who did the job faster and better than I would have, and apologized. He was unmoved, and said I was missing the point: you cannot settle a promise the way you settle a bill. What he was owed was me turning up, not the cash equivalent of my turning up, and had he been asked in advance he would have chosen me over the movers even knowing the movers would do a better job. I said something like: if what promising does is create and protect expectations, then the movers plus the apology left him exactly as well off as if I had come, so there is nothing left over for the slogan to be about. He said "a promise is a promise." Second, from my flatmate: before she died I promised my grandmother I would visit her grave each year. Nobody knows or could find out whether I do; no expectation is frustrated; no one is let down. And yet — "a promise is a promise" — it is supposed to still bind, which he took to refute my expectations story. I've also been toying with a thought of my own, that what separates a promise from a bare statement of intention is that promising hands the promisee a switch she did not have before, the standing to release me; if that is right, then a promise I would have been released from had I asked is a different animal from one I simply break, and I'm not sure which of my two cases that helps.

So: could you lay out, as a menu I can bring back to the group, the distinct precise claims that "a promise is a promise" might be expressing, from the trivial to the radical? I have some candidates in mind but I'd rather have your list than mine: the platitudinous ones (that promises count for something; that promising is not the same speech act as predicting or warning); the substantive and arguable ones (that promissory reasons are content-independent and not to be re-derived from the balance of reasons case by case; that promising is a normative power whose exercise changes what the promisee may demand and may waive; that the duty is directed, so only the promisee can release it and only she is wronged by breach; that what is owed is the promisor's performance rather than the promised state of affairs, so substituting a better surrogate is not performance; that breach leaves a residue no compensation clears, which only acknowledgement or apology addresses; and the Humean-flavoured reading on which the norm is a device of a useful practice whose content is fixed by the point of the practice, so that a promise with no point may not bind at all); and the ones that look false or too strong (that promissory obligation admits of no overriding excuse; that the words used fix the content regardless of what was understood, of coercion, or of whether the promisee had any stake; that a promise to do something trifling binds exactly as hard as a promise to do something momentous). For each, say in a sentence or two whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false — and, the part I most want — say which readings would actually do the work my friend and my flatmate wanted the sentence to do, and which would instead support me. In particular: is there a single reading that backs both of their uses at once, or is the sentence running together two claims that can come apart, one about what I owe and one about who gets to say? Please don't bother with the legal side, or with who argued what and where — I can chase that up separately. I want the conceptual geography.
