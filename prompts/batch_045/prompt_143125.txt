For about three years a reading group I belong to has been stuck on the same question: what is going on, evaluatively, in a hard choice — the familiar kind where you must pick between two options that are better in some respects and worse in others, that feel as if they are in the same league, and where choosing either seems rationally permitted but not rationally required. We agree on every case, on which cases are hard, and on what it feels like to be in one. We do not agree on what follows. Three positions have stabilized:

First: the standard trichotomy — better, worse, equally good — is not exhaustive. There is a fourth relation, parity: the options are comparable, in the sense that you can see that neither is better, yet they are not equally good either. Hard choices are the cases where this relation holds. Its main support is a tolerance argument. If A and B are on a par, then no small improvement to A makes A better than B; but equality would be broken by any small improvement; so A and B are not equally good, and since neither is better, the trichotomy has failed.

Second: the trichotomy is exhaustive. Values are always comparable, but comparisons are often vague, imprecise, or beyond our epistemic reach, and the claim that neither option is better, worse, or equal reports our situation rather than the values. Hard choices are cases where the balance is indeterminate or unknown, and the permissibility of either option follows from that.

Third: the values genuinely underdetermine the choice, and what settles it is the agent's own commitment. The reason for choosing comes into existence with the choosing, so there is nothing here for a theory of value to discover.

At our last meeting someone produced an argument that struck us as decisive against the first position and then, on reflection, appeared not to touch it. Take its tolerance premise and iterate it. If no single small improvement to A can make A better than B, then no finite chain of small improvements can either, so an enormous improvement to A is still not better than B, which is absurd. The advocate of parity must therefore admit a boundary somewhere along the chain that cannot be located step by step. But that is exactly the signature of a vague predicate: tolerance, a boundary, and no way to find it. So the structure the parity theorist points to is the structure of vagueness, and vagueness is precisely what the second position says it is. The reply was that the resemblance is superficial. In an ordinary vague predicate there is a single ordered dimension, and the ignorance is ignorance of a cut-off on it; in a hard choice there is no common measure at all, so there is no cut-off to be ignorant of. The boundary would have to be a frontier in a space of weightings, and it is unclear what could make one weighting the correct one.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. My hypothesis is that our dispute is not really about value at all: it is a disagreement about which kinds of indeterminacy there are and about whether comparison requires a common measure, so that progress here waits on questions none of us are working on. Is that right? Is some part of the disagreement merely verbal — a fight about what better and equally good mean — rather than substantive? Are the first and second positions perhaps answering different questions, one about what an agent has reason to do and the other about what is true of the values? Are the sides relying on different starting intuitions that neither can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result about multidimensional comparison without determinate weights, a theorem about rational permissibility, something about the phenomenology of deliberation or of post-choice regret, some change of question — or should we expect none, and why?
