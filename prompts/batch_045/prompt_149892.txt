I am the writer and editor for the permanent galleries and the anniversary series at a mid-sized science museum, and I turn out somewhere between forty and eighty short texts a year: object labels of roughly seventy words, wall timelines, and the occasional eight-hundred-word web piece for an anniversary. Once a label is installed it stays up for about a decade, it gets photographed by visitors and quoted by local press, and it gets read by specialists who will notice a wrong emphasis even when every individual sentence is true. Facts are not my difficulty; our curators check those. My difficulty is the shape of the story our texts keep falling into, in which the present is the destination, the winners were always going to win, the losers are either irrational or simply absent, and anyone who came close to a current view is described as having held it. That is the family of failure historians have called Whig since Butterfield gave it a name, and I do not need the concept explained back to me, nor an argument that it matters.

What I need from you is a procedure rather than a discussion: something I can run in about fifteen minutes, alone and with no library to hand, on a draft that is otherwise finished, which catches this drift sentence by sentence and tells me how to repair it. It should be a fixed sequence of tests, each with a question I can put to my own text, a criterion for what counts as a failing answer, and a repair move specific enough that I can carry it out in one pass without rewriting the label from scratch. It must also tell me where to stop: the point at which further correction would make the text unreadable to a twelve-year-old, or would smother a seventy-word object label under three sentences of qualification, and the point at which the remaining question is one of values rather than of diagnosis — what we owe a visitor's sense that things have improved, how much credit a named benefactor expects for the framing — and therefore has to be handed to the director rather than settled by a test.

The diagnostics I have been groping towards are these, and I would like you to tighten them, split them apart where they collapse into one another, replace any that are muddled, and add whatever else belongs. First, a reversal test: read the causal claims backwards, and ask whether the ending explains the beginning at least as well as the beginning explains the ending — whether the later outcome is quietly doing the explanatory work, as in claims that a view succeeded because it was on the way to ours, or that it took so many more years before anyone saw what one person had already seen. Second, a rival-swap test: identify the alternative that was live at the time, rewrite the text with the loser winning and the winner losing, changing only names and outcomes, and ask whether the same sentences still stand up; if the same structure of explanation, the same sequence of firsts, and the same adjectives of praise and blame survive the swap, the text is a genealogy of whoever happened to win rather than an account of why they won. Third, a default test: find every construction that makes the present the unmarked case and past practice a deviation standing in need of an excuse — the constructions of the form it was not until, still believed, failed to notice, only gradually — flip the default, and ask whether the sentence collapses; if it does, it was carrying a verdict rather than a fact. Fourth, a convergence test: ask whether strands that were separate at the time are being described as already converging on one another, and separately ask about the opposite error, because I do not want labels implying that nothing accumulates at all, since some things genuinely do — measured constants, the precision of instruments, the size of catalogues — and saying so is not the failing I am trying to catch.

There are three things the procedure must leave alone, and I would like it to say explicitly why. Choosing a topic because it matters now is relevance, not Whiggism, and no label owes anyone disinterest. Saying that later workers had more to go on is fine when the claim is about the growth of a body of data, technique, or instrumentation rather than about superior rationality or virtue. And praising someone for a real achievement is not the same as making them a forerunner of us. I would rather have a test that stays silent on these than one that flags everything and is ignored by March.

Below are three drafts as they currently stand. Please treat the individual facts in them as cleared by our curators, whatever you happen to think of them, and judge only the shape. The first is an object label: In 1847, in a Vienna maternity ward, Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that doctors coming straight from the dissecting room were killing their own patients. He made them wash their hands in chlorinated lime, and the death rate collapsed. His colleagues refused to listen, and it was not until Pasteur and Lister proved that germs cause disease, decades later, that the simple truth he had already discovered became ordinary practice. The second is the opening panel of a wall timeline: From Babbage's Analytical Engine of the 1830s to the phone in your pocket, the story of computing is the story of a single idea growing clearer, that a machine can be made to follow instructions. Each generation stood on the shoulders of the last, and every dead end along the way — the punched cards, the vacuum tubes, the room-sized machines — was a step forward really, since nothing here was inevitable but almost nothing was wasted. The third is the director's favourite, already approved and already printed on the tote bags: Ada Lovelace, 1843, the first computer programmer. Working on a machine that was never built, she wrote the first algorithm meant for a computer, and she saw a hundred years before there was a machine to run it on that such a device could do more than sums, that it could compose music. Her notes lay unread for a century, because the world was not yet ready for them.

Run the procedure on all three: show me the failing sentence, the test that catches it, and the repaired sentence at label length, in the voice we actually use. One of the three verdicts has to be one I will not want to hear, and I would rather be told than flattered: if the procedure clears a draft I am sure is corrupt, or condemns a line I am proud of, say so plainly and do not soften it on the way to the repair. Then tell me what the procedure cannot do. I suspect it has nothing to say about whose names appear at all, which is a different failure and the one my colleagues actually argue about, and I want to know where it goes blind, and where it would misfire in the hands of a volunteer with less background than mine.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, in the form of a long email addressed to me and my two curators, since that is the form in which it will actually be read; number the tests in prose, as the first test and the second test, rather than listing them. Aim for something I can read in one sitting, roughly a thousand to fourteen hundred words. Please do not point me at books or articles: our library access lapsed last year and nothing you name will be readable to me, so everything you need has to come from the drafts and from the diagnostics above.
