I am teaching a graduate seminar on indicative conditionals and I would like an audit of an analogy that a colleague used to wave away a worry of mine.

Some background, so that you need not reconstruct it: last week we worked through Lewis's 1976 triviality argument. Read informally, it says that if the probability of a conditional is always to equal the corresponding conditional probability — the Equation, P(if A, B) = P(B|A) — and if the conditional is a proposition in the algebra over which the probability function is defined, so that conditionalizing keeps you inside the same space of propositions, then that demand can be met only by a degenerate probability function: one in which any two propositions of positive probability are probabilistically independent, or equivalently one in which P(if A, B) = P(B) throughout. Take the formal core as settled for the purposes of this question. What interests me is what is supposed to follow from it.

In a department colloquium I described the result as a stand-off: the truth-conditionalist reads it as a reductio of the Equation, the suppositionalist as a reductio of the assumption that conditionals express propositions, and the mathematics alone does not select between the two readings. A decision theorist in the audience replied that the stand-off is illusory and that the conditional-bet picture settles it. On his telling, my credence in 'if A, then B' is just like the fair price I would pay for a bet on B that is called off, stake returned, if A fails. There is no event there to be bet on when A is false; hence there is no proposition; hence the Equation was never under threat — it is vindicated, and Lewis's result merely confirms what the betting picture already told us. He went on to use this to argue that we can stop worrying about how conditionals embed under negation and under probability operators, those data being, in his phrase, an artifact of taking surface syntax too seriously.

I want to know how far the picture actually holds.

1. Give me the correspondence explicitly; a table is welcome. What plays the role of the unconditional bet, of the stake, of the price, of settlement, of the bookie, of coherence or Dutch bookability?

2. Where does the mapping break, and which of the breaks matter for the conclusion he wants? I have two candidates but do not trust my own list: first, that a called-off bet is a three-outcome contract and its fair price is a conditional price rather than the price of anything; second, that the step from the fair price of a bet on B given A to the probability that if A, B requires identifying a conditional bet with a bet on a conditional, which is the very point in dispute — the picture goes silent exactly where the truth-conditionalist and the suppositionalist disagree, because nothing is settled when A is false.

3. What does the analogy import that the target lacks — prices, a market, a settlement rule, an agent who cares about money, the idealizations needed to make prices read as probabilities — and which of those imports are doing real argumentative work?

4. Does the conclusion survive? Concretely: two independent fair coins, A the first lands heads, B the second lands heads. The fair price of a called-off bet on B given A is 1/2, while a bet settled on the truth-functional conditional is worth 3/4. What does the betting picture predict here, and can it account for the gap without already assuming the non-propositional conclusion? And if the bookie is permitted a settlement rule for the A-false cases — any rule that pays off somehow — is there one that preserves the Equation? Say whether Lewis's argument, as you understand it, rules this out, and under which assumption.

5. He also dismissed the embedding data. Does the analogy have anything to say about embedded conditionals — 'the probability that if A, B is high', 'it is not the case that if A, B', a conditional sitting in the antecedent of another conditional? If the prices of contracts do not embed the way propositions do, does that undercut the analogy or merely mark its limit?

6. Finally, is there a better analogy? The one I have been toying with is the premium on a contract with a void clause — a price attached to a contingent contract, not the probability of an event — or the related idea of a conditional assertion: an assertion of B that is null and void if A is false. Tell me whether either does better at carrying the non-propositional conclusion while still making sense of the fact that we talk as though conditionals had probabilities, and what each of them loses.

On epistemics, since I will put this in front of students: separate what is settled in the mathematics, given the assumptions I stated, from what is genuinely contested among people who work on conditionals. That includes whether the cases in which a speaker's confidence in a conditional appears to come apart from the corresponding conditional probability are real counterexamples to the Equation, and whether restricting the Equation to some privileged class of probability functions is a legitimate escape route or an ad hoc one. Mark your own view as yours, do not present a contested reading as the consensus, and indicate which claims you would defend in the seminar and which you would relegate to a footnote. Please write in American English.
