Okay so I'm giving the toast at my best friend's wedding tomorrow and I've just spend three hours arguing with my housemate about what I'm even allowed to say, and I need you to settle it by actually writing the thing.

Context: the groom, Daniel, is a philosopher of mind. His view — the one he's known for around our department, and the one maybe eight people in the room will have strong opinions about — is a version of consciousness-as-user-illusion. No inner theatre, no private show that only the subject has access to, no Cartesian theatre where the real experience happens and the behaviour is downstream of it. What we call experience is more like a dashboard: real, doing work, load-bearing, but not a movie playing to someone inside. His line, which he trots out at parties and which I want used or at least nodded at, is "there's nobody upstairs, but the stairs are real."

The argument I just had: my housemate says the view has zero consequences for a toast — "it's not a theory of weddings, mate" — and that I should just say the normal stuff, may the love you feel never fade, and stop overthinking. I said it does have consequences, but not the boring ones: I think the view doesn't ban ordinary feeling-talk, it just stops me from locating it behind the glass, and it absolutely cannot let the difference between a marriage that's genuinely alive and one that's just going through the motions collapse into nothing. I ended up calling that the surface-or-nowhere principle: whatever is really there between them has to show up in what they do and say and notice and keep noticing, or it isn't there at all. My housemate says that's behaviourism with a nicer name. You decide which of us is closer to right, and write accordingly — do not give me both versions and let me pick.

So: one toast, about four minutes, roughly 600 words.

- The room is maybe eighty people: mostly family and non-philosophers who've never heard the phrase user illusion, plus eight or so of Daniel's colleagues who'll catch every move and will be listening specifically for whether I've mangled his view. It has to land for both without lecturing either.
- Daniel's one real request: nothing that implies a private inner show only he or the bride can access, and nothing that sneers at the view or says it's obviously false. No souls, no destiny, no I-can-see-the-love-in-your-eyes stuff if that's doing inner-show work — but you decide where exactly that line falls and say why in the note.
- One joke at Daniel's expense, warm not cruel, that both halves of the room can enjoy and that isn't just a philosophy in-joke dressed up as a joke.
- The illusion idea has to do structural work: the toast should be shaped by what the view does and doesn't let me claim, not decorated with it. If the honest version has to give something up — the usual may-you-always-feel-this-way — then give it up and say what replaces it, don't smuggle it back in.

Please commit: pick the reading of the view you think is right, or at least the best one for this purpose, and write in that voice — no hedging, no menu of alternatives, no some-philosophers-would-say. Then a short note afterwards saying which contested claim you've bet the toast on, where you've taken liberties with the ideas, and the single strongest thing someone on the other side would say about it.
