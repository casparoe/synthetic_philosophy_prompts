I'm part of a small reading group — half philosophy, half NLP — that has spent the term on the symbol grounding problem, and one line keeps doing an enormous amount of work in our discussions: 'you can't get semantics from syntax.' It was deployed most recently in a session on whether a system trained only on text could be said to understand the words it uses. One of the NLP people used it to close the question down: whatever statistical structure such a system picks up is, she said, structure *among symbols*, and structure among symbols cannot amount to meaning, so there was nothing further to argue about. I tried the obvious human countercase — I have never been to Antarctica, most of what I know about it came out of other people's sentences, and nobody concludes on that basis that my word 'glacier' floats free of the world — and she granted it but said the disanalogy is that my vocabulary is tethered to the world through a chain that eventually terminates in somebody's perceiving and acting, whereas the model's symbols terminate in more symbols. My supervisor then suggested we stop arguing about the model and first separate two things the slogan might be about: what *fixes reference* (what makes a term be about what it is about) and what *fixes competence* (what makes a use of it correct or appropriate), and also to distinguish first-hand grounding from what she called inherited grounding — grounding passed along a causal chain from people who did have the perceptual contact. She added a case we have not resolved: suppose the chain does not terminate in a perceiver at all, because the training corpus was largely synthetic, generated by an earlier model that itself never had perceptual contact with anything. Does inherited grounding degrade by degrees as the chain lengthens, or does it collapse the moment no link in it bottoms out in perception?

So what I want is a menu of precisifications, not a verdict on the model case. Please set out the distinct claims 'you can't get semantics from syntax' could be making, ordered roughly from the near-trivial to the very strong — including, for instance, readings about what a purely formal system can achieve in isolation, readings about what is needed for a symbol to have a referent at all, readings about what is needed for a symbol to be understood by its user, and any reading on which the slogan is really a claim about causation rather than about syntax or semantics. For each reading, say briefly whether it is a platitude, a contentious but defensible thesis, or something you think is false, and say what kind of evidence would settle it. Then tell me which of those readings would actually do the work my friend needed them to do: which ones really do entail that text-only training cannot yield understanding, which ones sit equally badly with the human Antarctic case, and — the bit I care most about — which ones say anything interesting about the synthetic-corpus case, where the causal chain from the words to the world either gets very long or stops. If it helps to make the discussion concrete, consider also a system that is given a fixed, never-trained-on mapping from camera input to strings of tokens, so that it can be shown images as text without having learned from them: is that system more grounded than the plain text-trained one on each of your readings, and is the answer different for the reference question than for the competence question? I'd like something I can bring to the next session as a handout, with the readings as separate numbered items and a one-line verdict attached to each.
