I am drafting my contribution to a collected volume on pain and well-being, and before I commit the position to print I would like an audit of whether the commitments I have assembled are genuinely jointly tenable. I do not need literature pointers — the volume's bibliography is settled — and what I want is the cost accounting rather than sources.

Here is the set, as I currently hold it.

(i) The respect in which a pain is intrinsically bad for the person in it is exhausted by its felt unpleasantness. Neither the sensory-discriminative dimension (its burning or stabbing character, its location, its intensity), nor the motivational dimension (the flinch, the urge to withdraw, the wanting it to stop), nor the cognitive-evaluative dimension (its being taken as a sign of damage or threat) contributes anything to the badness beyond what it contributes to, or indicates about, the unpleasantness itself.

(ii) Pain asymbolia is the load-bearing empirical case for (i). In the classic presentation, following focal brain lesions, patients discriminate pinprick from blunt pressure and from warmth, localize the stimulus correctly, and rate its intensity on a standard clinical scale much as healthy controls do, yet they do not flinch or withdraw, show no distress, and will classify a stimulus as painful while insisting that it does not trouble them; some make light of, or laugh at, stimuli that would be agony for you or me. My verdict is that such a subject is not badly off in virtue of the pinprick, or only trivially so, and that what the lesion has removed is precisely the bad-making feature. Asymbolia is thus a dissociation of pain from badness, not the loss of pain.

(iii) A state with no aversive motivational pull is not pain at all. Pain essentially involves being disposed to withdraw, to protect the affected part, and to want the stimulus gone; what asymbolics retain is nociception plus the sensory-discriminative labelling of it, together with a verbal habit acquired before the lesion. On this view the correct description of the case is the denial of (ii): pain has been lost, not stripped of its badness.

(iv) The badness of pain is indexed: a pain is bad for the person whose pain it is, and it makes no sense to speak of the badness of a pain apart from some subject whose life it figures in. At the same time I want to be able to compare pains across persons — to say that this patient's pain is worse than that one's — and to sum such comparisons when allocating scarce analgesics, setting triage priorities, or doing cost-effectiveness work.

(v) Self-report is our epistemic gold standard for how badly off someone is in pain: if a patient rates a stimulus at the top of the clinical intensity scale and maintains the rating under questioning, we should take that at face value, and I take the widespread clinical practice of treating such ratings as the measure of suffering to be basically sound.

(vi) A pain is bad for a subject whether or not they want to be in it. The masochist's and the marathoner's pains are still, as components, bad for them, even when the episode is welcomed and, all things considered, good for them.

(vii) Pain has a moral priority that other unpleasant states lack. In a scarcity case — one dose left, two patients, one with postoperative pain and one with an episode of chronic itching or nausea that I stipulate to be exactly as unpleasant, exactly as intense, and exactly as long-lasting — I hold that relieving the pain carries substantially more weight.

(viii) Consider a hypothetical mirror image of asymbolia — so far as I know not a clinically described condition, but a coherent one — in which nociception and sensory-discriminative processing are abolished while the affective response is preserved and intense: no localizable hurt, no bodily event to withdraw from, but sustained, full-strength unpleasantness with no object. Call it hedonic dissociation without nociception. I am inclined to hold that, per unit of felt unpleasantness, a subject in this state is exactly as badly off as an ordinary pain sufferer, and that the two cases together are the cleanest available argument that pain as such does no independent moral work.

What I would like from you: go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, ordered by severity rather than listed as equals; for each genuine tension say precisely what cannot be held jointly, what the least costly repair would be, and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set. Where a pair only looks inconsistent, say so and show why the appearance is misleading. Please do not simply tell me to abandon an item — I want to see, for each candidate revision, what I would have to give up downstream, including in the clinical and allocative verdicts in (v), (vii) and (viii). If a single revision would clean up more than one tension at once, identify it and state its total cost. And if one of my items is doing no real work in the package, or is ambiguous between a version that generates a conflict and a version that does not, tell me that as well.
