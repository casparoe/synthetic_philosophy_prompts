Before I retired I spent close to forty years in experimental chemical physics, mostly on phase behaviour in mixtures, and since then I have been reading philosophy and its neighbouring formal sciences with a small group of friends. We have come to a stop on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result, and the stoppage is conceptual rather than technical.

The result as I have it: for a deterministic ordinal voting rule with at least three possible outcomes, non-dictatorship implies manipulability — there will be some profile at which some voter does better by reporting an ordering that is not her own. (Proved independently by Gibbard in 1973 and Satterthwaite in 1975.) I can follow a proof sketch. What I cannot settle is what the theorem is a fact *about*. In physics a no-go result tells you something about the world; here I cannot tell whether we have learned (i) that honest collective decision-making is essentially unavailable to us, (ii) something much narrower about the expressive poverty of a ballot that records only an ordering, discarding intensities, or (iii) something about the particular way strategy-proofness is *defined* — immunity to misreporting that holds no matter what the others report, which is a far stronger epistemic position than any actual voter occupies.

To get a grip on this I have been playing with two quantities of my own. Call the *voter's* manipulability rate the share of other voters' profiles at which a given voter, with her preferences fixed, has some profitable misreport; call the *electorate's* manipulability rate the share of complete profiles at which at least one voter has one. Gibbard–Satterthwaite says, in effect, that only dictatorial rules drive the second to zero. But my instinct, such as it is, is that the two rates behave in opposite ways as the electorate grows: for a fixed voter, profiting requires being pivotal, and pivotality gets rare; yet the chance that *someone* among many is pivotal should grow. If that is right, then a theorem stated in terms of the electorate's rate may be nearly silent about what any particular citizen faces, and much of the practical force people attribute to it may rest on a slide between the two. I would like that instinct checked, corrected, or sharpened.

Three further things bear on it, and I would like them folded in rather than treated separately. First, the escape hatches I have half-gathered from reading: randomisation (I have the impression that strategy-proof randomised rules are, again, essentially mixtures of dictatorial rules and rules confined to a fixed pair of alternatives — is that right, and does it mean chance buys nothing, or only that it buys something other than what I wanted?); restricted domains such as single-peaked preferences; a strengthening I have seen mentioned which demands that truth-telling be optimal even for an agent who will not reason contingently about what others might do; and approximate strategy-proofness in large electorates. Do any of these change the picture in kind, or only in degree, and does my two-rates distinction survive under them? Second, existence is not magnitude: what I might really care about is the expected *size* of the gain from misreporting, and it is not obvious that an ordinal ballot lets one even formulate that. Third, if what one wants is either that citizens not be asked to model other citizens, or that outcomes track intensities the ballot cannot see, then strategy-proofness may be a proxy for the desideratum rather than the desideratum itself.

I should add that I am not asking for references or a reading list: I have no library affiliation now. Please argue from the definitions themselves. I am comfortable with formal machinery; what I lack is the intuition behind it.
