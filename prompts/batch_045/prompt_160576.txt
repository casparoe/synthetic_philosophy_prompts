Hey — I need a philosopher's read on a very un-philosophical problem, and I'd rather you diagnosed what's actually going on than sent me a rubric template.

I chair our teaching standards committee (we rotate: two of five of us are replaced every year). Last spring we passed a rule saying students may use AI tools for "brainstorming and copy-editing" but not for "substantial drafting." Now we're the people who have to apply it to real cases, and it's gone about as well as you'd expect. One student fed her own rough bullet points to a chatbot paragraph by paragraph, so maybe 40% of the final prose is generated but every idea is hers. One non-native speaker ran every sentence of his own draft through a tool that also rewrote his phrasing. One generated ten outlines, picked one, discarded the rest. One dictated a messy draft and had a tool "clean it up," which changed the argument in places. None of these is a clear violation and none is clearly fine, and they're not even ordered along a single scale.

The problem isn't which way they should go. It's that every line I can write down feels arbitrary in exactly the same way: for any boundary I state, someone can produce a case that sits on the other side of it while differing from a case on this side by an amount too small to matter, and I have nothing to say about why the line is here rather than a hair to the left. And it compounds: whatever we decide gets cited to next year's committee, which is mostly new people who never saw this year's cases, so a slightly lenient call becomes the floor for a slightly more lenient one the year after.

My colleagues have three attitudes. One says there's a real answer about where the line is and we're just not in a position to find it, so pick a number, publish it, and accept that we might be off. One says there's no answer at all, it's a judgement call, and we should say honestly that we're exercising discretion rather than pretending to discover something. One says the fuzziness is the point: it lets us handle cases we never anticipated and stops students optimizing right up to the edge, so we should keep it and stop trying to sharpen it.

What I want, and what I'm definately not going to get, is all three of: a decision in every case; a written reason I can give the student that isn't just "because we said so" (our registrar insists on this); and no case coming out differently from one that differs from it imperceptibly. It feels like I can have any two and not all three, and I can't see which one to drop. Our economist suggests a fourth thing: define a band, and inside the band flip a weighted coin and tell the student honestly that's what we did. I can't tell if that's admirably honest or a collapse into laziness.

I also keep suspecting the framing itself is the mistake — that "how much help" was never the right sort of quantity to be measuring, and any rule built on a graded notion like that was doomed before we passed it. If so, what shape should a rule have instead? I have a feeling this has a name in our discipline and it isn't "policy design," and I teach this stuff, which is the embarrassing part.

I have to send the guidance document out Friday. So: what is the structure of the thing I'm stuck in, which colleague is nearest right and what exactly is wrong with the other two, and what should I actually write?
