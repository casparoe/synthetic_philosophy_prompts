I'm on the board of a big housing co-op — roughly 900 lifetime leaseholders, about 600 people on rolling six-month licences (a fair few of them have lived here fifteen years), and around 200 neighbours in an adjacent block we don't own. We're holding a binding vote next month on a buyout offer that would dissolve the co-op: leaseholders get a payout, licence-holders just get notice, and the neighbours eat two years of construction. We can't agree who votes. Our rules say "members in good standing," which dosn't settle anything.

Roughly three positions on the board: everyone affected should vote; leaseholders only; and one member insists the licence-holders are in a different category from the neighbours because they already have to obey our rules and can be evicted for breaking them, whereas the neighbours are merely affected. Adding to the mess, the charter says the franchise can only be changed by a unanimous vote of current members — so if we widen it, we do it with the votes of exactly the people who gain from the narrow version.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting, 600–700 words. I need a concrete recommendation: who votes, and what we do about the charter clause if widening the franchise can't itself be legitimately voted on by the current franchise. Give me the reasoning in plain language a board member who's never opened a political philosophy book can follow — if there's a name for the distinction we're groping at, name it, but don't lean on the label — and give the strongest version of whichever position you reject, not a strawman.
