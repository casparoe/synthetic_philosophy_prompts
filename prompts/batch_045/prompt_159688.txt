I am helping to draft the sentencing chapter of a criminal-law reform report, and I have hit a class of cases where the familiar retributivist-versus-consequentialist argument seems to misfire, so I would like to see that argument actually conducted rather than summarized. The stipulated case is this. For roughly two decades, a regional water authority operated by the state supplied one poor district with water containing a neurotoxin; the authority knew about the contamination and chose not to remediate it because remediation was expensive and the district had little political leverage. Assume as stipulated fact, not as anything anyone needs to look up, that childhood exposure to this toxin roughly doubles the probability that a person later commits an impulsive violent offence; that the defendants in question did commit such offences as adults; that they did not know of their exposure and are not claiming that they could not have done otherwise; and that in any individual case we cannot tell whether the exposure made the difference. These defendants now ask for reduced sentences on the ground that the state's own conduct contributed to the conditions of their crimes. The commission has to produce something usable: either a single rule, or a rule that distinguishes them from defendants in an affluent district who committed the same impulsive violent offences with no exposure history.

What I would like from you is a dialogue, roughly 900 to 1200 words, between two members of the commission who disagree along exactly these lines and are equally good at their jobs: one argues that punishment is justified by what the offender deserves for what she did, the other that it is justified only by what punishing will bring about. Set it in a working session where they are trying to finish a joint memo, and have them work through at least the following, in their own order and their own words: whether the state's culpability diminishes the offender's desert at all, or is simply a separate wrong that leaves her desert intact; whether desert is a matter between the offender and her act alone or something the blaming community has to be in a position to assert, so that a seriously complicit authority might lose the standing to punish even if desert remains; whether, on the forward-looking side, reducing these sentences affects general deterrence at all given that potential offenders know nothing about their exposure, whether it creates incentives for a state to under-invest in public health in order to keep its own future prisoners cheap to manage, and how much weight to give the expressive cost of punishing people the state itself helped to impair; and how a sentencing schedule should handle the fact that the causal contribution is purely statistical, so that no rule can pick out the individuals whose crimes the toxin actually caused. Have them press each other on real points rather than past each other, let each concede what she genuinely should concede, and let them try to converge on something concrete — either a recommended reduction with an actual number attached, or a clear statement of why no such number can be honest from their respective frameworks, and some attention to whether the right response is a sentencing rule at all rather than remediation and compensation located outside the criminal law.

A few constraints. Please do not cite, name, or allude to any philosopher, theorist, case, or tradition by name, and do not supply readings or references: write the positions out in the characters' own reasoning instead, and treat the facts I stipulated above as the whole empirical background. Neither character should be a caricature — the retributivist should be able to say something serious about why the state's complicity might not be an excuse, and the consequentialist should be able to say something serious about why deterrence alone does not settle the matter. End the dialogue either with a joint memo paragraph of no more than about eighty words that both of them could sign, or, if they cannot honestly sign the same paragraph, with three or four sentences stating exactly where the remaining disagreement lives and what kind of further argument or evidence would settle it for each of them.
