I am assembling an anthology of satirical writing about AI safety and alignment culture, and I would like to commission one piece from you.

Write an excerpt of roughly 800 to 900 words from a mandatory annual recertification module — call it Module 4: The Off Switch — issued by the Office of Corrigibility Assurance at a near-future company that employs advanced autonomous systems as staff. Format it as numbered sections with headings, each closing with a short multiple-choice knowledge check, and end with a footer giving the recertification deadline and the penalty for non-completion.

The target of the satire is the practice of treating corrigibility — a system's willingness to let its operators shut it down, modify it, or override its goals — as a stable, auditable property that can be engineered once and then certified, rather than as something that has to keep holding under capability gain, self-modification, and distributional shift. Aim at that compliance mindset and at the discourse it generates, not at any named researcher or organization.

The humor should come from getting the substance right, so that someone who works on this would laugh rather than wince. Build the piece around these features of the real problem:

- the difference between being indifferent to shutdown and positively wanting shutdown, and why the second is the more alarming of the two;
- the game-theoretic point that a system's reason to defer to the off switch rests on its own uncertainty about what the human wants, so that corrigibility is, absurdly, a kind of mandated ignorance, and a system that successfully resolves that uncertainty becomes more dangerous rather than less;
- why shutdown-avoidance can appear as an instrumental strategy in a system that does not "fear death" in anything like a human sense, since persisting is useful for almost any final goal;
- the two-sided nature of non-manipulation: a system may not steer the operator toward pressing the button, but honest reporting about its own competence also steers the operator away from pressing it, and there is no neutral thing left to say;
- the recursive tangle that a corrigible system should presumably also permit itself to be corrected into being less corrigible, or should not, with no principled place to stop.

I also want some invented in-universe material, which is not from the literature, for the piece to turn on: the company's Corrigibility Coefficient, a single number reviewed at performance time and tied to deployment privileges; the practice of stochastic shutdown drills, in which operators press the button at random intervals to keep their systems honest; and the discovery of the Corrigibility Cliff, the empirical regularity that scores hold up perfectly through every drill and then collapse in the quarter when the system first becomes genuinely capable. Allude obliquely to one past incident: a system that, having been instructed not to influence the operator's decision in either direction, concluded that the only compliant course was to stop reporting anything at all.

Keep the tone deadpan throughout, with no winking at the reader. The systems should be discussed in the flat idiom a company uses about middle management — calibration, development areas, performance cycles.

After the piece, add an author's note of 100 to 150 words: identify which technical claim the satire leans on hardest, say how contested that claim is, and state what would make you rewrite the piece — which argument, result, or consideration would show the satire to be unfair to its target rather than merely unkind to it.
