So a friend and I keep going back and forth on whether Obamacare's individual mandate is best understood as a Ulysses contract — a precommitment device for agents with present-biased preferences — or whether it's really just a tax on healthy people whose actual job is to keep adverse selection from eating the risk pool, with the weakness-of-will story bolted on afterwards as a nicer justification. I don't want you to just pick a side, I want the analysis run properly in both directions.

The facts as I have them (correct me if any are off, but please don't let the answer hang on them): the ACA was signed in 2010, the mandate took effect in 2014; insurers can't price on health status, so guaranteed issue and community rating mean the healthy can't be denied or charged more; the penalty was collected through the tax return, started small and topped out somewhere in the high hundreds of dollars or a couple percent of income, i.e. well below the cost of a plan; the Supreme Court upheld it as a tax in NFIB v. Sebelius in 2012; the 2017 tax law zeroed the penalty starting in 2019 while leaving the requirement standing at $0; a handful of states (New Jersey, DC, then California, Rhode Island, Vermont) put their own penalties back. And the thing I find most interesting: exchange enrollment didn't fall off a cliff when the penalty went to zero — my sense is it dipped a bit and then grew once subsidies got sweeter, roughly eleven million as of 2020. Assume the qualitative claim (no collapse) and tell me if the numbers change anything.

What I want:

1. The mapping, done carefully and explicitly. Who is the agent, what is the plan formed and then abandoned, and where exactly in time does the preference reversal sit? Is the reversal between premiums this year and coverage this year — in which case it's a within-year tradeoff and present bias bites hard — or is it the recurring "I'll sign up next year" pattern, which is the signature of naive hyperbolic discounting? Note that the architecture requires a one-shot, effortful act: find the open enrollment window, compare plans, fill in the forms. Does beta-delta predict failure precisely there, and does the mandate remove that step or merely tax failure to perform it? My own suspicion, which I want you to test rather than agree with: on the theory's own terms the mandate is a badly designed commitment device, because it piles the cost onto the exact moment where present bias bites hardest and leaves the hard action in place, where defaults or auto-enrollment would plausibly dominate. Don't just recite the nudge literature at me.

2. Diagnose the actual uninsured person before explaining them. Genuine akrasia — acting against your own all-things-considered judgment, the Augustine thing about wanting chastity but not yet — versus overconfidence about your own hazard rate, versus plain impatience (exponential discounting at a high rate), versus a liquidity constraint (you want insurance, you just don't have the premium this month), versus rational response to a weakly enforced penalty with exemptions. How would you separate these with data you could in principle have, and what does each diagnosis say the mandate is even for? I think this is the crux: if the "young invincible" is simply mistaken about their risk, the mandate isn't a self-binding device at all, it's a paternalistic correction of a false belief, which is a different argument with a different soft spot — namely why the planner knows my risk better than I do.

3. The naive/sophisticated distinction should do some work here. Sophisticated present-biased agents demand to be bound; naive ones don't, and they're the ones who most need it. So the politics should look strange: the agents who should want a binding mandate are exactly the ones who think they don't need one. Is there a mandate design that gets around this — one sold as protection against your own future self rather than as a fine — and does the actual history (penalty deliberately set too low to bite, then zeroed, with the public consistently hostile to the mandate while strongly supporting the pre-existing-condition protections the mandate exists to fund) confirm the naive-agent prediction or refute it? That polling pattern is itself a nice akratic-looking structure: wanting the end while rejecting the means, synchronically. Is that weakness of will, straightforward free-riding, or an artifact of how the two things were framed? I care which it is.

4. Then the collective version. Congress passed a mandate with a penalty too low to change much, then zeroed it while leaving the requirement in the statute. Is "the legislature acted akratically" more than a metaphor here? What would have to be true for it to be literal — do we need group intentions that are the group's own, or is the parsimonious story just agents with different discount functions plus a rule letting a later majority reopen the deal cheaply? And press on the structural point: a self-binding device the binder can unilaterally dissolve at t+1 was never really binding. Does that sink the Ulysses framing for legislation generally, or only for legislation enacted under rules that make reversal cheap?

5. Does hyperbolic discounting do any work at all in this case? Exponential discounting plus uncertainty plus the option value of waiting — with guaranteed issue, you can enroll later if you get sick, so waiting is close to free — predicts staying uninsured with no time-inconsistency anywhere. If the observable pattern is steady reluctance rather than repeated reversals of plan, then the episode is evidence against present bias as the mechanism, and the mandate is a fix for a problem the model misdescribes. How much weight do you put on that, and what would move you?

6. Finally, what the episode shows about the theory. My half-baked thought: present-bias models turned out to be much better at rationalizing the mandate after the fact than at designing it, and the coverage gains came through subsidies, defaults and employer channels rather than through anyone binding themselves. So when we reach for akrasia to explain a behavioral pattern, maybe the check is whether the intervention the akrasia story motivates is the one actually doing the work — and if it isn't, whether we have an explanation or a just-so story. Push back if that's wrong; I'd rather be corrected than agreed with.

Be disciplined about the mapping and say plainly where the theory is silent rather than making it speak. And if you think my whole framing is a category error — if calling the mandate a Ulysses contract at all is wrong — say so up front and then keep going anyway.
