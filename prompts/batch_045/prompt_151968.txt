Bit of an odd one, and sorry in advance for how fussy it is — I'm a philosophy professor (metaphysics, mostly) and I have a standing bet with a friend who's an objects conservator at a museum, and what I want from you is a number, not an essay. Here's the case. A life-size unglazed earthenware figure — call it the Figure — gets knocked off its plinth by a contractor and shatters into about forty large fragments plus a good deal of dust. Rather than glue it back together, the conservation team mills the fragments down, mixes the powder with fresh clay in roughly a 70/30 ratio of original matter to new, and re-fires a new figure from a millimetre-accurate 3D scan of the original. The result is visually and chemically near-indistinguishable from what stood there before, it goes back on the same plinth in the same gallery under the same accession number, and the catalogue entry now reads "restored". My friend says, of course, that it's the same statue. I say, just as confidently, that it's an extremely good replica that happens to contain most of the original matter. To sharpen it, I've also been posing a variant: suppose that instead of one they'd made two such figures, each with about 35% of the original matter, and installed one in London and one in Osaka.

So: what is your credence that the object now on that plinth is numerically the same statue as the object that was on it before the accident — same in the strict numerical-identity sense, not "occupies the same curatorial role" and not "looks the same to visitors"? Treat the question as resolved by whatever the true theory of material objects says, if there is such a thing; if you think there is no fact of the matter here, say so and give me that number instead. I really don't want "it depends which theory of persistence you accept" as a stopping point, and I don't want a tour of the usual menu of positions — I know the menu. I want the decomposition. Give me a term for each of: (a) whether the question is substantive at all rather than a clash of conventions about how to count objects; (b) whether an artefact can survive total disassembly in the first place, whether scattered or briefly non-existent; (c) whether persistence for artefacts tracks matter or rather form, function and history; (d) whether it does any work that most of the original clay is physically still present but rearranged; (e) how much the two-copy variant should move your number in the one-copy case. Add whatever term you think I've left out, put a number and one line of justification on each, then combine and tell me where you land.

Then the part I actually care about. Which of those terms are you least confident in, and what single consideration — an argument, a thought experiment, something empirical about how ordinary people think or talk about objects — would shift the final number furthest, and in which direction? And given that my friend sits at about 0.85 that it's the same statue and I'm at maybe 0.15, where does our disagreement most likely live: is she simply weighting continuity of matter far more heavily than I do, or are the two of us probably answering different questions without noticing? Please write the whole thing in British English — spelling, idiom, punctuation conventions, the lot.
