I'm a master's student in computer science taking a graduate seminar on AI safety and governance, and the final assignment is a short piece of satire about some method or mindset in the field. I want to write mine on the control approach: the family of safety strategies that deliberately refuse to assume the system is aligned and instead ask what protocol would still be safe if it were actively trying to subvert us. I would like your help shaping it into something a safety researcher would find fair rather than cheap.

What I have in mind is a mock internal onboarding page: a numbered FAQ for new technical staff in their first week at a lab that has adopted the control mindset everywhere, written in the bright, reassuring voice of a human-resources wiki. Around 700 to 800 words, ten to twelve numbered entries, plus a short footer of housekeeping notes. The voice should stay deadpan and never wink: nobody in the document should treat the word alignment as a punchline, and the reader should feel the absurdity through the escalating policies rather than through asides.

The comedy has to come from exaggerating features the approach really has, not invented ones. Here are the ones I want landed, in whatever order and proportion you think works best:

- the asymmetry of the evidence. A system can fail an evaluation, but it can never pass one, since any observed performance might be sandbagging, so the only permitted official verdict is that no defection was detected under the current threat model, and the system can therefore never graduate to trusted status.
- the corollary that suspiciously good behaviour is itself a warning sign, so a model that performs notably well on its safety evaluation gets flagged for further review.
- honeypots. Staff plant fake opportunities to misbehave and must convince the system that it is unobserved, which requires the evaluation environment to be indistinguishable from deployment, which requires it to be about as dangerous as deployment.
- untrusted monitoring, where the monitor is another copy of the same untrusted model, so that the lab has in effect asked the system whether the system is lying.
- information control. Employees may not reveal the date, whether the current run is an evaluation, or the system's own weights, because anything it knows it can condition its behaviour on.
- uplift. Checking the work of a stronger model requires a stronger model, each monitor needs its own monitor, and the monitoring stack has quietly grown to several dozen copies of the thing nobody trusts.
- the control tax. The measures that keep the system boxed in, such as never letting it see its own output twice, never letting two humans share full context, and decomposing every task into fragments no single person understands, are also what makes the product useless, and the document should present this as a feature.

The reductio I want to work toward is that the logic generalises past the model. If a competent deceiver looks exactly like a cooperative colleague on every metric available to us, then the same assumption must be applied to the humans: tripwired keyboards, honeypot credentials, no single employee entrusted with enough context to defect, and a footer noting that the safety case the lab publishes is itself produced by a system the lab does not trust. The final entry should land the point that a methodology capable of certifying nothing ends up certifying nothing about its own operators either.

Two constraints. Please keep it legible to a reader outside the field: gloss each piece of jargon in a clause the first time it appears, and do not assume familiarity with the literature. And avoid naming real labs, real researchers, or real papers, and skip the easy targets: no killer-robot material, and no jokes at the expense of safety work itself.
