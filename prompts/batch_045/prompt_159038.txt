I'm an MA student in philosophy, currently in a seminar on computation and rationality, and I'm trying to write a paper on whether computational hardness results can do genuine philosophical work — whether they tell us something about the world and about what an ideal agent could be, or only something about how small and slow creatures like us happen to be. What I'm missing isn't more references; I have the standard ones already. What I'm missing is the shape of the argument, so I'd be grateful for an explanation rather than a survey of who has said what. Please answer in American English, and please pitch it at this level: I've taken one course in computability and logic and one in philosophy of mind, so you can use terms like NP-completeness, polynomial-time reduction, worst-case versus average-case, and functionalism without defining them from scratch, but please don't assume I know anything beyond the elementary results.

Here is the distinction I keep circling around and can't state properly on my own. Call a computational limitation *contingent* if it is removed by giving the agent more of what it already has — more time, more memory, faster hardware, more processors working in parallel — while leaving the description of the task untouched. Call it *structural* if no amount of more-of-the-same helps, because the cost of the task grows with the size of the input in a way that outruns any resource you could plausibly scale, so that the obstacle looks like a feature of the shape of the task rather than of the agent. My working gloss is that only structural infeasibility could constrain an ideal agent or a normative theory of rationality; contingent infeasibility is, I want to say, merely a fact about us, of a piece with the fact that we can't keep seven arbitrary digits in mind at once.

I think that gloss is too quick, for two reasons I can see and presumably others I can't. First, a hardness result is a worst-case claim about a formal problem, and worst-case hardness is not typical hardness: there are problems that are computationally intractable in general but which actual solvers dispatch almost all the time, so "this is hard" seems to be a claim about a distribution over instances and a class of algorithms, not about reality. Second, there is what I've started calling *the approximation dodge*: faced with a hardness result, you quietly replace the original task with a nearby one — settle for an approximation, use a heuristic, restrict attention to instances of the kind the environment actually serves up — and then announce that the limit has been overcome. I can't tell whether that move is a legitimate re-description of what the task really was all along, or a philosophical sleight of hand that lets a theory escape every empirical check.

So here is what I'd like explained. What would have to be true for a hardness result to be philosophically load-bearing rather than merely biographical — and, concretely, how should I think about the two cases my seminar keeps returning to? The first is the objection that if some mental capacity we attribute to agents requires solving an intractable problem, a computational theory of that capacity is empirically inadequate, and the standard reply that real systems use heuristics and meet only structured instances. The second is the objection that norms like full Bayesian updating or belief-revision over an unbounded space of hypotheses violate the thought that ought implies can, and the reply that normative theories are allowed to idealize. In each case I want to know whether the limitation at issue is contingent or structural in my sense, whether my sense survives the two worries above, and where, if anywhere, the distinction simply breaks down and turns out to be a false dichotomy. A single worked example traced all the way through would help me more than a general discussion, and please be willing to tell me if the distinction I've drawn is the wrong one to be drawing.
