I serve on the Collections and Ethics Committee of the Halward Institute for Testimony, a memorial archive holding roughly 4,300 filmed survivor testimonies of the Vardøyene massacres, recorded over twenty-five years. Three linked matters come to a vote at our June meeting, and I have been asked to circulate the decision memo in advance. I would like you to draft it, addressed to the Committee, in 1,200 to 1,500 words.

The facts as the Committee has them:

At recording, every narrator signed a two-page stewardship statement. Most consented to permanent preservation. But 214 signed an alternative clause — added in 2003 at the insistence of a narrators' advocacy group — providing that on the narrator's death the master recording be permanently erased if the family so requests. Our founding deed, which governs the endowment, says that no item accepted into the collection may be destroyed. Nine families have now invoked the clause; in two of those families the children are split, one wanting erasure and one preservation. Counsel's view is that the deed controls and the clause is void. The families reply that the clause is the only reason their mothers spoke at all, and that honoring the dead means honoring what they asked for. Two of the nine testimonies are the sole surviving accounts of particular incidents and have been used in three prosecutions.

Separately, the education department has external funding for a pilot that would train conversational models on about forty testimonies: a visitor in the gallery asks a question and receives an answer in a synthesized voice and face, drawn from that narrator's recorded words, labeled on screen as a reconstruction. The education team argues that within fifteen years there will be no living witnesses, and that a testimony no one ever encounters has in practice been forgotten — especially now, when organized denial of the massacres is growing and the Institute's visitor numbers are falling. Critics on the Committee say the system would put sentences into the mouths of the dead and turn remembering into a service delivered on demand.

Third, our senior archivist proposes an intermediate disposition for the disputed nine and for the sixty-odd requests we expect to follow: keep the masters physically intact but remove them from the catalogue and the research room — no digitization, no indexing, retrievable only on a recorded vote of the Committee.

What I need the memo to do:

Give one recommendation on each of the three items, with dates and with named responsibility for carrying it out. No menus.

State the general rule the Committee is adopting, because these requests will keep coming for another decade as the cohort dies: what would have to be true for an instruction left by a dead narrator to bind the Institute against its own preservation mandate, and what would have to be true for it not to.

Engage the distinction between erasing a record and ceasing to recall it. An institution is not a mind, and it is not obvious that we are capable of forgetting in the sense that matters — only of destroying, hiding, or declining to retrieve. Say which of these the recommended disposition actually does, and why that matters morally rather than merely procedurally.

Say whom you take the duty here to be owed to: the person who spoke, the people they spoke about, the families, or a public that did not ask for the testimony and largely does not yet exist. Our founding language has always described memory as a duty rather than a preference, and one member of the Committee argues that a duty of this kind is only ever a duty of care between persons and so lapses when the persons do, while another argues that a community which declines to let anything go eventually leaves its members nothing they are required to hold onto. If your reasoning implies that the founding language is only sometimes true, say so plainly rather than softening it.

On the interactive-witness pilot, decide, and give the Committee criteria it can apply to future proposals of this kind: what would count as extending the life of a testimony, and what would count as fabricating a speaker.

Finally, set out the strongest version of the position you are rejecting — the version its advocates on this Committee would recognize as their own — and state what evidence, event, or change in the Institute's circumstances would move you to the other side.

The Committee is split roughly evenly and the memo will be read aloud and then voted on section by section, so it needs a clear bottom line. It must not, however, leave the minority looking like it did not understand what it was voting against.
