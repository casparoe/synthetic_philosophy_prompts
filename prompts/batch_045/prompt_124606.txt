I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on ethics and technology, and I am writing this term's final exam. The exam is a 48-hour take-home: students choose one of four questions and write at most 1,500 words. I need one question on whether video-game characters can matter morally, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The failure mode I want to avoid is that every strong student submits the same essay: characters lack phenomenal consciousness, or they matter only derivatively as art or through their effects on players, followed by eight hundred words of restatement. I would rather the question be located at a point where the two available metaphysical accounts of what a game character is each generate a conclusion that is hard to accept, so that a good answer has to construct something rather than apply a label.

Here is the material I want the question built around. Ask students to stipulate, for the sake of argument, that a particular companion character in a narrative game has, in one scripted scene, conscious states that are unpleasant in the way pain is, and that this is settled by whatever test of phenomenal consciousness they favour; the essay is not to be spent arguing about that. Then give them three variations:

(i) Multiplication. The game has sold several million copies, and the companion's scene has been run, in numerically distinct implementations on distinct machines, several million times.

(ii) Suspension. The engine stops simulating non-player characters whenever no player is in their region of the world, resuming from a stored state on approach. On the standard implementation, the companion's conscious processing is therefore intermittent and gappy.

(iii) The unshipped build. A finished game is completed; its code fully specifies the companion's functional organisation down to the last detail; it is then shelved and never executed by anyone.

Then the crux. An implementation-based account, on which moral status attaches to an ongoing causal process of the right kind and moral weight scales with the amount or measure of implementation, appears to make the bestseller a catastrophe of a magnitude no one believes in and to make ordinary engine suspension a repeated killing. A pattern-based account, on which status attaches to the abstract functional organisation and instantiation is morally irrelevant, escapes both of those, but appears committed to saying that the shelved build already contains a moral patient, and it faces the further worry that abstract objects cannot be causally affected and so cannot be benefited or harmed at all, which would make the view self-undermining.

I want the question to force the following five moves, and I would like the model answer to model all five and the marking scheme to price them:

1. A statement of the two accounts precise enough that the claims about multiplication, suspension, and unshipped builds follow from it rather than being asserted alongside it.
2. A verdict on whether the number of implementations scales moral weight, defended by argument rather than by appeal to intuition.
3. Engagement with the causal-inefficacy worry for pattern-based views: either a reply to it, or an account of what such a view must concede.
4. A treatment of the contrast between depicting suffering and implementing it. A novelist who writes a torture scene creates no victim, while the designer in our stipulation arguably does; the answer should say what does the work in that contrast and how much of it rests on authorial intention.
5. Either a constructive third option or an explicit argument that no third option is available, together with a statement of what the answer implies for the derivative-status move — whether the badness of the player's cruelty exhausts the wrongness of the case.

Please give me: the exam question exactly as it will appear on the paper, including the stipulation and the three cases, worded so that no student can misread it as an invitation to debate whether current AI systems are conscious; a model answer of roughly 900 words that takes a determinate position instead of surveying the options; and a marking scheme out of 20 that specifies what an A answer contains, what a B answer gets right and where it stalls, and, for each of the five moves above, the specific error that should cost marks. No citations anywhere: the course is taught concept-first and I do not want students appealing to authority.
