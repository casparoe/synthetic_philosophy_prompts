I went down a rabbit hole about how hospitals actually decide that somebody is dead, and now I cant tell whether the whole thing hangs together. Heres what bugs me.

A patient who has permanently lost all function of the whole brain, brainstem included, gets declared dead — and this matters a lot in practice, because of the rule that you cant take organs out of someone who isnt dead. But the body then stays on a ventilator, warm and pink, healing wounds, fighting off infections, and I've read that when the patient was pregnant teams have kept the body going for months so the fetus could be delivered. Everyone involved calls that body dead. Meanwhile a patient with no awareness at all but a working brainstem — breathing on their own, sleep-wake cycles, the permanent vegetative state thing — counts as alive, and can be kept going for decades. And in the same hospital there's a machine that keeps a donated heart beating outside anybody's body for hours, and the people running it describe what they're doing as keeping the heart alive. Then, when death is declared by circulatory criteria instead, they wait a few minutes after the heart stops and call it irreversible, even though hearts that have been stopped about that long do sometimes get restarted in an ER — so it looks like what's really meant is 'we're not going to restart it'. (Relatedly: the same culture that will tell you viruses aren't alive will happily sell you a live attenuated vaccine and a killed one.)

So: what view of life — of what it is for something to be a living thing — does this package of practices presuppose? Is there a single notion of 'alive' doing the work in all of these cases, and if so is it something like 'the organism still runs itself as an integrated whole, and the brain is the part that does the integrating'? Or is the practice quietly tracking something else — the loss of a person rather than of an organism, or just the permissions we want around stopping treatment and procuring organs? Please seperate what the practice strictly couldnt make sense without from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it's underdetermined, i.e. where several quite different accounts of life would license exactly the same behaviour at the bedside. Then: which rival account of life would force the biggest change in what people actually do, and what would that change look like? And give me a test case — say we get good at growing a body with no brain and no prospect of one, which maintains itself, heals, and grows for years. Under the theory you reconstructed, is it alive? And if the honest answer is 'the theory doesnt say', what does that tell us about how much the death-declaring practice was ever really committed to a definition of life in the first place?
