I am putting together a seminar session on Albert Camus's abolitionism, and I want to press on what his position would amount to now rather than only on what it amounted to in 1957. Camus died in January 1960, and three developments since then bear directly on the case he made.

First, whole-life imprisonment. In most jurisdictions that abolished capital punishment, abolition was made politically available by the existence of a permanent incapacitative sentence — life without any possibility of release, whether as an American life-without-parole term or a European whole-life order. The pattern is close enough to a trade: abolition in exchange for the assurance that certain offenders will never get out. Camus himself did not treat the alternative as leniency. His own proposal was a maximum penalty of forced labor for life, which he described as harsher than death, justified partly by the possibility of judicial error — a life sentence can at least be reversed — and offered as a ten-year experiment.

Second, the error argument has become empirical. He had argued for a ceiling of life imprisonment on the basis that courts are fallible; after the post-1989 wave of DNA exonerations, fallibility is no longer an a priori worry but a documented record of people sentenced to death and later cleared. The retentionist reply this invites is a narrowing proposal: retain the penalty only for cases where guilt is established by, say, uncontested DNA plus a complete video record plus a corroborated confession, so that the residual risk of error approaches zero. If the objection is fallibility, the reply runs, reduce the fallibility rather than abolish the punishment.

Third, medicalization and delay. Executions are now carried out by lethal injection, in private, under clinical protocols that sometimes fail; and in the main retentionist democracy the interval between sentence and execution is routinely measured in decades. This last point has a particular bearing on one strand of his argument. He wrote that "Capital punishment is the most premeditated of murders, to which no criminal's deed, however calculated, can be compared. For there to be an equivalency, the death penalty would have to punish a criminal who had warned his victim of the date on which he would inflict a horrible death on him and who, from that moment onward, had confined him at his mercy for months. Such a monster is not to be encountered in private life." The period he had in mind was months.

There is also the structural argument I find hardest to place. His objection to the penalty in an unbelieving age was that it dismisses the condemned person as beyond remedy or salvation without handing him over to any higher judgment, and that no human being possesses the absolute innocence that would entitle him to deliver such a verdict. But a sentence with no possibility of release is exactly a permanent official judgment that someone is irremediable, arrived at by the same fallible institutions. So it is not obvious whether his own proposed maximum survives his own deepest argument, or whether the two pull in opposite directions.

Consider, finally, an institution I have seen floated in comparative sentencing work, which I will call the revisable life sentence: no fixed term, but a mandatory hearing every five years at which the state must show by clear and convincing evidence that the prisoner remains irremediable; if it declines or fails, the sentence converts into a fixed term with parole eligibility. Its designers present it as keeping the abolitionist trade while declining to make any permanent judgment about a person's capacity to change.

What I want from you is an extrapolation, not a summary.

(1) Separate the strands of the 1957 case — deterrence and effectiveness, judicial error, the beyond-remedy or no-atonement strand, the absence of any authority entitled to judge absolutely, the premeditation-and-forewarning strand, and the revenge-and-example strand — and say for each how it fares under the three developments above, and how much weight it can actually carry. Be explicit where the post-1960 evidence is contested rather than settled, and where it is silent.

(2) Where would he land on whole-life sentences? Is that simply what he asked for, or does the beyond-remedy strand indict it? If the two commitments conflict, say which is more central to him and why.

(3) Would he accept the near-certainty narrowing? Distinguish the strands that are empirical from those that are not, and say whether his case has any non-empirical residue once error risk is driven down.

(4) Does the revisable life sentence answer his objection, or is a judgment reaffirmed every five years the same judgment repeated?

(5) One wrinkle: he took forced labor for life to be harsher than death, and noted that the prisoner retains the option of suicide. Suppose it turned out that prisoners serving irreducible sentences often prefer death and some try to obtain it. Does that vindicate his severity claim or embarrass it?

(6) Flag the places where the prediction is underdetermined — where we would be inventing a Camus rather than extrapolating one.
