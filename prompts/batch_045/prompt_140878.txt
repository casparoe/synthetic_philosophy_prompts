hey — so I need a favor and its a slightly weird one. My partner works in city government and I've been reading over her shoulder too many strategic plans and budget requests, and at the same time my reading group is (slowly) working through Nietzsche's early essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life — the three modes, monumental / antiquarian / critical, the thing about a culture's plastic power, the claim that forgetting is as much a condition of life as remembering is, and the way each mode turns pathological if it's allowed to run on its own. Those two inputs have fused in my head and I want the artifact that results. Assume the role of someone who is both a professional philosopher who actually knows that essay well and a writer of deadpan institutional satire — think a municipal consultant who happens to be very good at Kant and Hegel — and take a few minutes to brainstorm before you write: give me a list of maybe eight to ten possible genres for the piece (a request for proposals, a risk register, an org chart with job ads, a consultant's slide deck, an internal audit, whatever) with the single funniest institutional detail each one would allow, then pick one and say in two sentences why, and then write it.

What I actually want: an in-universe document, around 800 words plus a small budget table, issued by the Bureau of Historical Services of the fictional city of Halvard and submitted to the city council — a Ten-Year Strategic Plan and Budget Request. It should have three operating divisions that map exactly onto monumental, antiquarian and critical history, each with a mission statement, performance indicators, recent achievements, and a funding ask; plus a fourth, much smaller unit, the Latency Office, which schedules and manages sanctioned forgetting — it has a queue, a waitlist, an intake form, and the peculiar property that it cannot report on its own activities without defeating the point of them — and whose budget line is proposed for elimination in the current cycle. Invent a live policy fight inside the document: say, an Interval Rule requiring forty years to pass before anyone can be monumentalized, and a proposed "provisional monumentalization" loophole for still-living Founders that the Monumental Division is pushing hard. Include a footnote apparatus, one or two quotes from an outside contractor — call the heritage consultancy Lethe & Co. — and close with a risk register or appendix listing the conditions under which the plan's own success would count as its failure, without anyone in the document noticing.

The rules of the bit: nobody in Halvard has heard of Nietzsche, nobody says "as Nietzsche warned," no winking, and no direct quotation from his text (paraphrase if you must). The three modes should read as plain municipal common sense to a reader who's never opened the essay and as a precise portrait of the essay to one who has. The humor has to come from the substance: each division should reproduce, in completely reasonable administrative language, the exact pathology Nietzsche describes — the Monumental Division chasing greatness-citation targets, the costume-shop effect in which the living are recruited to reenact dead excellence and are quietly demoted by comparison with it; the Antiquarian Division's equal-reverence rule, which stops it from discarding anything at all, including its own internal meeting minutes, and has produced a storage crisis; the Critical Division, whose entire function is prosecution of the past and which has started auditing the other two divisions and then, unavoidably, itself. Keep it deadpan and internally consistent — the jokes land because the bureaucracy is coherent, not because anyone in it is stupid. Don't give me citations or a works-cited; invent firms, policies and numbers freely, and don't worry if the whole thing ends up making a real argument about how empty "what serves life" is as a standard when an institution has to operationalize it.
