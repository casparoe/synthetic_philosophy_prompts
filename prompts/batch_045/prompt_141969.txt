I run the training for volunteer mediators in a neighbourhood restorative-justice program — twelve adults, no philosophy background, one 75-minute slot, and they'll have already done a morning on "active listening", so they'll arrive in a mode where the professional thing to do with any strong feeling is to set it aside. Someone decided the program needed something on blame and moral responsibility, and since I have a philosophy degree from a long time ago, I got handed it. I want to build it around Strawson's reactive attitudes: the participant stance versus the objective attitude.

Don't explain Strawson to me, I've read the essay — thats not what I need. Give me a plan for teaching it to them. Concretely:

- The order things have to come in, and why — what depends on what. My instinct is that they have to feel the difference between being caught up with someone and stepping back from them before I say the words "moral responsibility" or anything about free will, but I'm not sure. I'm also not sure whether the excuse-versus-exemption business (Strawson's pleas and the conditions that take someone out of the practice altogether, if I've got the terminology right) belongs early, as the way in, or late, as the payoff that the first half was setting up.

- The one case to carry the whole session, and what it has to have and what it has to lack. My draft is something they can picture from their own caseload — a teenager who smashed up a neighbour's shed — but I don't know what the case actually has to do for me. Should it be one where the resentment is genuinely live for them, or is that a trap?

- The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually form them, and where in the sequence to head each off. Here's what I'm braced for: (a) they'll hear "reactive attitudes" and conclude Strawson is saying blame is just a feeling you happen to have, so there's no such thing as blaming someone wrongly; (b) because of the morning session, they'll take the objective attitude to be the professional ideal and think the point is to get there; (c) someone will say "so you're saying that with a very young or very ill person you just stop treating them as a person" and I want to be ready before that lands rather than after; (d) someone will say "if nobody in a community feels resentment about X any more, is X not wrong any more?" Tell me which of these I'm wrong about — both whether they'll actually form them and what the right answer is — and add the ones I haven't thought of, especially the ones specific to this group.

- Here's the bit I really want help with, because I think it's the thing that could sink the session. Most of what these people do is hold someone responsible in a role, on behalf of someone who isn't in the room, often while personally feeling nothing at all: the facilitator who presses for an apology because the process requires it, or because the person who was harmed asked for it. Call it scripted blame if you want. As far as I can tell Strawson ties the reactive attitudes to being personally caught up in a transaction with someone, which makes this look like a counter-example rather than a case of anything. So: does it belong at the start, as a reason to open with the proxy case instead of a resentment case? At the end, as the thing that cracks the framework? Or is there a reading where the framework has room for it, and I should build the whole hour around that? Argue for one placement and tell me what it costs me.

- One question for the last five minutes that tells me whether they got it rather than whether they were paying attention. My draft is "is forgiving someone the same as adopting the objective attitude toward them", but I think that's too easy and they'll all say no for the wrong reasons.

- What I should flatly refuse to cover in 75 minutes, and how to say "that's a real question and we're not doing it" without sounding like I'm dodging.

Finally: tell me where your plan is most likely to go wrong with this particular group in this particular mood — what I should watch for in the room that would mean I should abandon your sequence halfway through — and say what would change your mind about where the scripted-blame case goes.
