I'm applying for a one-semester research-leave fellowship, and the application asks for a three-page proposal for a single article-length project. I have an idea I can't yet turn into one, and I'd like you to do it.

The sphex wasp anecdote as it circulates in philosophy of mind — a wasp that, if you move her prey while she goes in to inspect the nest, brings it back and inspects again, apparently without end — is standardly used to draw a contrast between mechanical rigidity and the flexibility that agency, and eventually free will, are said to require. I think that framing mislocates the property, and not merely because the empirical story is shakier than its retellings suggest. My half-formed suggestion: what the wasp lacks is not behavioral variability but something like progress-accounting — the capacity to represent which parts of a plan have already been secured, so that a local perturbation need not invalidate everything upstream of it. Call a system's revisability schedule the answer to the question which of my completed sub-goals must be redone when which things change. Sphexishness is then a degenerate schedule (everything is reopened), but so is its opposite (nothing is ever taken as settled), and flexibility in the ordinary sense is compatible with both. If that is right, the capacity that matters is neither rigidity nor flexibility but having the right schedule, and the usual moral of the anecdote has been drawn from the wrong dimension.

Turn this into a proposal. I need: a question stated so that it has a determinate answer; an account of what work progress-accounting and revisability schedule have to do if they are not to collapse into a truism about planning on one side and a piece of reinforcement-learning vocabulary on the other; why the flexibility-based reading cannot simply absorb the point; a method and month-by-month timetable for one semester with a concrete deliverable; a test case where my account and the flexibility account give different verdicts — I have in mind human checking rituals, and I want your judgment on whether that is the strongest case or whether something else serves better; whether the payoff here is really a claim about free will or only about agency, stated explicitly; and an honest section on what would count as the project failing. Say plainly how much of the argument should be allowed to depend on the entomology, given that the anecdote's provenance and accuracy are doubtful. Refer to positions and arguments by name as you need to, but give me no bibliography and no citations: I will supply the references myself and would rather not have the text padded with ones I haven't chosen.
