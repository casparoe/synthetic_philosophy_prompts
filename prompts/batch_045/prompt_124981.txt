I'm a high school senior putting together an independent project, and I've gotten stuck on a question that I think is actually a math problem in disguise. Here is the background. I've been reading about how much of moral psychology rests on samples from what researchers call WEIRD populations — Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic — and about what happens when the same questions get asked in small-scale societies instead. The variation is larger than I expected: what counts as a fair split of money in a one-shot bargaining game, whether it is permissible to shove one stranger off a footbridge to save five, whether a promise to a dying relative outweighs a rule against breaking the law. My lab partner's conclusion is that morality is something we invented, and that beyond what your community happens to endorse there is nothing to be right or wrong about. I don't want to accept that, but I also don't want to reject it just because I find it unattractive.

So I want a number, and I want to see how you got it. Define the claim M this way: at least one substantive moral judgment is true or false independently of what any person or any culture takes to be true, where M counts as vindicated if, after every party is given the same full factual account of a case and every remaining difference is a difference in what people endorse rather than in what they think happened, at least one society is still getting it wrong. Give me your credence in M, decomposed into the sub-questions you think it depends on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each, and then show how they combine.

Here are the ones I keep circling, in case they help. First, I want to separate three levels at which two societies can differ: which considerations they treat as morally relevant at all, how they weight those considerations against each other when they conflict, and what they take the non-moral facts of the case to be. My instinct is that divergence at the third level is no threat to M whatsoever, divergence at the first level is the serious threat, and the second level is where I genuinely don't know. Second, suppose some core norms do turn out to be near-universal — does that support M, or would a shared evolved psychology explain the appearance of agreement just as well and explain the truth away? Third, and this is the one I think people skip: does that debunking move actually work, or does it only work if you assume in advance that moral facts couldn't be the sort of thing our ancestors were tracking? Fourth, I'm not sure what objectivity is even supposed to require here. Maybe mind-independence is the wrong standard and the real question is whether there's some standpoint — full information, impartiality, whatever — from which the disagreement would settle.

I also invented a test that I can't run, and I'd like to know how much your number depends on it. Call it the stranger's question: instead of asking what you would do, ask what a stranger from a different society would be permitted to do to you or to people you love. The point is to hold the facts and the stakes fixed while stripping out loyalty to your own group. Suppose a well-run version of this found near-total convergence across societies on the stranger's question while first-person answers stayed as divergent as ever. How far does that move your credence in M, and in which direction? Now suppose instead that divergence survived both the stranger's question and full factual information, and was largest precisely on questions about who counts as someone you owe things to. How far does that move it? If those two hypothetical results move you by very different amounts, tell me why — that asymmetry is the part I most want to understand.

For reference, my own credence in M is around 0.55 and my lab partner's is around 0.15. Given your decomposition, which of the sub-questions is the one we most likely disagree about, and which single one is your final number most sensitive to?
