my roommate is in a pop-science phase and he will not shut up about how Berkeley was basically right and physics caught up with him 300 years later. His whole case is one analogy though: reality is like an open-world video game that renders on demand. Objects are the rendered frames, the parts of the map nobody is looking at are just numbers sitting in the server's memory, and that slogan about the moon not being there when nobody looks at it is render-on-demand with extra steps. Then he says this is just what the master argument establishes: try to conceive of a tree existing unconceived and you can't, because the trying is itself a conceiving. When Berkeley's own objector says something like sure, I can imagine trees in a park with nobody around, Berkeley's reply (as I remember it) is that the objector is forgetting that his imagining is itself a perceiving, so he hasn't imagined an unconceived tree at all. So, roommate concludes: the tree in the quad is a frame in God's pipeline and esse est percipi was the first engine design doc.

I don't buy it, but I can't point at the exact seam where it breaks, and some parts of the mapping seem annoyingly good. Can you audit it properly? Concretely I want:

1. The correspondence table. What plays the role of the server, the rendered frame, the save file, the player, the render distance, the physics engine — and what does Berkeley actually cash each of those out as (God, finite spirit, idea, law of nature, the arbitrary sign-relation between visual and tactile ideas, the language of nature stuff). Be explicit about which cells are forced by his texts and which ones the analogy is inventing.
2. Which disanalogies do real damage and which are just flavor. The one I keep circling: a game has a mind-independent substrate — the console, the disk, the silicon — and Berkeley's whole project is to deny unthinking substance. Does that make the analogy circular rather than supportive, i.e. does it quietly import the very thing being denied? Relatedly, render-on-demand implies things blink in and out of existence, which Berkeley very much does not want; his fix is that God is always perceiving everything. But then isn't God doing exactly the work the server does — a hidden state-holder introduced just to secure determinacy? Is that a disanalogy, or is it actually the load-bearing move of the whole system?
3. The other analogy people reach for, the dream one: a dream is mind-dependent in exactly the right way, but the room in the dream you never walked into has no determinate state — nothing settles whether there were three chairs or four. Berkeley needs unobserved objects to be fully determinate, otherwise the regularities he calls laws of nature and the sign-relations between ideas come out indeterminate too. So the dream gets the dependence right and the determinacy wrong, and the video game gets determinacy right and the dependence wrong. Is there any analogy that gets both, or is the gap between the two of them just the real problem, wearing a costume?
4. A case I made up: a trail camera in a forest records a tree falling, the memory card is never watched by anybody, and it degrades to nothing in a drawer. Is that harder on esse est percipi than the plain unobserved tree, or does it collapse back into the same case? It feels harder to me because there's a manufactured artifact that pins down one determinate record, and then no mind ever touches it.
5. Whether any of the quantum framing is load-bearing or whether it's decoration that actively misleads. My sense is that on a Copenhagen-flavored picture an unmeasured observable isn't determinate at all, whereas Berkeley's unperceived tree is perfectly determinate — so the moon thing would be a claim Berkeley should reject, not one he'd welcome. Correct me if I have that backwards.

Please answer in American English, keep it concrete rather than textbook-y, and tell me straight at the end whether the conclusion he wants survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argument.
