Two definitions I will hold you to. A two-person bargaining problem is a pair (F, d): F is a compact, convex and comprehensive set of feasible utility pairs, and d is the disagreement point. Normalize d = (0, 0) in both problems below, and say what one unit of utility means in your example. The Nash solution selects the point of F maximizing u_A · u_B. The Kalai–Smorodinsky (KS) solution selects the maximal point of F lying on the segment that joins d to the ideal point a(F) = (max_F u_A, max_F u_B) — equivalently, the frontier point at which the two parties realize equal fractions of their respective maximal gains. You may not rescale either party's utility between the two problems: one unit has to mean the same thing throughout.

What the case is for. I am writing a short teaching note on the axiomatic parting of the ways between these two solutions: Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives versus the monotonicity requirement that Kalai and Smorodinsky put in its place — roughly, that when one party's best case improves and the other party's does not worsen, the first party should not end up with less. I want a single example in which the two solutions begin in exact agreement, so that their later divergence cannot be written off as a prior asymmetry in how the set treats the two parties, and are then pulled apart by a change that is, in a specified sense, good for one side and a confiscation of nothing from the other.

Build me the following.

1. A story: one paragraph, a recognizable negotiation between two named parties over a concrete stake, with no science-fiction apparatus, payoffs interpretable as expected monetary values or as von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities of a stated unit, and one sentence saying what makes F convex (randomization over agreements, or a continuously adjustable contract term).

2. A baseline problem F in which the two ideal payoffs are unequal, the Nash and KS solutions select exactly the same point, and that point lies off the diagonal u_A = u_B, so that the agreement is not an artifact of symmetry.

3. A change of circumstances under which the feasible set expands from F to a superset G, the disagreement point stays at the origin, and: A's ideal payoff strictly rises while B's ideal payoff is exactly unchanged; nothing is taken from B, in the sense that for every level of A's payoff the greatest payoff compatible with it for B is never smaller in G than in F and is strictly greater over some range; and the maximum attainable total u_A + u_B strictly rises.

4. Verdicts that must fall out of the two definitions rather than be stipulated: the Nash solution of G is exactly the same point as the Nash solution of F, while the KS solution of G gives A strictly more and B strictly less than the KS solution of F — so that B is strictly worse off under KS after a change that removed none of B's options. Each strict inequality must hold with margin rather than at a knife edge, surviving perturbations of the coordinates by about ±0.005.

Then give F and G as vertex lists, with the disagreement and ideal points marked; show the work for each solution in each problem — for Nash, the product along each frontier segment together with the kink or first-order condition that pins the maximum, and for KS, the ray from d to the ideal point and where it crosses the frontier; state the monotonicity principle you are testing the case against, in the exact form you rely on; name, in one sentence each, the single feature of the geometry that pins each of the two verdicts and what becomes of that verdict if the feature is removed; give the strongest objection a defender of whichever solution looks worse here would raise against the case, and your reply to it; and close with two lines on what the case does and does not establish about the choice between the two axioms. Round numbers are welcome, and keep the arithmetic checkable by hand.
