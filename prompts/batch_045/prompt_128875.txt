Posting this because I got into the same argument again at brunch yesterday and I don't think either of us moved an inch. I was doing the standard rationalist-blog thing about voting: the instrumental value of a ballot is roughly the chance you're pivotal times the stakes, the chance you're pivotal in anything big is on the order of one in millions, so on pure consequences voting is basically a consumption good or a costly signal of being a cooperator, and if you skip it you haven't done anything wrong in the expected-value sense. My friend, who is much more of a civic-republican, "democracy is a practice not a market" type, answered with: if you don't vote, you can't complain. She clearly thought that settled it.

I definately don't think it settles anything, but I also can't say why, so what I want is the menu. What are the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, from the near-trivial to the actually substantive? Off the top of my head I can think of a claim about standing or forfeiture (you've waived the standing to object); a fair-play / no-free-riding claim (you're happy to consume what other people's participation produces); an expressive-consistency claim (you're performing concern while declining to pay a trivial cost for it); a generalization claim (what if everyone abstained — the result would be awful); a consent or lottery-acceptance claim (by sitting out you take whatever comes as given, like skipping a raffle and then objecting to who won); an epistemic claim (people who can't be bothered to vote mostly don't know what they're talking about); and a purely descriptive one (nobody will listen to you, and that's just how the norm works). Give me the readings I've missed too, and for each say whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false — and, the part I actually care about, whether it engages the pivot-probability argument at all or just routes around it. My suspicion is that the generalization and causal-participation readings die to the paradox (my abstaining changes the outcome no more than my voting does) while the fair-play and standing readings are quietly doing all the real work, but I'd rather have that checked than confirmed.

One more wrinkle that makes me suspect this isn't really one norm: the same people who say this will also say, to someone who did vote, "well you voted for her, so you can't complain now," or "you participated, you endorsed the system, own it." Is there a single principle that generates all three, or is the tension evidence that the sentence is doing social work — licensing blame, signaling which team you're on — rather than stating a norm anyone could state precisely? Seperate that question from the rest, and tell me which of your readings you think my friend actually meant.
