Last night I had a long, circular argument with a friend after a workshop on hospital data governance — she builds and signs off on the infrastructure side of it, I teach the occasional ethics-and-technology module — and it struck me that we weren't really disagreeing so much as occupying two different framings that never touched. Her view, as I'd reconstruct it charitably: privacy just is a person's effective control over who may access her or information about her; consent, scope-limitation and revocability are its operative mechanisms; and its value is entirely instrumental, a matter of autonomy, trust, freedom from discrimination and manipulation, nothing left over once you've counted those. Mine: privacy is an agent-relative normative status — being appropriately unavailable to a given party, in a given role, within a given context — fixed by norms of appropriateness and flow that no single individual gets to set or waive, and at least part of its value is non-instrumental, because certain relationships, roles and forms of respect cannot exist without it. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on the philosophy of privacy and information ethics: brainstorm first about where the real fault lines are rather than producing a bland both-sides inventory, and then draft the joint statement our two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

I want the usual deliverable. First, a numbered section of claims both of us could sign — about the formalism of the dispute if there is one, about what a theory of privacy has to explain (the felt difference between being overheard and being read about, the wrongness of some acquisitions of true information, the difference between losing privacy and having a right to privacy violated), and about what the standard counterexamples to each side actually show. Then a disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, my view stated in wording I'd accept and hers in wording she'd accept, with a note on whether the difference is substantive or merely verbal. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us — and for each crux a case, argument or formal result both of us would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance of the outcome. Here are the cases I'd want used or improved on, since they seemed to be where our intuitions came apart most sharply: (i) a sealed archive — everything about you is recorded, encrypted, and decryptable only after your death, and in fact almost nothing is ever decrypted, so your actual access-experience is unchanged but your control is not; (ii) the model that knows you — a system ingests everything, destroys the raw records, and keeps only trained parameters, so no person ever accesses any datum about you, yet decisions about your credit, care and parole are made by something that predicts you extremely well; (iii) your cousin's genome, or your face in a stranger's photograph — the most revealing facts about you become inferable from data about other people, over which your control is null and your consent unavailable; (iv) the glass village — a community in which everyone reflectively and non-coercively wants full mutual transparency, gets it, and by their own lights flourishes. I'd also want you to include the formal-guarantee case: a hospital releases statistics under a strong guarantee that no individual's record materially affects what is published, and I suspect we would disagree about whether that settles anything or merely relocates the question from access to terms of use.

Two things about how I want this written. Where an "agreed" claim would be vacuous — something no one could dissent from, or that only looks agreed because a vague word is doing all the work — flag it in the margin rather than padding the agreed section with it, and say plainly if any part of our dispute is really a dispute about words (about "privacy", "a right to privacy", or "loss of privacy") and which part that is. And some calibration for the answer: tell me how confident you are in each of the crux identifications, say which of them you'd expect a specialist to reject, and for each camp write down what would count as evidence against its own view — I don't want the control theorist to look unfalsifiable simply because nothing was specified in advance. Finally, if the framing is wrong — if one of my two camps is a straw man, if a case I've described is just an access case in disguise, or if the territory is better carved into three positions rather than two — say so directly instead of politely producing the document I asked for.
