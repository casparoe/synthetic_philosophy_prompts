Hi — I'm a science journalist, and I'm working on a 1,200-word feature (due Tuesday, send help) about whether what people literally see gets shaped by what they believe. The hook is the thing you hear constantly now — "your politics change what you see," "we don't see reality, our brains construct it" — and I want to anchor it in a concrete case: police officers and the split-second judgements in those shooter-bias style experiments, and what any of this should mean for how much weight a jury gives an officer's account of what they saw versus what's on the bodycam footage.

I've done maybe six interviews and I think I've been conflating three different things without realizing it, and one vision scientist kept cutting me off with "that's a decision effect, not a perceptual one," which I dutifully wrote down and didn't really understand. As I've been lumping them together: (1) a belief or expectation changes the actual content of conscious visual experience — the world looks different, not just gets interpreted differently; (2) a belief changes where attention goes or which features get picked up, so you genuinely fail to see the thing you weren't looking for; (3) a belief changes the threshhold for acting or reporting — same experience, different willingness to say "I saw a gun." I'd also assumed the whole question of whether cognition "penetrates" perception was basically settled back in the 1980s by the guy who coined cognitive penetrability, with the answer being no, which is why people say perception is informationally encapsulated. But then I read some stuff about predictive-processing models where the brain is doing inference all the way down, and that sounds like it settles the question the other way: if perception just is inference, then of course your beliefs and expectations are in there, so there's no fact of the matter about what someone saw apart from their background beliefs — and the legal upshot would be that officer and eyewitness testimony are just opinions with no special standing. My editor loves that version and I don't trust it.

So: where am I going wrong, and which of (1), (2) and (3) does the evidence actually support, and which of them would my piece need in order to earn the strong claim in the kicker? One more wrinkle — a psychologist I interviewed drew a distinction I can't find much on, between synchronic penetration (a belief you're holding right now changes what you see right now) and what she called "calibration," where a long history of beliefs, training and practice reshapes what your visual system is able to extract, so that experience changes even though no belief is operative in the moment. Is that a real distinction in this literature, and does it actually help, or does it just move the bump in the rug? Please be explicit about which parts of what you tell me are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this — I really don't want to print a contested claim as the consensus and get a furious email from a vision scientist a week after publication. Also flag anywhere my framing of the law-enforcement angle assumes something the philosophy doesn't give me. American English, please, and pitch it at someone who reads a lot of science writing but has no philosophy training.
