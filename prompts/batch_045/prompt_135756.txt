I'm writing a novel — a courtroom thing, mostly, set in a made-up island republic — and I've got stuck on the chapter where I actually have to describe how a verdict gets reached. I want it to feel like something people would really design, not just set dressing, and the more I read about how juries actually work the less I can tell whether the whole package hangs together.

As far as I can make it out, the real thing is roughly this. Twelve people pulled more or less at random off the electoral register. Nobody is chosen for being good at weighing evidence; if anything the people who do that for a living get kept off or struck off. They all go into one room and argue about it together. They're supposed to agree, but only up to a point — after a while the court will accept a lopsided majority instead of unanimity. Then they come out and announce a bare verdict, guilty or not guilty, with no account of why. And afterwards nobody is allowed to give evidence about what was said in the room, and the verdict can't be torn up on appeal just because the reasoning behind it was rubbish.

Someone told me there's a bit of maths about all this — Condorcet, I think — along the lines of: if you get enough people who are each only slightly better than a coin flip, and they vote independently, then the majority is right almost all the time, and it gets better the more of them you add. I might be mangaling it, but that's the gist I've been working from.

Here's the thing: half of that package looks like it was built by someone who believed the maths, and half looks like it was built by someone who didn't. Random draw, twelve, the fallback to a majority — fine, that fits. But making them argue it out in a room together should give them all the same blind spot, and a unanimity rule gives one stubborn juror a veto, which on that maths sounds like it would make twelve people worse than one. And don't get me started on the bit where they don't have to explain themselves and nobody may ever look inside the room.

So: what does the real practice actually presuppose? Is it one story about why twelve strangers are meant to get it right, or several bolted together, or just accumulated history? I need to know which rules are load-bearing for which story, because in my version I've changed two things and I want to know whether I've quietly made the whole set-up nonsense. In my republic the panel is still twelve, but two of the seats are always held by retired judges, and each of those two gets two votes; and the entire deliberation happens in public, in the town square, with anyone who fancies it shouting advice.

A few things I'd like:
— reconstruct the implicit theory of the real version, and tell me which bits it strictly requires and which bits it merely gets along with;
— tell me which of my two changes a believer in the Condorcet-ish story has to object to, and why, and whether either of them could be defended on its own terms (weighting the experts sounds like it might actually be fine?);
— say where the practice is just underdetermined, where two quite different theories would produce the same rules, because if half of it is arbitrary I'd rather know that before I write three chapters of characters making speeches about it;
— and if you swap the truth-tracking story for the other obvious one — the accused is entitled to the judgement of their peers, and the old line about it being better that a lot of guilty people walk than that one innocent person is convicted — which of the rules would have to change?

Plain language please, and no footnotes or paper titles, I won't read them. Then finish with one last line, nothing after it, exactly in this shape:

Verdict: [independence-and-majority | deliberation | underdetermined] + [both my changes are fine | one has to go | both have to go]
