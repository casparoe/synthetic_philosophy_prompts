Hey — I need something I can actually put on the wall and use, not an essay about it.

Im deputy director of comms at a regional public health agency. About 2 million people, four of us on the team, we push out two or three guidance lines or press lines a week, the chief medical officer signs off on anything with a number in it, and we usually have a few hours rather than a few weeks. Our recurring problem: the version of a claim that works is the confident one, and the version we can defend in front of a journalist is the careful one, and those arent the same sentence.

Two features of our situation that I think matter. First, a lot of our claims are what I'll call uptake-linked: their truth depends on people believing them, because the believing is what produces the behaviour that makes them come true. ('If everyone cuts their contacts by about a third for two weeks, the hospitals will hold.') Others are nothing like that — the water is contaminated or it isnt, and no amount of confidence from us touches it. Second, we have what our team calls the archivist: a permanent local opposition group that screenshots every line we soften or overstate and recycles it for years. So an overclaim has a long tail, not just a news cycle.

What I want is a decision procedure built out of the ethics-of-belief stuff I half remember from university — Clifford's flat rule about believing on insufficient evidence and his shipowner case, James's reply that when an option is genuine and the evidence genuinely cant settle it you're allowed to let your passional nature decide, and above all his thought that some facts only arrive at all if you've already banked on them. I dont want a summary of the debate and I dont want reading recommendations. I want: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says no, and where it hands the decision back to a person because its a values question rather than a factual one.

Three things it has to get right:

1. Dont collapse "should I believe this" into "should I say it." For us those come apart constantly — sometimes the right move is to say the confident thing while believing the cautious one, sometimes I think the reverse, sometimes neither. Show me exactly where the gap is and what the procedure does when the two answers disagree.

2. Give me an actual test for whether a belief is the kind that helps create its own object. Not "its motivating" — the shipowner's belief motivated him perfectly well and that was the whole problem. The test has to be about whether the believing is causally upstream of the fact believed. And most of our real claims are half-and-half: the hospitals hold if uptake is high AND staffing survives AND the variant isnt nastier than the modelling assumes. Tell me how to score a claim that's partly uptake-linked and partly not.

3. Put the archivist in the procedure. How much weight does long-run damage to our credibility get, and at which step — is it a tiebreaker, a veto, or just one input among many?

Then run it on three cases:

(a) Next week we want to publish "If everyone cuts their contacts by about a third for the next two weeks, the hospitals will hold." Our modelling puts that somewhere near 60/40 and the modellers wont go on the record with the number.

(b) A lab result on a municipal water supply is 36 hours late. We're maybe 80% sure the water is fine. Do we tell people its safe, or tell them to boil it.

(c) The one I already know I wont like. Our staff are wrecked after two years of back-to-back surges, and the CMO wants to stand up at the all-staff meeting and say "the worst is behind us." Nobody knows that. But the team's view is that if she doesnt say it, a chunk of people quit. Tell me what the procedure actually says, not what would be kind.

And last: where does it break. Which of these steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure, whats the failure mode if we run it hard for six months, and what kinds of decisions should we keep out of it entirely.
