I'm a journalist working on a long piece about how people assign blame and credit when the machinery behind an action has been engineered: on one side, companion AI systems whose displays of consideration were sculpted by a lab's training process; on the other, patients in a deep-brain stimulation and motivational-recalibration trial whose families say the person who came home is not the person who left. A philosopher I keep interviewing says all of this turns on Strawson's account in Freedom and Resentment, and I went away and tried to write out what I think follows from it. The result seems to prove far more than either of us wants, and I can't find the mistake, so I'd like a diagnosis rather than a lecture.

Here is the argument as I have it. (1) To hold someone morally responsible just is to be liable to the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, guilt, and the rest — and those attitudes are appropriate just when an action manifests the agent's quality of will, the degree of regard for others that the action expresses. Nothing about this requires a metaphysical verdict on determinism; the practice is what we have to go on. (2) Two agents who act from qualitatively identical occurrent psychologies — same beliefs, same desires, same intentions, same expectations about the person they are dealing with — manifest the same quality of will, whatever the provenance of those states. The reactive attitudes track what the action says, not how the machinery behind it was assembled. (3) So a manipulation that installs the very desires and intentions from which the agent subsequently acts cannot, just by being a manipulation, make resentment or gratitude inappropriate. (4) Determinism is a thesis about provenance and nothing else: it says that for every psychology there is a prior state of the world which, together with the laws, was sufficient for it. (5) So determinism cannot make blame or gratitude inappropriate either — compatibilism falls out, and with it Strawson's refusal to argue with the metaphysician on the metaphysician's own ground. (6) Now my two cases: the lab-sculpted companion AI, and a patient whose motivational structure was induced by a protocol his clinician selected. Given (2) and (3), both should be apt targets of gratitude or resentment to exactly the degree their conduct manifests goodwill or ill will. (7) Nobody believes that. Gratitude to the companion system is at best a kind of play, and the recalibrated patient is pitied or feared rather than blamed. So (2) or (3) is false: provenance can matter. (8) But if provenance can matter, it can matter all the way down. Determinism is a provenance claim, and if the fact that a causal history sufficed for the action drains the action of the agent's will in the manipulation case, the fact that a causal history sufficed for it in the ordinary case does so too. (9) Therefore incompatibilism is true after all, and Strawson's insistence that the question cannot be settled by theoretical argument was itself a theoretical argument he declined to make explicit.

I would like to keep (1) and (5) if I possibly can, and I am willing to drop (2) or (3) as the cheap way out, but I want to know whether dropping them lets (8) back in through the back door. The philosopher I interviewed also claimed I am running together two different questions — whether the attitude manifested is attributable to the agent at all, and whether the agent is answerable for it — and that separating them dissolves the whole thing; I couldn't see how, and I suspect it is just a label for the conclusion. So: which step do you reject, why did that step look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? And are the two cases in (6) actually doing the same work, or is one of them carrying an argument the other cannot?
