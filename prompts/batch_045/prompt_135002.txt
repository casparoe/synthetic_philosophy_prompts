I spent thirty-five years in experimental polymer physics and now sit in on a philosophy of science reading group — six retired scientists, one graduate student, and a convener who keeps insisting we say what we actually believe about a case rather than which author we last read. For the next session I want to hand out a key that sorts us by our answers, so I need a decision tree on reduction and emergence built out of cases rather than out of labels.

Please build it to these specifications:

Seven or eight branch nodes, each phrased as a question about a concrete case, each binary or ternary, yielding nine or ten leaves, and small enough to fit on one side of A4 for eight people to work through in an hour. No node may ask anyone to self-apply a label, and no node may turn on a bibliography — I want commitments, not attributions.

At least four of the nodes should use cases of the following kind, which I have roughed out myself and would like kept roughly as described. (i) The Rennert medium: a reaction–diffusion system whose wavefront speed is invariant to three significant figures across three decades of temperature, catalyst batch and vessel geometry, while the underlying kinetics differ wildly across those conditions — does the invariance belong to the macro law as an explanatory achievement, or is it merely a convenience? (ii) A contrast between a macro quantity fixed by laboratory practice long before anyone had the micro theory, and a macro quantity deliberately engineered out of the micro variables so that the bridge is trivial by construction — does the order of epistemic access, or the way the quantity was arrived at, do any ontological work? (iii) A case where the derivation of the macro law from the micro theory goes through only after one stipulates an initial or boundary condition that can itself only be stated in macro vocabulary. (iv) A case where the macro law is derivable in principle but only by a computation no attainable agent could carry out, while a two-parameter phenomenological model predicts it to experimental precision.

One further node should ask what one does when the two descriptions, each corrected as far as idealization allows, still disagree: which one gets to be revised.

Construct the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — then locate positions in it as leaves, characterised by their commitments with the usual label in parentheses only where it is unambiguous. The following must all appear: derivational reduction via bridge laws; identity without derivation; non-reductive physicalism with multiple realizability; autonomy understood as relative to a class of interventions; irreducibility with genuine downward influence; emergence as nothing more than unpredictability in practice; the deflationary view that the macro vocabulary is a convenient redescription and nothing else; and one leaf for the claim that the question as posed is ill-formed. Note the two or three answer combinations that no position occupies, with a sentence each on whether they are empty because incoherent, unattractive, or simply unoccupied. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, and add a short tie-breaker section for them.

Please keep every case inside bench-scale science: no minds, no consciousness, no quantum mystery.
