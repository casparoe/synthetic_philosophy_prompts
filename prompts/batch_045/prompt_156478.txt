I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, and next week I'm presenting a dissertation chapter at a departmental works-in-progress session — twenty minutes of talk, forty minutes of questions. The chapter defends a version of the view that there is a genuine moral asymmetry between lying and merely misleading, but locates that asymmetry somewhere other than where the usual accounts put it. I argue that what matters is not the distinction between asserting something false and merely implicating something false, but a norm I call 'question ownership': whoever has implicitly taken on the role of determining which question is live in an exchange — by inviting it, by framing the agenda, or by occupying a role on which others are licensed to rely — owes the other party a distinctive epistemic service, namely keeping the answer-channel clean, and lying is the canonical breach of that duty. On this basis I sort deceptive maneuvers into answer-misleading (you implicate a falsehood in responding to a question the hearer already had), frame-misleading (you reshape what the hearer takes to be the relevant question, using only true statements), and salience-misleading (you leave the question untouched and manipulate only which truths are perceptually or memetically salient — think of the committee chair who circulates the full spreadsheet but highlights only the rows favoring her candidate). My claim is that the asymmetry is at its strongest in the first class, noticeably weaker in the second, and effectively absent in the third, because question ownership does real work only where the hearer's own question-forming capacity has been preempted. I'm reasonably happy with the architecture and much less happy about the question period.

The audience is where I need help. It's about twenty-five people. There are three people working in the Kantian and Anscombean traditions who think the lying/misleading distinction is both obvious and absolute, and who will be unmoved by any talk of conversational roles; they care about intention, the will, and the historical casuistry around mental reservation. There are four broadly consequentialist or information-economics-minded people who think the distinction is morally inert and that only expected effects on the hearer's credences matter, and who will want to know why I'm not just reinventing a garbled version of their view. There are two experimental philosophers who have run studies in which ordinary participants' deception judgments track outcome severity and the perceived violation of a trust relationship far more than they track anything about assertion versus implicature, and who will want to know whether my three-way taxonomy predicts anything they could test. And there is one philosopher of language who will press hard on whether the assertion/implicature boundary is stable enough to bear the weight I put on the answer-misleading case at all, plus a handful of graduate students who mostly want to know what my view says about silence and omission in professional advising, which is the application I gesture at in the last three minutes.

What I'd like is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the chapter. Give me the objections I should expect from this specific room, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do if I handle them badly, and phrase each one the way that subgroup would actually phrase it — in their vocabulary, with their background assumptions visible, not translated into my terms. For each: a reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes, what I should concede outright before they force it out of me, and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which single reply has to land for the chapter to survive the session, where I should distinguish my position from the consequentialists rather than agree with them too quickly, and how I should handle the objection that my three-way taxonomy is really just a list of ways to mislead with no principled stopping point. Finally, include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, and be blunt about why it's the dangerous one.
