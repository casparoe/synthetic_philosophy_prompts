**Question (25 marks)**

Dutch book arguments are standardly presented as arguments for probabilism: the thesis that an agent's degrees of belief ought to conform to the axioms of probability. Suppose throughout that the agent's credence function c is defined over a space of states, that for every event A the agent is willing to buy or sell a bet that pays one unit if A obtains and nothing otherwise at the price c(A) (their betting quotient for A), and that the agent accepts any bet that is fair or favourable by their own lights.

(a) State the synchronic Dutch book theorem and its converse, and illustrate each with a short example. Be precise about what "sure loss" means in the statement of the theorem — in particular, whose credence function supplies the space of possibilities over which the loss is said to be sure. [8 marks]

(b) A critic objects as follows: at best the argument shows that agents with incoherent credences can be exploited by a bookie; exploitability is a prudential defect, not an epistemic one; so nothing in the argument shows that incoherent credences are irrational *as beliefs*. Assess this objection. In doing so, distinguish the reading on which the argument *justifies* the axioms from the reading on which it merely *demonstrates* their consequences, and say which reading the objection actually threatens. [9 marks]

(c) Now consider the following construction. Let the agent be fully coherent, with credence function c over a space of states Ω_c, and let a bookie whose credence function d is likewise coherent be defined over a proper subset Ω_d of Ω_c: the bookie takes to be impossible some state s that lies in Ω_c and to which the agent assigns positive credence. The bookie offers the agent a finite package of bets, every one of them priced at the agent's own fair betting quotient and therefore accepted, arranged so that the package yields a net loss in every state belonging to Ω_d and a net gain in s. Call this a *foreign-credence book*: a package the agent accepts whose net result is a sure loss by the bookie's lights, though not by the agent's. It has been argued that the availability of foreign-credence books shows that sure-loss vulnerability does not track incoherence at all, since an agent who violates no axiom can be exposed to one. Does the foreign-credence book refute the Dutch book argument, or does it only delimit its scope? Answer by reference to what part (a) requires about whose lights the loss is sure by, and say what, if anything, the construction shows about the objection in part (b). [8 marks]

**Answer under review**

*(a)* Let c be the agent's credence function over a partition of states, and let the agent buy or sell any bet paying one unit on A at the price c(A).

*Synchronic Dutch book theorem.* If c violates the axioms of probability, then there is a finite set of bets, each of which the agent accepts at their own betting quotients, whose net result is a loss in every state — a sure loss. Two examples. Suppose c(A) = 0.6 and c(not-A) = 0.6. The bookie sells the agent both bets; the agent pays 1.2 units and receives 1 unit whichever of A and not-A obtains, thus losing 0.2 units whatever happens. Suppose instead that A and B are disjoint, with c(A) = 0.3, c(B) = 0.4 and c(A or B) = 0.5. The bookie sells the agent the bets on A and on B and buys from the agent the bet on A or B; the agent pays out 0.2 units in every state.

*Converse theorem.* If c satisfies the axioms, no such set of bets exists. Each bet is priced at the agent's fair price, so each has expected value zero by the agent's own lights; since c is additive, so does any finite sum of them; and a package with expected value zero cannot be negative in every state. Coherence is therefore both necessary and sufficient for immunity to sure loss. Since the bookie may offer arbitrarily many bets, including infinitely many, the argument also covers failures of countable additivity, and so it establishes the whole probability calculus and not merely finite additivity.

By "sure" I mean: loss in every state of affairs, full stop. It is not relative to anybody's credence function. A package that loses in every state loses money whatever the agent believes and whatever the bookie believes, and that is what makes the theorem an objective result rather than a piece of armchair psychology. It is also why the argument is standardly credited to de Finetti's formulation in terms of betting quotients, rather than to Ramsey's, which derives an agent's credences and utilities from their preferences over gambles and is an argument of a different kind altogether — a representation theorem rather than an exploitation argument.

*(b)* The objection is best handled by separating two readings of the argument. On the justification reading, the argument is meant to give someone who does not yet accept the axioms a reason to accept them. On the demonstration reading, it only shows that incoherence has a further and vivid consequence, which is at most of interest to someone who already grants that incoherence is a fault. The objection has real force against the justification reading, and only against that reading. The argument needs a bridge premise to the effect that vulnerability to sure loss is irrational, and the mathematics does not supply it. Worse, the theorem is very close to equivalent to the axioms themselves, so as a justification it threatens to be circular: it restates the axioms in the vocabulary of betting rather than explaining why they bind. As a demonstration, by contrast, it is unproblematic and genuinely illuminating.

The objection can nevertheless be met, and the standard way of meeting it is to depragmatise the argument: replace money by epistemic utility, or better by accuracy as measured by a strictly proper scoring rule. Ramsey's own approach was the first step in this direction, since it measures credence by what the agent prefers rather than by what a bookie will offer. The result is this: for any incoherent credence function there is a coherent one that is more accurate than it in every possible state, so the incoherent function is accuracy-dominated. Accuracy is the good that beliefs aim at, not a prudential good like money. The accuracy version therefore vindicates the axioms rather than merely demonstrating their consequences: it shows that incoherence is an epistemic defect, which is just what the critic denied. So the objection is answered, and no further substantive premise is needed — the only premise required is that credences are assessed for accuracy, and that is not a premise anyone can seriously dispute.

*(c)* The foreign-credence book refutes the Dutch book argument, or at least refutes the use that is made of it. Fix a coherent agent whose space Ω_c contains a state s to which they assign positive credence, and a bookie whose narrower space Ω_d excludes s. Because every bet is priced at the agent's own quotient, the agent accepts the whole package; and because the bookie's space is narrower, the bookie can arrange for the package to lose a little in every state the bookie takes to be possible and to win a great deal in s. The agent thus accepts a package that is a sure loss by the bookie's lights while remaining perfectly coherent.

Nor is this merely a matter of someone's opinion. If the bookie's extra information is genuine — if s really is not a live possibility — then the agent simply loses money, and loses it whatever anyone believes. The loss is a loss in fact. So coherence is not sufficient for immunity to sure loss: a coherent agent is sure-loss vulnerable whenever a counterparty with better information does the arranging, and the converse half of the theorem stated in part (a) fails. The right conclusion is that Dutch book vulnerability tracks informational disadvantage rather than irrationality, since anyone who is uncertain about anything can be exploited by someone who knows more. The argument for probabilism must accordingly be given in some other way — by the accuracy argument of part (b), which does not rely on bookies, bets, or any claim about exploitation.
