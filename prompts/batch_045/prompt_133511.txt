I sit on a small advisory body that has to rank interventions by expected impact across candidate patients of very different kinds: insect populations on farms and in supply chains, decapod crustaceans, cephalopods, farmed fish, birds and mammals, human patients with disorders of consciousness, infants, cerebral organoids, and large AI systems. We must rank them under deep, unresolved disagreement about the metaphysics of consciousness, and we must publish reasons that a reasonable person who disagrees with us about the metaphysics can still engage with. Before I look at whether any existing account of moral status can be made to serve this purpose, I want the specification such an account would have to meet. Please write that specification for me.

What I want, concretely: the list of requirements a theory of graded sentience and moral status would have to satisfy to be usable by a body in our position — the phenomena it must accommodate, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results from biology and neuroscience it must not contradict, and the decision-theoretic properties it must have — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and each one marked hard or soft.

Among the cases I want the list to be answerable to, and which I ask you to sort into the criteria they generate rather than treat one at a time:

- An insect that learns to avoid a noxious stimulus and that grooms a wounded limb, but whose avoidance survives decapitation and shows none of the flexibility, attention-dependence, or motivational trade-offs that pain ascriptions to vertebrates rely on.
- A patient under adequate general anaesthesia, and a patient in a vegetative state, both of whom show coordinated nociceptive and autonomic responses; and, in the other direction, a patient who later reports having been aware during a procedure in which nothing behavioural was visible.
- Whether a very large number of insects can outweigh a single human in the ranking. This is not one case but a fork: it depends on whether moral status is a cardinal magnitude admitting interpersonally comparable units and additive aggregation, or merely an ordering, or a set of incommensurable dimensions that no ranking can honestly collapse into one number.
- Nested candidate subjects: a eusocial colony whose members are themselves candidates, or a human being together with the enteric nervous system she contains. Must the theory avoid double counting, and can it say what would count as double counting here?
- A continuum of cases in which a human's neurons are replaced one by one with silicon units that reproduce each cell's input–output function, ending in something functionally indistinguishable from the original person but physically alien. Where along that series does moral status change, does it change continuously or abruptly, and what is the theory committed to about the case where the replacement is total?
- The same question put as an interpolation problem: a thermostat and a human being are both negative-feedback control systems with sensors, set points, and effectors, and a continuous series of control systems can be constructed between them. A theory that gives the thermostat nothing and the human a great deal must locate the transition somewhere on that series and explain why that location is not arbitrary; and I would like to know whether its verdicts are stable across different series drawn between the same endpoints. If stability across interpolation paths cannot be had together with a non-trivial verdict, I want that stated as a result about the list itself rather than as a defect of one theory.
- A system built on a substrate we do not understand, whose observable behaviour is indistinguishable from that of an alert human being, and which claims to have experiences. What evidence is relevant to its case, and does the theory's answer presuppose a human-specific marker and thereby beg the question?
- A being with a wider sensory range than ours, finer discrimination, and a much longer window of temporal integration — a candidate with more of whatever it is, not less. Must the theory permit moral status greater than a typical human's? I suspect the demand that it not is a prejudice of our camp rather than a requirement, and I would like your view on that, and on whether its denial or its acceptance is the more costly commitment.
- Acting under uncertainty about whether a candidate is a subject at all. The theory has to be action-guiding when the probability that a given candidate has any experience is small, badly grounded, and unlikely to be improved by further study; it has to say what we owe in that condition without collapsing into paralysis, and without letting a minuscule probability of an astronomically large stake dominate every ranking we produce.

Two further things I want from the list. First, an argument about which of the demands commonly made in this area are genuine desiderata and which are preferences of a particular camp dressed as requirements — for instance the demand that the theory yield a single comparable number for every candidate, which I suspect is imported from cost-benefit practice rather than from ethics, and the demand that it never rest on a species boundary, which I am genuinely unsure about. Second, if some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, identify the subset and say which member you would give up and at what cost; and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive accounts.

Please structure the reply as follows:

- "The purpose the criteria are written for" — about 80 words, restating what a theory has to be good enough to do, and telling me plainly if you think no theory could be adequate for ranking interventions across substrates; either way, proceed to the rest.
- "The requirements" — about 550 words, numbered, each with its motivating case in a sentence or two and each marked hard or soft.
- "Genuine desiderata and camp prejudices" — about 200 words.
- "Where the list is unsatisfiable" — about 250 words, including the interpolation result if you think there is one.
- "The requirement that excludes the most" — about 150 words.
- "Scorecard" — about 250 words, grading four families of account against the list: simple threshold or lexical views, additive intensity-and-duration views, multi-dimensional capacity views, and precautionary or expected-value-under-uncertainty views. Keep the scorecard short; the criteria are the part I care about.

Argue from the cases and from first principles. I am reading this where I have no library access, so no citations, reading lists, or references to specific papers and authors, and please do not lean on any result whose details you would have to look up.
