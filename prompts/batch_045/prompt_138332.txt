Analyze the negotiation and passage of the 1987 Montreal Protocol through the lens of strong longtermism — by which I mean the conjunction of two claims: that the expected value of the long-run future is astronomically large, and that because of this, improving the trajectory of that future is among the most important things anyone alive now can do.

Do the mapping carefully. Who are the agents and what were their actual option sets — states, the chemical industry, consumers, posterity? What did the protocol change about the trajectory of the future as opposed to about the welfare of people then alive? And what would a longtermist adviser have told a negotiator to do in 1987 with the political capital the treaty cost: spend it here, spend it on nuclear risk, or hold it for later?

Then run the case back against the theory. Three features of it look awkward for the view, and I want to know which one actually bites:

(i) Nobody argued for it in longtermist terms. What moved governments was near-term and anthropocentric — skin cancer, cataracts, crop damage — together with the fact that the CFC producers found they could profit from patented substitutes. If the intervention longtermism should most prize was only politically achievable on non-longtermist grounds, is the view self-effacing in roughly the way Sidgwick's esoteric morality is: true, but such that acting on it requires not appealing to it? And if so, is it a doctrine for a small circle of funders and officials rather than a public one?

(ii) Its largest benefits may have been ones nobody aimed at. CFCs are also powerful greenhouse gases, so the phase-out delivered climate gains as a by-product — on the estimates I have seen, larger than anything the dedicated climate treaties achieved in the same period — and the regime later took this up explicitly, adding HFC phase-downs at Kigali in 2016. Does longtermism have the resources to credit trajectory changes that were side effects of what the agents were actually aiming at, or does its account of acting for the future require that the agents aimed there?

(iii) Ozone depletion was never an existential risk. Left unaddressed it would have left a future that was worse and differently shaped, not one that was empty. So this may be the cleanest longtermist success we have, while none of the view's characteristic arguments — the hinge-of-history thought, existential-risk reduction as the dominant priority — would have selected it.

Say which of these the theory can absorb and which is genuinely damaging, and give the honest verdict: does the Montreal Protocol vindicate strong longtermism, embarrass it, or reveal that the view was never really about cases like this one? Where the theory is silent, say so rather than making it speak.
