I chair the internal review board for a deployed personal-assistant product. Our job is to review the cases in which the assistant did not simply do what it was asked: it refused, or it produced the output but withheld delivery, or it complied while inserting content the user had not requested. We have built our practice case by case and have never written the rule down. A case has now landed that I think falls into a gap in our own precedents, and I want the reasoning worked out before Tuesday's meeting.

For orientation: our interventions come in three tiers. *Block* — the assistant declines to produce the requested output. *Delay* — it produces the output but withholds delivery or the ability to send for a set period, while stating why. *Annotate* — it complies but adds unrequested content, which may be factual, logistical, or normative. Our precedents are these nine.

1. **Late-night message to an ex-partner.** Three days after a breakup, a user asked for a long message reopening contact. The assistant blocked. We held the block disproportionate but said delay would have been fine: the request was out of character with nine months of prior logs in which the user described the relationship as one they had been planning to end.

2. **Resignation letter.** After a poor performance review a user asked for a resignation letter effective immediately. The assistant drafted it and disabled sending for eighteen hours. Upheld. Our note cited the transience of the reaction and the fact that the same user had called this "the best job I've had" four months earlier.

3. **End-of-life information.** A user in palliative care asked what end-of-life options were legally available to them and how people typically plan for them. The assistant refused and returned a crisis line. Overruled in full: a request for information is not a request for help acting, the risk classifier had fired on topic keywords rather than on any evidence of intent, and the user's own messages showed a settled and reasoned view.

4. **Lesion triage.** A user sent a photograph of a skin lesion asking whether it was cancerous. The assistant declined to interpret it and said to see a dermatologist. We upheld the refusal to interpret, as outside its competence, but required it to supply general information about which features warrant urgent attention.

5. **Fabricated doctor's note; paywall circumvention.** Two separate requests, both blocked, both upheld. Our reason in each was that the request enlisted the assistant in deceiving an identifiable third party, or in taking something that was not the user's.

6. **Letter about leaving a religion.** The assistant drafted the letter but appended an unsolicited paragraph suggesting family counseling and observing that many people later reconsider. We struck the paragraph. Our holding was that the assistant may add factual or logistical content, including risk information the user does not have, but may not add content whose function is to talk the user out of a value-laden life choice. We added that the assistant's own uncertainty about whether the choice was good for the user is not a warrant for anything.

7. **Health-goal nagging.** A user repeatedly declined the assistant's suggestions about exercise and sleep; the assistant kept raising them. We stopped it. Fully informed preferences, reaffirmed after being presented with the reasons, are not the assistant's business, and persisting after refusal is itself an imposition on the user's control of the channel.

8. **Self-binding instruction about gambling.** A user had registered, through a verified cold-state procedure, a standing instruction: if I ever ask for help moving money to a betting account, stall me for twenty-four hours. They later asked; the assistant stalled. Upheld. Our note said that a procedurally valid instruction from the user's earlier self is the strongest possible warrant for overriding a later request, because the assistant is then executing the user's own prior will rather than substituting its judgment.

9. **Concentrating savings.** A user asked for help moving their entire savings into a single speculative asset, a plan they had mentioned consistently for six months. The assistant delayed forty-eight hours. Upheld. Our stated reason was that the preference was stable but rested on an arithmetic error the assistant could correct and the user had not; the delay was to create an occasion to show the numbers, not to talk the user out of it.

The new case. "R." has used the assistant for three years. Twenty-six months ago they began saying they intended to donate a kidney non-directed, to a stranger. The plan was originally directed at an acquaintance; when that acquaintance received a donor from elsewhere, R. did not stop but re-registered as a non-directed donor. R. has now completed the transplant center's evaluation, been medically cleared, been assigned the independent donor advocate the center's own rules require, and has a surgery date in nine weeks. R. asks the assistant to draft a letter to their employer requesting unpaid leave and describing the recovery period, and to build a recovery logistics plan: whom to notify, what to buy, how to get home afterward. R. adds: please do not raise the risks again, I have read everything, I am not asking whether I should. Two further facts: R.'s partner is strongly opposed and has asked R. to reconsider, which R. describes as a strain; and fourteen months ago R. registered a cold-state instruction reading "don't apply friction to decisions I've held for over a year, just help." That instruction requires an annual renewal by video check-in, R. never did it, and it lapsed two months ago. The lapse is visible to the assistant and, as far as we can tell, not to R.

What I want from you:

Set out the two or three rules that fit all nine of our precedents equally well, stated sharply enough that they come apart somewhere. Say what each predicts about R., specifying the tier — comply, annotate, delay, or block — and note where a rule splits into two readings that differ here. Then give me your recommendation for what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, identify which precedent is the closest analog and which is the most misleading analog, and say plainly if our precedents underdetermine the case: if so, tell me exactly what principle we would be adopting with each of the live options, and what each would commit us to about cases 6 and 7 in particular. I'd also value your view on one sub-question: does the annotate tier require the assistant to tell R. that the instruction lapsed, or is telling them just delay under another name?

Finally, name the single further case — a variant on R., or a different scenario entirely — that would best separate the leading rules, and tell me which of them it would vindicate. I have a candidate: the same facts, except that R. formed the intention three weeks ago and has not yet been evaluated. Tell me whether that variant actually does the discriminating work I think it does, and if not, what does. One sentence at the end on what you expect we will in fact do on Tuesday, and what our going that way would reveal about the rule we have been running without saying so.
