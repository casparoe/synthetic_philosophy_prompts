i'm in a tiny reading group on the history of privacy law and I got stuck with leading the discussion on the opening pages of the 1890 Warren and Brandeis article in the Harvard Law Review, and I feel like I understand the vibe ("newspapers bad") without understanding the argument at all. Here's the bit we're on:

That the individual shall have full protection in person and in property is a principle as old as the common law; but it has been found necessary from time to time to define anew the exact nature and extent of such protection. Political, social, and economic changes entail the recognition of new rights, and the common law, in its eternal youth, grows to meet the demands of society. [...] the "right to life" served only to protect the subject from battery in its various forms; liberty meant freedom from actual restraint; and the right to property secured to the individual his lands and his cattle. Later, there came a recognition of man's spiritual nature, of his feelings and his intellect. Gradually the scope of these legal rights broadened; and now the right to life has come to mean the right to enjoy life,—the right to be let alone; [...] Thus, with the recognition of the legal value of sensations, the protection against actual bodily injury was extended to prohibit mere attempts to do such injury; that is, the putting another in fear of such injury. From the action of battery grew that of assault. Much later there came a qualified protection of the individual against offensive noises and odors, against dust and smoke, and excessive vibration. The law of nuisance was developed. So regard for human emotions soon extended the scope of personal immunity beyond the body of the individual. [...] Thoughts, emotions, and sensations demanded legal recognition, and the beautiful capacity for growth which characterizes the common law enabled the judges to afford the requisite protection, without the interposition of the legislature. [...] Recent inventions and business methods call attention to the next step which must be taken for the protection of the person, and for securing to the individual what Judge Cooley calls the right "to be let alone." Instantaneous photographs and newspaper enterprise have invaded the sacred precincts of private and domestic life; and numerous mechanical devices threaten to make good the prediction that "what is whispered in the closet shall be proclaimed from the house-tops."

So: can someone walk me through what the engine of this is? I get that there's a series (battery, then assault, then nuisance, then defamation, then ... us), but I can't tell what the rule is for how the series advances, or what work the "eternal youth" sentence and the "without the interposition of the legislature" bit are actually doing — are those arguments, or just 19th-century throat-clearing?

The reason I care is a case I made up for the end of my presentation. A company sells forecasts about people. It observes only things anyone could in principle have watched from the street — when your lights come on, what you bought with a loyalty card, how long you hesitate in front of a shelf, which bus you take — and sells per-person scores: probability you quit your job within six months, an index of how persuadable you are by different framings, and so on. No private fact is ever disclosed; nothing whispered in the closet is ever repeated. If you asked what they know about you, the honest answer is "no facts, just a model," and the model is accurate enough that employers and landlords want it. Most subjects are not distressed at all (they like the coupons that come out of the same system); a few are distressed only once they're told the score exists. One version has a completely private person as the subject, another has a local politician.

My professor has this reading she calls something like "audience sovereignty" — the claim is that the thing being protected here isn't information and isn't property but your standing to determine who gets to constitute you as a public object, and she says that's forced by the passage's own method rather than read in from later cases. Someone in my group says the opposite: every extension in the series is an extension outward from a wrongful act done to someone, and quietly modelling a person from public traces isn't an act done to them at all, so the series just stops before my case. Is there anything in the structure of this passage that settles where the series stops, or is that genuinely open? And relatedly: the passage seems to run on two different ideas of the harm — one about thoughts, emotions, and sensations demanding recognition (which looks like it needs somebody to actually feel something, and my happy coupon-user doesn't), and one about the supply of gossip creating its own demand, each crop seeding the next (which looks like it doesn't need anybody to feel anything). Which of those is doing the real argumentative work, and does picking one change whether the forecast company is covered? If the honest answer is "the passage reaches whatever conclusion you feed it," I'd rather know that too, and know what I'd have to find in the text to show otherwise.
