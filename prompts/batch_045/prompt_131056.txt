I'm a software engineer by training — I build and train machine learning systems — and I have no formal philosophy background. I've been reading about predictive processing and the free energy principle in my spare time, and I think I'm stuck on something structural rather than on a detail, so I'd rather ask than keep guessing.

Here is the confusion, stated in terms I'm comfortable with. Suppose an agent carries a generative model of its sensory stream and is, in some sense, minimizing a bound on surprise. In my own work, "reduce the loss" is ambiguous between at least three quite different operations:

1. Change the parameters so the model's predictions better match data that has already arrived — perception and learning.
2. Act on the world so that incoming data matches predictions the model already makes — action, or what the literature calls active inference.
3. Change the precision assigned to prediction errors — the inverse variance, roughly the per-term weight in a weighted loss — so that the errors which are expensive or impossible to eliminate stop counting for much. This is the one the papers seem to identify with attention and gain control.

Writers on predictive processing appear to describe all three as instances of the same thing: minimizing free energy. That worries me. A theory in which updating your model, changing the world, and turning down the weight on the errors you cannot fix are all the same operation looks like a theory that cannot lose: for any observed behavior I can tell a story on which it was minimizing free energy. If that is right, the principle itself has no empirical content, and all the real explaining is done by the particular generative model you write down — the principle is then a constraint on how to write models, not a hypothesis about minds. But I suspect I'm being unfair, and that there is a principled story about what constrains each of the three routes. In particular I can't tell whether (3) is a genuine third route or whether precision is itself something the model has to estimate, so that fiddling with it is itself scored and cannot serve as a free escape hatch.

So I'd like the following explained:

(a) Is my three-way split fair, or does it misrepresent the view? Specifically, is there a distinction I'm collapsing — between minimizing variational free energy on data already in hand and minimizing expected free energy over candidate policies — that dissolves the worry, because the second quantity contains terms the first does not, some of them pragmatic and some of them information-seeking?

(b) Take the dark room case: an organism that could apparently minimize prediction error by parking itself in a dark, silent room. What exactly blocks that conclusion? I have seen it answered by saying the organism's priors encode what kind of thing it is, so it can no more opt for the dark room than my laptop can opt to run at 500 degrees. I don't trust my grip on why that answer isn't just relabeling "the organism tends to want what it wants" in information-theoretic vocabulary.

(c) Is the name doing real work, or is it a pun? Is this the same quantity as the Helmholtz free energy of thermodynamics, an analogy to it, or a strict identity that holds only under assumptions nobody bothers to state?

(d) What would I have to observe, or fail to observe, to conclude the framework was wrong? I'd like something concrete enough that I could imagine an empirical paper reporting it — not a philosophical objection. If the honest answer is that the free energy principle is better understood as a modeling framework or a way of writing down hypotheses rather than as a testable empirical claim, please say so plainly and tell me what that implies for the stronger claims made in its name.

I'm happy to be told that one of my questions is confused, provided you say which distinction I've missed. Please don't give me citations, references, or reading lists — I can't look anything up from where I'm working. I'd much rather have the informal mechanism-level story than the derivation, though a little notation is fine if you explain it as you go.
