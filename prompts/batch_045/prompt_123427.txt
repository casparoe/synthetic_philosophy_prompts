I'm a third-year undergraduate taking an interdisciplinary seminar on agency and embeddedness — about half philosophy students, half CS — and I have to lead next week's fifty-minute tutorial. I've been assigned the cluster of problems around an agent's values depending on the ontology of its world-model: a utility function defined over high-level categories that a learned model supplies, where further learning can shift, split, or eliminate those categories, and where the agent then has to decide whether to keep tracking the original referents or to reinterpret its values inside the revised model. I also want to include the further point that some of what bears on that decision is not merely unknown but inaccessible: two candidate world-models can agree on every observation sequence the agent could ever receive while disagreeing about the extension of a category its values depend on, so no further data and no amount of inference discriminates between them.

Don't write the explanation for me. Give me a plan for the fifty minutes.

My draft order is: (1) utility as a function of model-relative features rather than of the world directly; (2) world-models get revised under evidence; (3) what revision does to categories — drift, eviction, splitting; (4) the resolution rule an agent would need, conservative versus reinterpretive; (5) inaccessible information; (6) why this is neither out-of-distribution robustness nor general skepticism about reference. I suspect (5) has to come before (4), because the resolution rule only looks like a real choice once the students see that evidence cannot settle it — but I'd rather you tell me what actually depends on what, which of my six items can be merged or dropped, and roughly how many minutes each block deserves.

I'm stuck on the example that should carry the whole session. Candidates: a system whose values are defined over persons, facing brain emulations; a conservation-planning system whose values are defined over species, which then learns that the groups it was tracking are not natural kinds — ring species, horizontal gene transfer — so that whether two populations are the same species turns both on facts about actual gene flow and on which of several competing species concepts its own values meant; and an endowed foundation whose founding document's terms eventually describe nothing. My worry is that the first reads as futurism and drags in personal identity and consciousness, and that the third makes the problem look like ordinary semantic drift, which I take to be a shallower and different problem. Pick one, say what the carrying example has to contain and to lack, and say precisely why each loser fails — including whether my diagnoses of their failures are right.

The misconceptions I expect, in the order I expect them: from the CS half, that this is just distributional shift and the fix is more diverse training data; from the philosophy half, that this is Quinean underdetermination of reference and there is nothing special about artificial agents; then, from somewhere, that defining the utility over low-level physical states or raw inputs makes the problem vanish; that a Bayesian agent with enough evidence converges on the right ontology; that the agent can simply keep asking the humans what they meant; and, at the end, that the answer is obviously to freeze the original referents. For each, tell me the exact point in the sequence at which it should be headed off, and which two of them are the same mistake in two vocabularies and should therefore be disposed of once, in terms both halves of the room can hear. Tell me which misconception I've failed to anticipate.

Two further constraints. The inaccessible-information point needs to land as a specific question with a determinate answer that the agent's evidence can never reach, not as a global underdetermination thesis — how do I keep it concrete in about two minutes? And I want to state openly, rather than silently skip, one or two things I refuse to explain at this level; my candidates are whether any existing system exhibits the problem, the mechanics of Bayesian model averaging, the metaphysics of natural kinds, and the safety-engineering literature around it. Which refusals are worth saying out loud, and which would look like evasion?

Finally, give me one closing question that distinguishes understanding from its appearance — something that can't be answered by repeating a slogan. My own candidate is to hand them a case of splitting and ask, in one sentence, what the conservative and the reinterpretive rule would each do and what would count as a reason to prefer one; use a better one if you have it, and tell me what an answer would have to contain before I should count the session as having worked.
