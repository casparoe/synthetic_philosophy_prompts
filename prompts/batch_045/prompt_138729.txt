hi — Im in the back half of a decision theory option this term and I also sit on the committee for my uni's student-run seed fund, which is where this is actually coming from. We hand out about twenty grants a term, £150 to £2,000, and we have to rank the whole pile in one two-hour meeting. Most are boring (society events, a bike repair workshop). But every term there's two or three of the "almost certainly nothing, tiny chance of something very big" shape — last term it was a group open-sourcing some protocol and someone doing a cheap high-throughput screen for a longevity-ish thing. And since we did the Pasadena game in class Ive got a knot in my head about whether our ranking exercise is even well-defined for those ones. You know the game: fair coin until the first heads on the nth toss, payoff ±2^n/n with the sign alternating, expectation comes out as a conditionally convergent series, so you can reshuffle the terms to make it sum to whatever you like — it has no expected value in the strong sense at all, only some weak, order-dependent one.

So what I want is a procedure, not more theory. Something five people with no more than first-year stats can run in ten minutes per proposal, in a spreadsheet, with three-point estimates rather than distributions, and that says where it stops with a no and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is about values not facts. (Anything needing a real elicitation session is dead on arrival, and the finance office signs off on anything over £1,000, so the output has to be something I can defend to them in a paragraph.)

The one idea of my own I want you to either build in or tell me is junk: rearrangement-invariance as a test. If ranking A above B changes depending on the order you enumerate the payoff states in, or on where you truncate the tail, the procedure should refuse to rank that pair and kick it to a human with a stated reason. I'd like that cashed out properly — how do you actually check for it with no distribution, and is invariance to reordering the same demand as robustness to truncation, or are those two separate tests? Also: at what point do we give up on expectations entirely and rank by something tamer (medians of a truncated distribution? stochastic dominance where it applies?), what we do when we simply can't get a number for the tail, and which steps are honestly just judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three: the longevity screen (probability of anything real maybe 1%, upside nobody can put a number on), the open-source protocol (heavy tail but there's a soft ceiling), and the bike workshop (small, near-certain payoff). Apply it straight — I should say I really want to fund the bio one and I'd rather be told the procedure ranks it below the bike workshop than have it fudged. British English please.
