So I spent about thirty-five years running an analytical chemistry lab — mostly HPLC and sample prep, some method development — retired four years ago, and for the past year or so I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the senior center, which is mostly retirees plus one very patient grad student who runs it. We did a session on knowing how vs. knowing that, and I got asked to write down where I'd actually landed so the others could poke holes in it. I never took a philosophy class in my life, so don't assume I know any of the jargon; plain language please. And one thing about the format: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email to someone.

Here's the set of things I believe, more or less:

(1) Knowing how to do something is having an ability, not having a stock of true propositions in your head. My evidence is my own hands: I could see a titration endpoint coming before the meter did, and I could tell a column was dying from the shape of the baseline, and I could never have written down what I was responding to even if you'd paid me. Also the reverse: we had a grad student once who could recite the entire separation mechanism from memory and ruined every prep she touched.

(2) But being told can genuinely be enough. One of my postdocs read our extraction protocol straight through once, never watched anyone do it, and ran it correctly the first time. So testimony can hand you the know-how — and I don't just mean the reading caused her to go practice and get good at it; I mean it was enough right then and there.

(3) Know-how comes in degrees. You can know how to run an assay better or worse, and that's a real difference in the person, not just a difference in how we happen to describe them.

(4) Knowing that doesn't come in degrees — you either know that the mobile phase is wrong or you don't. Though I guess people do say things like "she knows that assay inside out," so maybe I'm wrong about this one.

(5) Know-how survives being totally unable to perform. I'd of said I still know how to run a TLC plate, even though with the tremor in my hands now I couldn't do one to save my life. And I'd say our instrument guy knows how to fix the mass spec even when he's asleep.

(6) Kids and animals have know-how without know-that. Whatever my cat knows about landing on her feet, it isn't a bunch of propositions.

(7) Our whole lab knew how to run the old assay, and nobody in the lab knew that step 7 was load-bearing. Step 7 is a twenty-minute wait at four degrees in the middle of the prep, it came from a postdoc who left in the late nineties and left no note, and if you skip it the yield falls off a cliff. Everybody does it, nobody knows why, and we tried dropping it once — disaster.

(8) Same lab, different case: "does she know how to run it" seems to shift with the conversation. My old tech knows how to run it when we're chatting about who's on the schedule, and doesn't know how to run it when we're deciding who to put on the contaminated batch. I take that to be about where we set the bar, not about anything that changed in her.

(9) Methodological thing, and maybe the one I care most about: I want an account of know-how that somebody could actually go test. I have very little patience with made-up intuition pumps; I'd rather hear what the motor-control and procedural-memory people have actually found. Show me an experiment.

So: does that hang together? What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Tell me which of these actually pull against each other, ranked by how badly, and for each real clash say what would have to give and what it would cost me to give it. Be equally straight with me about the ones that only look like clashes — I don't want to drop commitments just because two of them sound odd sitting next to each other. If I'm quietly leaning on something I haven't stated out loud, name it. And if one of my items is doing less work for me than I think it is, say so. Don't send me off to read anything, by the way: I can't get to a university library easily these days, and I'd much rather have your reasoning than a list of names.
