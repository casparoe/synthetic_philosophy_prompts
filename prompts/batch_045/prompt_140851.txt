This started as an argument at work. I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond reading stuff on the side, and a few weeks back we had an incident where the service was overloaded and we had to shed load: kill one big nightly batch job that a single internal team was waiting on, or throttle a few thousand users' interactive requests by a barely-noticeable amount each. We throttled everyone, my mate thought that was obviously right, I wasn't convinced, and it turned into one of those 2am post-mortem conversations that nobody actually resolves. Since then I've ended up reading about Scanlon's contractualism and what people call the aggregation problem — the rough idea being that if rightness is about what nobody could reasonably reject, and complaints get weighed one person against another, then there's nowhere obvious for the sheer number of people on one side to do any work, which strikes me as both weirdly appealing and completely unusable for the decisions I actually have to make. The purpose I have in mind, if it helps pin things down: I want something I could put in a design doc as a rule for allocating a scarce resource, defend to colleagues who don't share my gut reactions, and apply again next quarter — not just a verdict on this one incident.

So what I want isn't the verdict, and defintely not a reading list. I want the spec: the desiderata that a theory of aggregation has to meet to be worth using. What it has to get right, which cases it has to handle and what each case is actually doing the work of showing, which distinctions people collapse that it shouldn't, and which of the usual demands are real requirements as opposed to a prejudice of one camp. Here are the cases I want it built around, spelled out so you don't have to guess at the setup: (1) the one that always comes up first — save one person's hand, or stop a few thousand people each getting a mild electric shock; (2) the pure tie — two strangers, identical need, you can help exactly one, is tossing a coin acceptable and does it change anything if it's two on one side and one on the other; (3) the near-tie — one person loses something big, or N people each lose something a hair smaller, with N sliding from two up to a million, and the question is whether a theory owes us an answer about where numbers start winning or is entitled to stay silent there; (4) the case I made up and can't get out of my head, threshold or emergent harms, where nobody has a complaint at all until enough people are affected: the service is fine at 900 requests a second and falls over completely at 1000, and no individual request is the one that broke it, or a language or a protocol dies once enough people stop using it. A view that only ever puts person A's complaint next to person B's — where does it even register that kind of harm, and is registering it a requirement or a nice-to-have; (5) the same thing under uncertainty, where you don't know whose hand or whose shock it will be, so it's risks rather than outcomes being weighed.

Two more things I care about more than the rest. First: which of these requirements can't all hold at once? My guess is there's no way to have pairwise comparability, some minimal Pareto-ish thought that a bigger number of people being better off can't just be irrelevant, and a clean transitive ranking of options all at the same time — something like a Condorcet cycle but for complaints — but I can't see where the trade-off has to be made, and if a different pair is the actually impossible one, say that instead. Second: which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and which ones are smuggled in as requirements when they're really just preferences — my own suspects are never adding up welfare across people, and always producing a total ordering with no ties left over. A couple of lines at the end on how the two obvious approaches do — add up the total versus compare one at a time — is fine, but the spec is the part I'm after. And tell me plainly where a theory is allowed to shrug instead of being forced to answer.
