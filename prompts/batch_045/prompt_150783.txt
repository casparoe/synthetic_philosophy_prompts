I'm in a reading group that's roughly half political scientists and half philosophers (plus two ML people who got pulled in through the whole "could it happen here" discourse after 2020), and we have now spent something like a year an a half on democratic backsliding without anyone changing their mind even slightly. One camp thinks it's a real and recently dominant pattern: incumbents who were legitimately elected use legal, incremental means to disable the things that could remove them — courts, election administration, the media environment, the civil service — so that democracy dies by a thousand cuts rather than by coup. Everyone cites Hungary after 2010 and Turkey around the 2017 constitutional referendum as the clean cases, and Poland under PiS as a case that then partially reversed when they lost in 2023, which some people treat as the most interesting data point we have. The second camp thinks the whole concept is basically a partisan honorific: any institutional reform the other side dislikes gets called backsliding, the label is applied retrospectively to governments that were popularly elected and legally acted, and there's no agreed point at which a country has crossed the line, so "backsliding" ends up meaning "a government I disapprove of changed institutions". A third group wants to restrict the term to cases where the incumbent's realistic ability to lose an election has been genuinely impaired, which puts Hungary and Turkey in, puts Poland in with an asterisk because it reversed, and mostly pushes the US out of the set — or puts it in only at the level of rhetoric and norm violation, like refusing to commit to accepting results, rather than at the level of changed capacity. And of course there's the parallel dispute about whether we're even talking about the same thing as "democratic breakdown" or "authoritarianization" or just normal constitutional hardball that democracies have always had.

Here's my own hypothesis and I'd like you to push on it rather than just agree. My guess is that very little of the stalling is about facts — nobody in the group disputes that Fidesz rewrote the electoral rules, that PiS went after the courts, or that a large share of one American party's voters came to believe the 2020 election was stolen — and that most of it is (a) a disagreement about which cases count as data, i.e. about the extension of the concept rather than its content, and (b) a hidden empirical question nobody can answer because we have maybe a dozen usable cases: what is the base rate at which these processes reverse, consolidate into something that clearly isn't a democracy, or just sit there indefinitely in a hybrid state. To try to break this up I've been using a three-way distinction in my own notes: formal rule change (the written rules are rewritten), rule application (the rules are the same but enforced selectively, in a partisan way, or not at all), and what I've been calling ecosystem capture (nothing official changes, but media ownership, the senior civil service, the business elite and the funding of civil society all quietly reorient toward the incumbent). I like it because it lets you say a country is backsliding on one axis and not another, but I can't tell whether it's actually carving at a joint or just giving me three nicer words for "the government did stuff I don't like."

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal, a substantive dispute about how much the concept should be allowed to stretch, several different questions wearing one name (a classificatory one for comparativists, a predictive one about where this ends up, and a normative one about when it becomes permissible for actors to break norms themselves to stop it), a factual dispute about base rates that we currently lack the cases to settle, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether democracy is a threshold you're either over or under versus a many-dimensional continuum? Tell me where you think the weight actually lies, using what each side really argues rather than a survey, and say whether my three-way distinction helps diagnose anything or is confused. Then tell me what progress would even look like here given your diagnosis — an agreed list of indicators fixed in advance, more cases and more time, a formal model of incumbent incentives, dropping the term in favour of more specific ones — or whether this is one of those disputes where we should expect no resolution and can say why. Please also be explicit about epistemic status: separate what's reasonably settled among people who actually work on this from what's genuinely contested, don't present a contested position as the consensus just because it's the one you find most plausible, and flag the places where you're guessing or where your own view is doing the work.
