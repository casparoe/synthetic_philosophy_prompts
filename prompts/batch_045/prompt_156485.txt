we keep having this argument in my forecasting reading group (very rationalist-adjacent, you can guess the vibe) and one guy ends it every time with "bayesians can't agree to disagree". he used it most recently to claim: if two calibrated superforecasters on the same question have swapped numbers five or six times and are still 20 points apart, then either someone is being irrational or someone is sitting on private evidence they wont share. i think that's doing way more work than the theorem actually does, but i cant say where exactly it overreaches, and whenever i push back he just restates the slogan louder.

so: lay out the menu of precise claims that one-liner could be expressing, from the near-tautological reading (something about a common prior plus common knowledge of posteriors) up through the genuinely wild ones (that a difference in priors is itself a species of irrationality, or that rational agents can't disagree about anything decision-relevant). for each, say whether it's trivial, whether it's substantive and defensible, or whether it's just false — and, the part i actually need — which readings would really get him his conclusion about those two forecasters, and what extra premise each of those readings needs that he hasn't stated (and probably doesn't know he's relying on). also tell me which reading people usually mean when they say it, because i dont think he means the same thing twice.

here are the cases i think break it; for each, tell me which readings it kills and which survive:

1. they stop after six rounds. neither ever said anything about knowing the other's number, and each privately thinks the other is probably miscalibrated on this class of question.
2. they openly dont share a prior. one thinks base rates on "unprecedented" events are basically uninformative, the other thinks you can always dig up a reference class. both know this about each other and neither budges.
3. what they disagree about isn't really an event: one says 0.08 that "the system would have been safe if we'd shipped it in march", the other says 0.3, and nothing will ever resolve it.
4. my own bugbear, made up by our group so there's no literature to recite: trust-weighted updating. each discounts the other's reported number by a private "how much do i trust her on this kind of question" factor, both know theyre doing it, and the factors differ, and the factors themselves get updated on the observed disagreement. does the slogan have anything to say here at all, or is it simply out of scope because the updating rule isn't bayesian?

be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the one claim you're least sure of.
