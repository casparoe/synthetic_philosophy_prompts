I write fiction, and there is a scene in the novel I am finishing that I cannot get right, because I cannot tell how much weight an argument one of my characters makes will in fact bear. A hospice chaplain, speaking to a mother who has just lost a child, says that God is like a novelist and that we are his characters. She wants three things out of the comparison: first, that a novelist cannot simply decree that his characters behave well — compel them and they are puppets, and the love he wants from them has to be freely given, so real freedom requires the real possibility of evil; second, that the author does not stand inside the moral order of the story in the way the characters do, so calling him cruel is a category mistake, of a piece with accusing me of assault because I wrote a fistfight; third, that a story without conflict is not worth telling, so suffering is not waste but material, taken up into a whole whose shape no one can see from inside a single scene. The conclusion it is meant to license is that an omnipotent and wholly good God's permission of suffering — including the suffering of children — is intelligible.

I would like the scene to be fair, so I would rather know what the comparison can carry than arrange the exchange around a verdict I have already reached. Please audit the analogy rather than adjudicate the conversation. Set out the mapping explicitly — what plays the role of the author, the characters, the plot, the reader, the conventions of the form, the choice to include or omit a given scene — and then say where it breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the three jobs above from the ones that are merely local failures of fit. I know a novelist is not literally omnipotent or omniscient, and I do not need to be told so.

Things I would like weighed specifically: the comparison appears to need the author to be constrained when constraint is wanted and sovereign when sovereignty is wanted, and I cannot tell whether that is an equivocation or two separable features; the suffering of a character is ordinarily suffered by no one, so making the analogy grip seems to require stipulating that the characters are conscious, at which point the author begins to look less like a parent than like someone running a simulation; novelists are in fact answerable to readers for cruelty that the plot did not require, and my own sense is that I can tell the difference between suffering the book could not be the book without and suffering I put into a scene for local effect, while from inside the story no character could tell them apart; and the analogy quietly imports closure, composition and an authorial purpose, which are precisely what is at issue, together with the charge that persons are being used as means to the value of the whole — the charge Ursula Le Guin makes vivid in her story of a city whose happiness rests on one miserable child. I would also like to know whether an argument of this shape does anything at all against the evidential version of the problem once it has answered the logical one, and whether the author version does better or worse than the older comparison in which God is a parent who lets a child fall off a bicycle: a parent, unlike an author, at least suffers alongside the child and is a moral agent within the story's own order, or so my other character says. Finally, tell me whether the consolation survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if some other analogy would do the same work better, or whether the work is not the kind an analogy can do. I have spent twenty years watching characters refuse to do what I want them to do, and I have never been able to say whether that is a limit on me or something I do to myself; that is the only authority I bring to the question.
