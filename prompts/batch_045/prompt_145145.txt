hey — I work on offline policy evaluation and bandits with heavy-tailed rewards, so most of my day is importance ratios whose variance is infinite, tail indices I can't estimate from the data I actually have, and estimators whose sample means just refuse to settle down. People keep telling me the St. Petersburg paradox is "the same shape" as this stuff and I want to know how much of that is real.

Here's the version I care about, because it's the one that makes me itch. Standard setup: coin, payoff 2^n where n is the number of heads before the first tail, expected value infinite, nobody pays more than a few bucks. Now make it worse: the coin's bias p is unknown, drawn from a prior with positive mass on p ≥ 1/2, and I get one play (or a handful). Then no finite run of data drives the posterior mass on p ≥ 1/2 to zero, and conditional on p ≥ 1/2 the expectation is infinite — so the predictive expectation stays infinite no matter what I observe. It isn't an idealization that more data could eventually correct. Related itch: if the payoff is 2^n/n instead of 2^n the expectation is still infinite, and the probability of a payoff that big is unchanged, so the "the tail is so thin you'll never see it" complaint doesn't obviously do any work.

So: restate the paradox and the main families of answer — Bernoulli's diminishing marginal utility / log utility line, the bounded-utility and truncation replies, and the newer "you're averaging over the wrong thing, average over time not over counterfactual players" growth-rate / ergodicity line — in the vocabulary I already use: importance weights, self-normalized estimators, α-stable limits, clipped weights, median-of-means, tail index, on-policy vs off-policy, long-run log growth. I'm fluent in all of that; don't explain it back to me.

Then the part I actually want, the residue report. What translates cleanly? What only translates if you quietly add an assumption my framework doesn't carry (that the gamble is repeated with reinvestment, that I'm being asked for a price rather than for a policy, that there's a bankroll to grow)? And what has no counterpart at all — does "what is this worth to you, once" express anything in a vocabulary where every quantity is a long-run average or a limit of an estimator? Be blunt about whether the residue is a gap in my framework or a sign that some of these "resolutions" are relabelings: is log utility a discovery about value or just a choice of concave function, and is switching to time averages an answer to the paradox or a change of subject? No literature tour, and don't leave me with "it depends on your risk preferences."
