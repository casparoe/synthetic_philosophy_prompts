Hi. I'm a novelist, and I definately let myself get talked onto the board of a small nonprofit press last year — they do translations of things nobody else will touch. We vote Friday and I'm supposed to circulate a written opinion beforehand, so please draft the memo I send the other four.

Here's the case. In 1979 the press put out an English translation of a novel set in a handful of mountain valleys that emptied out in the sixties (I'm not naming the real region here, and I'm changing the title). It's narrated by a young woman in a household where, when someone is wasting away and going to die slowly, the adults privately decide when to stop feeding her — and nobody tells her. That half people can sort of process. The half they can't is the rest: after that the household keeps up the ordinary fiction completely, right to the end, because in that world a person who knows she's being let go becomes — literally, in their idiom — a stranger in her own house, and the household's first job is to keep her whole. Their word gets translated as "keeping whole." The last scene is the narrator carrying a bowl of soup she knows won't be drunk up the stairs, and the book plainly wants you to see that as her becoming a grown-up.

A few hundred descendants are still around, mostly in cities, with a newsletter and two factions. One, mostly older, wants it back in print with a short preface by them saying it was the last thing that was theirs and asking readers not to judge it by hospital ethics. The other, younger, including an ICU nurse and a lawyer, says a reissue that doesn't say plainly that this was starving people and lying to them is an obscenity — and adds that it wasn't "their morality" at all, it was what the grandmothers did to people who couldn't argue back. Our lawyer says there's no exposure: it's historical, nobody identifiable. Money is real though: one reissue eats about a third of the season.

We've narrowed to three: (A) reissue with the elders' preface unedited; (B) reissue with an afterword we commission, naming in so many words what an outsider would call the practice, novel left alone; (C) no reissue, scan it into a research archive with notes. There's a fourth nobody likes: both framings, inside one first, press pays both groups, and we go out of our way not to say which is right.

In the meeting "relativism" is doing about four different jobs and I want the memo to pull them apart and say which ones actually support which option: the plain descriptive claim about what they believed; the truth-relative claim, plus the question of whose framework counts when the descendants disagree and one nurse belongs to two at once; the claim that their vocabulary has no slot for what we'd call lying, so that maybe there isn't a disagreement to have; and the "we should suspend judgment" move — which I'm pretty sure is compatible with the practice being wrong, and people keep treating it as if it weren't. Our board has a retired judge who keeps insisting on a conflicts-of-law analogy: a code with no rules for what to do when it meets another code isn't an alternative code, it's an unfinished one. Deal with that too.

One thing I care about as a novelist: the novel doesn't assert anything, it dramatizes. The afterword does assert. So the fight is really about the paratext, and the option that looks most "neutral" may be the one doing the most editorial work — I want that said explicitly.

Bottom line please: pick one of the four (or a fifth), say who commissions what by when and what we do if one faction walks, and give whatever option loses the best version of itself — I think the strongest one is that translating "keeping whole" into starving-and-deceiving is itself a seizure of the vocabulary, and a press like ours doing that to a dead community it can't consult is its own kind of arrogance. Two pages max, and no footnotes or bibliography, the board won't read them and I don't have time to chase them.
