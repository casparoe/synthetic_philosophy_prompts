A friend sent me this and I've been going in circles on it for a week. Take the gender-equality paradox: gendered career and field-of-study choices look, if anything, *more* pronounced in rich, egalitarian countries than in poorer, less equal ones. His argument:

(1) A cultural explanation of a group difference says the difference is produced by pressure and blocked options; so, other things equal, less pressure should mean a smaller gap.
(2) So gap size is a readout of how much cultural pressure there is — bigger gap, more culture.
(3) The gaps are biggest exactly where the pressure is lowest.
(4) So for these gaps the cultural story isn't just unproven, it's false: what you see in the Nordics isn't what women get pushed into, it's what they choose when nothing is pushing.
(5) And outcomes nobody is pushed into aren't the kind of thing justice can object to.
(6) So occupational segragation in rich countries isn't an injustice, and quotas and the rest are aimed at nothing.

I half believe (6) and I still think the argument is broken, because (1)–(4) prove way too much: run the same steps on any difference visible in a rich, free society — hours worked, who cooks, whether you eat meat, Danes vs. Americans on basically anything — and no cultural story is ever available for anything a rich society does, which is absurd. Run them backwards and you'd have to say poverty is what makes men and women similar in poor countries. So which step do you throw out, and what does throwing it out cost me? I'd rather keep (3) and (6) than give up (1) and (2). One more worry I can't place: "chosen freely" doesn't seem to mean the same thing as "not caused by culture" — people can want things because of the culture they grew up in without anyone forcing them — but I don't know where that bites, or whether making that move leaves anyone able to tell the two explanations apart from country-level data at all.
