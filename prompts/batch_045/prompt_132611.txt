I help run a weekend airsoft club — about forty regulars, we write our own scenarios, and I keep a spreadsheet after every game day so I can tell which ones were actually balanced. One of our regulars, Mara, has turned into the person we all ask whenever a scenario turns on probability: she used to do competitive forecasting online, she is completely at ease with base rates, likelihood ratios, Brier scores and that sort of thing, and she has strong opinions that she states as numbers. Her track record on the classic puzzles is very consistent. Given the standard Monty Hall setup, with a host who knowingly always opens a goat door, she says switch and quotes two-thirds; but if the host picks a door at random and just happens to reveal a goat, she says it is a coin flip and there is no point switching. In the three prisoners version she says one-third for the prisoner who asked and two-thirds for the other one, but changes it to even odds if you stipulate that the guard always names B when he is free to. Confronted with a sensor that is right 80% of the time and wrong 20% whichever way the truth goes, on a fifty-fifty prior, she moves the probability to 0.8 and refuses to round it to 1.

The reason I am writing is that she has also given three verdicts about *conditional* information, and I think they do not add up, or at least that they leave something open. (1) You already know for certain the card is red; a fully reliable informant tells you "if the card is red, it is a heart"; she moves hearts to one-half, treating the conditional as saying something. (2) You already know the card is black; the same informant says "if the card is red, it is a heart"; she says nothing changes about red or black, though she would want to know why he was bothering to tell her. (3) A doctor says "if the test comes back positive, you have the disease"; she said firmly that this is not remotely the same as being told the test did come back positive — but when I pushed her for a number she would not give one, which now looks like a tell. So here is the scenario I am building for next month. A player is blindfolded, driven around, and dropped somewhere on a square field divided into four equal quadrants: two in Blue (friendly) territory and two in Red, and within each territory one quadrant is the ridge and one is the swamp. Before the drop she knows nothing but the layout, so each quadrant starts at 0.25. Her radio operator, who is watching a screen that shows her position and is scrupulously truthful, says: "I can't tell you where you are, but if you are in Red territory, you are in the swamp." Multiple people in the club have given me different answers for the probability that she is in Red territory — a third, a half, and even 1 — and when I asked Mara she said it depends on something about the operator that I had not specified, which I found evasive but which I now suspect is the whole point.

So: what is Mara's number most likely to be, and what rule is she running? I would like the candidate rules that fit every verdict I listed above, what each of them predicts about the radio message, which prediction you would bet on and why, and — most useful to me — the further version of the scenario that would force the rules apart. My own two candidates are: run the same drop with one Blue quadrant and three Red quadrants (does she say Red is a half or three-quarters?), and have the operator say "you are not in the Red ridge quadrant" instead of the conditional, which carries exactly the same information but does not mention Red (if she gives different numbers for those two, is that a bug in her rule or a feature?). If there is a better test than either of mine, say so. And if the answer really does hinge on the operator's procedure — whether she mentions the territory you are actually in, whether her message was fixed before she saw the screen, and so on — tell me which fact I should nail down in the scenario write-up and which way it pushes the number, because I would rather design the scenario so the answer is determinate than leave it to an argument at the briefing. A friend of mine says this is a version of something philosophers have argued about for decades, involving a soldier dropped behind enemy lines, but I have never been able to find the paper and I don't want references or a reading list; I want the reasoning, in the same terms I have used here, and a number I can put in the spreadsheet.
