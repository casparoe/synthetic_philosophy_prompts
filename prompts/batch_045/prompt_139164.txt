Hi — I'm on the operations board of a housing association in Sheffield, we look after about 4,000 homes, and we're rewriting the KPIs for our repairs contract for the next financial year. I've been reading a bit about Goodhart's law and specification gaming lately — the general idea that once you start rewarding people for a number, the number gradually stops telling you anything about the thing you actually cared about — and the effect has been that I can no longer look at any of our draft metrics without immediately seeing three ways to game it. My colleagues are getting annoyed with me, so I'd like an outside opinion that's a bit more systematic than my paranoia.

Here's the shortlist we're arguing over (all of these are things we could technically measure with the data our contractor already sends us):

- average calendar days from a repair being reported to it being marked complete
- the percentage of jobs completed inside the 28-day statutory window
- average days to completion, but with any job that gets reopened within 7 days excluded from the calculation (we added this last year to stop people closing jobs prematurely to hit the target — I'm not sure it helped)
- the share of tenants who rate the repair 4 or 5 on our post-repair SMS survey
- our "comeback rate": the share of jobs reopened within 30 days
- the number of proactive block inspections completed per quarter
- a composite "repairs health index" that averages the timing metric, the satisfaction score and the comeback rate, with weights we set ourselves
- a one-in-twenty blind re-inspection: a different inspector revisits a random sample and scores whether the repair was actually done properly, and this gets reported to the board but isn't used to calculate anyone's pay

Could you sort these into tiers by how quickly you'd expect each to get Goodharted — say A for "probably survives a year or so", B for "starts degrading within a couple of quarters", C for "gamed almost immediately" — and give each one a 0–100 Goodhart-risk score with a sentence on why it lands where it does? Then tell me which single one you'd actually make the headline target and which you'd keep as background monitoring only. I'm especially unsure about the blind re-inspection: is it safe because nobody's paid on it, or does it just rot more slowly?

One more thing before you start. Our colleague Nadia has proposed building in what she calls a "worsening allowance": we'd formally commit in advance that the headline number is allowed to get, say, 10% worse over the year without triggering any escalation or penalty, her argument being that most of the gaming happens because people are terrified of crossing a red line, so if you remove the cliff you remove the incentive. I quite like it, but I definately can't tell whether it's a real idea or just quietly lowering the bar, and I'd rather find out now than in month nine. Please give me your honest verdict on it, even if the verdict is that it's confused.

Be straight with me about how much of this is guesswork: give me a rough confidence on each tier placement and flag which ones you'd actually bet on versus which ones you're basically extrapolating from a handful of textbook examples. And if the whole exercise is the wrong shape — if you think these metrics can't meaningfully be ranked against each other, or that how quickly they rot depends far more on how the contract is enforced than on the wording of the metric — say that plainly instead of politely handing me a tidy list, because I'm going to have to defend whatever I bring to the next meeting. Please write in British English.
