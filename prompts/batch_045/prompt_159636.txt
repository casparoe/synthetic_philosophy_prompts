hey — I'm putting together a four-month fellowship application (small AI-policy shop, they want about two pages) and I need the proposal sharpened up before I send it off. The half-baked thing I keep circling: everyone in the AI governance world talks about "the alignment tax" as if it's a single number — how much capability or speed you give up in order to make a system safe — and then argues about whether competitive pressure (firms racing each other, states racing each other) forces actors to skip paying it. But I suspect the argument changes shape completely once you disaggregate the tax, and I don't think anyone's done that carefully.

Roughly the distinctions I have in mind: a capability tax (safe systems are just weaker at the task), a time tax (safety work delays you), a deployment or inference-compute tax (monitoring and oversight costs that persist after you ship), and what I'd call a variance tax — safety work doesn't only lower your expected capability, it raises your chance of being the actor who simply never ships at all. Then two complications that I think matter more than the size of the tax: first, whether the tax is uniform across racers or falls differentially on whoever would otherwise win, since a genuinely uniform tax shouldn't change who wins, only when anybody wins; second, whether the tax is endogenous to your position in the race — maybe oversight gets harder late (systems that game their evaluations) or cheaper late (tools and methods compound), which would mean the race has a shape, not just a speed. And then the point that alignment research is partly non-rival: the leader pays for it alone and everyone else inherits it, which might argue for being second rather than first, or for openness as a way of making the tax uniform.
