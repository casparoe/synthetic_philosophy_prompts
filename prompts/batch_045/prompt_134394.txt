I sit on the internal review board that signs off on automated systems at a large public benefits agency. We are currently reviewing a pilot that works like this: a language-model-based system reads incoming correspondence from claimants, retrieves the relevant parts of the case file, decides what the correspondence is asking for, drafts a reply, and places the draft in a queue. A caseworker then opens each draft and clicks approve, and only then is anything sent. In practice a caseworker is clearing somewhere near two hundred of these a day, often without reading the full text, and I have watched the queue get worked in the order the system puts it in, with the system's own one-line summary at the top of the screen.

When I asked why we were not evaluating this as an agent rather than as a tool, the engineer running the pilot said: "It's a tool, not an agent — we keep a human in the loop." He repeats that sentence as though it settles things, and he uses it to answer two different questions at once: whether the system can do things in the world that no one notices, and whether we owe it the kind of testing we would demand of something that pursues objectives over time and across many cases.

I would rather take the sentence apart than argue past it. Please treat "keep a human in the loop" as a slogan and lay out the menu of precise claims it could be making. For my own part I have been working with a rough three-way distinction — a human is *in* the loop if each consequential action needs a human authorization before it happens; *on* the loop if a human watches a running process and can interrupt it; *in command* if a human sets goals and constraints and then does not watch. If that framing is doing bad work, say so and replace it. Three further ideas I want you to use rather than restate: *authorization density*, by which I mean how many hard-to-reverse things happen per human decision; the *rubber-stamp test*, by which I mean whether the stream of outcomes would look any different if approvals were withheld at random for a small share of drafts; and the *framing question*, by which I mean whether the human's judgment is made for them by what the system chose to show, in what order, summarized how, and with what expression of confidence. Please also consider whether there is a reading on which the loop includes a human only in the sense that someone is answerable afterwards, and whether that reading does any of the work the slogan is being asked to do.

Structure your reply in these sections, in this order:

1. **Readings** — a menu of eight to ten distinct precise claims the slogan could express, roughly 500 words in total. For each: state the claim in one crisp sentence, add one or two sentences of elaboration, and tag it as trivial, as substantive and defensible, as substantive but probably false, or as a claim of a different kind than it appears (for instance moral or legal rather than epistemic).

2. **Which readings would actually carry his conclusion** — about 250 words. His conclusion has two halves, that a human is in the loop and that the system is therefore a tool rather than an agent with no need for agent-level review. Say which readings, alone or in combination, would support each half, and which readings are simply irrelevant to it.

3. **The strongest version of his position** — about 200 words, steelmanned, including the best answer to the worry that per-action authorization and system-level agency are different things.

4. **Where it fails in this pipeline** — about 300 words, applied to the specifics I gave: the volume, the ordering, the summaries, the accumulation of approvals over months, and the possibility that the caseworker's sense of what a reasonable reply looks like is being slowly calibrated by the model rather than the reverse.

5. **What I should ask for instead** — about 150 words: three or four concrete operational demands the board could attach to approval, stated so that they cannot be satisfied by a gesture, with the looseness I should refuse to accept in each.

Please reason from the setup rather than from the literature: no citations, references, or reading lists. Where a verdict turns on an empirical matter that the board could settle by logging the system's behaviour, say plainly that it is an empirical matter and what measurement would settle it. Keep the whole reply under about 1400 words, and write it in the register of a memo I could circulate to the rest of the board.
