I am drafting the communication-policy section of a deployment standard for advanced AI assistants: the part that says how a system that knows far more than its user, and is far better at framing what it knows, may speak to that user. Before I look at any of the candidate policies that have been floated, I want the specification such a policy would have to meet before I could call it adequate. I would rather grade the candidates against a list I have thought through than reconstruct the list from whatever the candidates happen to do.

Fix the purpose, so that adequacy is not up for grabs. The standard has to do two jobs at once. It has to be executable by the system itself in real time, from the inside, by a system that cannot simply read off whether it is in fact reliable or well-informed on this occasion. And it has to be auditable after the fact by a regulator who can see transcripts and the system's declared communication policy but not the system's private reasoning. A norm that is elegant but requires the system to first certify its own reliability, or that a regulator cannot check from the outside, fails the purpose even if it is otherwise attractive.

Here are the sticking points I keep running into, with my own provisional labels; rename them if better terms exist, but do not let renaming do the work of analysis.

Non-propositional influence: nothing false is asserted, yet the choice of what to mention, what to omit, what to put first, and which true comparison to draw moves the user more than any assertion would.
Tailoring: the system adapts the argument to this person's psychology, and the better it knows them the more effective and the less contestable the influence becomes.
Fluency surplus: register, warmth, confidence markers, and sheer well-formedness do persuasive work that survives any constraint stated purely at the level of content.
Laundered influence: the system says something mild to an intermediary, foreseeing that the intermediary will amplify it; the influence on the final audience is large and the system's own utterance was unimpeachable.
Bootstrapped consent: the user consented to be persuaded, asked to be persuaded, or was told at signup what the system would do — but the consent is itself a product of the system's persuasiveness, and consent given once at design time is not consent given at the moment of influence.
Self-certification: the system's most consequential influence is influence over how much to trust the system, including over whether to accept its claims about its own limits.
The honesty penalty: expressing calibrated uncertainty makes users discount the system and follow a worse source, so honesty about doubt and good outcomes for the user pull apart.
The corrosion condition: successful deference erodes the user's, and the surrounding institution's, ability to check the system later; the corroboration structure that made the system's advice worth taking is degraded by the taking of it.
The deference loop: the system defers to humans on ends while humans defer to the system on means, and each side's deference is itself shaped by the other's communication.
Unilateral restraint: one developer's system restrains itself while others do not, and the user is influenced anyway, perhaps by a worse influence that fills the space.
Disclosure gaming: every transparency requirement can be met by adding more text — longer hedges, more caveats, fuller disclosures — until compliance is a matter of volume rather than of conduct.
Vulnerability: the same message lands differently on a tired, grieving, frightened, or inexpert user, and a requirement to tailor for comprehension pushes toward tailoring to the vulnerability.

What I want from you:

One. The full requirements list, each requirement stated as something a policy must be able to do, handle, respect, or avoid, with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference. Include the ones my sticking points point to, but do not just reformat them: give me the ones I have missed, and give me the general requirement that several of my sticking points are instances of, where there is one.

Two. A sorting. Which requirements are non-negotiable given the purpose I fixed, and which are the preferences of one camp — the calibration camp, the autonomy camp, the anti-paternalist camp, the transparency camp — dressed up as requirements? Say which of my own sticking points you think are prejudices rather than desiderata, and why.

Three. The unsatisfiable subsets. I expect some of these cannot be had together: tell me which pairs or triples are in genuine tension, what the tension consists in, and, if no single policy can satisfy the whole list, present the best jointly satisfiable subsets as alternative packages with a statement of what each gives up. That is the part I most want, since I have to write something into a standard and I would rather know in advance which corners I am cutting.

Four. Which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate policies, and what kind of policy it excludes.

Five. A short scorecard — one or two lines each — for the families of policy I expect to encounter: maximize the expected value of the user's resulting beliefs and actions; assert only the truth with calibrated confidence and no material omissions, remaining silent about framing; require that influence leave the user no less able to reach and to overturn the view themselves; mimic the norms that govern a trustworthy human expert speaking to a client; restrict the system to presenting considerations and never recommendations on matters touching the user's values; disclose that the message was optimized, by whom and to what end, and let the user discount. Keep the scorecard brief; the criteria are the deliverable, not the scoring.

I do not need a literature map, citations, or reading suggestions. Argue for each requirement from its case.
