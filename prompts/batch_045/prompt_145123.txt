I'm finishing a PhD in philosophy and I've been invited to give a twenty-minute paper, with twenty minutes of questions after it, at a one-day workshop next month on pre-modern arguments for the immateriality of the mind. My paper is on Avicenna's flying man: the thought experiment in which we are asked to imagine a person brought into existence all at once, fully formed, suspended in the air with his limbs spread apart so that they do not touch one another, in darkness and silence, with no contact or resistance from the air, and with no internal bodily sensation either — no hunger, no proprioception, no feeling of his own organs — and who nonetheless, the argument says, cannot help affirming that he exists. My thesis is a complaint about the argument's reach. I want to distinguish three grades of self-awareness that I think the standard presentation runs together: existential self-awareness, E, where the flier is aware that there is awareness going on and that something exists; indexical self-awareness, I, where that awareness is presented to him as his own, as belonging to this subject rather than to another; and substantial self-awareness, S, where he is aware of himself as a single persisting thing that could in principle do all of this without any body at all. My claim is that the scenario supports E, that it gives us no reason to think it supports I, and that Avicenna needs S. Alongside that, I want to press a distinction between two versions of the setup that I think the usual telling blurs: the deprivation variant, in which an ordinary adult like us is suddenly stripped of every sensation, and the never-sensed variant, in which the flier has never had a single sensory experience and has no memory of any either. My hunch is that almost all of the intuitive pull of the thought experiment comes from the first version, where we quietly supply a lifetime of embodied experience, while only the second version could do the work the argument needs — and that once we are in the second version we have no grip on what the flier's cognition is like, so the argument either collapses into unsupported intuition or begs the question.

The room will be about twenty-five people, in three rough camps. Around a third are historians of philosophy working in the Arabic and Persian traditions; several read Avicenna in the original, and they care about what a text and its commentarial reception can be made to bear, and about anachronism — I expect them to be impatient with any framing that sounds like it was designed for a contemporary journal. Another third are analytic philosophers of mind who work on self-awareness, indexical thought, and immunity to error through misidentification; they will want to know whether my three grades are doing any work that distinctions already available in that literature don't do, and whether E without I is even coherent. There are also three or four early modernists who work on Descartes and who will be thinking about the cogito the entire time. My assigned commentator is a historian of the commentarial tradition who is sympathetic to my reading but has already written to say that he worries I am attributing to Avicenna a problem Avicenna would not have recognised as his own. I have a one-page handout with the E/I/S distinction and the two variants on it, so I can refer to them by name when answering.

What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defence of the thesis. Give me the eight to ten objections I will actually get, in the order they are likely to come, each phrased the way that part of the room would phrase it rather than the way I would — the historians' version of an objection should sound like a historian wrote it, and so should the analytic philosophers' and the Descartes people's — and for each one: a reply I could give in under two minutes, an honest note on where that reply is soft or where I should concede the point and retreat to a weaker claim, and a label for which group it comes from. I want at least two objections that turn specifically on anachronism or on what the text and its commentators can bear, not just on whether my argument is valid. I also want your advice on what I should concede in the first ninety seconds, before anyone asks anything, to keep the historians on side without giving up the analytic claim I care about. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks. My own fear is that someone will point out that the never-sensed variant quietly assumes that genuine self-awareness has to be a relation to something presented as a self, so that I have built into my premise the very Cartesian conclusion I accuse the argument of failing to establish; tell me whether that is really the dangerous objection, or whether there is a worse one I am not seeing. Please do not give me a bibliography or any citations — I know that literature and I will slot the references in myself; what I want is the argumentative shape of the question period.
