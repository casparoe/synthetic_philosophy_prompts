I'm putting together an essay on Jain anekāntavāda for a general-audience philosophy magazine (they want ~3500 words, no footnotes, and they get twitchy if I use more than two Sanskrit words per paragraph), and I've got a draft that I think has one genuinely new idea in it but probably also a fatal flaw I can't see. Could you read the notes below and give me comments? For context: I came at this from a philosophy-of-language angle, not a Sanskrit one — everything I know about the doctrine itself comes from secondary summaries and one very patient friend who did a module on Indian philosophy years ago, so please flag anything in the background section that's just wrong or garbled.

The setup as I have it: anekāntavāda is the claim that real things have infinitely many aspects, nayavāda is the accompanying theory of standpoints or partial viewpoints from which a claim can be made, and syādvāda is the sevenfold schema of qualified predication — the list usually given runs something like: in some respect it is; in some respect it is not; in some respect it is and is not; in some respect it is inexpressible; and then three combinations of the first two with the inexpressible mode. My actual thesis is about how to read the qualifier. I distinguish a Standpoint-reading, where the hedge scopes over the whole judgement and means something like relative to some admissible standpoint, this claim is asserted, from an Aspect-reading, where it scopes over the predicate and means some part of the object answers to this predicate. I want to argue that the familiar triviality objection — either anekāntavāda is straight-up inconsistent, or it's the banal observation that things have many properties, which nobody denies — only has any force against the Standpoint-reading, and that the interesting version of the doctrine runs on the Aspect-reading. Then, and this is the part I think is new, I read the fourth mode, the inexpressible one, not as a fourth semantic value but as a refusal to answer the question as posed: the answerer is saying that any admissible re-issuing of the question has to carry a standpoint parameter, and that without one there's nothing to say. So on my reading anekāntavāda is primarily a thesis about question reform rather than a thesis about truth-value gluts, and it sits much closer to our practice of treating an unqualified question as ill-posed than to anything like dialetheism. Sections: (1) the background and the triviality objection; (2) the Standpoint/Aspect distinction, with the argument that the objection begs the question by assuming the Standpoint-reading; (3) the question-reform reading of mode four and modes five through seven, where I tentatively treat five through seven as meta-answers — you give a qualified yes or no and simultaneously register that the question needed repair; (4) an applied case study I'm rather fond of, where two perfectly calibrated instruments disagree about whether a treated alloy surface is harder than a specified grade — the hardness indenter says yes, the spectroscopic assay says no, both are working, and what the engineers actually do in practice is stop answering the bare question and start answering a family of parameterised ones. I want to use this to make the doctrine look like something we already do in labs; (5) the big worry, which I've started calling the self-effacing problem: I introduce a Swap test for whether a disagreement is standpoint-invariant (swap the parties' standpoints; if the dispute evaporates in both directions it was never real at the level the doctrine cares about, and if it survives both swaps the sevenfold machinery does no work on it), and then it looks like the more successful the anekāntic therapy is, the fewer genuine disputes are left for it to apply to — the doctrine would be a transitional instrument that abolishes its own subject matter. I can't tell whether that's a reductio of my reading or a feature I should lean into.

What I'd like from you, concretely: (a) corrections on anything doctrinally off in that background, keyed to the section it appears in; (b) an honest verdict on whether my Standpoint/Aspect distinction is actually doing work or whether it silently collapses into some distinction everyone already has — I genuinely can't tell from the inside; (c) which single section is weakest and most worth cutting for length, with the reason stated in one sentence; (d) whether the alloy case study really carries the load I put on it or whether there's a better worked example of the same structural point (feel free to propose one — no need for it to be a real experiment); (e) the strongest version of the self-effacing objection, and how you'd answer it in about 200 words; and (f) one short paragraph written as my most hostile reader — the person who thinks the whole project is motivated retrofit.
