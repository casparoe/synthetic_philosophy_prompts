I am a senior doing the year-long independent research project our school runs for the history and social science track, and my topic is the idea that history is chaotic: that large outcomes sometimes turn on details nobody could have anticipated, in the way that proverb about the lost nail and the lost kingdom suggests. My supervisor says my draft keeps sliding between two claims I have not separated, one about small causes having large effects and one about outcomes being genuinely unpredictable in advance. Before I revise, I want to get my own verdicts straight, because when I go case by case I do not think I am being consistent, and I would like to know whether there is a principle that captures me.

My verdicts, all gut and no method:

One. Sarajevo, June 1914. The driver took a wrong turn and stopped the car in front of a man who had botched an attempt earlier that same morning. I think the assassination on that exact day was fragile, but I do not think the war was: if not this, then something else within a couple of years. So the nail is real for the one event and fake for the other.

Two. The Cuban Missile Crisis, late October 1962. One officer on a Soviet submarine refused to agree to a nuclear torpedo launch. I think nuclear war that week really was avoided by that refusal, which makes it a genuine nail. This sits badly with case one: there too the structural pressures were enormous, and there I said the structure wins.

Three. Florida, 2000. A few hundred votes out of millions, and a confusing ballot layout in one county almost certainly redirected more votes than the margin. This is the most sensitive case on my list and the least interesting to me: sensitivity here just records that the race was close. It is a fact about the count, not about how history works.

Four. The Titanic. The officer swapped off the ship at the last minute kept in his pocket the key to the locker holding the lookouts' binoculars. I do not think this is a nail at all. The ship hits the iceberg anyway.

Five. The Challenger disaster, 1986. The specific launch date was fragile; the disaster was not. The shuttle had flown with damaged O-rings before, so unless something changed, one was going to be lost sooner or later.

Six. Penicillin. A mold spore drifted onto a dish Fleming had left out. I want to say the timing was fragile and the discovery was not, because other people had already seen mold kill bacteria and others were looking at antibacterial substances seriously by then. But I also think the credit was fragile, in a way that feels different from the discovery being fragile, and I cannot say why.

Seven. The asteroid that ended the dinosaurs. Mammals, and therefore we, are fragile in the strongest possible sense here, but it feels wrong to call it a nail. Nothing was building up to it; it just arrived. It is a bolt from outside rather than the last link in a chain.

The principle I half have is this: a small event is a real nail rather than noise when it decides which side of a threshold a system that was already loaded falls on. That seems to give me 1914 for the assassination but not for the war, 1962, and 1986 for the date, and to rule out 2000 and the asteroid. But it does not deliver my own verdict that the war itself was robust, it does not handle cases where many different small events would each have been enough, and it says nothing about why the credit in case six feels fragile while the discovery does not.

What I would like from you: one or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough to argue from, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it; an honest account of which of my verdicts each principle forces me to give up; and its verdicts on two cases where I do not have one. First, the financial crash of 2008: nail, or loaded gun? Second, the pandemic: if that particular spillover had not happened at the end of 2019, does my principle commit me to saying that something very similar was coming within a few years, and is that the penicillin verdict or the 1914 verdict? Please also say, briefly, what would make this question too big for a thirty-page paper in a single semester. Do not worry about references: I will add the footnotes myself.
