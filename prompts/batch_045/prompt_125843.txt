For roughly eight years I have opened my third-year seminar on concepts and induction with the same exercise: a shoebox of index cards, each bearing a Bongard problem — six small line drawings in a left-hand box, six in a right-hand box, and a rule that holds of everything on the left and of nothing on the right, which the students have to find. My question is not about how they find the rule but about how they reject rules. The pattern I keep seeing is this: someone proposes a rule that separates the twelve figures perfectly, that is no longer or more convoluted to state than the intended answer, and that nobody in the room — including, usually, the person who proposed it — accepts for even a second; and when I ask why not, the best anyone can produce is that it is not the point. Three cards in the box bring this out especially well, and I cannot tell whether they show one phenomenon or three. On the first, the left-hand figures each have a small dot inside a closed curve of varying shape (circle, ellipse, rounded rectangle, irregular blob) and the right-hand figures have the same dot outside a similar curve; a student proposes that every left-hand figure has an odd total number of straight edges, which is true, short to state, and would stop being true if I had drawn one of the blobs with a single extra corner. On the second, the left-hand figures are closed curves and the right-hand figures open ones, but all six of my left-hand curves happen to be convex; a student proposes that the curve is convex, which is also true, also short, uses a perfectly respectable geometric property, and — unlike the first rule — survives my redrawing any single figure with a wobbly hand, yet is plainly wrong, since a kidney-shaped closed curve belongs on the left and would refute it. On the third, the left-hand figures contain two shapes that overlap and the right-hand figures two shapes that do not; a student proposes that the centres of the two shapes are less than half a figure-width apart, which fits, is not fragile in the first card's way, rests on a genuine feature of the figures, and has a real gap for its threshold to sit in — but the number has obviously been read off the widest left-hand pair and the narrowest right-hand pair, and the students reject it for precisely that reason, even though a statistician would think one parameter fitted to twelve points a venial sin.

What I want is the carving, not a label and not a bibliography: I am writing this from a library with no reliable journal access, so please do not send me off to papers or tell me who to read. Specifically, is there an established notion — or several — for what these rules lack, or should I conclude that my three cards illustrate three unrelated complaints? A colleague in computer science, shown the cards, said overfitting, but that cannot be the whole of it: the convex rule has no free parameters and is shorter than the correct one, so nothing standard about model complexity ranks it below closure. A colleague in philosophy said Goodman's new riddle of induction, but nothing here turns on a predicate whose extension shifts at some future time — I could tell the students that all twelve figures were drawn last Tuesday and the difficulty would be exactly as it is. Someone else said natural kinds, but these are arbitrary line drawings and there is no kind here to be natural or unnatural. So what is the difference between a rule that captures the concept and a rule that merely fits the twelve, given that fit, brevity and respectability of vocabulary do not settle it? Do I have three varieties of one failing, or is there a single underlying requirement that all three violate — something about the rule having to be indifferent to how the sample might have been drawn, or to which instances happened to be included — and if the notion has a name, I want to know exactly what it covers and where it stops; if it has none, coin one, define it, and give me the boundary cases I should have been embarrassed to hand out.

Two further things I would like pinned down. First, is there a positive criterion — a specifiable condition a rule can satisfy — or does the distinction only ever do negative work, letting us strike rules out without ever certifying one? Second, does any of this depend on there being six examples per side? If I spent a weekend drawing sixty per side, would the phenomenon vanish, or merely get rarer, or change its character? My own suspicion is that it changes character: with sixty figures I could not keep the odd-number-of-edges rule working by accident, but I could very easily draw sixty convex closed curves by accident, which suggests to me that the first and second cards are not the same complaint at all. I would like to know whether that suspicion is right, and what would settle it.
