I'm closing the final session of an undergraduate seminar on early modern philosophy. We've spent the term on Berkeley, and the last meeting is on the master argument — the moment where Philonous asks Hylas to try to conceive of some ordinary object, a tree in a quad or a book in a closed room, existing unconceived, and then points out that the attempt defeats itself, because in trying you are conceiving it. My students split almost exactly down the middle: half think this is a cheap trick with an operator buried in it, half think it is the deepest thing we read all term and that the trick accusation misses the point. I don't want to end by adjudicating. A colleague suggested I end with a poem, and joked that the obvious form is a villanelle; I initially took that as a joke, but the more I thought about it the more I thought she was right for a reason she hadn't given. The villanelle's two refrains keep coming back no matter how hard the speaker tries to say something else. That is either a perfect mimesis of the master argument or a perfect exposure of it, depending on whether the returns are doing any work — and it seems to me the form lets a poem hold both halves of my class at once.

So: a villanelle, nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with refrain A1 ending lines 1, 6, 12 and 18 and refrain A2 ending lines 3, 9, 15 and 19, so that the two of them arrive side by side in the final couplet. Refrain A1 should be Berkeley's conclusion in its slogan form — esse is percipi, or an English equivalent you can make scan. Refrain A2 should be the objector's picture: a concrete, sensible thing nobody happens to be perceiving. The tree in the quad is fine if you can make it feel unhackneyed; another object is fine too, but it has to be something a reader could picture, not an abstraction like a state of affairs or a possible world. Write it in the voice of Philonous addressing Hylas, though the voice is allowed to thin out or turn against itself as the poem goes on. Loose iambic tetrameter or pentameter, whichever you can actually sustain; contemporary diction rather than eighteenth-century pastiche; no stage directions, glosses or footnotes, since whatever the poem has to argue has to be argued inside the verse.

The constraint that matters most is that the refrains must not be merely repeated — their repetition has to be the argument. Every return of A1 should be earned by a fresh, genuine attempt: the speaker really trying, in the lines before it, to do the thing Berkeley asks, to frame a thought of an object with the conceiving subtracted out, and failing in a slightly different way each time. And I want the last quatrain to register the strong objection without resolving it. The objection I have in mind is the one that moves from my not being able to think of X while not thinking of X to X's not being able to be unthought-of — the negation quietly changing scope, so that what has really been shown is something about what a mind can do at a moment rather than something about the furniture of the world. Ideally the closing couplet lets a reader who knows the literature see exactly this: that the two refrains are both still standing, that the poem has demonstrated that a villanelle cannot get out of its own form, and that whether this bears on trees is still the open question. If you think the refrains should be assigned the other way round, go ahead and swap them, but tell me in one sentence why. It should be accurate enough that a Berkeley specialist wouldn't wince, and it should still read as a good poem to someone who has never heard of him.
