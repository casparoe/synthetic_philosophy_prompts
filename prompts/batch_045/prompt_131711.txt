I somehow ended up on the board of the historical society in the old mill town I grew up in, which is mostly a volunteer thing — we've got one paid intern for the summer, about nine grand in the bank, and a leaky basement full of boxes nobody's opened in years. We're fighting about what to do with the money and I genuinely can't tell who's right. Dana wants a big mural downtown and a small scholarship in the name of the woman who built the mills back in the 1840s, on the theory that kids around here never see anybody from here do anything impressive and need someone to look up to. The catch is that a researcher over at the county library turned up evidence that a lot of the good stories about her — the taught-herself-engineering-in-a-barn stuff, the dramatic line on the old stone marker — were basically cooked up by a booster committee in the 1930s trying to get highway traffic to stop in town. Dana says that hardly matters, the boring true version is still a big deal and the point is what it does for a fifteen-year-old walking past it.

Marcus runs the archive and wants the whole nine grand to go to a dehumidifier, shelving, and cataloging: the payroll ledgers, the quilts, the church records, the boxes of letters, before they rot. He talks like the town owes something to the people who are gone and to their stuff, and like we're just the current custodians handing it on. But the town has changed a lot — most people here now moved in for the hospital or work remotely, almost nobody who's family goes back more than a generation or two, and a lot of the boxes belong to families that cleared out sixty years ago. Then there's Priya, who wants the money spent on a set of plaques and a walking tour that amounts to a prosecution of the mills: the twelve-hour days, the kids in the spinning room, the company store, the strike that got broken up. Her line is that the town is stuck, that everybody tiptoes around the founder stuff, and that nobody here will try anything new until we've publicly said out loud that this place was built on something ugly. Two board members quit over her proposal last month and I think she sort of enjoyed that, which bugs me.

A friend of mine who reads a lot of philosophy says she remembers some old guy arguing that there are a few different ways a person or a whole town can use the past, that each one is supposed to turn poisonous if you get too much of it, that you actually need more than one, and that a person can be too stuffed full of history to do anything. She couldn't remember who it was or what he called the categories and neither can I. So: what would you actually do with the nine grand and the intern, and why? I'd love something concrete — what to say to each of the three of them, whether the made-up stories sink Dana's project or not, and how I'm supposed to think about who's right here, because right now it feels like all three of them are obviously right and obviously wrong at the same time.
