I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar in formal epistemology: eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, a whiteboard and printed handouts but no computers in the room, and no teaching assistant. The students are comfortable with conditioning, with partitions of a state space, and with proper scoring rules. They have not met Aumann's agreement theorem, and I would rather the result went unnamed until the debrief. What I want is an activity in which they run into it themselves: two people who are honest, competent, and share a prior, discovering that they cannot agree to disagree — and, more surprisingly, that they never need to pool their evidence to converge. A single number per turn is enough, because a posterior is a sufficient statistic for someone's evidence once her partition is common knowledge.

Please design the activity, and include the following.

**1. The construction.** A small finite state space (six to nine states, I would guess), a common prior, an event whose probability is at issue, and two information partitions, the whole structure printed and public. Keep the cover story concrete — a card draw, an urn, a fault in a production batch — but supply the abstract version alongside it. Two constraints matter to me. First, the announcements have to be invertible: each number must pin down the announcer's current information set among those it could be at that point in the exchange. If a uniform prior forces two distinct information sets to produce the same posterior, break the tie with a non-uniform prior and tell me how you verified invertibility. Second, the example must be deep enough that the exchange takes at least four announcements in total on some states; a convergence that happens after one round each will not be felt.

**2. The protocol.** Students work in threes: two principals who each receive a private signal, and an auditor. Signals are drawn by the facilitator; the principals never see each other's signal or the state. Each round, one principal writes a single number — her credence in the event — on a shared sheet which is then passed to the other; no words, no gestures, no description of evidence, and a hard cap of six announcements each (if the cap binds, treat that as data). I also half-remember that alternating public announcements of this kind are guaranteed to converge after finitely many steps, whereas simultaneous announcements are not; if that is right, build the protocol around it and make the simultaneity failure part of what the activity shows, and if it is wrong, say so plainly.

**3. The auditor's role.** The auditor sees no signal. After the two opening numbers only, and before the exchange continues, she writes down her own credence and her prediction of the number the two principals will converge on. My conjecture, which I would like you to check rather than assume, is that she can compute it exactly: the two opening numbers identify both cells, the intersection lies inside a single cell of the meet of the partitions, and the common posterior is just the prior probability of the event conditional on that cell — so an eavesdropper with no evidence of her own ends up exactly as well calibrated as the people who had the evidence. If that is right, build the reveal around it. If it is not, or only holds under a further condition, tell me what is true instead and design the auditor's task around that.

**4. A second round that removes an assumption.** Same protocol, same prior, same event, but now each principal is told her own partition and only a coarse, non-invertible description of the other's — so that hearing a number tells her much less. I want the pair to visibly stall, and then to see that the stall is not irrationality or stubbornness on either side but a missing piece of common knowledge about the information structure.

**5. Scoring, timing, and materials.** No real money: use points, or a trivial token prize, scored by a proper scoring rule against the true state, revealed at the end for all groups at once. Give me a minute-by-minute allocation of the ninety minutes and a list of what has to be printed.

**6. Debrief, failure modes, and limits.** Map the moments of the activity onto the parts of the theorem, including which assumption each round isolates, and say what the students should be able to state in their own words afterwards — my target is that they leave able to say what would have to be different about two people for them to persist in disagreement: not stubbornness, not the bare fact that their evidence differs, but either a difference in priors or something not commonly known about what each knows about what the other knows. Then an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each, and a frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself in the debrief.

Please write the whole answer in British English.
