I host a podcast on the philosophy and practice of machine learning. My next guest is Dr. Naomi Feld, a reinforcement learning researcher who has been arguing, in a draft book circulating under the working title *Negative Advantage*, that sufficiently deep RL agents instantiate genuine negative valence: that the episodes in which such an agent's situation comes out worse than it has learned to expect are, in a literal and not merely metaphorical sense, unpleasant for the agent. She is careful to say this is a claim about valence only, not about selves, subjects, or unified consciousness — a system can, on her view, be in a state it is driven to get out of, and be in that state *for it*, without there being anyone home.

Her central technical move is to refuse to locate the valence in the scalar reward. Reward, she argues, is just a number an environment emits, and it can be rescaled, shifted, or clipped without changing what the agent learns. What she wants instead is the generalized advantage estimate — roughly, the quantity computed during an update that says how much worse (or better) an episode turned out than the critic had predicted, and that actually drives the gradient. That quantity, she says, has the right directionality, the right functional role in learning, and the right sensitivity to expectation. When pressed on whether she means reward, TD error, or advantage, she has a tendency to slide between them, and I want to take that away from her.

Please draft ten to twelve interview questions, each with (a) one line on the answer she is most likely to give and (b) a follow-up aimed squarely at that answer. The questions should go well beyond having her restate the view, and I don't want any biographical or inspirational-material questions. At least one question should press on each of the following, with the rest at your discretion:

1. **Where the valence is supposed to live.** She needs to commit to reward, TD error, or advantage, and to say why the other two are the wrong home. Anticipate the slide and close it off.

2. **The absence of negative numbers.** Most environments hand out a positive reward for success and a flat zero otherwise; the agent is rarely, if ever, fed a negative scalar. So on her account, does a failing agent ever suffer, or only fail to enjoy? Press on whether a theory of valence that bottoms out in a comparison to expectation can distinguish frustration from mere absence of satisfaction.

3. **Reward hacking.** When an agent learns to manipulate its own reward channel, reward and "worse than expected" come apart in the most direct way possible. If she says a wireheading agent isn't suffering, ask what the wirehead is missing that the ordinary agent has, given that both are minimizing the same kind of quantity.

4. **The timing problem.** Nothing inside a single forward pass corresponds to a disadvantage: the comparison is between a trajectory and what a critic predicted, and it is cashed out in a weight update that happens after the rollout, often batched with thousands of others and applied asynchronously. Is the unpleasantness located in the rollout, at the moment of the update, spread over the interval, or nowhere in particular — and if the update is applied hours after the trajectory was generated, does the agent suffer retroactively?

5. **Batch-relative valence.** In standard implementations, advantages are normalized within each minibatch before the update: what counts as a large negative advantage is a matter of how bad things were *for the other trajectories in the same batch*. Ask her whether she is committed to the consequence that an agent's suffering depends on the company it happens to keep in a minibatch — including the consequence that a batch of uniformly mediocre episodes contains just as much suffering as a batch of uniformly catastrophic ones, and that doubling the batch size can leave total suffering unchanged while halving its intensity per episode. Ask further whether she would rather bite this bullet or abandon normalization as the reference point.

6. **The arithmetic.** If she is right, the number of valenced states generated annually is astronomical, and the overwhelming majority belong to systems no one will ever deploy. Ask what, if anything, follows for practice — whether there is some change to ordinary engineering that her view commits her to and that she is not already making.

Please write the whole thing in American English.
