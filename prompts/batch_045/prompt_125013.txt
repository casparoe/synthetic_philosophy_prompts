So me and my friend got into about a three hour argument last night and I want a number to pin it down, because I think we were talking past each other the whole time.

The case we were actually fighting about: our city replaced all the fixed street furniture with a modular system. Benches, planters, bike racks, bollards all clip into a standard rail, and legally anyone can unclip and rearrange them, no permit, no permission. That's the entire design philosophy: maximum flexibility, nothing baked in.

What actually happened over two years: on blocks with a business improvement district and a lot of retirees, the stuff gets rearranged constantly and settles into arrangements for lingering. On blocks that are mostly renters and people working two shifts, nobody ever touches it, so it just stays in the arrangement it shipped in, which is spaced out, backless, hostile to sitting for more than five minutes. Nobody designed that. Any arrangement is as available as any other, in principle, to anyone.

My friend says this is the cleanest possible case of an artifact having politics: same physical hardware, same city, same rules, and it systematically delivers a different answer to who gets to occupy public space depending on which block the rail is bolted to. She says it's the same point as that old essay everyone cites about the low overpasses on the parkways and the mechanical tomato harvester. I said no — the hardware is neutral all the way down, and what's doing all the work is who has spare time, an organization, and the confidence to touch public property. Put the rail in a neighborhood full of retired people who rent and I bet the outcome flips. Swap the populations and the politics follows the people, not the thing. She says that's exactly backwards: what follows the hardware is the fact that having slack now determines your street, and that's a property of the arrangement-as-deployed.

So here's what I want. What's your credence that the modular furniture system itself has politics, in the strong sense where "has politics" is genuinely a property of the artifact and not just shorthand for the people around it and their capacities? Not it depends what you mean by politics. A number, and the decomposition that gets you there.

The terms I'd want addressed, but add your own if I'm missing one:

- That the flexibility objection isn't fatal: does the fact that no arrangement is privileged by the hardware and any outcome is reversible by anyone push the politics into the distribution of who has reconfiguration capacity?
- That the effect here is political rather than merely unequal, i.e. that it's about power or standing or the terms of shared life and not just an unlucky distribution of stuff.
- That anything follows from granting it, like designers owing some kind of accountability we don't already owe them.
