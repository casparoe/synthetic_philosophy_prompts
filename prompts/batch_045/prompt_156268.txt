I teach a bit of social epistemology inside an ethics course, and I'm reworking a session where I want to use Rwanda 1994 as the case study for the echo-chamber material. My worry is that the case only half fits the concept, and I'd rather show the students where it comes apart than force it. Ive been going back and forth on this for a couple of years.

The history, so we don't argue about it: from 1993 Radio Télévision Libre des Mille Collines broadcast in Kinyarwanda into a country where radio was basically the only mass medium, literacy was low, and the state station was the default; through the hundred days from April 1994 the broadcasts named people and places, and people behind the station and Kangura were later convicted of incitement by the ICTR. There was a UN force on the ground whose commander was saying things that contradicted the official line, and the station spent a lot of energy discrediting him and the foreign press rather than just never mentioning them.

So do the mapping for me. On Nguyen's distinction — an epistemic bubble is omission, you simply never encounter the other side; an echo chamber works on trust, outside sources are pre-emptively discredited so that exposure to them doesn't help — tell me which this is, or whether it's genuinely both in different respects, and what turns on the answer. Who is supposed to be the epistemic agent: the listeners, the announcers, or the Hutu Power network as a whole? What does the theory say went wrong for each of them?

Then the bits where I think it breaks, and push back on me where I'm wrong:

1. On the standard account an echo chamber is a structure that damages the epistemic agency of the people inside it. But the people who built RTLM seem to have known perfectly well what was true — the discredit machine looks cynical rather than self-deceiving, and its operators stand outside it, not inside. Does that force a third category (a chamber engineered by people who don't believe the content, aimed at producing action against outsiders), or does it just mean "echo chamber" is the wrong word and is doing rhetorical work the theory can't cash?

2. The normative target. The theory's worry is what happens to insiders' capacity to form beliefs well; here the overwhelming harm is to people outside the structure. Is that a plain misfire, or can the account be reformulated so the wrong-making feature isn't the insiders' damaged agency?

3. The remedies. Nguyen-style fixes lean on bridging trust, on trusted insiders carrying outside claims back in, on exposure that isn't already discredited. In a one-way medium with no real alternative channels, where carrying an outside claim in is close to lethal, do those remedies have anything to offer at all? And a counterfactual: suppose, purely hypothetically, the transmitter had been jammed from week three. By the theory's own lights would that have dissolved anything, or would it have fed the very narrative that outside voices are enemies?

4. If you happen to know it: a Dutch NGO later ran a reconciliation radio soap opera in Rwanda on the same medium, Musekeweya. Does anything in the theory license the thought that radio can rebuild what radio helped destroy, or is that simply outside what the concept can speak to?

Where the concept is silent, say it's silent. I'd rather hand the students the gaps than a tidy four-part answer.
