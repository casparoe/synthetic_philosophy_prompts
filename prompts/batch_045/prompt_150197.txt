**Heterophenomenology is reverse engineering, and reverse engineering produces specifications**

As I understand it, heterophenomenology asks us to do something slightly unnatural. When a subject says that the second flash looked brighter than the first, we log that report but we do not believe it, and we do not disbelieve it either. We suspend judgement on whether there really was a brightening, and instead we construct the best third-person account we can of a world in which somebody undergoes that experience. The constructed account is the subject's heterophenomenological world. It is a public object: we can argue about it, measure it, and even disagree with the subject about it, which is not something you can do with their private experience, if there is one. Dennett's claim is that this is the only way to make the study of consciousness properly empirical, and that the method itself is neutral — it does not decide in advance whether the reports are true, and it leaves it to science to say whether there are really qualia. Critics, as far as I can tell, tend to reply that the neutrality is cosmetic: that the method quietly guarantees a third-person answer and then declares the first-person question empty.

The point I want to press is that the object the method produces is, structurally, a specification rather than a description of an implementation.

In my day job, when we reverse engineer a black-box service, we do not get to read its source. We poke it, log the responses, and write down a contract: given these inputs, it will produce outputs with these properties. What we end up with is a specification. A specification is answerable to behaviour but not uniquely determined by it — many different implementations satisfy the same contract, and the specification is simply silent on which one is running. The heterophenomenological world seems to me to be exactly this kind of object. It is a contract that a nervous system can satisfy. It is complete as a contract, and it is deliberately, structurally incomplete as a story about what the nervous system is doing underneath.

This reframing is where I think the usual stand-off about the hard problem comes from. The complaint is that no third-person description, however complete, captures what it is like to see red. But that is not a complaint a specification can ever answer, and I suspect it is a category error rather than a mystery: it asks the contract to be made of the same stuff as the implementation. You would not complain that a specification of an HTTP endpoint fails to be made of TCP packets. What you can legitimately ask is whether the contract is behaviourally complete — whether some conforming implementation could still be missing something. That is a real question, but it is a different question, and it is one the specification framework lets us pose precisely instead of waving at.

Four consequences follow, and these are the parts of the essay I am least sure about.

First, *protocol drift*. Reports are not read off a subject; they are elicited from them, and the way you ask changes what you get. Free recall, forced choice, immediate versus delayed, pointing versus drawing versus speaking: these are different query languages against the same system, and in engineering terms the act of querying perturbs what you are querying. So the heterophenomenological world is protocol-relative. There is no language-neutral transcript underneath the various ones; there is only the family of transcripts, plus whatever invariants survive translation between them. I take this to be compatible with what Dennett says about the unreliability of introspection, but the framing of the world as an artefact of a query language, rather than as an approximation to a language-free truth, feels like a stronger claim.

Second, *versioning*. Reports arrive over time and they drift. The method has to aggregate them into one object, but which build is that object a specification of? My suggestion is to treat reports as commits on a branch: the heterophenomenological world is a branch tip, not a stable artefact, and it should carry a timestamp and a protocol name the way a build carries a version string. A corollary that I think is genuinely useful: reports about earlier reports — a subject saying on Tuesday that Monday's headache was worse — are metadata, not telemetry, and should not be aggregated as if they were first-class observations.

Third, *fixed holes*. Silence is the case the method handles worst. A subject says nothing, says they cannot say, or simply never mentions a dimension that we expected them to mention. The discipline of heterophenomenology is to leave the hole unfilled rather than fill it with theory, which is right, but I want to distinguish four things that the method tends to conflate: unreported, unreportable, unexperienced, and protocol-inexpressible. My proposal is an operational test for the last one. Vary the protocol as aggressively as you can — different sensory modalities of response, different delays, different framings, trained versus untrained subjects — and see whether the hole moves. Holes that track the protocol are artefacts of the query language. A hole that is invariant across every protocol we can devise I want to call a fixed hole, and I want to claim that a fixed hole would be the closest thing heterophenomenology can produce to an empirical mark of the ineffable: not a proof that something is there, but a well-defined target that survives every instrument we have.

Fourth, *diffs instead of absolutes*. What a subject says about a single condition is contaminated by the elicitation in ways we cannot fully subtract out. But the difference between reports under two conditions is much cleaner, because most elicitation bias is common to both sides and cancels. This is just differential testing — run two builds against the same input set and look at the diff — and I want to argue that heterophenomenology should be organised around report-diffs rather than around absolute reports, in the same way that we trust A/B tests more than we trust surveys. If that is right, a lot of the literature's energy has gone into the wrong unit of measurement.

The upshot I care about is that once you see the method's output as a versioned, protocol-relative, diff-shaped specification, the hard problem stops looking like a missing datum and starts looking like a boundary between two kinds of completeness. That may be less exciting than solving it, but it is the sort of thing an engineer can actually work with.
