I work in collection documentation at a small maritime museum — I came to it from a philosophy master's I never finished — and my question is less about the metaphysics than about our registrar's track record. Over fifteen years Miriam has had to say, for cataloguing, insurance and loan purposes, whether some present vessel is "the same vessel" as a historic one, and her rulings are these: (i) a cutter whose every timber was replaced one at a time across forty years of continuous service — same vessel; (ii) that same cutter's discarded timbers, bought at auction by a private yard and built into a second hull on the original lines — not the vessel, only a vessel made of its fabric; (iii) a sloop that the museum itself took apart beam by beam, trucked three hundred kilometres inland and reassembled nine months later with new fastenings — same vessel, the interval being a "dormant period" in the object's history; (iv) a trawler rebuilt after a fire over three seasons on her keel and about six per cent of her frames, relaunched under her old name and registration — same vessel, so gradualness was not doing the work; (v) a barge that broke up on a shoal, where two unconnected salvors each salvaged roughly half the timbers and each completed a vessel within a month of the other — neither is the barge, which she entered as "ceased to exist on the night of the wreck," both successors catalogued as reconstructions; (vi) a coaster whose owners scuttled the hull and moved the name, the registration, the bell and the logbooks onto a new hull bought from another yard — a namesake, not the same vessel.

The case now on her desk is not one I have seen treated anywhere, since the standard discussions all stop at some variant of (i) and (ii), and I would rather have your reasoning than a reading list. A single custodial project split a badly damaged 1890s pilot cutter between two yards: one yard kept roughly sixty per cent of the original fabric — keel, port side, most of the frames — and built a new starboard side around it; the other took roughly forty per cent — starboard planking, deck beams, transom, rigging — and built a new hull around that. The forty-per-cent hull was finished first and has been sailing continuously for seven years under the vessel's original name, carrying the original logbooks and the original registers' entries, and it holds the national registration. The sixty-per-cent hull sat under a tarpaulin for a decade, was launched only last spring, and has since been accepted by a regional register as "the" vessel, which is what has forced the question. There is also a standing proposal from a heritage trust to re-join the surviving original fabric of both hulls into a single display piece twenty years from now.

What I would like from you is this: set out the rules that fit (i) through (vi) jointly, and say explicitly which natural-looking rules the track record has already eliminated — I take (ii) to be hard on anything that lets fabric or provenance win outright, (v) to be hard on the standard four-dimensionalist treatment of fission, and (vi) to be hard on any view where a name, a registration or a chain of paperwork is what does the work, though my brother insists the new case is relevantly unlike (ii) precisely because both branches descend from one custodial project. Then say what each surviving rule predicts about the sixty-forty split, whether this case is better classified with (iii) and (iv) or with (v), which verdict you expect Miriam to give and why, what she should enter in the catalogue if her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, and what single further case would best separate the rules that still agree — a ninety-ten split, a case in which the minority-fabric branch is also the branch with the continuous service, or the re-joined hull.
