I am midway through the third chapter of my dissertation, on welfare-integrated dietary footprints, and I have argued myself into a conclusion I do not believe. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The model I use decomposes the difference a diet makes into two channels, summed in a common unit per thousand kilocalories. The bred channel is the number of sentient animals whose existence is counterfactually caused by my consumption, multiplied by the net welfare of a life of that kind. The land channel is the hectare-years my consumption occupies, multiplied by the difference that occupation makes to the welfare of the wild animals living there, measured against the marginal alternative use of that hectare rather than against wilderness — what I call the marginal-hectare problem, since the grain I decline does not stay in the ground but travels along a displacement chain into somebody else's production function.

Here is the argument I cannot escape, with the steps separated out so you can tell me which one to refuse.

(1) For the purposes of this chapter I grant that my only dietary obligation is to minimise the expected difference my diet makes to total suffering; no rights-based, purity-based, or expressive considerations are allowed to enter.

(2) That difference is the sum of the bred channel and the land channel, computed separately and then added, because they are effects on different animals.

(3) Between any two nutritionally equivalent diets I should choose the one with the smaller total; where the totals are equal, the choice between them is morally indifferent.

(4) On my central parameters, the bred channel is roughly two orders of magnitude worse for chicken than for beef, since a chicken-day contains vastly more individual lives than a beef-day, while the land channel is roughly two orders of magnitude worse for beef. The two channels very nearly cancel, so that the difference between a chicken-day and a beef-day is smaller than the width of my own error bars.

(5) The sign of that difference flips when I move any one of three switches: whether the soil invertebrates killed by tillage and harvest count at all and at what weight; whether pasture counts for its residents as habitat or as a source of lives that are on balance bad; and whether ending a life that is on balance bad is a harm to the animal or a benefit to it.

(6) This is not an artefact of varying the switches one at a time. When I integrate over a joint distribution of the three parameters, the expected difference between the two diets is still very close to zero, and its variance is enormous.

(7) If a comparison's expected sign is indistinguishable from zero and its sign is unstable across parameters I have no way to adjudicate, then there is no fact of the matter as to which option is better; and where there is no fact of the matter, permissibility is symmetric.

(8) So chicken and beef are equally permissible for me.

(9) The same three switches govern the footprint of every food: anything I eat has a land channel, and, through the displacement chain, an effect on what gets bred. So no food is morally preferable to any other, and dietary choice in general is morally inert.

(10) It follows that there is no moral difference between someone who eats two thousand kilocalories a day of pulses and someone who eats five thousand a day of grain-fed beef raised on ploughed and converted land.

(11) It also follows that if the land channel is genuinely negative, because the animals living on cultivated land have lives that are on balance bad and their deaths benefit them, then a diet's footprint falls as its land-intensity rises, and (1) to (3) instruct me to eat as much plough-intensive food as I can afford, and, if I wanted to do better still, to buy farmland and plough it.

I reject (11) and (10). I also notice that (9) destroys the enterprise I am working in, which consists of comparing diets and interventions and recommending some over others, and I am not willing to pay that either. So something before (9) has to go, and here is my difficulty: the steps I would least like to give up are exactly the ones I have the most invested in. I want to keep (1), because it is the working assumption of the chapter. I want to keep (2), because that is how the model is built and how every footprint number in the literature is constructed. I want to keep (4) and (6), because I have defended them in two workshops. I am aware that this is precisely what someone with a broken model would say.

I have been told that my beef-versus-chicken pairing is a false dichotomy, but I do not see why that helps: if (9) is sound it generalises to every pair, so pointing me at a third food does not by itself locate the error. Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs me. In particular: if the damage is in the move from (6) to (8), I want to know what the correct treatment of a comparison with a near-zero mean and a huge variance is, whether I have confused imprecision with equality or moved illegitimately from my inability to tell to there being nothing to tell, and what I would have to do with my parameter distributions to get an answer I could act on. If the damage is earlier, I want to know whether two channels defined against different counterfactual baselines can be summed at all, what a baseline-consistent version of the accounting would look like, and whether it would remain tractable. And if the damage is in (3), I want to know what replacing it does to the rest of the chain.

Please answer in continuous prose, as you would in an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no lists of further reading. Somewhere between six hundred and nine hundred words.
