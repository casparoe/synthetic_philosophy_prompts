hey — i'm putting together the closing bit for an undergrad seminar on direct democracy. we've done Switzerland (the double majority thing: for constitutional amendments you need both the popular vote and a majority of cantons, so one kind of winning doesn't buy you the other), the Brexit vote, and California's habit of putting what is basically the same proposition back on the ballot a cycle or two later in new packaging with new sponsors.

i want a poem, and i think a sestina is the right form — say so if you disagree and pick something better, but only if it does the same structural work. here's the idea: the six end-words are the six things every referendum runs on. something like hands, margin, quorum, measure, silence, again (swap any of them if you've got better ones). the permutation is the point: the words stay identical and their order changes, and the same words in a new arrangement say something different. that's how i want to get at what i've been calling the second-asking problem — asking the same electorate the same question twice is not asking the same question, because the first answer changes what the second one means. after the first vote you're no longer voting on the policy, you're partly voting on whether to reopen the thing, and the status quo has moved under your feet. so the poem should feel like the same vote happening again and again and meaning something different each time, not like a story with a moral arc.

voice: write it in the voice of the measure itself — the proposition, the sentence on the ballot — the way one sentence gets dressed up differently by its backers each cycle while staying the same sentence underneath.

constraints:

- the envoi is where i want the double majority to live: two different things have to be satisfied at once and satisfying one doesn't get you the other. this is the bit i care most about, so don't let it collapse into a tidy image or a resolved tension.
- silence has to earn its place. the people who didn't vote, couldn't vote, or weren't asked and will live with the result anyway — the electorate that inherits the decision isn't the electorate that made it.
