I'm working on a piece about the boom in claims to ancient nationhood: the ancestry-DNA kits that promise to tell you who your people were, a census that has started asking about tribal or indigenous affiliation again, and an independence movement in Europe whose case leans hard on being a thousand-year-old people with its own language and its own unbroken customs. Every interview I do ends the same way. Last week a historian said to me, more or less, "look, nations are invented traditions, all of them," and then used that one move to swat away three different things at once: the movement's claim to be ancient, the unionist side's counter-claim that the shared state is the older and more real thing, and my own question about whether any of this is supposed to matter for who gets to secede. I keep hearing "imagined community" deployed the exact same way, as a conversation-ender. The one line I did manage to look up, from Benedict Anderson's Imagined Communities (1983), says a nation "is imagined because the members of even the smallest nation will never know most of their fellow-members, meet them, or even hear of them, yet in the minds of each lives the image of their communion" -- which, honestly, sounds like its a point about how big countries are, not about whether they're fake.

So: what are the precise theses that "nations are invented traditions" (and "the nation is an imagined community") could be making? I want the whole menu, trivial to wild, with a verdict on each. Definitely cover these:

- What is "invented" being contrasted with -- ancient? spontaneously evolved? authentic? deliberate? Those give very different claims and I suspect people confuse them.
- Is there a useful taxonomy of the thing being labelled invented? Off the top of my head I want at least: a practice made up more or less from nothing; a messy local practice that got standardised, given a rulebook and a costume; a genuinely dead practice deliberately revived; an old practice that got a false founding date stapled to it; and -- this one is my own invention, so pressure-test it -- a practice whose claim to antiquity is part of its content, so that the antiquity claim isn't a mistake about the practice but a load-bearing part of what the practice does. Does that last category earn its keep or does it collapse into one of the others?
- Where does the real continuity sit? The people who push this line also stress that the raw materials -- language, memories, old grievances, origin myths -- have to be lying around already for anyone to build with. If that's right, how much strength does the "it's all just made up" reading actually have, and is that reading self-undermining? If tradition A is two centuries old and tradition B is nine, what -- if anything -- follows about legitimacy, self-determination, or historic title? Which readings of the slogan would actually licence my interviewee's triple dismissal, and which leave it hanging?
