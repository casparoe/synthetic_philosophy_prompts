I'm the operations manager for a supported-living provider — eight group homes, about forty adult residents, most of whom have an intellectual disability and many of whom also carry psychiatric diagnoses (schizoaffective disorder, emotionally unstable personality disorder, alcohol-related brain damage, and increasingly residents who are ageing into early dementia). Our staff are support workers with three weeks of classroom training and a two-day annual refresher; none of them have any academic background in anything. Incidents happen somewhere in the service two or three times a week — a punch during a personal-care prompt, a stolen wallet, sexual touching in a shared lounge, a violent episode during a relapse — and after each one a shift lead writes it up and, with the clinical lead signing off, decides what follows: a mediation, a behaviour support plan, a referral, a move, or nothing. Our current framework is essentially positive behaviour support: every incident is logged on an antecedent-behaviour-consequence chart and handled as behaviour with causes to be identified and managed. It has two failure modes I keep running into. Staff who let a resident see that they were frightened, hurt or angry get written up for unprofessional conduct and told to stay neutral. And residents and families tell me, in different words, the same thing: that they feel written off, handled like weather or like livestock, that nobody expects anything of them any more and so they have stopped trying.

I have no academic background myself, but I got talking to a friend who studied philosophy, and she mentioned an old essay — I think by P. F. Strawson, possibly called Freedom and Resentment — which, as she described it, says roughly this: that holding someone responsible isn't a theory you believe but a stance you take, a matter of having reactive attitudes like resentment, gratitude and guilt; that the alternative of treating someone as a thing to be managed or handled is available and sometimes right, but is not the neutral, scientific default it presents itself as; and that you don't have to settle whether anyone has free will before you resent being wronged, because the question is set up backwards. She also said something I've been chewing on: that there is a difference between cases where this person couldn't have done otherwise on this occasion — he was pushed, he didn't know, he was out of his mind — and cases where the person isn't the kind of thing you address as a person at all, and that confusing the two is exactly the mistake our incident forms make, since the chart has one box where you write the diagnosis and another where you write the consequence.

What I want from you is a written procedure my shift leads can actually run. Constraints: it has to fit on one page, be usable in twenty minutes at a debrief by someone who has never heard the theory, and produce a short written record the clinical lead can sign off or overrule. I'd want it as ordered questions — is this a case for addressing the person or for managing the behaviour, which of those stances is being taken toward whom, and if it's the managing stance, whether the withdrawal is total or partial and temporary or open-ended — with a statement, for each question, of what counts as a sufficient answer and what evidence on the file satisfies it, and with explicit stop points: places where the procedure returns a firm no (do not switch to the managing stance here) and places where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than fact. It also has to handle five things that I think break any naive version of this. First, the difference between excusing and exempting, including the cases where a resident is fully capable in some domains and not in others, so the honest answer may be hold him responsible about his money but not about his hygiene. Second, the fact that we only ever deal with the negative side: nobody has a procedure for gratitude, appreciation or restored trust, and I suspect our service is worse at that than at blame. Third, the loop — if we have spent three years treating a resident as a thing to be managed, some of his present incapacity is our doing, so the file is itself part of the cause, and a procedure that asks is he an agent now? is asking about a variable it has been busy depressing. I want to know what the procedure does with that. Fourth, how to keep resentment from sliding into punitiveness, humiliation or a row: what the difference is in practice, not in theory. Fifth, the cases where the proper object of the attitude isn't the resident at all, but a co-resident's grievance on someone else's behalf, or the staff member's own.

Then please run it on three residents from our service. (A) Marcus, mild intellectual disability and alcohol-related brain damage, has hit a support worker during a shower prompt for the third time this month; afterwards he says he shouldn't have, and means it, and it happens again. (B) Nadia has an emotionally unstable personality disorder diagnosis, steals small sums from co-residents, lies about it, and turns staff against one another; my staff say she knows exactly what she is doing, my clinical lead says it is trauma-driven behaviour and wants a plan with no blame in it, and my own instinct is with the clinical lead. (C) Terrence, floridly psychotic, attacked a co-resident with a kettle because he believed the man was poisoning him; he has since been hospitalised, medicated, and came back saying he remembers it like a dream, and the injured man's family want him moved out. I'd be grateful if at least one of the three came out in a way I'll argue with — I'd least like to be told that Nadia is a full participant and that our management-only approach is the wrong stance toward her, so if that's what the procedure says I want to see it argued rather than asserted. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle: whether to call the police, whether to end a placement, whether anyone is blameworthy in the deep sense, and anything else where a page of questions would be pretending.
