I’m a PhD student writing on restorative justice and relational conceptions of personhood, and I want to reconstruct the implicit theory of a practice I invoke often: South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission. Under the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act 34 of 1995, the Commission could grant amnesty from criminal and civil liability to perpetrators of gross violations of human rights — killing, abduction, torture, or severe ill-treatment — who made a full disclosure of all the relevant facts and whose acts were associated with a political objective; applicants had to admit the act amounted to an offence or delict, so anyone who denied guilt or successfully raised a defence such as self-defence was refused. The Act’s preamble states that there is "a need for understanding but not for vengeance, a need for reparation but not for retaliation, a need for ubuntu but not for victimization". What theory of personhood or responsibility does this amnesty practice presuppose? Does trading punishment for truth-telling and reintegrating the perpetrator into the political community only make sense on a relational, ubuntu conception, on which personhood is constituted by relationships and wrongdoing fractures a relationship that truth can repair? Or is the practice strictly compatible with more individualist or retributivist views — say, a nation-building consequentialism or a retributivism that treats amnesty as a regrettable necessity — so that ubuntu is a fitting gloss rather than a presupposition? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, say whether its parts (the admission-of-guilt requirement, the political-objective limit, and the ubuntu language) pull in the same direction, and tell me what would have to change if the same violations were handled on a straightforwardly retributive or purely individualist theory.
