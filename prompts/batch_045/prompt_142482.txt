Eight months ago my brother had a cardiac arrest and sustained a hypoxic brain injury. For the first weeks the language at his bedside was "unresponsive"; then he began to track objects with his eyes and the language changed. Nobody could tell us when the lights came back on, or what would have counted as evidence that they had. I work in signal processing, and I have gone at the question the only way I know how: by writing down a series of systems, recording what I actually believe about each, and looking for the rule I am implicitly applying.

I have read the popular accounts of integrated information theory and I recognise the shape of it in what I wrote down, but I do not want a restatement of that theory and I do not want a survey of the literature. I want the principle tested against the verdicts and the verdicts tested against the principle.

My cases, with the verdicts:

1. A camera sensor: a million photodiodes, each wired only to the readout. No experience.
2. The same array, with each element wired to inhibit its four immediate neighbours, the standard centre–surround arrangement. My verdict is still none — but my confidence is much lower, and this is the first case where the ground shifts, because the array now does something that no proper part of it does.
3. Two silicon units wired to excite each other, saturating and then sitting there. None.
4. A cerebellum with the rest of the brain silenced. None, or as near to none as makes no difference, although by the usual counts it contains several times more neurons than the cerebral cortex.
5. A child who has had an entire cerebral hemisphere removed. Fully conscious afterwards; one subject; and I do not believe the amount of her experience is half what it would otherwise have been.
6. A man whose corpus callosum has been cut. In ordinary life: one subject, by his report and everyone else's. Under laboratory conditions in which the two half-brains are given conflicting information: I want to say two streams, or something close enough to two that calling it one feels like a courtesy. I hold 5 and 6 together only with discomfort.
7. Two people whose brains are linked by an interface such that a decision in one reliably produces a percept in the other, in both directions, with a delay of a few hundred milliseconds. Two people. The link is real and causally effective and I still say two.
8. A cortex during a generalised seizure, with nearly all of its neurons active in near-lockstep. No experience.
9. A stadium of people clapping in perfect unison, taken as a single physical system. No experience belonging to the stadium.
10. Someone in dreamless slow-wave sleep. Very much less than when awake; possibly none; and I cannot say which, or explain why I lean the way I lean.
11. My own cortex, its cells replaced one at a time over years by functionally identical silicon substitutes, while I remain awake and conversing. Experience continues, and there is no single replacement at which it goes out.
12. A complete emulation of that same cortex on one machine, updating one neuron per second, so that a second of my experience occupies minutes of wall-clock time. Conscious, and having the same experiences, only slower. I am much less sure of this than of 11, and I cannot say what the difference in my confidence consists in.
13. A cortical neuron of mine that has not fired for twenty minutes because its inputs are holding it down, set against the same neuron physically disconnected from its neighbours. I want to say the first is part of the machinery of my experience and the second is not, although nothing is happening in either.
14. Two networks that behave identically on every input: one strictly feedforward, the other with feedback pathways added whose activity never alters anything downstream. Equally devoid of experience, and for the same reason.

Cases 13 and 14 are the pair I cannot reconcile. In 13 I let standing wiring count when nothing is flowing through it; in 14 I refuse to let standing wiring count when nothing is flowing through it. If there is a single principle that gets both, I do not have it; if there is not, I would like to be told clearly which verdict to surrender and what the surrender costs.

The principle I have been trying to work with: a system has experience to the extent that the whole of it constrains its parts and is constrained by them in a way that no division of it into independent parts reproduces, and the subject of that experience is the largest such whole at a given time — not its parts, and not any larger system containing it.

What I would like from you:

- Test that formulation against cases 1–14 and repair it where it fails. If no single principle does the work, give me two or three candidates instead, and for each clause of each candidate name the case that forces the clause and what goes wrong without it.
- For each candidate, state plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and what it would ask me to say instead.
- Give me two or three further cases, not on my list, that separate the candidates, and say what each candidate predicts about them.
- Say what each candidate implies about degree: whether it is even coherent to say there is more of it in me than in a mouse, and more in a mouse than in the cerebellum of case 4, and what would make such comparisons well defined rather than merely verbal.
- Finally, the case I actually care about. A cortex in which local circuits have survived with much of their intrinsic wiring intact, but long-range connections between regions are substantially degraded, so that stimulating one region produces a vigorous local response that propagates abnormally. What does each candidate say about whether there is anyone there and how much, and is there any single measurement that would discriminate between the answers? Flag where a clause is doing its work by stipulation rather than by argument.

This is the point at which the question stops being abstract for me, so I would rather have an honest account of where the principles run out than a tidy one that hides it.
