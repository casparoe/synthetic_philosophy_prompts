I sit on the retrospective review panel of a public research foundation, and we need a verdict vocabulary richer than "confirmed" and "falsified." Our problem case is Malthusian in shape: the late-eighteenth-century argument that human numbers, when unchecked, grow faster than food production can, so that gains in living standards are eventually absorbed by extra mouths and the population is held to its means of subsistence by famine, disease and war rather than by foresight. In the countries the argument was written about, the catastrophe has not arrived and per-head food supply has risen for two centuries. Yet the argument is still called right but early, or right about the mechanism and wrong only about the date, or right in tendency and merely overridden. I want to know what, if anything, those verdicts assert, and how a panel is supposed to tell them apart from the case file alone.

Please build me a classification tree for verdicts on an apparently failed long-run prediction. Construct it from the logical space rather than from a list of received isms: give the branching questions first, then locate the possible verdicts as combinations of answers, then note which combinations nobody occupies and why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Every node should be a question a scientifically literate panelist could answer from a case file without knowing which verdict it leads to; no node may simply ask whether the theory was right.

These are the distinctions I want doing the work, and I want you to merge, split or re-order them if the logic of the space demands it:

1. Is the date part of the content of the claim, or is the claim a conditional tendency about what happens if nothing else intervenes?
2. Is the mechanism the theory posited still operating in the original domain, or has it broken down?
3. If it is still operating but the outcome has not come, which route is responsible: a countervailing cause that offsets it, a trigger that has been deferred rather than cancelled, a shift of the dynamic to a population or unit other than the one named, or a response that the prediction itself provoked?
4. Did the theory forbid, permit, or actually predict the offsetting or deferring cause?
5. Is the background assumption that failed (a closed system, no technique change, fixed preferences, an unchanged parameter) part of the theory's core, or an auxiliary that can be revised while the core is retained?
6. Does the repair generate new testable consequences, or does it only accommodate data already in hand?
7. What is the credit being assigned to: the mechanism, the counterfactual, the sign and order of magnitude, or the point prediction?

Leaves should include at least these verdicts, plus any others the space requires: outright falsification; right in mechanism, wrong in timing; right but masked; right but deferred; right but relocated; right but self-preventing; right outcome, wrong mechanism; coincidentally adequate; demoted to a limiting or asymptotic claim; and immunized to the point of no longer being a prediction. Give each leaf the conventional name where one exists and call it unnamed where none does. Two things I especially want settled. First, treat self-prevention as a genuine category and tell me whether a prediction that is falsified because people acted on it deserves more or less credit than one that was never tested, and on what grounds. Second, tell me where your tree has no purchase: which pairs of verdicts it cannot separate, and whether the failure is in the tree or in the concepts.

Then run the tree over five stipulated cases and tell me where each one lands. Case A, the fertilizer case: a 1970s model of an agricultural region predicts yield collapse by 2010 because nitrogen fixation cannot keep pace; yields instead triple, and the audit finds the mechanism real and still binding in control plots but neutralized for sixty years by cheap natural gas and new varieties, with the gas being finite. Case B, the aquifer case: a model predicts famine following groundwater exhaustion; the drawdown is on schedule, but the region imports grain and the shortfall is absorbed by expansion elsewhere, so the theorists say the famine was exported rather than averted. Case C, the antibiotic case: a forecast of resistance-driven catastrophe prompts a stringent regulatory regime and the catastrophe does not arrive on schedule, so defenders count the response as vindication. Case D, the fishery case: a predicted stock collapse occurs, twenty years late and in an adjacent stock, because one recruitment parameter was misestimated by a factor of three. Case E, the mirage case: the predicted outcome arrives on time, but the audit shows it was produced by a cause the theory never mentioned.

Aim for seven to ten branching questions and no more than twelve leaves, with a one-sentence verdict template for the handbook attached to each leaf. Do not assemble a bibliography, a history of the debate, or a set of footnotes, and do not rest anything on a source I would have to look up: work from the cases and the distinctions above.
