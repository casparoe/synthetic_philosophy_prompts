Hey — hoping you can help me pressure-test a position I've been sitting on for a while. Some background: I teach a mid-level course on action theory and moral psychology, and every year my students reduce every case of weakness of will to "it's just hyperbolic discounting," which drives me up the wall, but I've drifted into something adjacent and I want to know how exposed it actually is. Call it the *judgment-shift* account of akrasia — my own label, I don't think anyone uses it, and I half-announced it at a department brownbag last week and got pushed on it immediately. The claim is that strict akrasia — freely and intentionally doing B while, at the very moment of acting, still occurrently judging A better all things considered — never really happens. Everything we lump under "weakness of will" is supposed to be one of three things: (i) ordinary preference reversal of the hyperbolic-discounting sort, where the near option looms larger simply because it's near; (ii) genuine re-evaluation, where at t2 the agent's judgment really has changed because of arousal, attention, salience, blood sugar, whatever, and the "against her better judgment" description is cheating by importing the t1 judgment; or (iii) cases where the better judgment isn't occurrent at all — she just doesn't have it in mind, which is listlessness or forgetfulness, not weakness.

So: what would count against this? I don't want a reading list, I want the map of its exposure. Concretely, here are the things I keep circling, and I'd like you to take them seriously rather than just agree with me. First, my own favourite hypothetical defeater, which I'll call the *zero-delay* case: suppose we set up a choice where both options are delivered immediately after the decision — no waiting period to discount, the smaller reward is not temporally nearer in any interesting sense — and agents still reliably take the one they'll tell you, in the same breath, is worse. Does that kill the account, or can the temporal asymmetry be replaced by some other asymmetry (attention, arousal, cue-reactivity) that does the same explanatory work and leaves the structure intact? Second, the phenomenology of effort: the feeling of dragging yourself, of expending something, of resisting, and the way that resistance fatigues within a session but seems to transfer across totally unrelated domains for at least some people. Third, ongoing rather than discrete cases — the person who keeps scrolling, keeps pressing, keeps not getting up, while occurrently thinking "I should stop now," with no re-evaluation and no future reward getting discounted, since there isn't one. Fourth, pre-commitment: if my judgment at the moment of action is always *my* judgment, why do I treat my future self as a problem to be managed with Ulysses-style arrangements? I know there's a standard reply there, but I want to know how much work it can really do.

Then the meta-questions, which matter more to me than the individual cases. Where would a committed judgment-shift theorist — imagine the most stubborn version of me — be forced to concede, and where would she be entitled to dig in and redescribe the counterexample? Because I can see the insulation risk clearly: whenever someone reports clear-eyed akrasia, I can always say "then it wasn't a real all-things-considered judgment, just a dispositional one," and at that point I seem to have made my view unfalsifiable by stipulation. Please tell me honestly whether that's what I've done, and if so whether it's fatal or whether it's the kind of thing a view like this is allowed to do. Which defeaters are fatal, which are merely awkward, and which would a defender just absorb? Also: what does the account predict that its rivals don't — I take it something like the disappearance of akratic conflict once the asymmetries are removed and the better judgment is made vividly occurrent, and no such thing as a domain-general capacity that gets depleted — and how cleanly could those predictions fail? Rank everything from fatal down to merely embarrassing, flag which of my three sub-cases is doing the most stolen labour, and tell me which one consideration you'd press if we were continuing this argument over drinks.

The reason I care, beyond the course: I'm rewriting my lecture on moral akrasia, the "I knew it was wrong and did it anyway" thing, and I want to know whether I'd be doing my students a disservice by telling them strict akrasia is a myth. If the judgment-shift account collapses, that lecture has to look very different — blame, resolutions, and the whole practice of holding yourself to something you predict you'll abandon all start to look coherent in a way my view can't easily accommodate. So be straight with me about how much of that I'm commited to giving up.
