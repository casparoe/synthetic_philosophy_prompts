I'm putting together materials for an undergraduate seminar on moral luck and the social management of envy, and one unit keeps stalling. The unit contrasts two habits: the American one of treating a person's success as evidence about them — as something earned, and therefore as grounds for admiration, deference, and a certain narrative of the self-made life — and the levelling habit found in Nordic consensus culture (the "don't imagine you're anybody" rule) and in the tall-poppy reflex of the Antipodean countries, where the visible success of a neighbour is treated as an injury requiring corrective censure. My students pick a side within about ninety seconds and then just trade intuitions. I want something that makes the deadlock funny in a way that exposes its structure rather than restating it.

So please write a satirical internal memorandum, no more than 700 words excluding the header block, from the fictional Directorate of Social Levelling of a small, prosperous, consensus-minded northern country, proposing a new national instrument: the Desert Adjustment Index. The Index is meant to break the stalemate by measuring, for each citizen, the split between the portion of their standing that is genuinely earned and the portion attributable to brute luck, inherited advantage, and the uncredited labour of collaborators, and then to issue a calibrated official response: a Praise Quota for the earned portion and, above a threshold of unearned advantage, a Levelling Tariff of scheduled ceremonial censure. The comedy has to come from taking the underlying philosophy seriously — the luck egalitarian thought that differences traceable to brute luck are not deserved, the difficulty of ever separating the two portions in a single life, the way any attempt to measure the split requires exactly the kind of expertise and authority that then has to be levelled in turn. The memo must deadpan its way through the problem of applying the Tariff to its own staff and must not resolve it. It should also note, with evident approval, that a visiting American consultancy sells a rival product — a Self-Made Certification that runs on the same underlying measurement engine — and that the Directorate can therefore license the software rather than build it; the reader should register that the two cultures have arrived at the same machinery without anyone in the memo noticing.

Format it as a memo header (To / From / Date / Subject) followed by short numbered sections, and include one worked numerical example of a citizen's assessment, laid out as a small table of line items (earned share, luck share, inherited share, uncredited-collaborator share, resulting Tariff band), plus one short quoted complaint from a citizen who has been issued a Tariff. Keep the tone bureaucratic and unwinking throughout: no italics for emphasis, no nudging, and the memo should never name the view it is quietly reducing to absurdity.
