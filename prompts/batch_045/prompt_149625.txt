Odd request, and sorry in advance for how long this is. For the last eight months I've been secretary of the town lido committee — an open-air pool built in the 1930s on land left to the town in perpetuity, committee minute books going back to 1937, the whole bit — and in June the council voted to close it and hand the site over to a trust that will "reimagine" it as a splash park. Half the committee say the lido has collapsed. The other half say nothing has collapsed at all: people will still swim, there's a twenty-five metre pool at the leisure centre two miles away, and the swimming club has already been offered lanes there. Both of those feel true to me and both feel like a way of avoiding saying something, and I've been going in circles about it for months — partly because my dad taught me to swim there and my daughter learned there last summer, so I am not remotely neutral.

So: I want a short story. Twelve hundred to eighteen hundred words, give or take. No headings, no bold, no bullet points, no tables — just continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter; blank lines between scenes are fine. British English throughout, please.

Here's the part I actually want tested, and I want it tested rather than explained. The definition I've been toying with goes like this: a thing — a polity, a city, an institution — collapses not when its people die, or its skills vanish, or its buildings fall down, but when the longest promise it can make becomes shorter than a human life. Farming, swimming, singing and child-rearing can all carry on exactly as before while that happens, which is why the people inside it mostly don't notice: from the inside, a shortening horizon just feels like being busy. On that definition the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, Rapa Nui, an English market town in 2026 and my pool can each be collapsed or not collapsed, and the interesting cases are the ones where the answer is yes and nobody involved would ever have used the word.

What I'd like the story to do, as quietly as it can:

Hold the asymmetry. From outside — say, someone digging the site in five hundred years, or me reading the 1937 minutes — the same situation looks like catastrophe; from inside it looks like a slightly awkward summer. I want both to be true at once and I don't want the narration to adjudicate between them. I've read the popular version of the Rapa Nui story where the islanders cut down all their trees and I've read the version where they didn't and most of the damage came from disease and slaving raids after contact; I don't need either one re-litigated, I just want that sense of a thing that reads as disaster from the outside and as ordinary life from within.

Make me feel what the word is for. "Collapse" licenses grief and blame; "change" licenses being cheerful; both of them seem to do something to what the people involved owe each other afterwards. I'd like that to come out of what happens in the story rather than out of anybody's stated opinions about it.

Give me at least one moment where a character nearly sees the horizon shortening and doesn't. Not an epiphany — a near miss, the way you nearly notice a smell and then don't.

Constraints: nobody gives a speech about any of this, no character explains the definition, and no last line that snaps the whole thing into a moral. I'd also rather nobody said "collapse", "resilience", "sustainability", "community asset" or "stakeholder" — if one slips in it should be in a flat, mundane sense. Set it wherever handles it best: my town, a fictional Late Bronze Age harbour, a Rapa Nui valley before the missionaries came, an office in 2031 — I don't mind, as long as it's concrete and particular rather than a parable with characters called the Archivist. Concrete beats wise, here.

If you want to add a couple of sentences at the end about what you were going for with the ending, do it as a plain paragraph with no heading. I've definately overthought this.
