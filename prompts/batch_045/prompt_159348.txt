You are a philosopher of mind who also writes literary short fiction — the kind of story in which the philosophy is carried by the situation and by what people decide under pressure, rather than by characters explaining ideas to one another. Take your time with this: before you write a line of prose, work out for yourself the shape of the dilemma, whose side you find genuinely forceful, and what the ending commits you to. Then give me the story, beginning with its first sentence and no preamble.

Write a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, third person, past tense, set on a neurology ward, built around the following case.

A patient in her early forties, nearly a year into a locked-in syndrome that has left her able to communicate only by coded eye movements, has a surviving slab of cortex — posterior insula and anterior cingulate — that was cut off from her thalamocortical broadcast system by a stroke. It is metabolically alive and it still responds to nociceptive input, but nothing it does reaches the systems that let her remember, speak, reason or act. Clinically she reports no pain at all; her blood pressure, pupils and face, however, disagree with her.

As part of a trial she has been implanted with a closed-loop device the staff call the bridge. A recording channel reads the isolated slab; a stimulation channel feeds that activity back into the intralaminar thalamus, and a single dial sets the gain — how strongly the island's output is broadcast to everything else. At gain 0 she rates her pain 0 out of 10 and says she feels fine. At gain 3 she reports a dull, grinding ache of about 4 out of 10, can describe it, remember it and be distracted from it by conversation; at gain 6 she rates it 9 out of 10, abandons her speech therapy mid-exercise, and begs them to turn it down. The recording channel shows something the team cannot stop arguing about: the activity in the isolated slab is exactly the same at every gain setting. The island appears to be doing the same thing whether or not anyone, including her, can have it.

The trial protocol expires at dawn. The team must choose between setting the gain to zero and leaving it there — in which case she will be discharged to a ward with no bridge, saying she is comfortable, and will be given no analgesia beyond what her autonomic signs prompt — or keeping the bridge live and treating the pain properly with opioids, at doses that will leave her too sedated to finish her therapy or to have what may be her last coherent conversation with her daughter, who is flying in that night.

What the story has to do:

Make the decision the climax, and make it a real decision: the reader should be able to tell which way it goes, and should feel why the other way is also coherent. The disagreement your characters are living inside is the one between treating conscious experience as nothing over and above global availability — the availability of a content to memory, report, reasoning and voluntary control, the sort of identification that global workspace theory invites — and treating what it is like for a subject as something that can in principle come apart from what is available in those ways. Your story must not assume either side is correct, and it must not wave the question away: nobody gets to say that in the end we cannot know, because the gain has to be set to something.

Dramatize the structural reason our ordinary tests fail here. Every method anyone on the ward has for finding out whether a person is in pain runs through the very channel whose status is in question — report, or behaviour that itself depends on broadcast. Show this by what happens, not by a speech about it: for instance, at gain 0 she grimaces at precisely the moment a catheter is moved, and when asked about it confabulates an explanation that refers only to something she does have access to, the tape pulling at her arm.

Give me at least two concrete, non-decorative technical or clinical details — numbers on the dial, timings, a specific thing she says in eye-code, the exact pattern on the recording channel — and make one of them load-bearing for the decision rather than local colour.

Constraints: no character may name a philosopher, a paper, or a theory by its label; no one may deliver a miniature lecture on the science of consciousness; no AI, robot, upload or simulation; and no closing twist that reveals the whole thing was a dream, a hallucination, or that the patient was never conscious to begin with. Do not include any bibliography, citations or list of further reading.

After the story, in at most 150 words and with no citations, state which horn your ending commits you to, and give the single strongest objection to the choice your protagonist makes.
