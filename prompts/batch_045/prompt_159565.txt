Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether I should keep driving to work, keep buying supermarket chicken, and keep bothering to vote, and I came away unable to locate the mistake in a chain of reasoning I am sure is somehow mistaken. He set it out like this.

(1) A moral reason against an act must be grounded in a difference the act makes: I have a reason not to φ only if, holding fixed everything I cannot affect, something that matters would go better were I not to φ. Reasons of complicity, expression, integrity and the what-if-everyone-did-that variety are bracketed: they are not reasons about what happens.
(2) In the three cases at issue my contribution sits below the grain of any response. The processor schedules flocks in batches of tens of thousands and the retailer orders in pallets, so the bird I decline is discounted, frozen or eaten by somebody else; my exhaust enters a system whose harms are thresholded and cannot be laid at the door of any marginal molecule; my vote shifts the result only in an exact tie. In none of the three does any outcome that matters differ.
(3) So I have no moral reason to refrain from the chicken, the drive, or the failure to vote.
(4) The same holds one level up. My letter to my MP, my petition signature, my attempt to talk round my colleagues are equally below the grain: no policy outcome differs, and whether any organisation gets off the ground is not up to me. No reason there either.
(5) Therefore, with respect to any large collective harm, nothing within my individual reach is morally required of me.
(6) And the requirement does not trickle down. The market, the industry or the electorate may be the sort of thing that ought to act, but an obligation borne by a collective is not an obligation borne by me: I cannot act as the collective, and we ought does not entail I ought when my part is inert.
(7) Conversely, additions that make no difference are not wrong: the litre of solvent tipped into the already-lethal lake, the five-hundred-and-first boot in a crowd that was already fatal.

I cannot accept (5) to (7), but I cannot say which step to reject, and three things about the argument make me more suspicious rather than less.

First, (2) looks like a contingent engineering fact, not a truth about collective action. If the retailer moved to daily replenishment with real-time demand forecasting — a software change at a depot I have never visited — my refusal would be read as signal and would cross a threshold, and by (1) I would acquire an obligation overnight. The argument seems to make my duties a function of the resolution of a distant response function. Is that a reductio of (1), or a consequence to swallow?

Second, hold the outcome fixed and vary only how the causing is distributed. One outlet whose effluent crosses the lethal threshold leaves its owner with a plain reason to stop. Five hundred micro-outlets, each below the grain, with no coordinator, produce the same effluent while, by (1) and (6), no obligation survives anywhere — and anyone who wanted the outcome could restructure their activity so as to obtain it obligation-free. I find it incredible that obligation can be annihilated by distribution alone. He says that is just what the argument shows, and that my discomfort is the complicity intuition he bracketed in (1).

Third, the buffering that makes (2) true is partly sustained by the behaviour that (5) licenses: it holds because people like me keep buying. Were enough of us to abstain, the batches would be re-cut and each abstention would start to register. So the premise is self-stabilising and the argument may be self-confirming. Is that a defect in the argument, or only an unremarkable fixed point?

I tried two repairs. Repair A: even if my act is usually inert, there is some small probability that it registers, so there is a small but real expected difference and hence, by (1), a small real reason. He replied that the reason is then scaled by a probability I cannot estimate and so cannot act on, and that the same calculation one level up gives a far larger expected difference for campaigning than for abstaining — so on my own repair what I am required to do is stop fretting about my own consumption and go and organise, which is not the conclusion I was reaching for. Repair B: I have a reason to play my part in a pattern that would make a difference if enough others joined. He replied that this presupposes the collective requirement (6) denies, that it is symmetric — it would give me a part to play in a lynching, or in a boycott certain to fail — and that it gives me a reason to do whatever my neighbours will do anyway, which is not a reason I recognise.

If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than keep my intuitions about fragmentation and redundant additions: I would sooner believe that distribution can dissolve obligation than believe that morality is not about what happens. But I would rather give up (6) than accept (5). My own guess is that (6) is where the chain breaks, and he says (6) is its strongest step; I cannot show why not.
