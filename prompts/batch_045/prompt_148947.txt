QUESTION (20 marks). In his reply to Benjamin Constant, Kant denies that we may lie to a murderer who asks whether an intended victim is hiding in our house. (a) Reconstruct the argument, and say whether it is best read as an application of the universal-law formulation, as an argument from the doctrine of right about the conditions under which asserting and promising can create obligations, or as something else. (b) A critic objects that the argument is self-undermining: it makes the wrongness of the lie depend on what happens afterwards, which is consequentialism in disguise, so the position collapses into the very thing it was meant to exclude. How does Kant reply to this, and does the reply work? (c) Someone in the seminar proposed an escape route: Kant holds that a promise or contract extracted by coercion is void, since a right can only be acquired through a free act of choice, so by the same token an assertion that a murderer forces out of you cannot ground a duty of truthfulness either, and the lie at the door is permitted after all. Does this escape route work inside Kant's own framework? Answer yes or no and say why.

STUDENT ANSWER. Kant's essay is a reply to Constant, who had mocked the claim that truthfulness is an unconditional duty by pointing out that on such a principle it would be a crime to lie to a murderer hunting for his intended victim. Kant bites the bullet: the duty of truthfulness admits no exception, not even to save a life. The argument has two parts. First, the universal-law test: I cannot will as a universal law that anyone may lie whenever it suits them, since in a world where statements and promises could be false whenever convenient, assertion and promising would lose the function that makes them worth using at all, so the maxim destroys what it depends on and cannot be universalised. Second, and more deeply, a lie treats the person deceived merely as a means. Assertion is addressed to a rational agent who is entitled to make up his own mind about what to believe and what to do; a lie bypasses his reason and steers his action in a direction he never chose, which is exactly to use his rationality as a mere instrument. That is why the duty holds even against someone who is himself threatening murder: he is still a rational agent, he has a claim against us that we speak truly, and nothing he does cancels a duty we owe to him. Note also that the duty attaches to assertion only, so we remain free to refuse to answer, or to barricade the door and use force against him, since coercion against coercion is permitted. On the charge that Kant is a closet consequentialist: that charge confuses liability with wrongness. The lie is wrong in itself, and the point about consequences is a separate juridical point, namely that if the lie brings about the death then the liar can be held responsible for it before a court, because the consequences are imputed to him as their author, whereas someone who answered truthfully could never be held liable, since he caused nothing. A court asks what you caused, not what makes an act wrong, so nothing in Kant's appeal to consequences makes the wrongness of lying depend on outcomes, and Constant's objection fails. (c) Yes, the escape route works. Kant says that a promise or contract extracted under coercion is void, because one person can bind another only through an act of free choice and coercion destroys the very condition under which a right could be acquired. The murderer's question is coercion, so the transaction of assertion never gets off the ground: there is no duty of truthfulness toward someone who is forcing the statement out of you. This lets Kantians keep the absolutism about lying in ordinary cases while permitting the lie at the door.
