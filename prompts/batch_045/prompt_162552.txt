I run a fortnightly evening class at a secular meditation centre, and next month I have a single ninety-minute slot in which I want to introduce the Buddhist no-self teaching to a room of people who have each done at least one ten-day retreat, have no philosophy or religious-studies background, and who have so far absorbed no-self almost entirely as a slogan — "the self is an illusion" — which they repeat fluently and cannot think with. No slides, a whiteboard, cushions on the floor, and an expectation that whatever is discussed will be of some practical use. What I need from you is a plan for the session, not the explanation written out for me.

The part I am least sure about is the order, and what depends on what: whether to open with the analysis of a person into the five aggregates, or with the felt sense of "I" and "mine" that attaches to experience and that the teaching is supposed to be a claim about, or with the prior question of what sort of thing the word "I" could be doing if the teaching is true. Tell me what has to be in place before the claim can even be stated so that it doesn't sound like nonsense or like a paradox. Then give me the single example or case that should carry the whole session, and say why the obvious candidates are worse: the old chariot analogy, the river or flame passing on, the image of a vehicle with no driver behind the eyes. One constraint on the example — I do not want the session to rest on the "look for the self and you won't find it" move, because a physiotherapist in the group will point out that you cannot find a body's health among its parts either, and yet health is perfectly real; I want that objection to be absorbed by the sequence rather than answered after the fact.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they will actually arise, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. From last term I can predict four: that nothing exists or nothing matters; that there must still be a witness standing behind experience which is what is really meant; that this is just the contemporary neuroscience story about the ego being a confabulating narrator — I take this to be the most dangerous one precisely because it feels like confirmation and quietly changes the subject; and the question of why I should care what happens to me in twenty years if there is no self, which I would like handled so that it falls into neither indifference nor a narrowly self-interested prudence. A fifth is coming as well: someone will ask what it is that carries on, and I have agreed with the centre's main teacher not to open the question of rebirth in this session, so I need to know what to say at that moment and where that moment should sit. Relatedly, one of the sharper students last time put it to me that if a person is merely a convenient name for a bundle, then the bundle is real and the name is decorative, and nothing has actually been denied — I would like that too to be dealt with inside the plan rather than bolted on. Finally: one question I can put to the group in the last five minutes that would show me the idea has genuinely landed rather than merely been accepted, and one or two things I should openly decline to settle at this level and say out loud that I am declining them. Please write your answer in British English.
