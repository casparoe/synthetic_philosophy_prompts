Please answer as a political philosopher who works on corruption, clientelism and the ethics of the gift -- someone who has actually sat in on an integrity committee and has a considered view about the familiar definition of corruption as entrusted power used for private gain. Take your time and think it through in stages: before you commit to anything, brainstorm at least four distinct decision rules that would jointly generate every verdict in the list below, write each one out explicitly in a sentence, and test each against the precedents. Only then turn to the new case. If fewer rules survive elimination than you expected, say so plainly rather than quietly falling back on your favourite.

Why this has stopped being academic for me: in March I took a lay seat on the integrity committee of the regional referral hospital in the city where I now live, about three weeks after my mother was told she would need dialysis. She waited fourteen months for a slot and got one three weeks ago; the panel that assigns slots re-ranks the whole list every twelve months, so she will be re-ranked in nine months. My family is now arguing about whether to give a gift to the clinician who chairs that panel. My brother says it is obviously a bribe. My aunt says the neighbourhood will say we ate her help and forgot it. I have recused myself from anything touching nephrology, so I am asking in the abstract: what would we say, and what should we say?

Our precedents, as minuted:

1. Cash in an envelope to the theatre nurse on the morning of a relative's surgery, so that the patient is not left till last in the list -- corruption, serious; nurse disciplined; the family not sanctioned.
2. A tin of biscuits and a public word of thanks from that same family after discharge -- not corruption; the committee minuted that to refuse would be discourtesy; no register entry required.
3. A junior doctor's lecture fee from a supplier whose product is on the formulary, disclosed in advance and entered in the register -- not corruption, but a managed conflict: register entry, and no vote on that product.
4. A supplier-funded scholarship for the procurement officer's daughter, fully disclosed, the daughter genuinely top of the merit list, awarded while the tender was open -- corruption, serious. The minute records that disclosure did not cure it.
5. A village water committee giving the district engineer's mother a goat and a length of cloth at the borehole commissioning, in public, months after the engineer ranked that village first on the published criteria -- not corruption; minuted that this is how gratitude is discharged here, with the proviso that had the ranking been improper the gift would have been evidence of it.
6. The records clerk accepting the equivalent of the official fee to lift a file from the bottom of the pile to the top -- corruption, minor; clerk sanctioned; the family expressly not sanctioned, the minute saying they were paying for what was already theirs by right.
7. A ceremonial shield presented to the hospital director by a visiting delegation at the opening of a wing -- a gift to the office rather than to the person; declared, displayed in the foyer; not corruption.
8. A senior surgeon's annual New Year hamper from a long-standing supplier, worth roughly two and a half times the register threshold, given when no tender was live -- not corruption, but registerable, and the supplier must not be sole-source.

The new case: my mother's slot was allocated properly, on the published criteria, by the panel and not by its chair alone. My family proposes to bring a ram -- worth about a month of my mother's pension, roughly six times the register threshold, but not extravagant by local standards for a thanksgiving of this kind -- to a small gathering with our pastor and some neighbours, and to present it to the chair of the panel, in public, with thanks. Nothing has been said to her in advance, nothing has been asked for, and nothing is expected; the giving is entirely backward-looking. But she will chair the re-ranking in nine months. Two further facts that may matter: the hospital's written policy covers gifts from suppliers and contractors and says nothing at all about gifts from patients or their families (a gap the chair has already flagged); and the ward runs a December collection for the unit's doctors which everyone contributes to and nobody declares.

What I want from you:

First, the surviving rules, written out explicitly, and your prediction -- corruption, permissible gift, or managed conflict with conditions -- with your reasons and a confidence level attached to the prediction itself.

Second, separately from the prediction, what we should rule if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which precedent is the closest analog, and -- if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it -- what principle we would be adopting with each option, and which minute we would effectively be overruling.

Third, the single further case that would best tell the surviving rules apart, with each rule's prediction for it. I have my own candidate: hold the gift, the donor and the gratitude fixed, but vary whether the recipient will ever again have any discretion over the giver. Tell me whether that is the right probe and whether a better one exists -- for instance one that holds the forward-looking discretion fixed and varies whether the transfer is one our local gift code recognises at all.

Fourth, and importantly: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. My sense is that there is real disagreement about whether the wrong of corruption lies in the giver's motive, in the distortion of an allocation that was supposed to be impartial, in the breach of a duty owed to the hospital as principal, or in the erosion of other patients' confidence in the ranking; about whether a purely backward-looking gift is structurally different from a bribe or merely a bribe paid in arrears; and about whether any of these standards are universal or are instead internal to a particular gift economy. Correct that map if it is wrong. In particular, I want to know whether a definition of corruption that was drafted with procurement and export credit in mind even reaches a gift from a patient's family to a clinician -- and if it does not, whether that is a defect in the definition or a sign that the case is not really about corruption. Finally, flag anywhere my own framing of this case smuggles in a Western assumption.
