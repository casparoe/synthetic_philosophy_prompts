I'm in a seminar on comparative economic history and global history, currently in the unit on the Great Divergence, and before I start writing my paper I want to settle on the standard I'm going to be held to. So: write me the desiderata that any adequate answer to the Needham-style question — why Britain and not China? — has to meet. Take the purpose as fixed: an answer is adequate if it would survive the strongest objection each camp in the literature would raise against it, at seminar-paper length. I don't want a bibliography or reading list, and please don't lean on my being able to look anything up: everything you use should be either in the givens below or reconstructible from what any educated reader already knows.

The givens we've been working with (if you think one of them is false, argue it in the collisions section rather than quietly dropping it):

(a) On the measures our readings use — real wages, urbanization, commercial density, agricultural productivity — the richest regions of China and of Europe look broadly comparable well into the eighteenth century.
(b) The sustained break — self-reinforcing growth in output per head, tied to a shift toward fossil-fuel energy and mechanized production — happened first in one small corner of northwestern Europe.
(c) Both sides had large bureaucratic states, extensive markets, and deep traditions of technical skill.
(d) "Britain" and "China" are not comparable units: one is a small island polity, the other a continent-scale empire, so the civilization-level comparison compares objects of very different size and internal variety.

What I want is the specification, not yet another answer. Here is my own half-finished list, which I'd like you to correct, extend, and re-cut rather than simply confirm — the thinking is supposed to be in the criteria, so tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudices of one camp:

- contrast-class sensitivity: an answer must say why the contrast is drawn where it is — why not India, Japan, or the Ottoman Empire as the comparator — and whether the answer would change shape if a different comparator were substituted;
- an intra-European test: if the answer is really about Britain, it should also explain why the break happened there and not in the commercially advanced regions of the Low Countries or northern Italy, which shared many of the same supposed advantages;
- the grain problem: the causal claim has to be stated at a level of aggregation at which it could be checked, given (d);
- a swap test: if the answer cites factor F, it must explain why other places that also had F did not break through;
- a symmetry test: the same feature must be described in vocabulary that applies to both units, so that, say, a strong state counts as capacity on one side and as despotism on the other only if the asymmetry is earned;
- a mechanism requirement: a named pathway rather than a correlation, with the step from wages, energy prices, or state finances to the actual investment and invention decisions;
- a timing requirement: why then, and not during the earlier commercial and technical flowerings on either side;
- a splintering question: whether there is one divergence or several, since the crossings in urbanization, real wages, energy use, and scientific practice need not coincide;
- a falsifier requirement: some specification of what would have to be true for the answer to be wrong, and of what kind of evidence could in principle discriminate;
- no definitional cheating: the explanandum must not be defined so that only the winner could have satisfied it;
- the scale of the causal claim: difference-maker against a background of shared conditions, versus sufficient condition, versus mere triggering contingency;
- microfoundations: whether the account has to work at the level of household and firm decisions, or whether structural and Marxist-style accounts can refuse that demand.

Write it in American English, in the following sections:

1. "The explanandum" — about 150 words. Fix what exactly is being explained, and give your verdict on whether the question as posed is well posed; if it isn't, state the reformulation (or the several narrower questions it should be split into).

2. "The desiderata" — about 700 words. A numbered list of the requirements an adequate answer must meet, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, each tagged as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and each with a note on what it excludes. Include any requirement I missed.

3. "Where the desiderata collide" — about 300 words. Which subsets cannot be jointly satisfied, and what follows from that: does the list itself show that no answer of the kind people want is available, or only that answers must be conjunctural, hedged, or reformulated? Name the single requirement that knocks out the largest number of familiar answers, and say why.

4. "Scorecard" — about 300 words. A compact table rating the familiar families of answer against the list: the coal-and-colonies ecological windfall, the institutions-and-property-rights answer, the culture-and-religion answer, the high-level-equilibrium-trap line, and the revisionist answer that treats the late date of the break as the main point. One row each, with the single cell that most damages it.

5. "My rubric" — about 100 words. The one sentence I should put at the top of my paper as the standard I'm trying to meet.
