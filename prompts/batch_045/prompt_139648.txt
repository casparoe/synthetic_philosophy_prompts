I've been asked to draft the consent section of our university's student conduct code as part of a wider review, and to supply the reasoning behind it rather than just the wording. Before I circulate anything I want to know whether the position I've arrived at is actually a coherent package, because I suspect I have stapled together conclusions that other people reached for incompatible reasons. Here, more or less, is where I have landed.

1. Consent is a mental state, not a performance. What makes an act permissible is that the person actually willed it. Words are evidence of that, and not checking is a separate wrong, but silent and unmistakably enthusiastic participation is consent, and a spoken yes accompanied by genuine unwillingness is not.

2. Deception invalidates consent only when the lie concerns what the act itself is or who is performing it, not when it concerns the surrounding inducements: money, prospects, intentions, feelings, whether the person is involved with someone else.

3. From 2 I want to infer two things: that misrepresenting one's STI status invalidates, and that removing a condom that was a condition of the agreement invalidates, since in both cases the physical act that occurs is not the act that was agreed to. I am not certain 3 follows from 2, and I am not certain where it stops.

4. Coercion requires an agent issuing a threat; hard circumstances never invalidate consent, however severe. The person who agrees because rent is due consents. The person who agrees because she has been told she will otherwise fail does not.

5. But I also want a cost-of-refusal condition along these lines: a yes is valid only if refusing would not have left the person significantly worse off than the proposal found her. I want this to rule out the professor who propositions a student he grades, and I want it not to rule out the person in 4. I do not think it does both, and I cannot see how to fix it.

6. A yes given while blackout-drunk is invalid, flatly, even where the person was enthusiastic, remembers it, and says afterwards that she would have agreed sober.

7. A yes obtained by impersonating someone's partner is invalid. I do not know what to say about the variant in which the impersonator is someone the person would in fact have chosen, and who afterwards says she got exactly what she wanted.

8. Separately, and as a flat rule: a student can never validly consent to a faculty member with supervisory power over her, whatever she says and whatever she feels.

9. Consent is to a specific act at a specific time. Consenting to one thing at midnight does not extend to another thing at three, and consent to one kind of contact does not extend to another.

What I want is an audit, not a tutorial. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it. Order the real tensions by severity. For each, say what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set, and then say whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most. Where you think one of my distinctions is unworkable rather than merely in tension, say so and say what should replace it. Please also state how confident you are in each of your main claims and name the one you are least sure of: I would rather have that on the record than have the package look tidier than it is.
