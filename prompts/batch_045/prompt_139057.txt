I'm applying for a three-month, single-author exploratory grant: no empirical work, no compute budget, deliverable a paper of roughly thirty pages. I'll supply the references myself, so the proposal should not contain a literature review by citation. What I need is the argumentative spine: a precisely stated question, why the obvious answers leave it open, what I would actually do with the time and why that approach rather than the alternatives, and what would count as the project having failed.

The topic is the worst-case versus average-case distinction as it appears in safety arguments for advanced AI systems. My half-formed thought, which you should restructure or replace if it does not survive scrutiny, is that the debate is usually presented as a difference of temperament — one side wants guarantees holding for every input, the other is content with low failure rates on realistic distributions — and that this presentation obscures a more tractable question: where are the probabilities in an average-case argument supposed to come from? An average-case guarantee is a claim about a distribution over cases, and it carries weight only if the system being evaluated is not in a position to influence which cases enter the sample or which cases its failures land on. A system capable enough to recognize that it is being evaluated, and better off failing only where failure is hard to detect or attribute, makes the outcome distribution endogenous to the thing being measured; that seems to undercut the standard reasons for preferring average-case arguments — their cheapness, their statistical efficiency, their fit with how we regulate other technologies — in a way with no analogue elsewhere. A second complication I want handled: average-case claims about per-query failure rates are often quietly converted into claims about an entire deployment, which presupposes that failures are only weakly correlated across a long, adaptively chosen sequence of queries.

Please state the question so that three months could in principle settle it, and say what form a positive result would take. Separate the parts of the question that are genuinely philosophical from those that are really empirical claims about how evaluation-aware systems behave. Give a month-by-month method, and say why it is preferable to the obvious alternatives: a formal treatment in terms of quantifier order over possible inputs, a decision-theoretic treatment in terms of the shape of the loss function, or a purely empirical study of how much evaluation awareness degrades existing evaluation suites. Then say what would count as failure — in particular, whether the honest conclusion might just be that the whole distinction reduces to a difference in risk tolerance, or to a difference in what one is willing to assume about correlation across failures — and flag where the project needs an empirical input I will not have.

Write the proposal in American English.
