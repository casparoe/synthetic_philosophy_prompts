I staff a small committee that allocates a fixed budget across social programmes. We price each outcome at what the affected people say they would pay for it, with a hypothetical equal-budget adjustment so that differences in ability to pay are held constant, and then rank programmes by the sum of those prices. Our economist member defends the whole method with an analogy I keep hearing in this area: money is to how much someone cares as a thermometer is to temperature. There is a single underlying magnitude, it is cardinal and comparable across persons, our instrument reads it off with some noise, and the standard objections — that willingness-to-pay and willingness-to-accept diverge, that the numbers move with framing, that self-reports are unstable — are measurement error to be reduced by better instruments, not evidence that interpersonal comparison is a mystery.

I want an audit of the analogy before our next meeting. Give the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the magnitude, the instrument, the fixed points, the noise, the calibration procedure, the fact of the matter that a better instrument would converge on — and then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use we are making of the analogy from the ones that do not.

Two worries of my own, which I would like assessed rather than merely agreed with. First, a temperature scale has its unit and its zero fixed by convention against independent checks; in our setting each person's scale is normalized by that person's own best and worst feasible outcome, and I have noticed a consequence: because the range depends on what else is on the menu, adding a third programme that offers generous options to one group shrinks the measured size of that group's losses everywhere else and can flip the ranking of the original two. Is that a defect in our normalization convention, repairable by fixing units and zeros independently of the menu, or does it indicate that the quantity we are pricing is not the kind of thing that has a menu-independent magnitude at all? Second, a thermometer reading is a measurement; getting the readings right does not by itself say how two people's readings should be weighted against each other, and yet our method simply adds them. Does the analogy quietly supply that ethical premise by presenting aggregation as measurement, and if so, what is the premise we would have to defend in its own right?

If the thermometer picture is carrying more than it can bear, tell me what comparison would do the job better for the decision we actually face — an exchange rate between currencies whose rate depends on the trader, a translation between idiolects, a ruler that stretches — and say what each candidate would imply about whether we should keep summing at all.
