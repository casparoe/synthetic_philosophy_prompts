My friend and I argued about Clifford and James until well after midnight and got exactly nowhere — which is unsurprising, since published discussion of Clifford's ethics-of-belief essay and James's will-to-believe lecture has been going for well over a century and nobody seems to move. We agree on the stock data: both of us condemn Clifford's shipowner, who talks himself into believing his fleet seaworthy and then sends emigrants out on it, and both of us think something is off in the case of someone who treats Pascal's wager as a licence to manufacture convinction. The interesting region is between those, and there my friend says the two men are not even addressing each other: Clifford is laying down a duty about how you form beliefs you could in principle have checked, James is talking about forced, live, momentous options where the evidence has genuinely run out, and once the cases are sorted that way there is nothing left to argue about. That makes a century of argument look like a labelling accident, and I don't buy it.

My own hypothesis is that both of them run the argument on propositions whose truth is fixed independently of what anyone believes — whether a hull is sound, whether a God exists — and that this choice distorts the whole dialectic. Consider what I'll call looping propositions: whether my colleagues will trust me with the difficult part of a joint project, whether a friend who has gone quiet still wants the friendship, whether a marriage or a currency will hold. For these, holding the belief is partly what makes it true, and Clifford's characteristic costs (walling a belief off from evidence, spreading credulity) and James's characteristic gains (arriving at a truth you could not have reached by waiting) stop pulling in opposite directions. If that is right, the debate has stalled because it has been fed a diet of inert propositions, and the live question is not whether it is ever permissible to believe on insufficient evidence but which propositions admit of a will-formed belief at all.

So diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Is my friend right that it is verbal, or two questions sharing a heading? Is it substantive — a clash about the standard of evidence, about whether belief answers to a norm of accuracy or to practical rationality, or about intuitions neither side can argue for? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work, for instance whether anyone can form a belief at will, or whether a belief adopted on pragmatic grounds then leaks into the rest of what one believes? Is my looping-propositions distinction any good, and does it reshape the dispute or merely add a case that both sides can absorb? Given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, an empirical finding about doxastic control, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected and why.
