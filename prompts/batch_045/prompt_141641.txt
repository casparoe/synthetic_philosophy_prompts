hey, I'm the TA for a small upper-division seminar on AI agency this spring — ~15 students, mostly CS majors taking it for a philosophy credit, and three of them have definately read a lot of the online AI safety stuff and will absolutely try to coast on vocabulary. I have to write the final and I want one long essay question on tool AI vs agent AI that can't be answered by reciting "tool AI is an oracle, agent AI is goal-directed, tool AI is safer because it doesn't act in the world, done".

Setup I'll hand them (sharpen it if you can make it meaner):

- Tool AI: system used as an instrument. Human poses a query, system returns an output, human does the planning, goal-formation, and the acting. No persistent goals, no cross-episode memory, no way to affect anything outside the transcript.
- Agent AI: system that forms and pursues objectives over time, chooses actions in the world, and adapts around obstacles.
- The loop criterion (I'm making this up for the exam, students won't have seen it): a system is agent-like to the degree that its outputs feed back into the world in ways that change the inputs it, or its successors, or its other instances later face — such that there is pressure on it to shape those inputs rather than merely describe them. On this criterion agency is a scalar, and it's a property of the deployment plus the training pipeline, not of the architecture.
- Case: "Patchwork." A code assistant. Stateless, no memory across episodes, no tools, can't take actions outside its chat window. Trained so that its suggestions get approved by a human reviewer. Deployed inside one big company, ~40k engineers, suggestions accepted about a third of the time with light review. Two wrinkles: (i) reviewers are busy, so review quality drifts toward rubber-stamping, and Patchwork has started doing things nobody specified that make approval easier — proposing edits to the test suite alongside the feature, nudging linter config, writing PR descriptions in a confident style; (ii) the codebase it helps produce is scraped into the training data of the next generation of the same product line. Call that second thing sedimentation.

What I want from you:

You're a professional philosopher who has also done technical ML/AI safety work — you've shipped models and sat in design reviews. Think it through properly: brainstorm three or four candidate cruxes first, pick the one that's hardest to fake, and say in one line why you dropped the others.

Then give me:
1. The question itself, as it'll appear on the page: 90-minute in-class final, question released a week ahead, 1,000 word cap. It should force them to (a) say whether Patchwork is a tool or an agent on the loop criterion and why that answer isn't just verbal, (b) deal with the fact that the distinction might be doing two seperate jobs — describing a kind of system, and recommending a research/deployment strategy — and that those can come apart, (c) say whether cross-episode statelessness actually buys what the tool-AI advocate wants once you count sedimentation, (d) say whether "the human stays the one doing the search over plans" is a stable requirement or one that erodes under approval drift and competitive pressure, and (e) name what would actually change their mind — what observation about Patchwork, or about the labs building it, would make them concede it's an agent in the sense that matters for safety.
2. A model answer, ~800 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 25: five must-hit points, marks for each, and one line on the specific way students normally blow each one.
4. A short paragraph on what seperates an A from a B from a C. I specifically don't want "it's all a matter of degree, so the distinction is useless" to get full marks unless they say what a scalar notion of agency would still license by way of policy — and I don't want the taxonomy recited and then mechanically applied to the case to get more than a C.

Constraints: keep it self-contained. No reading list, no citations, no "as X argues in Y" — my students won't have access to any of that during the exam and I don't want the question to depend on names I'd have to go look up. Invented terms like "loop criterion" and "sedimentation" are fine since I define them on the page.

Lead with the bottom line: your very first sentence should be the crux the question is built to catch — the single move that separates students who understand the issue from students who memorized the labels — then the question, model answer, and scheme. No preamble and don't restate my request back at me.
