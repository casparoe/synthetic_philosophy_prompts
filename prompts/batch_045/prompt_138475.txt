Hi — I'm in Year 13 and doing an EPQ (5000 words, due sometime around March, my supervisor is lovely but basically just nods at everything I say) and I want to do it on guilt versus shame: which one is actually the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can deliberately pick one over the other. Everyone I've read so far runs the same line — guilt is the healthy one because it's about what you did, so it pushes you to own it and repair it, whereas shame is about who you are, so it makes people hide, lash out or just give up — and that feels too neat to me. The bit I keep getting stuck on is that basically every argument for shame being the worse emotion seems to quietly assume shame is fundamentally about reputation: about being seen, being found out, losing face in front of other people. But I'm pretty sure I've felt something shamelike completely on my own, lying in bed about something nobody will ever know about, and it doesn't feel like guilt to me — there's no "I should fix this" in it, it's more like I've become a smaller person. So my half-formed idea is that the debate has stalled because "shame" is being treated as one emotion when the arguments only really land on the audience-facing half of it, and I want to split it — call the other half "witnessless shame" or something less clunky — and then ask whether the standard case against shame even touches it.

The second half-formed bit is about timing. I think guilt gets ranked higher partly because we keep asking which emotion we'd want in someone who's already done the thing and now has time to think about making amends, and guilt obviously wins that contest. If you instead ask which emotion you'd want firing in the two seconds before someone does something awful — the flinch, basically — the ranking might flip, since guilt needs a done deed and shame can in principle stop you beforehand. I've been calling this the timing test but I've no idea if that's already a thing with a proper name. Then the culture question: can a society actually choose which of these it leans on, through things like how schools do discipline, restorative justice, public apologies, the whole call-out/debate thing — or is that just something that happens to a culture over centuries and the "choice" bit is an illusion?
