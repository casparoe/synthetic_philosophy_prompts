I sit on a faculty committee that has been asked to abolish our department's century-old oral qualifying examination, and a colleague who opposes abolishing it has circulated a short memo built on Chesterton's fence. I have to answer the memo in writing next week, and I want to know how much of it I should concede before I do. I am fairly sure the last third of it is too clever, but I cannot say why.

The memo first quotes Chesterton's own formulation, from his 1929 book *The Thing*: "In the matter of reforming things, as distinct from deforming them, there is one plain and simple principle; a principle which will probably be called a paradox. There exists in such a case a certain institution or law; let us say, for the sake of simplicity, a fence or gate erected across a road. The more modern type of reformer goes gaily up to it and says, 'I don't see the use of this; let us clear it away.' To which the more intelligent type of reformer will do well to answer: 'If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it.'"

The memo then glosses this as follows: an institution that has lasted a long time has passed a kind of test of time, and so carries a presumption of validity; the burden of proof therefore lies with anyone proposing to remove it; and until the reformer can produce positive evidence that removal would be an improvement, the rational course is to leave it standing. It presents this as a direct application of Burke — the thought being that inherited practices embody the accumulated practical knowledge of many generations, knowledge that no individual reasoning in the abstract could reconstruct, so that established arrangements should be treated as justified until shown to be harmful.

The part I distrust is the memo's own extension, which its author calls the unbuilt fence. He argues that the argument is symmetrical. The status quo contains absences as well as presences, and an absence can have a history too: a requirement that was deliberately considered and rejected, a practice that lapsed after a review, a committee that decided in 1996 not to have one. If the reason we may not tear down a fence we do not understand is that somebody may once have put it there for a reason, then, he says, by the same reasoning we may not decline to erect a fence we do not understand, since somebody may once have decided against it for a reason. Chesterton's principle applied consistently is therefore not a conservative principle at all: it forbids every possible decision, including the decision to do nothing, and so it can give my committee no reason either way about the oral exam.

Please give me three things. First, sentence by sentence on the gloss: how much of it would Burke or Chesterton accept, and where does it slide — if it does — from a claim about what a reformer is entitled to *do* into a claim about what is probably *worth keeping*, and from "it had some reason" into "it survived because it was good"? Second, is the Burkean view and the Chestertonian view really the same view here, and would Burke accept a principle whose stated upshot is that you may destroy the institution once you understand it? Third, what exactly is wrong, if anything is, with the unbuilt fence: does it expose the principle as self-effacing, or does it depend on treating an absence as though it were the kind of thing that can have purposes — and if some absences really do have histories of the sort he describes, does that yield a symmetric principle or only a much narrower one? I would also like to know, at the end, whether any version of the fence gives the committee a substantive reason about the exam itself, or only a reason to go and find out more first.
