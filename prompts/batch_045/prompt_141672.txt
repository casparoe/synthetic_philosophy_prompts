My father died in February, leaving equal shares of the small manufacturing company he ran for thirty years to my brother, my sister, and me. The three of us now communicate only through lawyers, and everyone involved agrees that the alternative to litigating the company into the ground is to write a buyout mechanism into the operating agreement, which has to be filed in about seven weeks. My brother and I have exchanged more than forty letters about what the company is worth and still disagree by roughly thirty per cent; my sister thinks both of us are being absurd. A friend recently pointed out that the standard no-disagreement result — agents who share a prior and have common knowledge of each other's posteriors must have the same posterior — seems to say that what my brother and I are doing is impossible, so that one of its assumptions must be false in our case, and that which one is false matters a great deal for how the mechanism should be written.

I made the mistake of describing all this to two people I was in graduate school with, who have now spent four months arguing about it, at me. One of them works in epistemic game theory and holds that common knowledge of rationality, together with common knowledge or common belief of the payoff structure, is a legitimate idealization — the right *kind* of idealization — and that its deliverables (rationalizability, the epistemic characterizations of equilibrium, the agreement result) are the correct benchmark against which any strategic situation, including mine, should be read. Call this the Idealization camp. The other works on robustness and bounded rationality and holds that any conclusion which holds only exactly at the limit — probability-one belief, zero trembles, unbounded belief hierarchies — and fails at every interior point of the parameter space is a conclusion about no situation that has ever obtained, and that designers should instead work in models of common p-belief, epsilon-rationality, unawareness, or finite-depth reasoning, where conclusions vary continuously with the parameters. Call this the Perturbation camp. Each is free to relabel its own position in the document.

They have agreed, at my request, to an adversarial collaboration, on the understanding that I draft the joint statement. Please draft it.

What I want is a document both of them could sign, not a verdict and not a dialogue. Specifically:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should not be vacuous: I want the formalism both accept (states, type spaces or information partitions, priors, the distinction between factive common knowledge and non-factive common p-belief and their different fixed-point behaviour), the agreed diagnosis of why my brother and I can disagree at all, and whatever substantive claims about the models both are willing to assert. I believe both accept that literal common knowledge of a contingent, non-trivial proposition is never physically realized among human agents, and both accept that this is consistent with the idealization being useful — but if that is wrong, say so and put the item in the disagreement section instead.

2. A disagreements section, in two columns: for each disputed claim, the formulation the Idealization camp would sign and the formulation the Perturbation camp would sign, with a neutral gloss. No hedging for its own sake and no invented consensus: the point is to make the residual disagreement exact, not to split it. If some of the four months of argument was verbal, say which part and why, and say what the substantive question underneath it is.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. My guesses, which you should correct, drop, or add to, are: whether the discontinuity at probability one is a boundary curiosum or a reductio of the operator; whether the analysis survives when belief hierarchies are truncated at finite depth, and whether the finite-depth predictions converge to the limit as depth grows; whether the rationality in common knowledge of rationality carries logical omniscience with it, and what of the deliverables survives once it does not; whether the factive/non-factive choice of operator changes what the framework predicts about near-common-knowledge situations; whether the framework's output is meant descriptively or as a coherence constraint on outcomes, and whether the Perturbation critique lands differently in each role; and whether there is any notion of approximate common knowledge that supports approximate versions of the limit conclusions.

4. For each crux, a case both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I have two games for this, which I would like used as the shared testbed, because they are close to my actual problem.

**Game A (two players).** Ada and Bo. Nature picks a state, H or L, each with probability one half. Ada learns the state; Bo does not. If the state is H, Ada's software automatically sends Bo a message naming H; every message received automatically triggers an acknowledgement, sent back and forth without limit; each transmission is independently lost with probability p, and a lost transmission ends the exchange with neither side learning that it was lost. Nothing is sent in state L, and both know the protocol. After the exchange stops, the two choose simultaneously between Trade and Walk. Payoffs, as (Ada, Bo): in state L, Walk is strictly dominant — (Walk, Walk) gives (1, 1), and anyone who plays Trade gets −2 while the other gets 0. In state H: (Trade, Trade) gives (4, 4); (Walk, Walk) gives (0, 0); (Trade, Walk) gives (−3, 0); (Walk, Trade) gives (0, −3). Note that in state H, Trade-Trade is both payoff-dominant and risk-dominant. My understanding is that at p = 0 the efficient coordination is available in state H, whereas for every p greater than zero, however small, the pessimistic action is forced at every node, so that the limit as p goes to zero is not the p = 0 case; both camps tell me they accept this analysis, and draw opposite morals from it. If my understanding is wrong, correct it before drafting rather than building on it.

**Variant A′.** As Game A, except that before the automatic exchange begins, Ada may pay a cost c to convert her message into a public announcement that both hear, and that both hear with probability one, its publicness being transparent to both. In state H, for which c does the efficient outcome get restored, and does the answer depend on whether the analysis is run under common knowledge or under common p-belief with p close to one? Each camp should commit to an answer.

**Game B (three players).** The same structure with Ada, Bo and Cy, the automatic chain cycling Ada to Bo to Cy and back, and each of the three choosing Trade or Walk. In state L, Walk is strictly dominant as before. In state H, a player who plays Trade gets 4 if all three play Trade and −3 otherwise; a player who plays Walk gets 0. I am told there is a result, which the Idealization camp relies on and which matters to me because there are three of us, to the effect that with two players rationality and common belief in rationality, together with common belief of the payoff structure, already force the profile played to be a Nash equilibrium, while with three or more players the implication fails unless something further is assumed, such as common belief in the independence of the others' conjectures. State the result in your own words, precisely enough that it could be checked, and correct me if I have garbled it; both camps should sign whatever the correct formulation is rather than argue about who said what. Then have each camp predict whether the pessimistic unravelling of Game A survives here, and whether the three-player failure makes the Perturbation critique stronger, weaker, or simply orthogonal.

Please do not give me a reading list, references, or citations: I do not need them, and neither does the argument. I would also like the statement proper to stay under about fifteen hundred words, since it has to be short enough that both will actually read and sign it.

Finally, in a short appended section that is not part of the joint statement, tell me what follows for my deadline. The three candidate mechanisms are: sealed bids with a matching right for the siblings who do not win; a shotgun clause under which one sibling names a single price at which the other two must either buy that sibling out or sell to them; and an independent certified valuation that any one sibling can trigger by paying a fee, making the number public before bids are submitted. For each, record what each camp predicts about how it will behave among the three of us, and say whether any of them is recommended by both camps for the same reasons. If the honest answer is that no mechanism is robust across the disagreement, say that, and tell me which crux I should be betting on.
