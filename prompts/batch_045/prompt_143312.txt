My instinct is firmly with the fundamentalist picture most of the literature seems to assume: there is a base level of ungrounded facts, everything else is derivative, and the point of positing a grounding relation is to trace dependence back to something that doesn't itself depend on anything. But whenever I try to explain why infinite descent is supposed to be impossible or incoherent, I end up either asserting it or changing the subject, and I'm starting to worry my resistance is aesthetic rather than argumentative. Your target is the following package of claims, which I'll define so we're arguing about the same thing:

1. *Bottomlessness.* It is at least metaphysically possible for grounding to be non-well-founded: a world in which every fact is grounded in some further fact, with no ungrounded base. Call the toy version the Cascade: facts arranged in an endless sequence of levels, where each level's facts are fully grounded in the level below it, where each level has its own causal and nomological structure that is empirically adequate for the inquirers living at that level, and where no level is privileged as the basic one.
2. *The Insulation Principle.* For any fact F and any full ground G of F, F inherits no explanatory debt from whatever grounds G, and none from whether the chain below G terminates. Once the full grounds of F are in place, F is as fully explained as any fact could be; explanatory debts do not accumulate up a chain. (I made this principle up as a way of stating what I take the infinitist to need, so treat it as a stipulation you're free to refine, weaken, or replace if you can state the underlying idea better.)
3. *The parity claim.* Bottomlessness costs nothing that a terminated chain would have bought us — in particular, it does not make the world as a whole any less explicable than a world with a base is.

The objection I want you to defeat head-on is the Explanatory Deficit Objection: the thought that explanation is essentially an achievement with a stopping point, so that if every explanation owes a further explanation, the debt never gets paid and nothing is ever really explained — the picture of a chain hanging from a hook in the ceiling versus a chain with no hook anywhere, where the second one is supported by nothing at all. I'd especially like you to handle the following wrinkles rather than skate past them, since these are where I keep losing confidence in the infinitist:

- The transitivity tension. If grounding is transitive, then the top-level fact in the Cascade is grounded not only in the level immediately below it but in every level beneath that, so the local grounding story looks like a mere truncation of a much longer one. Does the Insulation Principle then amount to advising us to be satisfied with a partial story? Say why or why not, and say whether the infinitist should give up transitivity, restrict it, or reinterpret what transitivity delivers.
- The comparative question. In a bottomless world, does *more fundamental than* remain a well-defined relation? Is it still asymmetric and transitive? Is the infinitist committed to saying that nothing is fundamental, and if so is that a genuine cost, a verbal triviality given that fundamentality just means ungrounded, or something the infinitist should embrace?
- The global wrinkle. Now add to the Cascade, at every level, a fact about the tower's global structure — say, the fact that the whole structure has no bottom. If the global fact depends on the levels and the levels depend on each other downward forever, is that a benign mutual dependence, or has the vicious circularity come back in through the whole-parts relation? If your argument for bottomlessness doesn't extend to this variant, say so and say whether that limits the position.
