I convene a graduate reading group that works through decision-theory-adjacent questions in ethics and epistemology, and I want to build a one-page diagnostic handout — a decision tree, not an essay — that sorts participants by what they actually believe about cooperation among similar agents in large worlds. The handout has to work on people who know the one-shot prisoner's dilemma and Newcomb's problem but have never read the literature on the topic, and it should sort them by their answers about cases rather than by which label they find attractive.

The deliverable: a decision tree whose root is the fixed starting commitment, "in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against an exact psychological copy of myself, who faces the same choice with the same information, cooperation is what I should do." From that root, six to nine branch nodes leading to ten to twelve leaves. Each node is a yes/no or two-to-three-way choice about a concrete case, phrased so that a reader with no vocabulary for the debate can answer it — no node may name a decision theory or use the words evidential, causal, functional, acausal, superrational, or anthropic. The branch labels, by contrast, should say in one clause exactly which claim does the splitting (for example, whether a merely statistical correlation between your decision and another agent's is enough to act on, or whether the reason to cooperate scales with the number of correlated agents). Each leaf carries the name of the position, or 'unnamed' plus a descriptive label if nobody defends it, and one sentence on its least comfortable commitment.

I want the tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: tell me first which combinations of answers your questions permit, which of those cells are occupied by a view someone actually holds, and, for the empty cells, a one-line diagnosis of why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unoccupied and worth visiting. Then tell me which pairs of leaves your nodes cannot separate, with a sentence each on whether some further case would pry them apart or whether the collapse is principled.

Four cases must appear as nodes, because these are the ones my group stalls on. First, the Hazy Mirror: scattered through a very large world are roughly a thousand agents whose reasoning is a slightly degraded copy of yours — same history and dispositions, plus one idiosyncratic experience each — so that they match your choice in such dilemmas about eighty percent of the time. Does the case for cooperation survive, and if it does, do their payoffs count in full, in proportion to how closely they track you, or not at all? Second, copy-spam: an adversary who wants you to defect mints fifty thousand very low-fidelity copies of you, each facing such a dilemma and matching your choice about fifteen percent of the time. Does this give you more, less, or the same reason to cooperate as before, and what does your answer imply about whether the view can be exploited by anyone able to manufacture copies? Third, the twin who thought about it longer: your exact copy has spent one more hour on this very problem and concluded that defecting is correct, and you have no reason to think their reasoning is worse than yours. Your move. Fourth, a node that isolates scale: does the strength of the reason depend on how many correlated agents there are, and what does the view say when there is exactly one, or when the world is only probably large?

I also want one node that tests whether a participant's cooperation in the root case is genuinely decision-theoretic: stipulate for them that the correct theory of rational choice says defect in the copy case, and ask whether they still cooperate on moral or axiological grounds. That node should separate views that locate the reason in the structure of the decision problem from views that locate it in what is good, and its leaves should not be collapsed with the rest.

Among the leaves, these positions must appear: the baseline that cooperation needs a causal or contractual channel; the large-world correlational view in both its exact-copy-only and its graded-similarity forms; the view on which what matters is running the same decision procedure, so that similarity is a proxy rather than the point; a variant that discounts or ignores correlations produced by adversaries or by simulations; a view that treats the right response as building commitment devices or institutions that track real-world correlation, and defecting without them; and a view on which cooperation here is grounded in identity or in what is impersonally good rather than in any facts about correlation. Where a leaf has no settled name, label it descriptively rather than inventing a school.

Keep the whole handout under about fourteen hundred words: an indented or ASCII tree, one line per node on why that question is diagnostic, the occupancy table, and the list of inseparable pairs. No citations, reading lists, or footnotes — I want the conceptual scaffolding only, and I want it in a form I can hand to someone and have them work through in ten minutes.
