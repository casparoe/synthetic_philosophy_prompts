I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a chapter on higher-order evidence, and there is one configuration I keep running into that I can't find a settled name for or a settled treatment of, so I want to know whether it has one before I coin something. Let me give three cases. (1) A large meta-analysis in my area reports that studies of a certain common design produce false positives at a very high rate. The meta-analysis is itself a study of that kind: same statistical machinery, same publication pipeline, and its headline result is a single estimate of a false-positive rate that it says you should trust. So if I credit it, I have strong reason for distrusting the very method that produced it; if I discount it on account of what it says, I seem to have no reason to discount anything at all. (2) The clean contrast: I learn from a blood test that a drug I'm taking impairs the formation of new long-term memories. Nothing in that bad news has to travel through the faculty it indicts — the test result sits in front of me in writing, and I can re-read it. No circularity, no puzzle, ordinary undermining defeat. (3) The messy middle, which is the one I actually care about: the indictment is delivered by a method that is nearly, but not quite, the method it indicts. The meta-analysis uses the same broad statistical framework but a far simpler, preregistered design and a huge sample, and the critique it makes is explicitly about small-sample, flexibly-analysed studies. Whether it implicates itself now depends on how coarsely I describe the method: under a coarse description it is self-indicting, under a fine description it isn't, and it seems I can't settle which description is the right one without already taking a stand on the first-order question. There is a further wrinkle in both (1) and (3) that I think matters: the indictment is one-directional. It doesn't say the method is unreliable tout court; it says the method systematically over-produces positive findings of a certain kind, and it is itself a positive finding of that kind. So trusting it tells me to be more suspicious of exactly the sort of claim it is, and not of the opposite claim — which means the usual symmetry of undermining defeat is missing.

I've had two unsatisfying responses. My supervisor said this is just the generality problem for reliabilism wearing a hat: how finely to individuate the belief-forming process type. Someone in my reading group said it is 'self-undermining', but as far as I can tell that label is standardly used of theories or principles that discredit themselves — e.g. conciliationism applied to the disagreement about conciliationism, or an argument whose conclusion is that arguments of its kind are untrustworthy — not of a piece of evidence that discredits its own source. And the neighbouring literatures seem to run in the wrong direction: there's a well-known family of cases, one of which I half-remember involving a gas gauge, where a source's own deliverances are used to establish the source's reliability, and there the complaint is that the reasoning seems to work too well, licensing knowledge too cheaply. My cases are corrosive rather than credential-granting. Also, epistemic akrasia and the enkrasia/evidentialism debate look like they concern the output state — believing P while taking your belief that P to have been badly formed — whereas my worry is upstream, about what such a piece of evidence requires of me in the first place.

So my question is not really for a label. I want to know what this phenomenon is and where its edges are. Does it have an established name or literature of its own, and if several established concepts are nearby, which fits best and how exactly does my case differ from each? Concretely, I'd like you to address: (a) whether the self-application means such evidence can never oblige a first-order credence shift and can only move one's confidence about one's own confidence, or whether there is a non-trivial fixed point — some stable intermediate degree of trust in the indicted method that the evidence supports, and if so how you would find it; (b) whether the diagonal, non-diagonal and near-diagonal cases really differ in kind, or whether (2) is just the limiting case of a continuum; (c) whether the grain-sensitivity in (3) is genuinely an instance of the generality problem or whether self-application adds something to it, since there the choice of grain seems to become something one can have reasons to prefer rather than a descriptive matter; and (d) whether the one-directional form is a different phenomenon from the symmetric form, or the same one with the sign changed. Please also give me two or three boundary cases that look like this but aren't, and say what distinguishes them. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and tell me what would count against the definition. I'd also find it useful if you said whether any of this bears on practice — for instance on how a field should weigh its own meta-science during a replication crisis, or on how to treat a source that volunteers that sources like it are not to be trusted.
