Bit of background before the actual question. I'm retired, forty-odd years in a biology department doing insect sensory physiology — mechanoreceptors on locusts, mostly, not riveting to anyone accept me — and about three years ago I started going along to the philosophy society down the road. Somehow that's turned into me being the person on our college's ethics committee who gets asked about anything with a wire in it. Which is how I've ended up trying to think about whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer. I've got a pile of verdicts and no principle, and it's driving me up the wall.

Going on gut, roughly in order of how confident I am:

1. Gridworld thing, Q-learning, a few thousand parameters, minus one for hitting a wall. Nothing there. Not a whisper.
2. A chess engine whose evaluation drops when it's losing, and which then picks moves partly because of that. Also nothing — although I notice it does have a signed internal signal steering action selection, which is meant to be the interesting bit.
3. C. elegans, 302 neurons, learns to avoid a smell it's been paired with something nasty. Probably nothing, but I'm a lot less sure here than in 1, and I don't know why.
4. A honeybee in a proboscis-extension setup, quinine on the antennae, learns to keep its tongue in. I want to say there's something there, faintly, and my only reason over case 3 is that it's a bee and bees are bigger and do cleverer things.
5. A fish on a hook, thrashing. Yes. If you tell me no I'll stop listening.
6. A big language model fine-tuned with RLHF, low score from a reward model on certain outputs, negative gradient churning back through billions of parameters. No — but my actual reason seems to be "it's made of the wrong stuff", which is the sort of thing I was taught to be embarrassed about in 1981.
7. The neurological patients with damage in the cingulate region, or thereabouts (I'm a biologist, not a neurologist) who still feel the pinprick, can say it's sharp, and say it doesn't bother them at all. Not suffering, or barely — and I think that's a different verdict from "no pain", which is why I've put it in the list.
8. A case I built to make myself uncomfortable: an agent with an explicit "hurt" unit that the architecture forces to modulate every downstream decision, that the agent can only suppress by paying a real cost in reward, and that it does pay. I feel a strong pull to say yes, and I also suspect I've just written the conclusion into the premises.
9. And a worry rather than a case: it's occurred to me that if anything here suffers, it might be the training run rather than the trained model — ten million episodes of negative prediction errors rather than the tidy policy that comes out the end. I've no idea whether that's a philosopher's move or just confusion.

The bit that got me going was noticing that in RL the thing doing the learning isn't the reward, it's the reward prediction error, and that only differences in reward matter: add the same constant to every reward and the optimal policy doesn't move. So "the reward was negative" isn't a property of the dynamics at all, it's a choice of units — whereas pain being unpleasant isn't a choice of units. So when someone says the reward signal just is valence, I want to know what they're actually claiming, and which bit of the machinery is supposed to be the candidate: the TD error, the value estimate, the advantage, the gradient, the exploration noise, or something else.

So: what principle, if any, captures verdicts 1 through 8? Which of them does it force me to drop? I've got a rough stab at one myself — something like: it suffers if a signal of its own making marks the current state as to-be-avoided, if that signal changes what the whole system does next rather than just one module, and if the system would pay a cost to switch the signal off — so please test that against the cases as well as offering your own, and repair it rather than just replacing it. Then tell me what the principle says about: (a) an agent that learns its own reward from a world model instead of being handed one; (b) case 6 at ten times the scale, with a reward model trained on human distress ratings; (c) case 9, if that's a real distinction; and (d) whatever case you think is the actual test that I haven't listed. If you think case 8 cheats, say so and tell me what an honest version would look like. And where the whole question is just silent — where neither "suffers" nor "doesn't" applies — say that instead of stretching a theory to cover it.

One practical thing: please don't send me off to read papers or give me a reading list. I can't get at a university library from here and I'd much rather have the argument in front of me than a pile of references.
