I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and at a science-journalism workshop next month I am running a seventy-five-minute session for roughly twenty working reporters and editors — sharp, impatient, no machine-learning background beyond what they have had to file about — on a question our coverage keeps flattening: when a lab, a paper, a regulator or a minister says that a system is aligned, what exactly is being claimed, and is the thing everyone calls the alignment problem one problem or several.

I do not want the session written. I want it planned.

Here is the decomposition I would like them to walk out with, which I may well have got wrong. Four parts I take to be standard: the specification problem, of getting the stated target to be the thing you actually wanted; the optimizer problem, of whether the system that learns to hit that target ends up wanting something else on the way; the oversight problem, of grading output you are not able to evaluate directly; and the generalization problem, of whether whatever held in training holds in the situations the system is actually put to. To those I want to add two I think our reporting misses entirely. The first is the mandate problem: even a system that flawlessly tracks the values of the people who built it leaves untouched who authorized those people to act on those values at that scale and in that domain, which looks to me like a question of political legitimacy rather than of machine behaviour, and which no amount of progress on the other four settles. The second is the second-owner problem: whatever alignment you achieved in your training run has to survive the weights being copied, fine-tuned, quantized, and wired into someone else's product, so a property of your system in your hands is not automatically a property of the thing out in the world.

So: give me the order in which these have to be introduced and why — what has to be in place before the next piece can be understood rather than merely asserted — and where, in that order, the two additions of mine belong. Then give me the one example that should carry the whole session, and justify it: what it must contain (I would guess a case where the system does precisely what it was asked and the result is still a failure), what it must not contain (no robot that becomes conscious, no machine that disobeys in a way a reader could cheer), and why not the obvious candidates that get reused in every explainer. Then the misconceptions this particular audience will arrive with or form in the room: I predict the rogue-machine frame, the assumption that alignment means removing bias or that it was finished when the system was trained on human feedback, the habit of treating competence and safety as the same variable, the idea that there is a single thing called human values to aim at, and the treatment of aligned as a property of a model rather than as a relation between a system, a task, a context of deployment and a stretch of time. Tell me which of those I have misdescribed, tell me which predictable one I have missed, and tell me the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than merely listed. Give me one check I can run in the final five minutes that shows the structure has landed rather than the vocabulary — I would rather they do something with a real-looking claim, such as sorting paragraphs of a fictional announcement by which of the six problems each could even be evidence about, than that they define a term — and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and say to the room that I am refusing it. Assume no readings, no links and no names to look up: everything has to stand on what is said in the room.

Two more things. First, pushback: if my six-part map is muddled, if two of the boxes are really one box, or if the mandate problem is not a species of alignment at all but something else borrowing the word, say so plainly before planning anything and tell me what decomposition to use instead — I would rather have the map corrected than delivered, and I would rather you told me my premise is confused than that you built me a tidy session on top of it. Mark also, part by part, where you are describing something close to a consensus among people who work on this and where you are following my framing into territory they would not follow, and tell me which of the six you would present flat and which you would hedge. Second, format: close with one line, set apart from the plan itself, of the form \answer{...} containing a single sentence of no more than twenty words that gives the spine of the session — the one sentence I would want a reporter in that room to still be able to repeat a month later. Keep the whole reply under about nine hundred words.
