I host a podcast on AI governance and epistemology, and next week's guest is an ML researcher we will call Dr. Lena Voss. She argues that work on scalable oversight via debate is aimed at the wrong failure mode. The standard worry in that literature is that a weak human judge cannot check the individual steps of a superhuman debater's argument, so a debater can win with long or obfuscated arguments whose flaws the judge has no way to locate. Her claim is that the deeper problem is the payoff structure: under winner-take-all, zero-sum judging, both debaters are rewarded for whatever moves this particular judge, including style, confidence, and selective framing, so training models to win debates selects for persuasiveness to a fixed judge rather than for tracking the truth. Her proposed repair keeps the adversarial protocol but defers the scoring: each debater's individual claims are scored later, against evidence that arrives independently, using a strictly proper scoring rule, so that reporting honest probabilities is what pays.

Draft seven or eight interview questions that press her past her own abstract. I want at least one each on: (i) whether her critique of the payoff structure actually escapes the original assumption that honest claims are easier to defend than dishonest ones, or only restates that assumption at a different level; (ii) whether deferred scoring simply assumes trustworthy ground truth for exactly the claims where human judgment is weakest, given that if such a check reliably arrives one might not need debate at all, and that the systems being overseen may influence what the check reports; (iii) credit assignment, since a debater's later score may depend more on its opponent's errors than on its own contributions; (iv) whether the adversarial legal system she cites as precedent really supports her, given that courts surround partisan advocacy with rules of evidence, discovery, and a judge empowered to strike arguments, none of which her protocol contains; and (v) what experimental result would make her abandon the protocol. For each question, give the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and no question should amount to asking her to explain what debate is.
