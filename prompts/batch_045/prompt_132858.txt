I translate song lyrics and short poems as a hobby and have no training in translation theory. My judgments about which versions count as faithful are consistent enough that I suspect there is a rule behind them, but every rule I try returns the wrong answer on at least one case. I would like the rule, or to be told which judgment to give up.

My cases:

1. A love poem in a strict rhyme scheme. To get the English to rhyme I write *dawn* where the original is closer to *daybreak*, and I drop a colour word that had no room. Fine.
2. The same poem in the original's metre and rhyme, but with the setting moved from a Provençal village to a New England one, because the New England place names are what rhymes in English. Not fine.
3. The original puns on a word meaning both *to weigh* and *to grieve*. My version has no equivalent pair, so I put a pun on *still* — unmoving, and also *yet* — at a different point in the line. Fine; in fact my favourite thing I have done.
4. The original is in a metre that was already old-fashioned and slightly creaky when it was written. My version is smooth contemporary English with the same line lengths and all the dictionary meanings intact. Not fine.
5. A refrain repeated word for word four times in the original. I vary the wording on each return because exact repetition sounds singsongy in English. Not fine.
6. A poem built on the fact that two words sound identical in the source language. English cannot do this, so I drop the rhyme and impose a constraint the original never had — fixed syllable count per line, and the line-initial letters spelling the addressee's name — so the reader can feel that something structural was going on. Fine, or at least less bad than the alternatives.

The principle I keep groping toward: what a translation should carry over is not the words the poet ended up with but the shape of the problem they were solving, the constraints they were under and the way they bumped against them. Two things make me doubt it. In 2 I reject substituting target-culture material for source-culture material, but in 3 I accept substituting a target-language pun for a source-language one. And in 4 I insist the translation reproduce what is arguably a defect of the original, while in 6 I let it invent a constraint the original does not have at all.

Is there a single principle that returns all six verdicts, and if not, which verdict is the cheapest to give up? Please state the principle precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause in it, and tell me what it predicts for the case I keep reversing myself on: a song whose melody fixes how many notes can be sung per line, where the singable version changes the images and the faithful version cannot be sung at all.
