I teach an undergrad seminar on the ethics of work and I keep hiting the same shape of argument, and I don't have a word for it. Nobody, on whichever side, will say the thing that seems to be at stake — that there is something good about not working. The case for limiting work is always made in the vocabulary of work itself: rest is an input, burnout is a cost, shorter hours make people more productive, or, as with the French 35-hour week, they spread the same amount of work around so fewer people are idle. Even Russell's essay in praise of idleness, which comes as close as anything I know to just asserting that leisure is good, spends a good deal of its energy on the unemployment numbers. And on the other side, overwork gets condemned by pointing at karoshi — the wrong of it seems to become legible only once it kills someone and shows up as a loss of capacity. Then there's a second thing I'm not sure is the same thing: when people actually get the time, they rebuild it in the image of work — coached hobbies, tracked sleep, holidays that are basically a shoot — and unstructured time makes them feel guilty.

Does this have a name? And are the two halves one phenomenon or two? I know Weber's protestant work ethic thesis, and I've heard that Adorno has something about free time and Arendt something about labor versus work, but those all look to me like stories about work being honored, or about leisure being hollowed out from the outside by the culture industry; what I keep seeing is the critic of overwork conceding work's own measure in the act of attacking it. I don't want a bare label — I want the carving: what would and wouldn't count as an instance, whether the contemplative or sabbath cases are counterexamples, where the edges are, and whether a right to leisure can be argued at all without reproducing the pattern. If nothing established fits, say so and coin something, with a definition and boundary cases.
