Hey — quick context before the actual question: I'm helping out (volunteering, not my day job) with a city housing agency that's building a model to rank applicants for a really scarce rental-assistance program, so what actually matters isn't the risk score as such, it's just the top ~200 slots on the list. The thing we're predicting is whether someone re-enters homelessness within a year, and of course getting the assistance changes that outcome, so we never observe what would have happened to the people we do help. A colleague on the team and I have been arguing about which fairness criteria we can even coherently state here, and I keep failing to articulate a distinction that I think is doing all the work.

I know the familiar impossibility results in outline: if the groups have different base rates and the predictor isn't perfect, you can't have both a score that means what it says within each group and equal false positive and false negative rates across groups. What I can't figure out is whether that conflict is really about the *pair* of criteria or about the *variable you condition on*. My colleague proposed a criterion we've been calling matched-record error parity: for any two applicants who look identical on every recorded feature except group membership, the model's error rate conditional on that shared feature vector should be the same across groups. So it conditions on the features, not on the true outcome and not on the score. I'd love an explanation of three things, and please just reason it out — I can't get to a library and I don't want citations or a reading list, I want the machinery.

First: what is the mathematical content of these impossibility results, really? Is it a statement that the joint distribution can't simultaneously satisfy two constraints, or is it a case where two rival normative demands just happen to be expressible as distributional constraints, so that the "impossibility" is doing less work than it sounds like? I've never been sure whether to read these theorems as bad news about the world or as a taxonomy of things you can't want at once. Second: where does my colleague's criterion sit relative to the usual ones — is it secretly equalized odds in a costume, or is it independent, and does it collide with calibration-by-group for the same reason or a different one? Third, and this is the bit I care most about: I have a hunch that the whole thing turns on how fine-grained the conditioning set is. If the feature vector is very coarse the criterion seems to collapse back into something like the ordinary equalized-odds story, and if it's fine enough that you essentially never observe two people with the same features in different groups, it starts to look vacuous — unfalsifiable from data, or trivially satisfiable. Is that the right shape of the dial, and does the conflict get stronger or weaker as you turn it? And does any of this change in our top-k setting, where only behavior near the cutoff matters and where the outcome we're scoring against is partly caused by the decision itself, so the "base rates" the theorems talk about aren't even a fixed quantity but depend on which policy we run?
