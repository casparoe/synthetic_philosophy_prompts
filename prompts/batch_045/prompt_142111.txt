I write novels, and I am in my second year on the programme committee of my national writers' association, which represents roughly four thousand authors of fiction and narrative non-fiction. We meet on Tuesday to settle one item, and I need a document I can circulate in advance.

Our annual conference opens on a Thursday evening with a platform slot that has traditionally been given to a humorous address. This year it went to a member who writes satire. Under a policy we adopted two years ago, after a reading that went badly and a committee statement afterwards that satisfied nobody, platform scripts are now submitted in advance for committee review. Hers arrived three weeks ago. It is a deadpan sequence of memoranda from a fictional Bureau of Verbal Neutrality, each one superseding the last: the Bureau replaces gendered compounds in official prose with neutral coinages, and then discovers that each neutral coinage has, within a single season of use, been colonised by whatever it was coined to avoid — the furniture sense of chair, the student-rank sense of first-year, the corporate sense of staffing — so that the Bureau is driven to ever more contrived formations and ends by mandating a term the audience has never heard and cannot interpret. Its implied claim is that neutrality is not a property a word can have but only a temporary absence of noticing, and that the right response to a default that has become visible is to keep dislodging it rather than to arrive at a formulation that is finally clean.

Two committee members want it withdrawn. Their first argument is tactical: the association's house-style reform — replacing gendered compounds in our own publications and committing us to prefer language that does not presume a default kind of person — is on the agenda of the annual general meeting the following morning, and a room of six hundred people laughing at invented neutral vocabulary the night before will hand the reform's opponents a joke they can repeat all day. Their second argument is that this is not really an accident of timing: a satire whose mechanism is laughter at reformed language will be received as a satire of the reform, whatever its author says she meant, and the association is responsible for the uptake of what it platforms, not merely for the intentions behind it. Our content policy, they note, bars us from platforming material that holds a group defined by a protected characteristic up to ridicule, and while the script ridicules no group, it does hold up for ridicule the activity of reforming language.

The satirist's reply is that the piece endorses the reform and only denies that it settles anything; that a conference unwilling to laugh at its own verbal pieties has no business legislating them; and that programming on the basis of how a slot might sway the next morning's vote is exactly the kind of stage management the committee exists to prevent. She adds — and this is why I am not willing to settle it by intuition — that the model for her piece is Hofstadter's 1985 essay A Person Paper on Purity in Language, in Metamagical Themas, which is built on the same device and which most of our members first met as a defence of reform rather than as an attack on it.

Two further facts complicate it. The satirist has herself been on the receiving end of the language the script parodies. One of the two members calling for withdrawal has publicly opposed the house-style reform, though I do not think the committee should be in the business of ruling on his motives, and I want the memo to say how much weight that fact can bear.

What I need is the committee's decision memo: a clear recommendation — keep the slot as programmed, keep it with conditions (a framing introduction, a printed note of what the piece does and does not claim, a respondent, a different place in the programme), or return it for revision or withdraw it — a short statement of the reasons, and an honest account of the strongest version of the case we are voting down. Assume the committee contains people who admire the piece and people who are genuinely hurt by it, and give me something both could accept as a fair description of what we decided and why, even if only one of them is pleased.
