I'm on a small committee drafting the allocation protocol for a regional disaster-relief fund: a fixed pool distributed once, among claimants we can identify by name in advance, using information they self-report, with no repeat interaction, no possibility of later side-payments, and a written explanation owed to every claimant we turn down. That last institutional requirement is why I can't just reach for total-welfare maximization. Every draft we produce ends up giving refusals of the form: your claim failed because honoring it would have produced less total benefit than honoring theirs. Several of us think that sentence does not treat the person we are addressing as a separate person at all — that it is a statement about the world rather than an answer to them, and that a claimant who asks "what about me?" has not been spoken to. So before I argue about which rule to adopt, I want the specification I should be holding candidate rules up against.

Write me the desiderata an adequate account of aggregation across persons must meet: a theory that takes seriously the thought that persons are separate, each with their own single life to lead, while still being a theory an institution could actually operate. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. At minimum I want you to deal with: the many-versus-one rescue (you can save five strangers or one stranger, and whoever you don't save dies), including whether the theory owes a verdict there at all and whether a refusal to aggregate generalizes into paralysis; the transplant-style case where the numbers are even better but the benefit is produced by conscripting an unwilling person, where I want your view on whether the distinction between aggregating benefits across recipients and using one person as a resource for others is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of one tradition; the trivial-benefits case (enough very small improvements to outweigh one serious harm), where the theory has to say where, if anywhere, numbers stop mattering; what I'd call addressability — whether the justification can be stated to each losing claimant in a way that speaks for them, or whether some form of "you were outweighed" is unavoidable; the divisible-pool case where one claimant's stake is much larger than each of five others' but smaller than their sum; and the different-people case, where option A benefits these people and option B benefits entirely different ones and no one is made worse off either way, where I want to know whether "no one is made worse off" can do the work that "no one is used" was doing in the transplant case.

Two further requirements I'd like you to assess rather than simply grant, because I'm not sure they are real: first, invariance under redrawing the boundaries between persons — call it no-gerrymandering-of-persons — the demand that verdicts stay stable when we re-describe one long-lived claimant as two successive claim-stages, or when several household members pool what is in effect a single claim. My worry is that a strongly separatist theory positively rewards this kind of re-description, and so might be unable to meet the requirement without ceasing to be separatist; tell me whether that is a genuine desideratum or a trap. Second, the operational constraints our setting imposes: our evidence is ordinal, self-reported, and noisy, so the theory must be usable without cardinal, interpersonally comparable welfare levels, or must say plainly that it cannot be; and it should not let claimants improve their position by splitting, merging, or misreporting their stakes.

Then the part I most want: which subsets of the list are jointly unsatisfiable? My two suspicions are that the separatist core cannot be combined with no-gerrymandering, and that addressability cannot be combined with the refusal to level down (the refusal to make some worse off for the sake of equality when that helps no one), but tell me where you think the impossibility really lies, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring; a brief scorecard at the end — no more than a short paragraph each — against four families is enough: straightforward summation of benefits; refusal to aggregate with equal or weighted chances instead; priority to the worst off, including a leximin variant; and a sufficiency-threshold view that stops caring once someone has enough.
