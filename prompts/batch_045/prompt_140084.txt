I have no training in philosophy or neuroscience; I have been reading about split-brain cases on my own, and I have ended up holding a view I want stress-tested. Call it the disunity view: when the corpus callosum is severed, what had been one subject becomes two. Not one person with a damaged connection, but two streams of consciousness, each with experiences of its own and each largely unaware of the other. The familiar conflict cases look to me like the behaviour of two agents sharing a body: one hand buttoning a shirt while the other unbuttons it, the speaking hemisphere inventing a reason for a choice the silent hemisphere made. And the standard deflationary explanations, cross-cueing, subcortical transfer, confabulation, strike me as machinery that can be wheeled out whatever the data turn out to be.

What I want is a map of what would count against this view, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and honest about its insulation.

First, the defeaters themselves: which findings or arguments should actually move me? I take two to be the strongest candidates, patients who have had an entire hemisphere removed yet seem to remain a single ordinary subject, and the apparent absence, in the patients themselves, of any first-person sense of being two. Tell me whether I am overrating either, and what else belongs higher on the list.

Second, what would defenders of the disunity view themselves concede as counting against it? I do not want the critics' list of objections; I want the ones the view's own friends would accept as genuine costs.

Third, and what worries me most: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? Suppose a future surgical shunt allowed the connection to be switched off and on in the same person, and that with it switched off the person reported no sense of division and no change in what it was like. I can already hear my own side's reply: the reporter in the switched-off state is confabulating, or there is no single person reporting, or the memory of unity is reconstructed after the fact. Is there any version of that experiment my side could not absorb? If there is none, is that a defect in the view, or simply the kind of claim it is?

Fourth, a problem I arrived at myself and have not seen addressed. If severing one bundle yields two subjects, why stop at two? What fixes the number of subjects in a brain, and is there any principled stopping point short of one? If the honest answer is that the count depends on what our experiments can isolate, then the number of subjects becomes a function of our instruments, and that reads to me as a reductio rather than a result.

Fifth, I have found it useful to distinguish having one experience from having one place where experiences get reported and acted on. Tell me whether the disunity evidence is really evidence about the second being read as evidence about the first, and whether any evidence could bridge the gap.

If it matters: I am moved most by first-person reports from the patients, and least by arguments from what consciousness is supposedly for.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
