I teach an upper-division seminar on the philosophy of work, cross-listed between our philosophy department and the labor-relations program, and I'm writing this year's midterm. The unit on collective action went badly last time: almost every student answered with some version of "workers own their labor, so they have a right to withhold it," and then stopped, as if that settled everything. I want a question that makes that answer look like the beginning of the problem rather than the end of it. The crux I have in mind is that a strike's leverage rarely consists in merely not working. Two features seem to do the real work. First, employment contracts are radically incomplete: a workforce can impose large costs while doing exactly what the handbook requires, by declining the uncodified flexibility — staying late, answering messages off-shift, skipping a redundant check — that actually keeps throughput up. Call this the work-to-rule case. Second, a walkout is effective only if the employer cannot readily replace the strikers, which is why picket lines and appeals to replacement workers matter; the strike therefore seems to contain an exclusionary or quasi-secondary element that a pure withdrawal-of-what-you-own model cannot obviously license. So I want the question to force students to choose between a withdrawal model and something else — a voice model, a reciprocity or corrective-justice model, a "countervailing power" model — and to say what their chosen grounding implies about both the work-to-rule case and the exclusionary element, including the case of the replacement worker who wants the job and crosses the line anyway.

Practical constraints. It's a closed-book, in-class exam: 45 minutes, no library, no sources, so the question must be self-contained and must not require any knowledge of actual labor law doctrine, real cases, real unions, or the scholarly literature — please set the whole thing in a hypothetical jurisdiction and say explicitly in the question text that students should not appeal to what any real legal system holds. The scenario I've been sketching: a large distribution warehouse with a few hundred workers whose contract has just expired, a dispute over a scheduling algorithm, a detailed but largely unenforced procedures manual, a work-to-rule campaign that cuts throughput by roughly 40 percent, an employer response of deducting pay for "partial performance," and then an attempt to bring in agency temps, some of whom turn back at a picket line and one of whom crosses it. Use that if it helps, or improve on it. I'd also like a short final part that asks what their account of the strike implies about the related right to organize: specifically whether it is only a right of association, or whether it must include a right to be recognized and bargained with, given that a union whose employer may simply decline to deal with it has no strike to threaten.

What I need from you: (1) the question as it would actually appear on the exam paper, with the scenario and part-structure written for students; (2) a model answer of about 700 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth, a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong, and grade descriptors that name the specific moves separating an A from a B and a B from a C. Please also give me a single sentence, to print at the top of the model answer, stating the crux in a form a grader could use as a heuristic for spotting answers that only restate the withdrawal model. Please write everything in American English.
