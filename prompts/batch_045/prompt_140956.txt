Here is my situation. I am writing the framing document for the opening weeks of a seminar on infinity, and I want to begin with Zeno. The purpose of a solution, for my purposes, is not to reassure anyone that motion happens — they already believe that, and so did Zeno, presumably. A solution is adequate for me if it leaves a student with a sharper grip on four things: what it is to complete infinitely many things, what a continuum is and whether physical time and space are one, what an instantaneous state of a moving body could be, and how a mathematical model relates to whatever it models. That's the standard I care about, and I'd rather have a list keyed to it than a generic one.

I also have three stress tests I want every candidate to be run against, because I think they separate solutions that have located the real difficulty from solutions that have located a difficulty.

The Eternal Runner. Take a body moving at constant velocity that never starts and never stops — no starting gun, no finish line, no landmarks, just uniform motion. The usual presentation of the Dichotomy leans on getting started, and that framing loses its grip here; but one can still ask whether it gets through the infinitely many nested halves of any hour you care to mark out, and the Arrow's difficulty — that at each instant the body occupies a region exactly its own size, and occupying a region seems to be the opposite of moving — is entirely untouched. I take this to show that the worry about completing infinitely many tasks and the worry about what motion is at an instant are different worries. Confirm or deny that, and tell me what follows for anyone who wants a single unified answer.

The Quantized Course. Re-run the race on a track graded in whole centimetres with a clock that ticks in hundredths of a second, so that the regress halts after finitely many steps. Does a solution that works equally well in the continuous and the discrete case thereby show that it was never really about infinity? And is it a cost or a virtue if a solution works only in the discrete case?

Self-application. Point the reasoning at time itself rather than at a body in space, and then ask whether the apparatus a proposed solution uses — limits over a continuum of instants, measure, instantaneous velocity, summed durations — already assumes the kind of structure the argument calls into question. I suspect this test does a lot of work, and I suspect a lot of standard answers fail it. If I'm wrong about that, say so.

Here is my own first pass at the desiderata, which I expect is partly confused:

1. It must locate a specific error — a premise that is false, or an inference that is invalid — and not merely reassert the conclusion everyone accepts.
2. It must leave our ordinary conviction that motion occurs, and our ordinary ways of talking about it, more or less intact.
3. It must explain why the arguments grip us; refuting them without diagnosing the pull is only half a solution.
4. It must be uniform: one treatment covering the Dichotomy, Achilles and the tortoise, the Arrow, and the one about the moving rows.
5. It must not lean on mathematics unavailable to a fifth-century Greek, on pain of anachronism.
6. It must not presuppose what is in question (this is my self-application test, promoted to a requirement).
7. It must say what Zeno was arguing for — on the usual reading, something in support of Parmenidean monism or against plurality — and engage that, rather than rebut a decontextualized puzzle about whether runners finish races.
8. It must not settle the matter by stipulation, e.g. by simply declaring that supertasks are possible, or that there are no instants, or that instants are sets of events.
