I'm writing up my position on imprecise credences for a decision-theory seminar and would like an audit before I present it. Here is the package I am inclined to defend:

1. Rational credence is sometimes irreducibly imprecise: a set of probability functions rather than a single one, where in such cases having a sharp credence would be a defect rather than merely one permissible option among others. The width of the set is supposed to track how far one's evidence falls short of settling the question.

2. Credal sets should be convex.

3. Learning E is entirely a matter of conditioning every member of the set on E and discarding the members that gave E zero credence; there is nothing more to updating than that.

4. In choice, options are ranked by their worst-case expected utility across the set (Γ-maximin).

5. Dilation is a real phenomenon and not a reductio of the framework: there are cases in which learning something widens the interval, and widening can be the correct response.

6. I want to keep what I take to be the sound core of the Dutch book arguments: rationality rules out accepting a package of bets that together guarantee a net loss, and that constraint does genuine work.

7. Cost-free information can never make a rational agent worse off: such an agent never declines free evidence and never pays to avoid learning.

8. In the Ellsberg set-up, the preference for betting on the known-composition draw rather than the ambiguous one is rationally required, not merely rationally permitted.

Which pairs of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look as though they are? Please treat this as an audit of the set rather than eight separate comments: rank the real tensions by severity, say for each what would have to be given up and at what cost, and identify the single revision that would clean up the most. Do not list everything as equally problematic, and do not resolve a tension by quietly dropping the commitment that generates it. Where a tension only emerges in a particular kind of case, construct the case; where an apparent tension dissolves on inspection, state the distinction that dissolves it. I am not attached in advance to any item on the list.
