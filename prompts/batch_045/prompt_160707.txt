In most hospitals pain is recorded as a routine vital sign on a 0–10 numeric rating scale, with protocols that escalate analgesia once a patient's own report crosses a threshold; yet the same protocols also treat two cases in which that report is unhelpful or absent: patients with asymbolia for pain, who describe a full-intensity, well-localized burning or crushing sensation while insisting that it does not bother them at all, show no distress, and never ask for relief; and patients who cannot report at all — infants, intubated or demented patients — where clinicians fall back on grimace, guarding and withdrawal. What theory of the badness of pain does this package presuppose: that the badness lies in the sensory-discriminative state itself, that it lies in a distinct unpleasant or hurtful affect, that it lies in pain's motivational role in producing avoidance and demands for relief, or that any of these will do so long as the subject is averse to what they feel? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined — could an "affect only" theorist and a "sensory state" theorist each sign on to the whole protocol for different reasons? Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if the badness of pain were exhausted by whichever component asymbolia removes, and whether treating asymbolic patients would then be treating something not bad at all. Finally, take up a mirror case that no protocol I know of addresses: a patient who reports no intensity, no location and no sensory character whatever, yet is emphatic and visibly distressed that something terrible is happening to them and begs for it to be stopped — does that require treatment, and does your reconstruction have the resources to explain why or why not?
