Two friends of mine got into it at dinner the other night and I've been chewing on it since. The topic was split-brain patients — people who've had the corpus callosum cut for epilepsy, so the hemispheres can't exchange information. You know the finding: something flashed to the right hemisphere gets picked out correctly by the left hand, while the patient — who is naming something else entirely — denies having seen anything and improvises a reason for what the hand just did. Josh said this is a case of two conscious subjects in one skull. Maya said it's one subject whose consciousness has come apart functionally. It went roughly like this.

(1) Josh: two streams, each with its own information, each steering its own behavior, neither available to the other — that's what two subjects looks like from the inside. The only reason we ever say there's one subject in a head is that normally everything gets pooled; remove the pooling and you can see there were two experiencers sharing one body all along and we just had no way to tell.

(2) Maya: that inference needs a premise you haven't given. Distinguish access unity — contents being jointly available for report, reasoning, and action — from phenomenal unity: two experiences being had together, as aspects of one experience, such that there's something it's like to undergo both at once. The surgery data show access unity failing. They don't show phenomenal unity failing, and no behavioral evidence could settle that, because being phenomenally together just isn't the same property as being informationally together.

(3) Josh: then phenomenal unity does no work. If the only evidence anyone could have — mine, or the patient's — for two experiences being co-conscious is that they're integrated, reportable together, reason-guiding together, action-guiding together, then a unity that survives the total loss of integration is unfalsifiable. In a normal brain, integration is all the evidence we've got; you're asking me to believe in an extra fact that floats free of every possible test.

(4) Maya: not free of every test — I've got introspection right now. The visual field, the hum of the fridge, the itch on my ankle: I don't infer that they're together, I'm directly acquainted with their being together, and that acquaintance is the datum any theory of unity has to accommodate. So there is a positive criterion, it's just first-personal — and since the patient's reports come from one hemisphere only, we can't run it on the case that matters.

(5) Josh: which is exactly the problem, and it cuts your way too hard. On your own criterion, either the right hemisphere's stream isn't conscious at all, or it's conscious and there's no accessible evidence either way. If it isn't conscious, there's no second subject and my claim is false; if it is conscious but cut off from the reporting subject, that just is my two-subjects claim. And the fact that we'd naturally call the speaking hemisphere "the patient" smells like an artifact of which side has language, not a fact about which one is the subject.

(6) Maya: fair — but the tie shouldn't be broken by counting. Take a case I made up on the spot: both hemispheres fed pixel-identical input, perfectly synchronized, same scene, no cross-talk. On your view there are two subjects undergoing numerically distinct but exactly similar experiences. On mine there's one. And maybe in that setup nothing distinguishes the answers, which suggests the count can't be the primitive thing; something about the streams has to make them one or two.

(7) Josh: fine, here's what makes them two — a bandwidth dial. Say the surgeons can install an adjustable cross-hemisphere link, start it at full bandwidth and dial it down continuously to zero. At zero you have my two subjects, at full you have one, so somewhere on the dial the number of subjects changes. If phenomenal unity is all-or-nothing there has to be a cliff: a millisecond more latency and a subject pops into existence. I'm fine with that. Thresholds are everywhere in nature and a sharp transition isn't a contradiction.

(8) Maya: I'm not fine with it, and I'll offer it as an objection rather than a rebuttal: a count of subjects that flips on an arbitrarily small physical change, with nothing else about the streams changing at that instant, is a brute fact with no explanation available — which is a reason to suspect the wrong kind of question is being asked, not a reason to deny the cliff. Maybe "how many subjects are here" can be indeterminate in the middle of the dial without there being some further fact we're ignorant of, the way "how many clouds are in the sky" can be.

They went round for another twenty minutes and I genuinely coudn't tell who was ahead. Its been bugging me. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say which points are right, which are confused, and which are right only given a premise that's still up in the air? And what is each of them missing — the thing neither one got to that settles more than any of the individual moves? I want an adjudication of these arguments as they actually went, not a survey of how philosophers have framed the issue.
