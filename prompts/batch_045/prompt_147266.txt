So I'm retired now — thirty-odd years of measuring grain-size distributions in estuary sediments, which is probably why any of this grabbed me — and I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the library (mostly retirees plus one very patient postdoc who comes for the tea). This term we've somehow ended up on infinity. We did the diagonal argument last week, the one where you imagine a complete list of the reals and then build a number that can't be on it, and I think I followed it: there really are more reals than naturals, not just "more" in the folksy sense. The bit I can't get my head around is the next question, whether there's a size in between — a collection of real numbers too big to be listed but too small to be everything. Someone said it's independent of the usual axioms: you're free to suppose such a set exists and you're free to suppose it doesn't, and neither assumption wrecks anything, so the axioms themselves will never settle it (assuming they're consistent in the first place, which apparently you can't show from the inside, another thing I'm still chewing on). Half the group concluded from that there's no fact of the matter at all; the other half concluded our axioms are just too weak. We were still at it when they turned the lights out.

I've taken up writing verse in retirement — nothing fancy, I mostly send poems to my granddaughter — and I said I'd bring something to the last session. I want a poem about this, and I have a structural idea, which you should absolutely bin if it doesn't work. My thought was a pantoum: the rule that lines 2 and 4 of a quatrain become lines 1 and 3 of the next, with everything recycled and the last stanza folding back on the first, is basically what countable means — every line that shows up is scheduled to show up again, and in principle you could say where. The reals are then whatever escapes the schedule: something the recycling can never turn up no matter how many stanzas you add, which is the diagonal. And the open question is the middle case, a body of lines the rule can never reach but that also doesn't fill up the whole space. I can't see how to make that middle case actually live in the machinery instead of just being asserted next to it, so if my conceit is broken, fix it, or swap in another form that carries it better and tell me why in a line.

Content-wise, I'd like it grounded in the stuff I actually know: grains you can count versus a distribution you can only measure, a coastline that keeps getting longer the finer your ruler gets, silt that is neither a list of particles nor a smooth curve. But it has to be right — someone who properly knows set theory should read it and not wince. So: don't imply we don't know how big the continuum is (we know it's two-to-the-aleph-nought, right?); don't slide from "independent of the axioms" into "there's nothing to be right about"; don't drag in the axiom of choice as if it were the same issue; and don't make the middle set sound like it's merely unknown, since the whole puzzle is that positing it and denying it are both consistent. Ending-wise, I'd like an image that works either way, something that holds both halves of my group at once instead of awarding the argument to one of them — the session honestly ended in a draw and I'd like the poem to end there too. Call it 30 lines or so, and don't add an explanation after it: I'll be reading it out loud and I want it to stand on its own.
