I'm a retired geophysicist — thirty-odd years of seismic and borehole inversion, mostly groundwater, a bit of mineral exploration. So the vocabulary I actually think in is: forward model, data vector, noise model, likelihood, prior, posterior, non-uniqueness, regularization, the resolution/variance tradeoff, identifiability, parameters versus nuisances, calibration against core, and cross-validation on wells you held out.

I've recently started going to a philosophy reading group at the local university (they seem to tolerate me) and I've wandered sideways into this fight in archaeology about whether you can get from objects to beliefs and institutions — whether a deposit tells you about ritual, whether a settlement layout tells you about property or inheritance or who was allowed to eat which animal, whether some class of fancy objects tells you there was a chief. From what I can gather there's a camp that says yes, provided you build proper bridging arguments between behaviour and the stuff that ends up in the ground (something they call middle-range theory, and there's a phrase about a "Pompeii premise" that seems to be about whether the record is a snapshot or a mess), and another camp that says material culture is meaning-constituted, so any inference to belief is basically you reading your own assumptions into the dirt, and that what looks like evidence is just your prior coming back at you.

I'd like you to translate that disagreement into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then tell me what didn't survive. Concretely:

1. Restate both sides using forward model, likelihood, prior, posterior, identifiability, regularization. I suspect the second camp's complaint is going to come out as "your posterior is your prior with extra steps, because the data don't constrain the parameters you care about" — but tell me if that's a distortion, and if so what the real shape of their argument is.

2. Two things I've invented for myself, which I want put through the same translation and then shot at. First: the record is a low-pass filter. Deposits time-average, sites are palimpsests, so if the timescale over which some rule or institution operated is shorter than the time-averaging in the deposit, no quantity of data can resolve it — a Nyquist-ish point. Is that argument actually available to archaeologists, and what happens to it when the forward model (how behaviour becomes deposit) is itself the uncertain bit? Second: my "two independent channels" rule — I'd only believe an inference to a belief when two channels with genuinely different physics converge, say faunal remains and house floor areas, on the same site. Where does that fail? I have a worry that the channels aren't independent at all because they share the same bridging assumptions, which if true would make my rule a lot weaker than it sounds.

3. The residue report. What in the archaeological disagreement has no counterpart in inverse-problem talk? My guesses, which you should correct: there is no independently known forward model, since the map from behaviour to deposit is itself a social claim; the thing being inferred is partly made of the agents' own concepts, so the target isn't passive the way a subsurface is; the hypothesis space can't be written down in advance, so there's no way to say what you're selecting among; and there is no core to calibrate against — no independent in-situ measurement of "they believed X". For each of these say whether it's real content, whether it's just the ordinary hard case of an inverse problem dressed up, or whether it's a sign the original claim was never clear enough to be true or false.

4. The bit I actually care about. In my field you can tell whether you're in the data-dominated regime or the prior-dominated one: sensitivity to the prior, width of the posterior, whether predictions hold up on held-out wells. Is there an honest analogue available in archaeology — could anyone actually run that check on, say, inference to ritual — and has the debate stalled partly because nobody can? And if the answer is that the whole business is prior-dominated, does that vindicate the sceptics or does it just describe a young field with thin data?

One last thing: someone in the group said this is all just Duhem-Quine underdetermination, which sounded to me like non-uniqueness, but I'm not sure it is — non-uniqueness has a fairly precise meaning for me and I can't tell if theirs does. Settle that too, and tell me whether the vocabulary I brought is missing something or whether the philosophy is thinner than it sounds.
