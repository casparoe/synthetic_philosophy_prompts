I teach a third-year seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and this year I want to set the final differently from the way I have set it for the last six years. The pattern I keep seeing is that my students can recite the entire dialectic — the consequence argument, Frankfurt-style cases, Pereboom-style manipulation cases, reasons-responsive accounts in the Fischer and Ravizza vein, hierarchical and mesh accounts, Strawson's reactive attitudes — and then, when asked whether the compatibilist's free will is the variety worth wanting, they simply assert that it is or assert that it is not, and defend the assertion by restating whichever theory they already favour. What they never do is say what worth wanting is supposed to mean: worth wanting for which purpose (moral responsibility, self-creation, the dignity of authorship, meaning in life, the fittingness of blame and resentment), wanted by whom, and whether the wanting in question is a psychological claim about what people actually care about or a normative claim about what they would have reason to care about. My colleague in the politics department thinks the whole exercise is pointless and that I should examine them on the metaphysics instead, but I think the question is the one place where the metaphysics and the value theory actually touch, and I would like the exam to force them into that junction rather than let them skirt it.

To stop them pattern-matching, I have been building the exam around a distinction of my own that is not in the literature, as far as I know. Call an agent's control over her own motives closed-loop when every revision she makes of her desires, cares or higher-order standards proceeds by applying standards she already has, so that no exercise of her agency could put her entire practical standpoint up for revision from outside itself; open-loop control would be the alternative, in which the agent can somehow author the standpoint from which she authors. The case I want to put to them is an agent — call her Ada — who is thoroughly reasons-responsive in the usual sense, who satisfies the hierarchical conditions with reflective stability and wholehearted identification, who is not manipulated by any person and whose history contains no covert control of any kind, but whose entire starting standpoint was fixed by an ordinary deterministic causal history she did not choose, and whose control is closed-loop in my sense. Plainly she can do otherwise in every compatibilist-friendly sense and plainly she is not the ultimate source of her own standards in the incompatibilist's sense; the question is which of those facts, if either, answers the question of whether she has the free will worth wanting, and what would have to be settled first for it to be answered at all.

What I would like from you is the exam as I would hand it to them: a closed-book essay to be written in ninety minutes with a twelve-hundred-word ceiling and thirty marks attached. Give me the question itself, then a model answer of about nine hundred words of the kind a genuinely strong student could produce under those conditions, then a marking scheme organised around five must-hit points with the marks allocated to each out of thirty and, attached to each point, a single line about how weaker answers characteristically go wrong. The five I have in mind are roughly these: separating the several things the free will in question might be wanted for; showing why both the claim that Ada has all the control that could matter and the claim that she is not the ultimate source beg the question until some desideratum has been fixed; saying whether open-loop control is even coherent and what follows for the worth-wanting question if it is not; locating where the compatibilist-incompatibilist dispute actually sits once the desiderata are fixed, whether it turns out to be verbal, a substantive disagreement about value, a disguised empirical question about what people would in fact miss, or two different questions sharing a label; and saying what, if anything, would count as settling it. Do not simply take my five as given — if one of them is a poor test of what I say I am testing, replace it and tell me why in the prose. The whole point is that nobody should be able to score well by producing a tidy exposition of a named theory, however accurate. And since the exam is closed-book and my students will have no library access, nothing in the question, the model answer or the scheme should depend on citing or looking anything up. One last request about presentation: please write the entire response as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables — I read these on my phone and paste them straight into an email to my teaching assistant, so I would rather have something letter-shaped than something structured.
