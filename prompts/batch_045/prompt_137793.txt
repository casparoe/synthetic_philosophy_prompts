I run the TA training for our big intro epistemology course, and every single term, right after the Cartesian week, the same thing happens: a student turns up in office hours with a doubt that isn't really a doubt about the course material. Sometimes it's the simulation thing, sometimes 'you can't know anything about the past because nobody alive observed it', sometimes 'maybe I'm the only mind there is', and occasionally something nastier, like 'the moon landings were staged and every source that says otherwise is part of the staging'. Six TAs, three blocks a week, fifteen minutes per student, maybe a dozen such conversations a term, and I'm the one who signs off on whatever they do. Right now they oscillate between two seperate failure modes: they try to refute the student with an argument that any philosopher would take apart in thirty seconds, or they panic and say something like 'well, that's a matter of faith', which is worse. So what I want is a procedure: a short series of tests, in order, that a TA can run on what the student actually says out loud — no library, no looking things up, one prior epistemology course, and ideally no jargon in the steps themselves, because if the steps mention hinges my TAs will freeze.

The grounding I want preserved is the late Wittgenstein's material on certainty: the thought that some propositions stand fast for us not because anyone has checked them but because they are what checking consists in, so they aren't knowledge or ignorance or error in the ordinary propositional sense, and meeting a doubt about one of them with an argument is a category mistake rather than a weakness in the argument. But I need that turned into something operational, and there are two bits of it I think the literature handles badly and that I want slots for. First, what I've started calling leaky hinges: propositions that behave as hinges in one region of practice and as perfectly ordinary checkable claims in another — 'I have two hands' in a seminar versus 'I have two hands' in a hand-surgery consult, or the age of the earth in most conversations versus in a dating lab. Second, what I call idling propositions: claims that have the whole surface grammar and social role of empirical claims, so everyone would say they're answerable to evidence, but which in fact nothing in our actual practice could ever count as overturning, so the evidence question never actually gets asked. Tell me whether either of those is doing real work or whether I'm inventing distinctions for their own sake, and where in the order they belong. What counts as cheating, for what it's worth: a test like 'ask whether a philosopher would call it a hinge' just defers the question, 'if there is evidence, produce it' misfires badly (there is a great deal of evidence for an external world, and yet arguing is exactly the wrong move), and 'if it's unfalsifiable, stop talking' misfires too. The tests have to generate the verdict from something a TA can observe in the conversation.

What I want at the end is the procedure in a form I can hand over, plus it run on three cases so I can see where it sticks: the simulation skeptic, where I expect the answer to be something other than argument and I want to know what the something is and how it is meant to help; the student who doubts everything based on testimony about the past, where I expect the answer to be argument and I want to know which specific move in the procedure puts it there; and then a case I won't like — the moon-landing student, or really any case where the doubt keeps extending itself to swallow every source you offer it — because my honest suspicion is that the procedure will either tell my TAs to argue forever with someone who isn't arguing, or will hand them 'this is a framework difference, bear witness' about something I absolutely do not want them to treat as a protected framework difference, and either way I need it to say so out loud, including whatever the procedure cannot do: how it tells a genuine framework difference from bad faith or distress, where it has to stop and send the student to a person instead, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as tests. One more thing about the form, sorry: it's going in an email to my TAs and they will read an email and will not read a manual, so I need continuous prose, no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables. And please don't point me at the secondary literature or tell me what to read — I've read it, and I need the procedure itself, not a map of where to find one.
