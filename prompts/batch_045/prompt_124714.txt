I've gotten weirdly into hand lettering this year — I'm painting the window sign for my brother's record shop, and I have now spent something like four weekends on the 'a' in "Records" alone. My teacher is a retired sign painter who says things like "youre drawing the flesh, not the bones" and "you have to find the a-ness of it", which sounds deep but I cant tell what it actually comes to. And now I can't unsee the puzzle: every a I draw comes out different, each one is obviously an a, and I have no idea what makes that true. (Someone at the shop said this is like some old AI project about designing letterforms, which I havent read, so please dont assume I know it.)

So: compare two answers to "what makes every 'a' an 'a'".

(1) The reader's answer. The a's are all a's because they all land inside one perceptual category — a rough prototype, or a pile of remembered exemplars, plus a tolerance around them — and a token is an a just in case a competent reader of the script takes it for one. On this account the unity lives in us, not in the shapes: the boundary of the letter is a legibility boundary, so it should be fuzzy at the edges and should move around if the readers change.

(2) The builder's answer. The a's are all a's because they're all outputs of one abstract generating structure: the parts (stem, bowl, counter, terminal, entry and exit stroke), the relations holding them together, and the constraints on how they may vary — with style being just a free setting of the leftover parameters. On this account the unity is in the recipe or "spirit" of the letter, whether or not anyone can currently read the result.

But please don't just describe them; run them against the cases where they come apart, which are the ones I keep hitting at the bench:

- A token where my teacher says the bones and stroke order are textbook and only the proportions are off, and four out of five people read it as an 'o'. (1) seems to say it isn't an a; (2) seems to say it obviously is. Who's right?
- The reverse: a shape that everyone reads instantly as 'a' but that no one who actually writes the script would ever produce as an a — my tired-hand scrawl, or a letter from a deliberately ugly grunge face. (1) says yes, and it's not clear (2) can say anything.
- Case: capital 'A' and lowercase 'a'. Their parts are just different — no bowl on the A. So on each account, what makes them one letter rather than two? Does (2) have to go extremely abstract, or just fall back on history, and is that a problem for it?
- Sliding: I can morph continuously from my a to my o without any moment where the shape changes qualitatively, yet at some point it stops being an a. What does each account predict about where that happens, and is either prediction actually right?
- Context: in some hands the very same squiggle is an a next to one letter and something else next to another. Can either account let letterhood depend on neighbours, or does that break one of them?

And then the bit I actually care about: which account gives me something usable while I'm standing there with a brush? If (2) is right, is there a real procedure — hold this fixed, vary that freely — for finding the a-ness, or is "bones" just a vibe? If (1) is right, am I ultimately just taking a poll of readers, and is my teacher's talk about bones only a guess at what readers will tolerate? If you think both are wrong, say so and say what the third option is.
