I'm a PhD student in modern European history, and I sit as the graduate member of the academic committee of a state-funded remembrance institute. We vote next week on a proposed permanent gallery module, and the committee is split almost exactly in half.

The proposal is a single wall-mounted diagram covering four twentieth-century episodes of mass violence. An earlier version ranked them by a composite magnitude score and was withdrawn after protests, so the designers now propose a partial ordering instead: case A is placed above case B only if it is rated worse on every one of five listed criteria — death toll, proportion of the targeted population killed, duration, documented intent, and degree of state involvement — and where the criteria conflict the pair is left unordered, joined by a grey line rather than an arrow. Their claim is that this compares without grading: it ranks only where the evidence forces a rank and visibly withholds judgment everywhere else. Two of our historians also want the criterion of documented intent struck from the list, on the ground that including it already decides the moral question before the diagram does.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate before the vote: roughly 700 words, addressed to the committee, with a clear bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and, if you recommend conditions, phrased so the exhibition team could actually implement them. Two constraints matter to me. First, I do not want a memo that rests on the thought that ranking is imprecise or that the underlying numbers are disputed; the designers concede that freely and it settles nothing, since one can compare without measuring. Second, I want the strongest case for refusing comparison altogether — the argument that the obscenity lies in putting the cases into one diagram at all, whatever the arrows do — stated at its full strength before you answer it. Whichever way you come down, give the best argument on the other side honestly, including the point that declaring cases incomparable is not the modest, neutral option it looks like, since claims of uniqueness and incomparability are themselves comparative and can shelter a case from scrutiny. If you end up recommending that we proceed in some form, say what the wall text beside the diagram must tell visitors about why the grey lines are there.
