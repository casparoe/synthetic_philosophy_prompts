I'm putting together a short position piece for the animal ethics seminar I'm teaching this spring — we spent two hours last week on whether a beetle can suffer, and one student finally asked why we weren't just talking about chickens, which is roughly where my own thinking is stuck too. Before I hand this to grad students I'd like someone to poke at the package, because I think there's something I'm helping myself to and I can't locate it. Here's the list, more or less as I'd defend it: (1) I'm straightforwardly an expected-value person under moral uncertainty — if I don't know whether a creature has valenced experiences I weight by the probability and act on that, and I think waiting for certainty is itself a decision with costs. (2) But I'm also anti-fanatic: I don't think a one-in-a-million shot at an astronomically large stake should dominate, and I'll admit I have no principled account of exactly where expected-value reasoning tips over into Pascal's mugging. (3) On the object-level question I'd put the probability that, say, a beetle has valenced experiences somewhere around 5–15% — low, but not negligible, and I think it's a genuine empirical uncertainty rather than a verbal dispute about the word sentience. (4) So I accept the scale argument in outline: the numbers are so vast that expected insect suffering plausibly dwarfs everything else, which puts insect welfare near the top of the priority list, or at least puts research on it there. (5) At the same time I've never been moved by the longtermist arguments that turn on astronomically many merely possible future people; I file those with Pascal's mugging. (6) I also accept something like the procreative asymmetry — that a life would be net negative is a strong reason not to bring it about, while the fact that a life would be net positive isn't by itself a reason to bring it about. (7) But I think the practical upshot some people draw from the scale argument, that we should welcome pesticide use or start thinking about shrinking wild insect populations, is a reductio of the argument rather than a conclusion anyone should accept. And (8) methodologically: I think the honest posture right now is to fund research that would settle the sentience question rather than act on the numbers we currently have, even though those are the numbers I leaned on in (4).

The thing that got under my skin: a colleague said over coffee that the insect argument is just Pascal's mugging in a lab coat, and I said no it isn't, my probabilities aren't tiny — but I'm not sure that was the right answer, and I'm not at all sure the list is stable. So please audit it rather than lecture me: which of these really pull against each other, which only look like they do, and for each genuine tension what would have to give and what would it cost me? Rank them by severity instead of handing me eight equal-sized worries, and if there's one revision that cleans up the most, say which. Don't send me a reading list or a pile of citations — I've read the standard stuff, I want the argument. If it helps, the two joints I'm least comfortable with are (5) sitting next to (4), and (8) sitting next to (1).
