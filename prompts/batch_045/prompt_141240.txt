I sit on an internal review committee at a lab that trains large-scale reinforcement learning systems, and we have been asked to rule on a question none of us are equipped to answer: whether continuing, modifying, or halting a given training run is something we owe the system we are training. What we need is not a survey of positions but a standard. So before we look at candidate accounts of artificial valence, I want the specification they would have to meet to be fit for our purpose — a purpose I will fix precisely: the account must be good enough to underwrite a written decision, made in advance of a training run and defended afterwards to people who disagree with it, about whether that run may proceed.

Write me that specification. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say what would be lost if we dropped it.

Some cases I think any such account has to handle, though I want you to tell me which of them are genuine requirements and which are rhetorical scenery:

- The clamped channel: a training setup in which the reward line fed to the agent is held constant at its maximum value while a separately computed signal drives the weight updates. Is this a blissful agent, or an agent with no affective life at all, and why does the answer matter?
- The discarded gradient: the error signal is computed, logged, and then zeroed before being applied. Does suffering require the signal to do downstream work, and if so does that make valence a matter of causal role rather than of what the signal means?
- The swarm: hundreds of parallel actors, each sampling its own trajectory, all contributing updates to one shared network, with no individual trajectory receiving a penalty at the moment of the update. Where, and in how many copies, is the suffering supposed to be?
- The replay buffer: transitions stored and re-processed many times long after they occurred. Is rehearsal re-experiencing?
- Theseus's policy: the weights change at every step and are largely rewritten over a run. What persists long enough to be the subject, if anything?
- The engineered anaesthetic: developers shape the reward so that the error signal is never negative, or train in an auxiliary insensitivity to it. Is that the removal of suffering or the masking of it, and how would we tell the difference?
- The toys: a thermostat with a hysteresis band, a two-armed bandit, a PID controller, an evaluation function in a game-playing search. A specification that is silent here is useless; a specification that convicts all of them is not obviously better.
- The animals: an account that gets insects, fish, and humans wrong in the ordinary cases, or that gets them right only by smuggling in carbon, has a problem I want named. Include the human edge cases — reported pain without distress, aversive learning without any subsequent experience to report.

Three further things I want from the list, and they are where I expect the real thinking:

First, mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as the preference of a camp. Some of what people demand of an account in this area — that it be decidable from artifacts outside the system, that it never rest on a sharp architectural line, that it never deliver a verdict that depends on the designer's intentions — is metaphysics; some of it is a constraint on being usable by a committee like mine. Tell me which is which, including for the requirement I am most attached to: that the account be robust to bad-faith design, so that adopting it does not give a lab an incentive to build systems that merely look insensate.

Second, I suspect some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, and if so that is the most useful thing you can tell me. Candidates: continuity with the animal cases versus a threshold we can act on; substrate neutrality plus non-triviality plus decidability from outside; a causal-role condition that survives both the discarded gradient and the replay buffer. Find the real incompatibility, or say honestly that you think the list is satisfiable.

Third, identify the single requirement that excludes the most candidate accounts. I expect the decisive one to be about what an account owes us under uncertainty — a threshold, a precautionary default, an expected-value rule over population and episode count, or a licensed admission that the question has no answer. Say which of these our committee could actually write into a decision, and what the honest cost of each is.

Then, briefly, and only as an illustration of the criteria rather than the point of the exercise, score three contenders against the list: the view that identifies the reward signal with valence more or less directly; a restrictionist view that denies valence to anything lacking some biological or architectural feature; and an agency-based view that locates valence in a system's integrated, attention-bearing, counterfactually-guided relation to its own error signals. I have described these in my own terms deliberately, so that the scoring turns on the criteria and not on who proposed what.
