hey so I run this philosophy club at my school and next week it's my turn to give the talk, and I picked suffering-focused ethics / negative utilitarianism. It's me and like 12 other people, mostly juniors, 35 minutes, whiteboard if I can get one, and I've been going back and forth with my friend about this for a month and we've completely stalled so I figure teaching it might force me to get clear.

The audience: they've all heard the greatest-good-for-the-greatest-number thing and about half of them think that's obviously correct and the other half are vaguely into rights or virtue or whatever. None of them have ever encountered the idea that preventing suffering is the whole of what matters morally, or that it matters in a totally different way than making people happy does. Two of them will definately show up ready to debate.

What I actually want is not an explanation of the view for me — I want a plan for explaining it to them.

The main thing: I think the reason my explanations keep falling apart is that suffering-is-what-matters is really several seperate claims glued together, and people slide between them without noticing, and then they argue past each other. My guess at the seams: (1) a claim about what you tally up when you compare outcomes — total suffering instead of net wellbeing; (2) a claim that suffering has priority or veto power, like there's no amount of mild happiness that buys off one bad agony; (3) a claim about which people or which acts even register — that making a new happy person accomplishes nothing while making a new person who will suffer accomplishes something bad. Are those the right cuts? Are two of them secretly the same claim? Is there a fourth one I'm missing?

Then: the one example that carries the session. I've been using a made-up thing where you can press one button that deletes someone's chronic pain forever, or another that gives someone else a big but ordinary happy year, and you can only pick one, and then I repeat it with smaller and smaller amounts of suffering, and then I repeat it a thousand times in a row. It kind of works but I don't know if it's the right one. What should the carrying example be, and what does it need to contain and to leave out? I've noticed big numbers make everyone shrug and anything with torture in it makes them get stuck on the gore instead of the structure.

Misconceptions, in the order they'll actually come up, and where in the sequence to defuse each. My predictions: they'll think it's just pessimism or being edgy, they'll think it's the same as do-no-harm in medicine, they'll realise it might imply painlessly ending everyone would be fantastic and treat that as a refutation rather than a consequence to be weighed, and someone will say so then nothing is ever worth doing.

Also this is the argument that actually stalled me and my friend: even granting suffering is all that matters, in any real choice you're trading a bit of happiness against a bit of suffering, and if the exchange rate tilts toward removing suffering at all and you face enough small choices in a row, you end up maximising happiness anyway — so the view quietly collapses back into ordinary utilitarianism and the whole disagreement was fake. I don't know if this is a good thing to put in the talk or a trap. If I do use it, does it go early as motivation or late as a cliffhanger?

And give me one question for the last five minutes that shows me whether they got the difference between the versions, versus just absorbing suffering is bad. Plus what I should explicitly refuse to get into in 35 minutes with this group.
