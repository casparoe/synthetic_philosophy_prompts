Last night, over a long and increasingly circular discussion, my friend Priya and I argued about intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism. I am currently taking a course on the philosophy of mathematics, and we had been reading about the Brouwer–Heyting tradition, but the textbook was frustratingly brief on the philosophical upshot, so we tried to work it out ourselves and soon found ourselves at an impasse. We both accept the standard setup: intuitionistic logic rejects the law of excluded middle and double-negation elimination, so that a proof of an existential claim must provide a construction of a witness, and Heyting arithmetic is just Peano arithmetic formulated with intuitionistic rather than classical logic. Two further points we took as given, perhaps naively, are these. First, Heyting arithmetic and Peano arithmetic prove exactly the same Π₂ sentences, meaning statements of the form ∀x∃y R(x, y) with R a decidable predicate, so the two theories have the same provably total computable functions and thus agree completely on the finitary computational content of arithmetic, even though they differ on the logical law of excluded middle. Second, in intuitionistic analysis, unlike classical analysis, every total function from the reals to the reals is continuous, and on the unit interval every total function is uniformly continuous; this means that classical monsters such as the Dirichlet function, which takes the value 1 on the rationals and 0 on the irrationals, cannot be defined as a total function at all, because to define it one would have to decide of an arbitrary real whether it is rational, and that is precisely the sort of non-constructive inference that the law of excluded middle would illegitimately license.

The dispute was about what follows from these two points. Priya argued that the conservativity result, namely the agreement on Π₂ sentences, shows the whole intuitionist critique to be largely inert for actual mathematical practice: if the classical and intuitionistic theories of arithmetic recover exactly the same computable content, then the rejection of the excluded middle costs the number theorist nothing and changes no theorem that matters, and the intuitionist's real motive is merely a philosophical suspicion of Platonism. On this view, the continuity theorem is simply a heavy price paid for that suspicion, a self-imposed mutilation of analysis that throws away perfectly good classical mathematics, such as the Dirichlet function, in order to satisfy an epistemological scruple. I argued, in reply, that this gets the dialectic backwards. The conservativity result is itself a vindication of the constructivist claim that the excluded middle adds no genuine computational information to finite, decidable mathematics; the real philosophical weight of intuitionism lies in the continuity theorem, which shows that when we move to the continuum, the constructivist demand that existence be tied to construction reveals the classical continuum to be populated by functions that are not effectively computable or even constructively definable. On my view, the continuity theorem is not a cost but a discovery: it shows intuitionism to be a genuine rival to, not a mere restriction of, classical mathematics, and it exposes a defect in the classical picture rather than a limitation in the constructive one.

We were too tired to resolve who was right, and I suspect we both may be partly wrong. Please write an essay that weighs these arguments and reaches a definite conclusion. Do not end on a non-committal note: I want you to defend a verdict on the specific question of whether the Π₂-conservativity of Peano arithmetic over Heyting arithmetic defuses the constructivist critique of classical arithmetic, and whether the intuitionistic continuity theorem is best seen as a fatal cost or as a philosophical vindication of constructivism. Justify your verdict with argument. Finally, as an epilogue to the essay, since I want to know how much confidence to place in the verdict, please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where do you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail, for instance, are you relying on a contested interpretation of Π₂-conservativity, or on a particular reading of what it means for a function to be total, continuous, or definable in intuitionistic analysis?
