Hey — I'm putting together a big printable study-guide centerpiece for the undergrad philosophy of mind module I TA, and what I want is a decision tree (flowchart, whatever you want to call it) that walks someone from the starting commitment "ok, at least some internal states of a system are genuinely about stuff in the world" all the way down to a named position in the naturalizing-content literature. The seminar is full of ML people this year, so the tree also has to work when the system in question is a trained network rather than an animal, and I'd like each branch node to be phrased as a single yes/no or forced-choice question about a concrete case that a student can answer without already knowing which theory it leads to — so no node may mention a theorist's name, an ism, or a theory label, and no node may just be the abstract question "does history matter?" (they'd guess from that alone).

Cases I definitely want doing the work, because these are the ones my students actually argue about: the frog whose retinal cells fire at small dark moving spots but which cannot tell the difference between a fly and a well-timed pellet (and a variant where the frog is a newly created molecule-for-molecule duplicate with no ancestry at all); a structure that was selected for thermoregulation but has since been co-opted so that it now reliably co-varies with nearby predators; a lineage whose tokenings of state S were fitness-boosting for a thousand generations but where the correlation has now completely broken down because the environment shifted; a brand-new beneficial mutation that produces a state which really does carry information and raises fitness, but which has no selection history yet; a state whose effects on other members of the species explain why it was selected, rather than its effects on the bearer; a mismatch case where the producer mechanism was selected to indicate one thing, the downstream consumer system only ever got reinforced for treating it as indicating something else; and a deep net trained by gradient descent on a cost function with no evolutionary history whatsoever. Aim for roughly eight to ten branch nodes and about ten to twelve leaves.

Leaves have to include at least: selected-effects/etiological views, the consumer-side variant that makes content depend on what the downstream system needs, non-etiological informational or indication views, nomic-covariation views (the asymmetric-dependence sort), structural-similarity or isomorphism views, conceptual- or inferential-role views, some kind of interpretist or deflationary "content is what a good attributor would read off" position, and one eliminativist or error-theoretic leaf where nothing is really about anything. For every leaf give the name, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and a note on whether it thinks the trained net has contents. Then — this is the part I care about most — list the combinations of answers your tree allows but that nobody actually occupies, and say briefly why each is empty (incoherent? just unattractive? merely unexplored?), and explicitly flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate from each other no matter how the nodes are ordered, rather than pretending the cases sort them. No bibliography or further reading at the end please, I'll add that myself.
