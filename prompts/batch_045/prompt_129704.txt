The topic is evidential cooperation in large worlds. I know the basic moves, so don't spend time on them: EDT picks the action with the best expected utility conditional on your performing it, rather than on your causally producing an outcome; in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a psychological twin, or against anyone running a sufficiently similar decision procedure, that gets you cooperation, because your cooperating is evidence that they cooperate; causal decision theory can't reproduce the result, and functional decision theory gets it a different way. What I want is the extension I half-remember reading about somewhere: a very large universe, lots of agents who can't causally reach each other, agents with *different* values rather than shared ones, and the point of cooperating being gains from trade rather than mutual defection-avoidance. Explain that version to me -- and not at the level of "you scratch my back acausally and I'll scratch yours." I want the actual mechanics.

Four things I keep tripping over:

1. What is the trade, exactly? If the counterparty is in a causally disconnected region, they can't send me anything and I can't send them anything. Nothing in the world changes except what I'm rationally allowed to believe about what they're doing. So spell out what it even means to "do something for them": am I performing an action that's costly by my lights and valuable by theirs, and being paid purely in the evidential update about their symmetric action? Then say plainly whether that deserves the name cooperation at all, or whether the honest description is that each agent is unilaterally buying good news about the other. And if it's the latter, does the usual "EDT just manages the news" objection land here in a way it doesn't land in the twin case?

2. The weights. My understanding is that for a group of agents doing this to land on a Pareto-optimal outcome, they all have to be maximizing the same weighted sum of everyone's utility functions, and that how each person's utility function gets scaled or normalized is what silently fixes those weights. Explain why that's true, why normalization is not the neutral bit of bookkeeping it looks like, and what happens when two agents normalize differently: do they simply fail to cooperate, or do they cooperate on terms that are systematically tilted against one of them?

3. Equilibrium. Standard game theory builds in that the players' choices are probabilistically independent, which is exactly what's false here. I've seen it suggested that you need a notion where each player's action maximizes expected utility conditional on that very action, over a fully mixed distribution. Explain what the ordinary concept can't express, what the conditional-on-your-own-action version buys you, and -- the part I care about most -- what the consistency requirement is. With N agents each treating their own move as evidence about everyone else's, what stops all of them from doing that in mutually incompatible ways? Is there always a joint distribution on which all those conditional beliefs are true at once, or are there cases where the whole construction has no fixed point?

4. Size. Huge universe, astronomical number of candidate counterparties, each of them individually unlikely to exist or to be relevantly similar to me. Does largeness help or hurt?
