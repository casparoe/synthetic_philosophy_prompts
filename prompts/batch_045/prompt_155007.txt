You are a professional philosopher who also writes formally accomplished verse. Before drafting, work in stages: first brainstorm four or five candidate refrain lines and test whether each can survive six repetitions without strain; then choose the two rhyme sounds and check that you have enough non-rhyming -b words to carry the middle lines; only then write the stanzas, and finally check your line count and the refrain positions against the villanelle scheme (aba aba aba aba aba abaa, nineteen lines, refrain A1 at lines 1, 6, 12, 18, refrain A2 at lines 3, 9, 15, 19) before showing anything.

Write a villanelle about the twin structure of collective action problems: over-extraction from a commons and under-contribution to a public good. The two refrains should be the two faces of one and the same dominant strategy — the speaker's own small, wholly-owned benefit, and the shared, divided cost — so that their first and only meeting in the closing stanza does the work of showing that the herder who adds one animal and the neighbour who declines to pay for the pump are making the same move seen from opposite sides. Keep the refrains short, plain, and spoken: one in the first person singular, one in the first person plural or impersonal.

Three constraints. First, accuracy: a reader who has actually worked on collective action should not wince. Get the incentive asymmetry right — the extractor keeps the whole marginal gain and bears only a fraction of the marginal damage, while the potential contributor would bear the whole cost and receive only a fraction of the benefit — and do not let the poem collapse into a one-shot prisoners' dilemma between two named players with a tidy betrayal. Second, build in the threshold structure: commons degrade non-linearly. The pasture carries grazers without visible harm until it passes a carrying capacity and then collapses, and no individual herder can tell whether her animal is the harmless N-th or the fatal N-plus-first, because every previous animal was harmless too. That epistemic point is what makes this a poem about a commons rather than about greed, and it is what the poem should leave open: end with the two refrains side by side and the question of whose animal was the last one still unanswered, resisting both the turn into a sermon about regulation and the shrug about human nature. Third, keep the diction concrete and unpoetic — animals, a fence, a pump, a lamp that stays dark — and keep jargon out of the refrains, rhyming properly rather than slanting.
