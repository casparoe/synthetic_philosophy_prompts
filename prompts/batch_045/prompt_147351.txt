Hi. I work on a three-person "emerging contaminants" triage panel at a regional water company — about 900,000 customers, mix of groundwater and surface water. A few times a year we get an alert: our own monitoring, or a lab contractor, flags a substance in raw or treated water that nobody has a proper health-based value for. Within 48 hours we have to land on one of four things — do nothing, step up monitoring, issue a precautionary advisory to a specific group (bottle-fed babies, dialysis patients, pregnant women), or advise people not to drink the water, which in practice means bottled water distribution and a wall of angry phone calls. A consultant in public health medicine signs off whatever we propose, the comms team want one unambiguous sentence they can put on the website, and we're not allowed to pretend we have powers we don't have: we advise, the regulator decides.

Right now we're completely inconsistent and we know it. Two years ago we issued an advisory about something that later turned out to be harmless, and before that we sat on something that in hindsight we should have acted on much earlier. Both were judgement calls made over email. So I want a written procedure we can actually run under time pressure, by people who aren't toxicologists.

A few things it has to survive, because these are the bits we keep getting stuck on.

One: the interventions have their own risks. A do-not-drink pushes people onto bottled water, which is expensive, badly regulated and a plastic waste problem; on one estate a few years back it pushed people onto a private borehole that nobody tests. Boiling concentrates some things rather than removing them. So whatever principle tells us to act under uncertainty has to be applied to our own remedy too, not just to the substance.

Two: my colleague Dave has this thing he calls the "reversibility test" — he says precaution is only licensed when the harm you're guarding against is irreversible relative to the cost of waiting, and that's what separates genuine precaution from just being jumpy. Our public health consultant says the test is backwards, because the whole point is that you don't yet know whether the harm is reversible, so the test will always tell you to wait. I'd like the procedure to settle that, or to say why it can't.

Three: what I think of as the two-file problem. The file on the new mystery compound is thick — someone measured it, so we have a number, a study, a report. The file on the legacy contaminant we've quietly lived with for thirty years is empty, because nobody ever looked. Any procedure keyed to evidence will systematically over-attend to whatever happened to be measured most recently, and I don't want a procedure that's just a bias with a flowchart attached.

Four: we usually can't get a number. No health-based value, no dose-response, sometimes one 90-day rat study at doses thousands of times higher than anything in water, sometimes two studies that disagree where both were funded by somebody with an interest. The procedure needs to say what we do on the day with a thin file, not what we'd do if the science were better.

Please run it on three composites (made-up names and numbers, roughly the shape of things we actually see). Case A: a solvent from a plating works that shut in the nineties, detected at 2 micrograms per litre, health-based value of 30 derived from a rat study with a big safety factor, stable for years but now creeping up about 5% a year. Case B: a replacement fluorinated compound from a plant still in operation, no health-based value anywhere, liver effects in rats at high doses, detectable in everyone's blood, source still active. Case C: an algae metabolite after heavy rain — zero toxicological concern, but the water tastes and smells earthy, call volumes have tripled, a councillor is demanding bottled water, and a local Facebook group is telling people the supply is poisoned.

At least one of the three should come out somewhere I'd rather it hadn't — either we've been doing nothing and the procedure says that's been wrong, or the reverse — and please say plainly which one that is and why. Also say where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a human because it's a values call and not a factual one, and what it simply can't do for us.

Two more things: don't point me at specific guidance documents or papers, I can't get hold of them and I'd rather have the reasoning than the references. And write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; describe the steps in order in paragraphs, inline numbering is fine if it helps — and in British English please.
