Posting this from a throwaway-ish account because I'm out of my depth in one specific direction and could use a prep sheet.

I'm giving a talk in three weeks at my department's formal epistemology workshop. Philosophy department, small room, roughly: four Bayesian confirmation-theory people, one imprecise-probability person who distrusts any single prior on principle, an emeritus Hume scholar who will flatly refuse to engage with anything containing a summation sign, and one computational learning theorist visiting from CS who actually knows the standard no-free-lunch theorems properly and will be the one who kills me if anyone does.

My thesis, short version: the no-free-lunch theorems aren't a skeptical threat to induction, they're a price list. What NFL really establishes is that induction isn't free — you have to spend structure (prior, inductive bais, whatever you want to call it) to buy predictive leverage. So the real question isn't "can induction be justified a priori" but what the exchange rate is: how much expected predictive accuracy you get per bit of structure you commit to up front.

The positive claim, which is the part that's actually mine (I can't find it in the literature, though I'm not certain its not there somewhere): call it the concentration conjecture. The exchange rate is brutally favorable and extremely concentrated. A tiny budget of cheap structural commitments — temporal locality, exchangeability of observation order, sparsity in some fixed basis, plus a general preference for short descriptions — accounts for almost all of the inductive success we actually observe, and the marginal hundredth bit of structure buys you nearly nothing. Corollary: Hume's problem never gets solved, it gets converted into a quantitative research program, and once you see how concentrated the exchange rate is, the leftover worry has roughly the practical status of "but what if the laws change at midnight."

What I want from you: the seven or eight objections I'll actually get in *this* room, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — the learning theorist's version in his vocabulary, the imprecise-probability person's version, and the Humean's version which will be one plain-English sentence with no formalism in it and will somehow be the hardest to answer. Each with a roughly two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Also: what should I concede in the first two slides, before I'm forced into it. Skip the LessWrong objections, I've got those covered — I know someone will say Solomonoff already does all of this and I have that answer ready.

And include the one you think I'm hoping nobody asks. My guess is it's about whether "cheap" is doing hidden work: sparsity is cheap only relative to a basis, and if the basis has to be learned from the data then I've smuggled the structure back in and my budget was never small. But if you think there's a worse one — e.g. that the concentration claim is itself an a posteriori generalization about our particular universe, and where does *that* get its warrant — say so and tell me why its worse.
