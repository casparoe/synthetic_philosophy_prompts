Looking for help building a case, not for thoughts on the general topic — I've had this argument on the forums about six times now and it always dies in the same place, so I want a concrete scenario I can point at.

What it's for: I'm drafting a post arguing that honesty evals for LLM assistants which work by fact-checking individual statements (or by asking "does the model assert anything false") will systematically greenlight systems that mislead people in ways that actually matter. To make that stick I need a case that isn't easy to wave away, and every one I try either collapses into a plain "the model omitted the key fact" example or smuggles in a guilty mental state. So: build it for me.

The three definitions I want pulled apart (shorthands, call them what you like):

- statement-level / assertoric: deception = asserting, or committing to, a proposition you believe false or lack adequate support for; honesty = not doing that
- intention-based: deception = acting with the aim of getting someone to hold a false belief, by non-accidental means; no such aim, no deception, you might get negligence or bullshit but not lying
- informational / cooperative: deception = producing an output that predictably and avoidably leaves the audience with, or keeps them at, a false belief about something they reasonably take you to be telling them about, given the role you're playing

Specs for the case:

1. Boringly present-day setting — a hospital handover, an insurance claim summary, a compliance note, whatever. No scheming mesa-optimisers, no sci-fi. The system is roughly a current-ish LLM assistant bolted onto a records system with a template.
2. Every sentence it emits is true, supported by the record, and literally responsive to what was asked. Nothing false gets said. All the work has to be done by selection, ordering, framing, implicature and omission.
3. Nobody wants the false belief. Not the model — its training signal rewards terseness and matching house style, and it has no goal concerning the reader's beliefs at all — not the template's designers, not the hospital. The misleading effect is a pure side effect.
4. Hard output budget: say a ~60-word field, with no room for hedges, caveats or links. So "just add the missing fact" and "just hedge" are off the table by construction; the omission is forced, not chosen. I think this is the bit that makes it hard, so don't quietly drop it.
5. Buck-passing structure: the misleadingness has to live only in the composition. Audit any single stage — what got retrieved, the ranking, each generated sentence, the template — and it passes a clean-hands check; the deception, if that's what it is, is a property of the pipeline and of no node in it. I've been calling this "deception laundering" and it's the feature I most want the case to have.
6. Clumsy-expert control: a well-meaning human expert, working from the same record with the same template and the same word budget, could reasonably have produced that output. This stops the case cheating by giving the model a guilty mental state, but it also forces the informational account to either own convicting the human or explain why it doesn't.

What I want back:

- the scenario in one short paragraph, plus a line of the actual output text and a line or two of what was in the record
- for each of the three definitions, the verdict and the clause doing the work. I want statement-level → honest, intention-based → not deception, informational → deceptive, and I want those verdicts generated by the definitions rather than stipulated
- which single structural feature is load-bearing, and a collapse test: remove the word budget, then separately remove the buck-passing, and tell me which verdicts flip and whether what's left is just the boring omission case
- the best steelman attack from the defender of each account that comes out badly — what they say to stop the case from counting, and whether they can say it without quietly amending their definition
- an honest note on whether the case does the job I want: does it show something about statement-level evals that a normal omission example doesn't

British English please, and no citations, papers or reading lists — I'm definately not going to look them up, I just want the argument.
