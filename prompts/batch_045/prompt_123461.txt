I am working through Aristotle's *De Anima*, Book II, chapter 1, in an older translation, while thinking about a question I keep returning to: whether any of the familiar definitions of life — the self-maintenance-plus-evolvability one, or the plain metabolism-and-reproduction one — has the resources to return a graded or third verdict on the standard borderline cases, instead of forcing a yes-or-no on each. I had hoped Aristotle's "except in name" move would supply that third verdict, and I cannot tell whether it does. The stretch I am stuck on runs as follows:

> Now the word actuality has two senses corresponding respectively to the possession of knowledge and the actual exercise of knowledge. It is obvious that the soul is actuality in the first sense, viz. that of knowledge as possessed, for both sleeping and waking presuppose the existence of soul, and of these waking corresponds to actual knowing, sleeping to knowledge possessed but not employed. [...] That is why the soul is the first grade of actuality of a natural body having life potentially in it. The body so described is a body which is organized. [...] Suppose that the eye were an animal - sight would have been its soul, for sight is the substance or essence of the eye which corresponds to the formula, the eye being merely the matter of seeing; when seeing is removed the eye is no longer an eye, except in name - it is no more a real eye than the eye of a statue or of a painted figure. [...] We must not understand by that which is 'potentially capable of living' what has lost the soul it had, but only what still retains it; but seeds and fruits are bodies which possess the qualification. Consequently, while waking is actuality in a sense corresponding to the cutting and the seeing, the soul is actuality in the sense corresponding to the power of sight and the power in the tool; the body corresponds to what exists in potentiality; as the pupil plus the power of sight constitutes the eye, so the soul plus the body constitutes the animal.

Four things, specifically.

First, the qualifier on "having life potentially in it." He seems to sense that the word "potentially" invites the wrong cases: read as a bare possibility, the matter of a corpse would seem to qualify, so he restricts it to what "still retains" the soul. I want to know what work "retains" is doing here, and whether the two-grade analysis of actuality — knowledge possessed versus knowledge exercised — is really what carries the load, or whether it is only an analogy that happens to handle sleep.

Second, the "except in name" claim. Is this a thesis about reference and kind-membership, or about what the thing essentially is? The comparison with the painted eye suggests he thinks the functional collapse destroys the kind, not merely our warrant for applying the word. If so, can a contemporary account that defines life by current activity plus a disposition accept the move, or does accepting it commit you to something stronger than any such account wants?

Third, and this is where my own reading may be wrong: I want to distinguish two ways of failing the test. One is functional collapse — the axe that cannot cut, the eye that cannot see. The other is what I would call relational separation: the thing is structurally intact and internally disposed to do its work, but the conditions for exercising that disposition lie outside it. A dormant seed is metabolically inert and in that respect closer to a corpse than to a growing plant, yet he explicitly puts seeds and fruits among the bodies that "possess the qualification." That makes me think his criterion is about a retained internal capacity rather than current activity — which should matter a great deal for how the framework judges an extracellular virus particle, a cryopreserved embryo, a dormant bacterial cyst, and a cell in culture that has lost the capacity to divide. But it also makes me worry that the capacity reading is nearly empty, since almost any inert but intact structure can be said to retain a disposition under some description.

So here is what I would like from you. Walk me through what the distinctions in the passage are actually for, in your own words, and then apply the resulting criterion to four cases: a dormant seed, a frozen embryo, a virus particle outside a host cell, and a sterile hybrid such as a mule — which metabolizes and grows but cannot reproduce, the reproductive power being the one he elsewhere calls most characteristic of the living. Tell me which of these he has the materials to call alive, which he must call alive only in name, and which the text is simply silent on. Tell me where my relational-separation reading is a projection rather than an implication. And tell me whether the concession at the end of the chapter — that it remains unclear whether the soul might be the actuality of the body in the way a sailor is the actuality of a ship — undercuts the whole "first actuality" formula, or only qualifies one part of it. I am not looking for a survey of the secondary literature; I want to know what this argument does.
