I write about evolutionary biology for a national newspaper, and in six weeks I am giving the annual Science in Public lecture in a university's ecology and evolutionary biology building. The room will be about sixty people: mostly working evolutionary biologists from that department, a dozen or so graduate students, two or three people from the university's science-communication master's programme, and a visiting historian of science who works on the adaptationism disputes of the 1970s and 80s. The departmental split matters: a large group of field and organismal biologists who do reciprocal transplants and local-adaptation work and regard selection as something you measure rather than something you debate; a smaller group of quantitative/theory people who care about whether an hypothesis is identifiable from the data at all; two developmental-evolution people who think the structuralist critique was directionally right but under-specified and who bristle at the word constraint; and a handful of sceptics who think this whole fight was settled decades ago and wonder why a journalist is reopening it. The historian cares whether I am describing the 1979 spandrels paper or describing its reputation. The science-communication people care whether any of this survives contact with an eight-hundred-word news story and an editor on deadline.

My thesis has two halves. The descriptive half is that popular science writing still runs on what I have started calling narrative adaptationism: the default template in which a trait appears in a story only once someone has found the problem it solves, so that the explanation is always of the form trait X exists because it did Y for an ancestor, and the alternative hypotheses are mentioned, if at all, in a final paragraph that readers and headline writers discard. The prescriptive half is the thing I actually want to defend, and it is a style rule rather than a theory: call it the alternatives line. No paragraph may attribute a trait to selection without one adjacent sentence naming the strongest non-adaptive explanation for that same trait, chosen from a fixed menu I have drafted — developmental bias or constraint, phylogenetic inertia, pleiotropic byproduct of something else that was selected, demographic contingency in the loose sense people call drift, exaptive co-option of a structure built for another job, geometric or physical necessity, and ecological time-lag, where the trait is useful now but did not arise for its current use. Two wrinkles I have built in deliberately, and expect to be attacked for: the sentence does not have to endorse the alternative, only name it; and the rule applies even to studies that tested a selection hypothesis rigorously, because in my experience the tested component is smaller than the story implies.

The argument I will give runs in three steps: that adaptive stories are cheap to generate and expensive to kill, since a plausible function can be invented for almost anything and the correction never runs; that this asymmetry is invisible to readers precisely because the hedges are stylistically optional; and that a mandatory naming sentence is a low-cost intervention which restores the asymmetry to view without asking reporters to adjudicate evolutionary biology. I plan to concede in the first two minutes that I have no systematic content analysis behind the descriptive claim, only a decade of reading both the journals and the clips, and that the menu is my construction and not something Gould and Lewontin handed down.

What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, with likelihood noted separately from severity, each attributed to the kind of person who will raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it — the field biologist's version should sound like a field biologist, the historian's like a historian, the editor's like an editor. For each: what I should say in about two minutes, what I should concede rather than fight, and where my reply is soft. Tell me which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive the room, and tell me which objection I should volunteer before anyone raises it, because pre-empting it costs me less than answering it. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks; for what it is worth, mine is that one of the clips I intend to project as evidence of the problem is a feature I wrote myself, and that someone in the third row will notice before I get to it.
