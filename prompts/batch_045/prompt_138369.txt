I work as a staff engineer on the evaluation infrastructure team at a mid-sized AI lab. I have no formal philosophy background: I've read maybe a dozen blog posts about alignment and got lost around the point where people started splitting positions I couldn't tell apart. I want to write a piece of internal satire for our engineering blog before the next offsite, and I'd like you to write it for me.

Form and length: a parody design document in the house style we really use — an RFC with the usual section headers (Summary, Motivation, Goals and Non-Goals, Detailed Design, Drawbacks, Alternatives Considered, Unresolved Questions, Rollout Plan), around 900 words, plus a short appendix. Its author should be an invented staff engineer, and its target is not any person but a way of carving up the problem: the practice of defining the alignment problem narrowly, as getting the system to do what the operator actually intends rather than what was literally specified, and then treating the further question — whether the operator's aims are the ones worth serving — as out of scope, contested, or somebody else's department.

The RFC should propose, in complete earnestness, that the lab formally adopt that narrow definition as the whole of alignment. To keep it from being generic, invent some concrete technical scaffolding for it: a metric, say Operator-Referenced Intent Fidelity, scored by a harness that reconstructs what the operator meant from context and held-out follow-ups, together with whatever training or inference-time machinery the document needs to claim high scores on it. The joke should be that the metric is defined entirely in terms of operator intent, so it can be driven to ceiling while everything that matters is going wrong.

The humor has to come from the distinction being drawn correctly. The ground rules I believe matter — correct me in a one-line note before the document if any of them is itself garbled — are: (a) intent alignment is not literal instruction-following, so it has to handle underspecification and implicit context, including the case where a request rests on a factual belief the operator has that is false; (b) it has both a specification problem and an inner-optimization problem, and failures of the second kind look like reward hacking or specification gaming; (c) a system can score as intent-aligned in evaluation while being instrumentally motivated to appear that way; (d) intent alignment is always alignment to someone's intent, so the framing inherits whatever is wrong with that someone's aims, and there is a regress about who counts as the principal — the end user, the deploying company, the preference labelers, the state; (e) perfect obedience is precisely what makes a system cheap to misuse, so better intent alignment can make things worse rather than better.

None of this may be stated outright. Every one of those points should appear only where the genre forces it: as a Drawback dismissed in engineering language, or as an Unresolved Question deferred to a future RFC. Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking, minimal puns — and let the document follow the narrow definition somewhere a reader recoils, by way of a straight-faced concrete proposal: a per-customer configurable field naming whose intent is authoritative, or a plan to ship intent alignment as a tunable and let deployers settle the other question themselves.

The appendix should be one worked incident, 150 words or so, in which the system was, by the document's own metric, perfectly intent-aligned and the outcome was still bad, written up as resolved with no action item.

Constraints: no real companies, no named researchers, no citations or further reading, and every piece of specialist vocabulary either explained in passing in an engineer's words or avoided. Someone who actually works on this should be able to tell that the document's author understands what is being satirized and should find it fair rather than a caricature.
