hey — I need a draft of a joint statement for an adversarial collaboration I'm trying to set up, and I want it to be one real working document, not two position papers stapled together.

The two camps:

Camp O, the optimizer: bounded rationality is just optimization with the costs of thinking, searching and attending priced into the objective function, the way economists treat attention as a scarce resource an agent allocates. On this view satisficing isn't a rival norm of choice at all, it's what maximizing looks like once deliberation costs something, and every heuristic gets evaluated by how much expected payoff it leaves on the table per unit of computation.

Camp S, the satisficer: in a lot of real environments the optimizing description isn't even available from the inside. Finding out what one more step of search costs often requires taking it, and what that step is worth depends on what you'd then do with whatever you found — so the stopping rule has to come from somewhere else, and for them it comes from an aspiration level adjusted by feedback. They also think some of what heuristics buy you isn't accuracy, or isn't only accuracy.

Here are the bits I want you to work with, which are why I think this collaboration is worth doing rather than another round of the same argument:

- The self-referential search cost. The optimizer's rule — keep searching while the expected gain from another step exceeds what that step costs — isn't executable at the moment of decision in the cases that matter, because both quantities are partly revealed by the search itself. Can both camps sign that this is a structural problem and not just a nuisance, or does the optimizer think it dissolves at a higher level of description?

- Flat payoff terrain. Where lots of options sit within a hair of the best one, the two procedures pick the same act nearly always, and the argument becomes purely about how to describe what happened. I want a crisp statement of exactly when the disagreement has no behavioural consequences.

- Two properties I want named so we can argue about them. Swap-sensitivity: the expected size of the change in the chosen act when you permute the order in which options show up. Auditability: the degree to which a third party, given a short account of how you decided, can reconstruct why you chose what you chose and check it wasn't arbitrary. My suspicion is that satisficers are buying low swap-sensitivity and high auditability, and that in institutional settings those are worth having for reasons not reducible to expected payoff — you have to defend the decision afterwards, and a decision you can't explain is a decision you can't defend. The obvious optimizer reply is that both are just more terms in the objective function; the interesting crux is whether that move has any principled stopping point, or whether it can absorb literally anything and therefore explains nothing.

- The regress. If choosing a decision procedure is itself a decision, and Camp O says decisions are made by optimizing, then procedure-choice is optimizing too, and so on. I want each side to say what stops it, or why it doesn't need stopping, and I definately want this seperate from the search-cost point above rather than merged into it.

Sections, in this order:

1. "What we both sign" — numbered agreed claims, 8 to 12 of them, roughly 500-700 words. Substantive: what a model of a bounded agent is for, what makes a procedure good, the cases where the camps already coincide. Nothing either side could sign while thinking the other is an idiot.

2. "Where we differ" — a table, about 400 words. Columns: the disagreement; the wording the optimizer would accept for its own view; the wording the satisficer would accept for its own view. Each wording has to be one that side would actually put its name to.

3. "Cruxes" — 3 or 4, about 350 words. For each, why settling it would move at least one side, and which side it moves more.

4. "Tests, cases and predictions" — about 500 words. For each crux, one concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it. Invent the cases; make them the kind of thing a bounded agent actually faces — unknown number of options, options arriving in some order, search costs known only partly in advance, a decision that later has to be justified to somebody else. Write down in advance what each camp predicts, and what each would count as coming out wrong.

5. "Verbal or real" — about 150 words. Which parts are disputes about words, and which parts survive translation into a question about what to do.

Tone: two people who've argued for years and are trying to work out what they'd actually have to see. Don't let either side win by definition, and don't let the agreed claims be so thin they're worthless.
