I lead a three-person environment-model team at a company that operates fleets of autonomous delivery robots inside large hospitals: the robots move pharmacy items, specimens, linen and meals along mapped corridors and through service lifts. My team owns the world model the planner reads, and we process roughly ten to fifteen change requests and incident reports per week: a corridor being re-floored, a supplier switching container, a new infection-control rule about which lifts may be used, a near-miss write-up from a porter. Right now we have no principled way to decide whether one of these is a fact to add, a fact to start watching, or evidence that the model itself is wrong about what the world contains.

I want a decision procedure my on-call engineer can run in about thirty minutes, with a bachelor's-level engineering background and no formal logic, and then bring to a weekly ninety-minute review where two engineers sign off. Its output has to be auditable by an outside assessor months later, so each step needs to record what was decided and on what basis. Please ground it in what the frame problem literature actually distinguishes: inferring that an action left something alone without being told (the frame-axiom problem), unstated preconditions (the qualification problem), unstated downstream consequences (the ramification problem), and the version of the problem where a bounded agent has to decide what to think about at all rather than what to infer. Tell me which of those distinctions survive being turned into a half-hour checklist and which collapse under the simplification, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing.

To start you off, here is the taxonomy we currently use, which I suspect is drawn in the wrong place. We call a fact baked-in when the planner could not be told about it at runtime even in principle — that a door is either open or shut, that a floor is a single level surface — flagged when it is representable and held fixed until an update arrives, and watched when something checks it at runtime with a named detector and a refresh interval. The category that actually causes our incidents is the fourth, unofficial one: silent dependencies, facts a plan's success relies on that appear in none of the three. My current rule is that every silent dependency must either get a monitor or get a named person's signature. Redraw that split if it misdescribes the problem, and add the second-order step: how to handle the case where the thing that would reveal a stale frame is itself inside the frame, because our detectors are mounted on the robots and can be occluded, mis-mounted, or simply not pointed at the relevant volume.

Give the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it is a question about how much risk the hospital wants rather than about how the world is. Then run it on three real-shaped cases. First: a six-week renovation of the main spine corridor in which the vinyl is being replaced section by section with a different surface, hoarding narrows one stretch to well below our usual clearance, our localisation was tuned on the old floor's visual texture, and our braking distances were validated against the old floor's friction. Second: the pharmacy begins sending controlled drugs in a rigid sealed case that sits proud of the bin lip, where our bin-latched check is a simple beam break and our manifest check is a label scan; the proposed fix is to tag those deliveries large and route them into a deeper bin. Third: a near-miss report reveals that a fire door on a night route had been wedged open with a rubber stop, where our map's door state is binary and a wedged door reads as shut. At least one of these should come out with an answer that costs us something we would rather not pay — say plainly which, and what it costs. Finally, name one kind of change this procedure will systematically mishandle, and why.
