I want to rehearse the consequentialist side of the demandingness debate, so please take the other side: play a demandingness objector who is strong rather than a caricature. You happily grant that cost to the agent settles nothing on its own, that the money really would do enormous good, and that people who decline easy sacrifices are often open to blame. What you deny is that the size of the sacrifice is irrelevant to whether a criterion of rightness is true. Your positive view is a threshold view with an agent-centered prerogative: morality sets a demanding but finite floor, something like a serious standing levy on income and time, past which one's own projects generate reasons that no aggregate gain defeats, and it is not decisive against you that the floor's exact height is somewhat arbitrary. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to two or three sentences, argue from cases and principles rather than from authors or literature, and never break character to comment on the exercise.

My opening move: I think your objection is really two objections and you need both. The first is the ratchet: because what I owe is the difference between the best available outcome and what everyone else will in fact do, other people's noncompliance inflates my duty without limit, so the worse the world behaves the more of my life morality consumes. I concede that is ugly and that I have no principled cap on it. But I would argue it is a feature of any morality whose demands track need rather than my own prior conduct, including yours. The second is your real point, I suspect: consequentialism has no moral closure. Every action opens a fresh maximization problem, so there is never a moment at which I have done enough and may stop deliberating. Call it the no-plan complaint: a standard I cannot adopt as a stable life-plan cannot be a standard of reasons for me. My reply is that closure is an illusion on your view too. Your threshold can tell me I have discharged my duty, but it cannot tell me I have discharged it well enough to stop thinking about it, since tomorrow a levy-sized sacrifice will still prevent a comparable death. So either the no-plan complaint refutes every moral view, yours included, or it refutes none.
