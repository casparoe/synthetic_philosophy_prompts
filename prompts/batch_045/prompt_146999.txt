I write a column on science, memory and public argument for a national magazine, and about a year ago I let a city councilman I had interviewed talk me into serving as one of two lay members of my city's Commemoration and Naming Advisory Panel. We meet monthly, we get a case file roughly ten days in advance, we have no research staff and no budget to hire one, we may not commission polling or archival work beyond what is in the file, we must publish a one-paragraph written rationale for every recommendation, and the council treats our recommendations as binding in practice even though formally it is free to ignore them. We handle perhaps fifteen items a year: petitions to remove or relocate a statue, petitions to rename a street, a school, or a clinic, applications to install something new, and increasingly proposals that split the difference by adding an interpretive plaque or moving the object indoors. What I have discovered is that my own reactions are inconsistent in ways I could not defend in print: I argued for keeping a monument I would have wanted gone had the same person's name been on a school, and I argued for renaming a street in a case where I would have left an identical statue alone. I want to stop improvising, and I would also like to write the column about the framework rather than about my gut.

So give me a decision procedure the panel can actually run on a case file in an afternoon: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no because the file does not support going further, and where it deliberately hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. A few things I want it to do, because I have become convinced they are where these cases actually turn. First, treat public commemoration as a speech act by a speaker with an audience, and give me an explicit rule for fixing the content of that act, since a bronze or a street sign has no determinate propositional content: my own candidate proxy is what a reasonably informed passerby would take away in the few seconds they give it, read alongside the dedicatory inscription and the circumstances of the original installation, and I want you to keep that proxy, replace it with a better one, or say why the attempt is misguided. Second, distinguish the available responses rather than framing everything as keep versus remove: leave as is, add context, rewrite the inscription, relocate to a museum or cemetery, remove to storage, remove and destroy, and install something new under a fixed term with a sunset clause. Third, build in the variable I have started calling compulsion, meaning how far the city forces residents to repeat the name in the course of ordinary life, which seems to me to run from school or street addresses through building names to a statue in a park that one can simply walk past, and the related variable of inescapability, since a name on a bus route or an attendance boundary is not avoidable in the way a monument is. Fourth, treat a plinth or a name as a scarce and rival resource, so that retaining is also a decision not to commemorate anyone else, and say what the procedure does with the fact that the marginal signal from yet another monument of the same kind is close to zero while the marginal signal from a renaming is not. Fifth, handle separability: the case where the honored deed is detachable from the wrongdoing, the case where the deed is constituted by it, as with discoveries that depended on coerced subjects or a fortune made in the slave trade, and the case where the point of the original installation was itself an act of intimidation rather than of honor. Sixth, tell me what to do about a name whose commemorative force has simply drained away, where no one in the neighborhood any longer knows who the person was, and about the asymmetry that removal is nearly irreversible in practice while retention imposes a continuing cost on identifiable living people. Where a step looks like a judgment call dressed up as procedure, label it as one rather than hiding it, and where the panel as a lay body should defer to someone else, say to whom.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases, all of them composites I have built from several real disputes, so please treat the facts as given rather than as claims to check. One: an 1894 bronze in a city park, paid for by subscription among professional colleagues, honoring a surgeon whose celebrated technique he developed by operating without anesthesia on enslaved women; the plinth names only the technique; his name is also on a street and a clinic; a descendant association has petitioned for removal, the county historical society wants retention with a new plaque, and a teaching hospital has offered the piece a place in a collection. Two: a street named for a nineteenth-century textile industrialist who endowed the public library and once published a pamphlet arguing that immigrants should not vote; no living group identifies as injured by that pamphlet; nobody on the street knows who he was; renaming would cost the city about a hundred and eighty thousand dollars and force re-addressing on roughly a hundred and forty households and businesses. Three, the one I expect to argue with: the founder of the city's tuberculosis sanatorium system, credited in her own time with saving thousands of lives and the subject of an admiring column I wrote in 2019, newly documented from her own correspondence as having drafted and lobbied for the state's compulsory sterilization law, with several people sterilized under that law still alive and willing to testify. I am inclined toward retention with a large new interpretive installation, and I want to know whether the procedure underwrites me or overrules me, so commit to a verdict on each case and give your reasons instead of laying out what a panel might say. Finally, be blunt about the limits: tell me what this procedure cannot do, which of my stipulations you dropped and why, which step you are least confident in, and what would have to be true for the procedure to be giving the wrong answers systematically.
