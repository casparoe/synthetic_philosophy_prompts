I sit on a small committee drafting a pilot in which a regional transit authority would pick between competing capital projects by futarchy: the elected board would ratify a welfare metric, and whichever project the conditional prediction markets said would score higher on it would get funded. One colleague closes every objection with the line "vote on values, bet on beliefs." The slogan did real work twice at our last meeting: he used it to brush off my worry that the metric will have to be revised after the first few rounds (revision, he said, is just another vote, so nothing is lost), and again when I pointed out that our metric is partly forward-looking — it weights projected fare revenue and projected land-value uplift over twenty years, quantities that themselves depend on which project traders expect to win.

Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims this slogan could be expressing: a division-of-labor claim about what voting and markets are each good at; a claim about what democratic legitimacy requires; a claim that ends and empirical expectations can be cleanly separated; a claim about measurability and operationalization; a claim about incentives and information aggregation; and any other reading doing real work. For each, say what kind of "vote" and what kind of "bet" it presupposes, and mark it as trivial, substantive but contested, or false. Then say which readings, if any, actually support the two moves my colleague made — in particular whether the slogan can treat the choice of welfare metric itself as a value rather than a belief, and what it implies when the quantity being bet on is sensitive to the market's own prices. Give verdicts rather than a survey of options, and press the objections yourself instead of only explaining the slogan sympathetically. Please answer in American English.
