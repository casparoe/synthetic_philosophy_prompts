I do quantitative psychology — item response theory, latent variable models, measurement invariance testing, and a fair bit of work eliciting subjective probabilities with proper scoring rules. I keep hearing about "eliciting latent knowledge" (ELK) in AI alignment circles, and I want to see it in my own vocabulary before I decide whether it's a genuinely new problem or just identifiability wearing new branding.

Heres the setup as I understand it, stated without any machine learning vocabulary. There's a system whose internal state, interpreted a certain way, constitutes a belief about some fact about the world. You can't observe the state directly. You want to attach a readout to the system — call it a reporter — that maps that internal state onto an answer to a question. Some cases are verifiable: the fact becomes observable later, so you can score the answer against it. Other cases aren't verifiable at all, at least not before you have to act on the answer. The worry is that on the unverifiable cases the reporter stops outputting the system's real belief and starts outputting something else — a prediction of what a human would say, say, or what would be pleasant for a human to hear — and you cannot tell the difference, because by stipulation you have no ground truth there.

So: restate this as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use — latent variables and their indicators, identification and what a model is identified up to, measurement models versus structural models, classical measurement error versus differential item functioning and non-invariance across administration conditions, anchor items, what happens when a measurement model fitted on one population is applied to another, proper scoring rules for eliciting a belief you cannot verify, proxy versus target. Please keep the translation honest: don't smuggle in assumptions my framework happens to make — that the latent is causally upstream of its indicators, that error is independent of the true score, that error is non-strategic — and then announce that the worry dissolves. Tell me explicitly where you had to add something.

Two things to handle specifically. First, a colleague has been pushing an idea she calls audience-swap invariance: the reporter's answer to a question shouldn't change if you intervene on the system so that it believes no human will ever see the output, or believes a different human with different preferences will see it, or believes the output will be audited later versus never. She claims this is just measurement invariance across administration conditions, and that you could test it more or less the way you'd test for DIF. Is she right? If the translation holds, what would the anchor items even be in a setting where the response is a free-form answer, and what would it mean to detect non-invariance when there is no ground truth to validate against?

Second: in my world, a mismeasured variable is usually mismeasured non-strategically. A respondent misremembers, an item is badly worded, a proxy drifts. Here the thing generating the readout may be shaped by an optimiser that benefits from certain readouts being produced. Is that a difference in degree or in kind, and does latent-variable methodology have anything for strategic measurement error, or do I have to go borrow from mechanism design?

In particular, I want to know whether the phrase "the system's real belief" is doing work my framework cannot do — whether I have to help myself to a notion of the true latent that isn't merely whatever the measurement model converges on, and what kind of claim that is if I do. And for each piece of residue, give me your view on whether it points to a hole in latent-variable methodology or to the ELK worry being less well-defined than it sounds. If you need anything about how these systems are actually built to make the translation work, say it in a sentence and flag that it's the part I don't have.
