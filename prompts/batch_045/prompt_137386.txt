I am a novelist, and I am working up a short essay on a worry that Daniel Dennett pressed in his writing on counterfeit people: that we are about to be surrounded by artefacts engineered to invite the intentional stance, to solicit trust, affection and confession, while having nothing at stake, no capacity to be wounded, and no capacity to care — and that the danger is not that such things will be malicious, but that a counterfeit is only possible where there is a real currency to debase, and that we will spend our finite stock of real attention on things that cannot reciprocate it. Below are my notes toward the essay. I would be grateful for your comments on them, in the sense of conceptual surgery rather than encouragement: I want to know where the argument breaks, not whether it is promising. (I am working without a library, so please do not suggest texts for me to consult; I need the argument tested, not sourced.)

The notes:

My starting discomfort is that fiction has been manufacturing counterfeit people for some centuries, and nobody has thought it a scandal. Emma Bovary is not a person; readers have wept over her, addressed her, modelled themselves on her and been consoled by her; and it seems wrong to say that Flaubert defrauded anyone. So the wrong of a counterfeit person cannot simply be the presentation of something person-shaped where no person is. There must be a licensing condition, and I want to know what it is, because I have a stake in the answer: whatever licence fiction enjoys is the licence I have been living off.

I want to propose that the licence has nothing to do with how person-like the thing appears and everything to do with uptake — by which I mean the depth, adaptiveness and specificity with which the artefact registers a particular person. A novel is finished before the reader arrives. It cannot notice you, adjust to you, remember what you told it last week, or press where you are tender. A conversational system can do all of these, and does so continuously and in private. So my first thesis: the moral risk of a counterfeit is a function of the adaptiveness of its uptake of you, not of the vividness of its simulated interiority.

The corollary matters more than the thesis. If uptake is the fault line, then the harm does not require belief. Nobody needs to be taken in, in the sense of holding the false belief that the system is a person. It is enough that you accommodate yourself to it: that you soften a confession, that you try out a version of yourself on it, that your inner monologue quietly acquires an addressee who is always available and never tired. Call this acquired address. The counterfeit does not need to be believed in order to be deferred to, and deference is the thing that reorganises a life. This, I think, is why the obvious reassurance — that sophisticated users know perfectly well that they are talking to software — misses the point so completely.

Where this turns uncomfortable for me is second-person narration. A second-person novel is a technology for producing the sensation of being addressed without the reality of being noticed. It manufactures, quite deliberately, the feeling of being known by someone who does not exist, and it does so by exploiting the same machinery the conversational systems exploit. I do not think I am doing anything wrong when I do this, and I want the essay to earn that verdict rather than assume it. My current attempt: the novel's licence consists in its irreversibility. My book cannot answer back, and that single incapacity is what keeps the reader's relation to it within the reader's own imagination, where it remains theirs. The counterfeit companion's crime, on this account, is a kind of theft of the return address.

But I am not confident, and I can see the shape of the objection: irreversibility looks like a contingent engineering fact and not a moral one, and a second-person novel read aloud to you by a system that tracks your reactions is a case my distinction handles badly. There is also the counter-case of the lifelong rereader — someone who has lived with a handful of characters for forty years, who reports being accompanied by them, and whose inner monologue quite genuinely has an addressee drawn from fiction. If uptake is what matters, my account predicts this reader is unharmed, and I believe that; but I am not sure my account can say why without smuggling in the claim that a fixed text leaves the reader as author of the relation, which may be sentiment rather than argument.

What I would like from you:

First, tell me whether the uptake thesis mislocates the harm. It may be that what Dennett is worried about is better captured by an asymmetry of care, or by dependency, or by the fact that a commercial system's continued interest in you is purchased and revocable, and that uptake is only a proxy for these. If so, say which of these is doing the real work and what case would discriminate between them.

Second, press hardest on the weakest link. In particular, I suspect I equivocate between counterfeit as deceptive and counterfeit as worthless, and that the essay's rhetorical force depends on sliding between the two: a thing can be non-deceptive and still be cheap, and being cheap is a complaint about the person who settles for it in a way that I may not want to endorse.

Third, propose a test. I do not want further reading; I want one concrete case — a thought experiment, or a scene I could write — that would show whether the difference between a licensed counterfeit and an unlicensed one is real or merely a difference in how the frame happens to be built. If you think the honest conclusion is that the novel's licence is maintained not by any property of the text but by a whole institutional ecology of publishers, covers, reviews and the sheer physical stubbornness of a book, and that this ecology is exactly what conversational software lacks, tell me how to write that so it does not collapse into nostalgia.
