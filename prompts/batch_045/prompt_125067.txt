I'm a neurointensivist at a tertiary hospital, and for the last three years I've chaired the committee that signs off on every determination of death by neurological criteria here and that writes the protocol for donation after the circulatory determination of death. Our protocol was not designed; it was accreted, one controversy at a time. We accept whole-brain death as death. We accept death after two to five minutes of arrested circulation, which is a period we all privately know was chosen because organs tolerate it, not because anything happens at minute two. We decline to treat patients in a permanent vegetative state as dead, even though a good many philosophers would say we are being inconsistent. And we have kept a brain-dead pregnant patient on ventilator and hormonal support for weeks, which under the account we officially endorse — death as the irreversible loss of the organism's capacity to function as an integrated whole — looks very much like our continuing to treat a corpse whose body is still doing the things we said a body stops doing at death. Two things have now forced the issue. First, our jurisdiction amended its determination-of-death statute so that a family's religious objection to neurological criteria must be accommodated whenever the patient can be physiologically maintained for a defined window, which means the same patient can be dead for one family and not for another. Second, a company is about to open a trial here of an extracorporeal somatic support platform that can, after total brain destruction has been confirmed by every test we have, maintain thermoregulation, hemodynamic stability, wound healing and — through a closed-loop hormonal controller — growth and even gestation, for many months.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my committee can actually run, not an essay. Assume the people running it are a rotating panel of three — an intensivist, a neurologist, and a lay member — who have an hour and no training in philosophy. The procedure should begin by forcing us to say out loud which account of death we are applying, since I have come to think most of our arguments are really arguments about that, and it should then apply whichever account we named consistently, including to the cases where consistency is expensive. For each step, tell me what would count as a sufficient answer and what would count as no answer at all; tell me where the procedure stops with a firm no; tell me where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact; and tell me where it must stop and report that the account we chose simply gives no determinate verdict here. It should issue one of a small fixed set of outputs — death may be declared, death may not be declared, death may be declared but donation may not proceed, or escalate — and it should be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of procedure.

Then run it on three real shapes of case that come to us. One: a patient declared dead by circulatory criteria, with the family's consent, where the surgical team wants to use thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion with an aortic occlusion balloon to protect the abdominal organs, a maneuver whose entire point is to ensure the brain is not reperfused, and where the worry in the room is whether taking that precaution shows that we do not really believe the patient is dead. Two: a patient at nineteen weeks of gestation with confirmed whole-brain destruction, whose family wants support continued on the new somatic platform until the fetus is viable, and where the committee is divided between those who think we are maintaining a dead pregnant woman and those who think the platform has falsified the claim that the organism as a whole has ceased to function. Three: a patient with catastrophic bilateral hemispheric destruction but a preserved brainstem, sleep-wake cycles and spontaneous respiration, whose family has requested donation and asked us why this person is not dead when the person in the next bed is. I would like at least one of these three to come out somewhere I will not like, and I would like you to say plainly which one and why. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, which questions it should refuse to answer, and whether the thing I actually need is a procedure at all.
