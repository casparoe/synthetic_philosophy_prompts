I hosted a dinner last week for two people who turned out not to know each other: a psychophysicist who works on multisensory integration, and a philosopher of perception. They spent most of the evening on Molyneux's question, and I lost the thread somewhere in the middle. I'd be grateful for a careful reconstruction and adjudication of the argument as it actually went. Roughly, it ran like this.

Nadia (the psychophysicist) opened by saying the question has an empirical answer and that the answer is no: in the recorded cases where sight has been restored to people who were blind from early childhood, the newly sighted do not, on first looking, pick out by sight the objects they previously knew only by touch. So the negative answer that Locke gave stands, and the armchair version of the debate has been overtaken. (I don't know whether that empirical claim is right — please treat it as her assertion rather than as something to check.)

Owen (the philosopher) replied that a negative result on an identification task doesn't reach the interesting question. Molyneux's question, he said, is about whether a visual experience and a tactile experience of one and the same shape have any content in common. A subject could fail the test while having exactly that common content: she may not yet know that what she is seeing is the sort of thing she could touch; she may not be able to bring the new sense under voluntary control on demand; she may see the shapes perfectly well and simply be unable to indicate what she sees to an experimenter. Each of those is a way to fail a forced-choice task without there being any deficiency in the content of her visual experience. So the empirical no is evidence only against a thesis much stronger than the one actually in dispute.

Nadia granted the confounds but said they are avoidable: you can test for transfer without asking the subject to judge anything, report anything, or believe that the thing seen and the thing touched are identical. Her example was the way a person reaches. Someone who has seen a sphere but has never touched what she is now seeing will, if asked to pick it up, scale her grip and orient her wrist for a round object, and that can be measured before any contact. Her other example was anticipatory looking: which of two objects the eyes go to when a name or a haptic cue is given. A positive result on a measure like that, she said, is transfer without judgment, and it answers Owen's objection about report and aboutness.

Owen said he would accept such a result as evidence, but not yet as evidence for what Nadia wants it to be evidence for. He drew a distinction between two ways transfer might be achieved. The first is a shared amodal format: vision delivers something with the same shape-structure that touch delivers, and the connection is immediate because there is nothing to translate. The second he called a bridge feature: a single low-level feature available in both modalities that lets the subject single out the object even though there is no general shape space in common. A person blind from birth might know the sphere as the object with no edges and no corners; vision might tell her, without any help, that the thing now in front of her has no edges and no corners; and she could thereby succeed at the test while her visual sense of roundness is nothing at all like her tactile sense of roundness. If that is possible, then a yes on the classic cube-and-sphere case is ambiguous between two very different hypotheses, and Molyneux's question as posed — one question, to be answered yes or no — is ill-formed, because the real question is how much structure transfers.

Nadia said that if that is the objection, the fix is straightforward and it also improves on the original design: stop asking for identification and ask for similarity structure. Give the subject six or eight unfamiliar shapes — smoothly varying blobs, with no ordinary name, no familiar function, and no single stereotyped feature that could serve as a bridge. Blindfolded, have her rank how similar each pair feels to the hand. Restore sight, and then, before any sighted touching, ask her to arrange the same shapes into pairs by how similar they look. If her visual ordering reproduces her earlier haptic ordering at better-than-chance rates, that is a whole relational structure transferring, and a one-feature bridge cannot counterfeit an entire ordering.

Owen's reply was two-sided, and this is where I got lost. He said that if that experiment came out positive he would regard the empirical core of Molyneux's question as settled, and as settling more than he had originally conceded — because, he suggested, what it is for a visual and a tactile state to represent the same shape might just be for them to be integrated with each other in the way the experiment would demonstrate, in which case there is no further fact to wait for. But he added that even with perfect structural transfer there might be a residual question — whether the two experiences are alike in themselves, and not merely alike in what they dispose the subject to do — and he said he was genuinely unsure whether that residual question is an empirical one, or whether it is a real question at all.

Please don't give me a survey of the literature or a list of positions in the history of philosophy. I want a verdict on this exchange. Go through it move by move and tell me who is right about what at each step, which moves are only partly right, and what each side has left unanswered. In particular: would the similarity-structure experiment Nadia proposes actually discriminate between the shared-format hypothesis and the bridge-feature hypothesis, or does it inherit some version of the same ambiguity? And is Owen right that a positive result on it would settle more than the original question asked, and right to be unsure whether the residual question is empirical? Finally, say what, if anything, both of them are missing.

For each verdict, state how confident you are in it. Then single out the one claim in your answer that you are least confident about, and say what would change your mind on that point.
