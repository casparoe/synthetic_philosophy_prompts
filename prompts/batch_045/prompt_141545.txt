I'm doing a 50-minute evening session for a classics-in-translation reading group next month — about fifteen people, mostly retired, a couple of undergrads, nobody has read the Laws. What they have done is read the Republic in a book club a few years back, and they came away with Plato = hates pleasure, bans the poets, wants everything censored. So I have to get across why the old Athenian spends ages defending the drinking party, with a sober man put in charge of it, as a piece of moral education — not as a lapse, not as a joke, and not as Plato finally relaxing.

Don't write me the explanation itself, I can go find that. I want the plan for how to explain it, and where I've got it wrong please say so.

- What has to be in place before the defence even reads as a defence rather than as a punchline. My guess: they need the idea that for Plato being educated isn't being told things, it's having your pleasures and pains trained from early on, and they need the complaint that a courage trained only against pains — the austere Spartan/Cretan setup he's arguing against — is lopsided. But I don't know which of those has to land first, or whether there's something even more basic underneath both.
- The one example to carry the whole thing. I was going to use a driving instructor letting a learner feel a skid in a wet, empty car park: real hazard, low cost, repeatable, someone sober in the passenger seat. But I worry it makes it sound like habituation or innoculation, and that what the wine specifically does gets lost — it isn't just a hazard, it undoes the shame-and-reputation brake and makes people childishly frank. Tell me what the carrying example has to have and what it has to lack, and whether mine is a trap.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arrive. I'm expecting: so it's really about moderation; it's about meeting the authentic self underneath; the sober supervisor is basically surveillance or a chaperone; and it's just exposure therapy. Which comes first, and where in the arc do I head each one off? The one I'm not seeing is the thing I most want from you.
- Where the puppet image of the soul with the golden cord for calculation goes, if it goes anywhere at all — I half remember it and can't decide if it earns its five minutes.
- How I'll know at the end that it landed rather than them just being delighted that Plato defended a booze-up. I was going to hand them some modern institution I pick and have them ask whether it trains people against pains only or against pleasures too. Is that the right check, and what would a wrong answer sound like?
- What I should openly refuse to get into at this level. I'm inclined to skip the bit about judging music by pleasure in the next book, but that may be a mistake.

No further readings or refs, I just need the shape of the session.
