I am writing a seminar paper on Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as a case study of hard choices between incommensurable values, and I would like a reading that complicates the standard picture rather than merely illustrating it.

Stevens looks tailor-made for the hard-choices literature. He gives up love (Miss Kenton), his father's deathbed, and any refusal to serve a man whose politics have turned rotten, all for an ideal of professional dignity: being a butler who is, in some sense, the moral centre of the house. The literature I have in mind treats such choices as ones where the options are incomparable, so that no amount of weighing settles the matter, and where the agent's own commitment partly constitutes the value of what she picks.

What interests me is a distinction that literature, as I have read it, does not really draw. Call a choice prospectively hard when it is agonizing at the moment of decision, and retrospectively hard when it hurts only much later, once the agent has at last come to see what the option she rejected really was. Stevens barely seems to deliberate: he comes upon Miss Kenton weeping and answers with something about the household; his father dies upstairs while he goes on serving the guests below; the cost arrives only decades later, on the motoring trip west and on the pier at Weymouth.

Give me your reading as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) whether Stevens has a hard choice at all, or only a terrible mistake; (2) whether retrospective hardness is a genuine species of hard choice or merely ignorance, self-deception, or bad faith narrated as tragedy; (3) whether the phenomenology of agony should be treated as evidence of incommensurability, given that Stevens feels almost none of it at the time; (4) one scene where the incommensurability reading genuinely illuminates the novel and one where the novel resists it; (5) what the novel adds to the debate that argument alone does not.
