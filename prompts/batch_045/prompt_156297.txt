I serve as the secretary of the Charter Revision Commission of a mid-sized American city, and I need a written opinion I can circulate to the other eight commissioners ahead of our recommending vote in three weeks.

The background is this. In March the city council passed, 7–2, an ordinance rezoning a vacant industrial parcel to permit a 120-unit supportive-housing facility with on-site services. Turnout at the two public hearings was unusually heavy: of the roughly four hundred residents who spoke, about three to one opposed the project, most of them owners of nearby homes. Several speakers described the vote as officials overriding the will of the people, and two council members who voted yes said privately that the hearing had changed their view of the merits but not their vote, because the regional housing authority had committed funding that would lapse if the site were lost.

In response, a neighborhood coalition has petitioned the commission to place on the November ballot a charter amendment with three parts: any ordinance adopted by the council may be suspended and sent to a citywide vote if five percent of registered voters sign a petition within thirty days of passage; the resulting referendum is decided by a simple majority with no minimum turnout requirement; and an ordinance that survives the referendum may not be reconsidered by the council for two years. A separate good-government group has urged us to reject the petition and instead propose a standing citizens' assembly of forty residents selected by lot, which would hear contested ordinances and issue a non-binding recommendation with reasons before the council voted.

What I need from you is the commission's decision memo: a single recommendation we can act on, together with the reasoning. If you recommend adopting the petitioned mechanism, specify the parameters you would adopt rather than the ones proposed—signature threshold, petition window, any turnout floor, whether the council may amend or withdraw the ordinance once a petition is certified, whether emergency and budget measures are carved out, and whether a surviving ordinance is really insulated from reconsideration. If you recommend rejecting it in favour of the assembly or of some hybrid, say so equally concretely.

Two things the memo has to do beyond that. First, it should say plainly which reading of the popular will the city's procedures ought to be designed to track: the aggregate of present preferences on the issue, the judgment the electorate would reach under conditions of information and deliberation, or the authorization voters gave their representatives at the last election. Our commissioners are using the phrase differently and it is doing a lot of hidden work. Second, the memo should engage the strongest argument on whichever side it rejects, and it should deal with the boundary problem: the facility's future residents are mostly not current voters of this city, a substantial share of nearby residents cannot vote in municipal elections, and the deliberative option enfranchises people the electoral option does not. If the mechanism is meant to defeat elite capture, the memo should also explain why it would not simply hand the same power to whichever side can organize or pay for signature gathering and turnout on a low-salience off-year ballot. Please keep it under twelve hundred words and write it so that a commissioner who disagrees can see where the argument turns.
