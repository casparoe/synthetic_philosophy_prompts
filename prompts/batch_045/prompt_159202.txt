I'm taking an upper-level seminar on minds and machines, and we're reading parts of *Gödel, Escher, Bach* alongside some philosophy of mind. Before the semester I read a chapter on Hofstadter in one of those "big ideas explained in ten pages" books, and its summary of his argument went roughly like this:

Gödel showed that any formal system rich enough to contain arithmetic has a sentence that says of itself that it cannot be proved in that system. Since the sentence really is unprovable, it is true — so there are arithmetical truths the system can never reach, and since a computer is nothing but a machine for following rules, there are truths we can see that no computer ever could. Hofstadter's strange loops are the same point moved into psychology: once a system's symbols can refer to the system itself, the system acquires a self, and that self-referential loop just is consciousness. The same result also shows that no machine could be conscious, since a machine is a formal system and a formal system cannot step outside itself to see the truth of its own Gödel sentence, whereas we can — which is why Hofstadter concludes that the mind is not a formal system. On this view consciousness even comes in degrees, measured by how self-referential a system is: a thermostat that feeds its own state back into itself has a trace of a self, and a language model whose own outputs are fed back in as input has considerably more.

My TA said several of these claims are things Hofstadter explicitly denies — especially the Gödelian case against machine consciousness, and the idea that the loop is a matter of a sentence referring to itself rather than of a system's symbols coding for its own structure the way Gödel numbering does. The chapter sounded confident, so I can't tell who is right. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what Hofstadter is actually committed to, what is a fair simplification, and what gets him backwards? I'd especially like to know whether the truth of the Gödel sentence is something the theorem delivers outright or needs an extra assumption, and whether the closing claims about the thermostat and the self-fed language model survive once the summary's errors are corrected — my own hunch is that a model using a word for itself is not the same as a system encoding its own structure, but I don't know whether that distinction is one Hofstadter draws.
