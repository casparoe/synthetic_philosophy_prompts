hey — I'm in a historiography reading group this term (mostly people from history and HPS, plus a couple of stray philosophers) and we've now spent about six weeks on periodization and we are going round in cirlces. The question was supposed to be whether the Middle Ages, the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution are real things or later inventions, and we've sorted into three camps that haven't moved at all.

Camp one says the labels track real joints, however crudely: there genuinely is an acceleration in things you can count — printed titles per decade, the share of natural-philosophy books that report an actual experiment or a mathematised result rather than a commentary on an ancient text, the growth of correspondence networks — so 'Renaissance' and 'Scientific Revolution' name real discontinuities that a better-measured version of the same history would sharpen rather than dissolve. Camp two says periods are stipulative conveniences: you're cutting a continuum for teaching and citation purposes, nobody can be wrong about where the cut goes, and the fact that every survey book picks a slightly different date is the whole story. Camp three doesn't really want to answer 'real or invented' at all — they say the interesting claim is what the labels did for whoever coined them, that the Middle Ages was named by people who thought they were out of it, and that the real object of study is nineteenth-century historiography's need for a story about modernity having a starting gun. (Two things keep getting waved around as evidence and I don't know what to do with either: the old book arguing there was already a renaissance in the twelfth century, which if true means 'the Renaissance' doesn't pick out a unique event, and the well-known book arguing there was no such thing as the Scientific Revolution.)

What frustrates me is that nobody disagrees about any fact — nobody denies that printing spread, or that Copernicus published, or that patronage and universities changed shape — so it doesn't look like an evidential dispute, and yet it also doesn't look like a merely verbal one, because both the realists and the constructivists get visibly annoyed when someone says 'oh, it's just a dispute about the word real'. Two other things happened that made me think there's something more structural going on. A friend in digital humanities ran a change-point/boundary-detection thing over a big digitised corpus and got sharp breaks, but different ones per genre — legal Latin breaks in one decade, natural philosophy in another, devotional writing in a third — so the 'data' doesn't pick out one periodization, it picks out several. And a grad student in the group proposed that we stop asking whether a period exists and instead ask whether a boundary is invariant across independent evidence channels: a period is real, on her view, only if it shows up in sources that don't share a transmission history — like requiring a signal to appear in independent instruments rather than one. I half love this and half think it just relocates the bump in the carpet, especially since I have a nagging worry that finding a discontinuity is too cheap: any long noisy series can be cut somewhere at some resolution, so maybe the 'empirical' test isn't capable of failing, which would make the whole realist programme unfalsifiable in a boring way rather than an interesting one. (Someone also pointed out that historians argued hard for a couple of decades about a 'general crisis of the seventeenth century' and then more or less stopped, which feels like a clue about how these entities live and die.)

So I'd like a diagnosis, not a verdict, and not a survey of what different historians have said. What kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it verbal; is it about what counts as data (which corpus, which genre, which metric of discontinuity); is it about what periods are for — explanation, pedagogy, or the constitution of a disciplinary identity; are the three camps answering different questions under one heading, e.g. discovery vs. stipulation vs. the function of a label; is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, and is it well posed? My own guess, offered as a guess, is that it's partly a verbal dispute about 'real', partly a genuine disagreement about the units of history (events, practices, mentalities, rates of change), and partly a decomposition problem — that 'was there a Scientific Revolution?' is really several questions about printing, mathematisation, instrumentation and patronage that happen to overlap in time and get different answers. Tell me if that's too easy a way out. And given whatever you diagnose: what would count as progress — a distinction, a robustness criterion like the channel-invariance one, a negative result about change-point detection, a change of question — or is this an impasse we should expect and stop trying to fix, and why? Please answer in British English.
