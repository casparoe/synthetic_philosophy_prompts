Me and my mate got into a proper long argument in the pub last night about what emotions actually are — whether they're basically judgements (that's an insult, I'm in danger), or perceptions of something's value, or just feelings of the body doing stuff — and we ended up both half-remembering the same bit of Spinoza and using it to say completely opposite things. So I need help working out what the passage is actually saying before I go back for round two.

It's from Part III of the Ethics (published 1677), in an old translation — the three opening definitions plus the note:

I. By an adequate cause, I mean a cause through which its effect can be clearly and distinctly perceived. By an inadequate or partial cause, I mean a cause through which, by itself, its effect cannot be understood.

II. I say that we act when anything takes place, either within us or externally to us, whereof we are the adequate cause ; that is (by the foregoing definition) when through our nature something takes place within us or externally to us, which can through our nature alone be clearly and distinctly understood. On the other hand, I say that we are passive as regards something when that something takes place within us, or follows from our nature externally, we being only the partial cause.

III. By emotion I mean the modifications of the body, whereby the active power of the said body is increased or diminished, aided or constrained, and also the ideas of such modifications.

N.B. If we can be the adequate cause of any of these modifications, I then call the emotion an activity, otherwise I call it a passion, or state wherein the mind is passive.

My mate's line is that this is bascially an early version of the perceptual theory: the emotion just is the mind's registering of a change in its own power of acting, which is why Spinoza needs "the ideas of such modifications" and not only the modifications, and why every emotion ends up being of something. I said no, it's a feeling theory in disguise: all the real work is done by "modifications of the body", and the idea clause is only there because for Spinoza every bodily modification has an idea attached to it anyway, so it isn't doing any explanatory work.

Tell me who's closer, and walk me through the three bits I couldn't settle:

1. Why two pairs — "increased or diminished" and then "aided or constrained"? Is the second pair saying something different (power vs. direction of the change?), or is it just rhetorical doubling?
2. What exactly does "and also the ideas of such modifications" add? If the idea of a bodily modification and the modification itself are, for him, the same thing described two ways, then either the clause adds nothing or it smuggel in a mental component he shouldn't be entitled to. Which is it?
3. Given the note, an emotion where we're the adequate cause is an "activity" — but an adequate cause is one whose effect can be clearly and distinctly perceived, whereas passions are supposed to involve confused, partial understanding. So is an "active" emotion still really an emotion on this defintion, or does it turn into something more like clear knowledge?

And try one test case for me: suppose the bodily change happens but the mind has no idea of it at all — someone under heavy anaesthetic, or a shift too subtle ever to register. On the letter of definition III is that an emotion or not? I think whichever way that cuts decides the argument between us, but I can't see which way it cuts.
