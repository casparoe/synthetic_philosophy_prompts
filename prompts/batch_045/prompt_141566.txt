I work the night shift servicing imaging equipment at a hospital, and a couple of years ago I got into an argument with a nurse anaesthetist friend of mine that I have been chewing on ever since. She told me that a large part of her job is making sure that a patient has no experience at all during a procedure, that she takes this extremely seriously, and that when I said — half as a provocation — that experience might not really exist in the way we assume it does, she replied that if that were true she had wasted twenty years worrying about nothing. I have been reading around on my own since then, with no philosophy background at all: I did a two-year course in electronics and then drifted into this job. Where I have ended up is roughly this. I am a physicalist: I do not think there are any non-physical properties or substances. And I have become convinced by the sort of view I have seen called illusionism: I do not think anything has phenomenal properties, I do not think there is, strictly speaking, something it is like to undergo an experience in the rich sense people mean when they say that, and I think our enormous conviction to the contrary is manufactured by our own introspective machinery rather than by any acquaintance of ours with a property of that kind. I also think that this conviction is not the sort of thing you can talk someone out of: learning the neuroscience does not make the red stop looking red to you, and I take that to be a fact an illusionist has to explain rather than explain away.

But I hold a cluster of other things alongside that, and I cannot tell which of them are load-bearing and which are smuggled in. First, I do not want to end up saying there is no difference between being awake and being under a general anaesthetic, or between attending to a toothache and not noticing it: I think that difference is perfectly real, perfectly objective, discoverable by science, and — this is the part I care about — I think it is what we were always talking about with the word consciousness, just confusedly, so that illusionism is a correction of a bad theory about a real thing rather than a discovery that the thing is not there. Second, I think the famous arguments for the opposite conclusion — the one about the scientist who has never seen a colour, the one where you are asked to imagine a creature physically identical to a person with nothing at all going on inside — are bad arguments, but I do not think they are stupid, and I think what they are really doing is forcing you to notice how hard it is to dislodge the intuition; my line is that the intuition is a real psychological fact in need of an explanation, not a datum to be respected. Third, I have a distinction of my own that I use to think about this, which I call thin versus thick illusions: a thin illusion would be like a mislabelled dial, the system merely tagging its own activity with a property it does not have, whereas a thick illusion would be one where the system builds something like a quasi-perceptual inner model of its own states, representing them as objects that bear qualities. I think only the thick version explains why the illusion resists correction, and I do not think the thick version sneaks back in whatever I denied, because a model is not the thing it models. Fourth, I have a test I apply, which I call the Substitution Test: take any sentence about consciousness, such as the hurt of a migraine is indescribable, or there is something about being a bat that no amount of science could tell you, and substitute for the phenomenal property the subject's being in a state that is introspectively represented as having that property; if the sentence still does the work you wanted it to do, I take that as evidence for illusionism, and I find that it usually does. Fifth, on the ethics: I think the badness of suffering does not depend on phenomenal properties at all, and that what matters is being in a state a creature is disposed to avoid, that wrecks its plans, that it would do a great deal to bring to an end — so a fish or a pig can genuinely matter, and a machine with the right functional organisation could matter too, and I think illusionism makes this clearer rather than shakier. And I would go further and say that nothing has value except in so far as it affects some creature that is conscious.

The case I keep testing all of this on is one I made up, as far as I know it is not a real syndrome. A patient, call her Marta, has a condition such that while it is active she sincerely reports no experience of pain whatsoever, avoids nothing, and insists she is fine; once the condition is treated she says that those earlier reports were nonsense and that she was in agony the whole time. My verdict is that the later report is the one to trust, that the earlier claims were a malfunction of the introspective system rather than a fact about her, and that she really was suffering throughout the period during which she denied it — and I notice that this sits oddly beside what I said to my friend, since I am entirely happy to take her word for it that a properly anaesthetised patient has no experience at all and that this is a serious moral matter. So, before I try to explain any of this to her, I would like an audit of the package. Which of these commitments actually pull against each other, and which only look as though they do? I would like them ordered by how severe the tension is rather than all given equal billing, and for each genuine one I would like to know exactly what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me: whether the illusionism goes, or the claim that I am correcting a theory about a real thing, or the thick version of the illusion, or the ethical part, or my verdict on Marta, or something I have not noticed I am relying on. If there is a single revision that would clean up the most, say which. I would also like to know whether my home-made kit — the thin and thick distinction, or the Substitution Test — is doing work I have not noticed, or quietly settling the question in advance. Please do not point me at books, papers or names to look up: I want the argument, not a reading list. And please write your reply as continuous prose, the way you would write an email to a friend: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered list in your answer, no bold text, no tables — I read this kind of thing on my phone between jobs, and formatting makes it harder rather than easier to follow.
