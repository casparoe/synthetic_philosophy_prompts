I'm in a graduate seminar on collective intentionality and the evolution of cooperation at the moment, and I keep losing the thread in the same place. Every week we read someone who explains a social regularity bottom-up — preferences, expectations, equilibria, maybe some learning dynamics — and someone else who insists that the regularity's being *a norm* is doing explanatory work of its own, that "because that's what's done here" is more than a redescription of what everyone expects everyone else to do. My housemate, who is firmly in the first camp, says queueing is just a coordination convention: a recurrent problem, a salient solution, everyone prefers to conform given that the others conform, and the only sanction for deviating is the failure to coordinate itself. I think that is too fast, and before I commit I want the standard both of us should be held to.

So: write me the desiderata for a theory of social norms — not a theory, but the specification any adequate theory has to satisfy, given the job I want it to do, which is to tell us whether normativity is doing explanatory work that convention, expectation and preference cannot do for us. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, say whether it is non-negotiable or only a demand of one camp, and keep the distinctions sharp. The cases I want handled, at minimum: the escalator-standing regularity, where the side people stand on differs between cities, no one is harmed if you stand in the middle, and yet strangers glare and mutter — arbitrary content with non-arbitrary force; the phantom norm, where nearly everyone privately thinks the rule is silly and complies only because they think the others approve, which then collapses in a season once that fact becomes public — so a theory must distinguish compliance, acceptance and enforcement, and explain decades of stability and overnight collapse with the same machinery; the undetectable, unenforced violation that most people still won't take, which forces the difference between conventions that are self-policing because deviation costs you (which side of the road, which units) and norms enforced by third parties who lose nothing (queue-jumping, dress, food taboos); and the boundary problem, of who a norm binds, why we think newcomers are bound by it, and why violations by outsiders annoy us more than violations by insiders. It also has to be consistent with what we know empirically: large between-society variation in what people treat as fair and in willingness to punish at cost, the transmission biases that make common or successful variants disproportionately copied, and the fact that norms can die inside a single cohort. It has to say what norms are made of — whether a norm exists when no one accepts it but everyone complies — and it must not collapse normativity into either mere regularity or mere preference. Where you think a pair of requirements cannot both be met, say so explicitly: I suspect the tension is between explaining compliance bottom-up from individual expectations and payoffs, with no irreducible shared representation, and explaining why an undetectable, unpunished violation can still be something one has a reason not to do. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive theories, and where my own list is itself a prejudice. A short closing table scoring the Lewis-style convention account, the social-expectations accounts that add a normative expectation and a conditional preference to conform, and the cultural-evolution-plus-punishment accounts is welcome, but the criteria are the deliverable — don't send me to the literature, I have the syllabus.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the one-sentence version of the specification — and then give the reasoning and the list. No preamble, and don't restate my question back to me.
