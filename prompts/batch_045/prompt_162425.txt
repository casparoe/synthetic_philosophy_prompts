ok so our reading group on emotion is making everyone put a one-page "where I stand" up on the shared doc and then get grilled on it, so I want to run mine past someone before the others do. Here it is, roughly:

(1) Emotions aren't judgments or beliefs — they're more like perceptions of evaluative properties. Fear presents its object as dangerous, grief as a loss, indignation as an offense. I'll call this the perceptual view.
(2) But the perceptual view as I usually hear it undersells the body: an emotion with no felt component at all isn't an emotion. Whatever emotions are, they're essentially something you feel and not just a way the world shows up as mattering. My evidence is just personal — after a bad stretch of sleep my emotions flatten and I stop caring about things I cared about last week, and it doesn't feel like I've changed any view about what matters.
(3) The big selling point of (1) is recalcitrance: I can be scared of what I judge perfectly safe, and that isn't a contradiction in me the way believing p and believing not-p would be. It's like a perceptual illusion — the state says danger, judgment says safe, no irrationality involved.
(4) Except that I also want "you shouldn't be scared of that, it's a garter snake" to be a real criticism, not just a report that my affect and my judgment are out of sync. And I want emotions to be the kind of thing that can be educated: my friend has handled snakes professionally for twenty years, is completely fine with the same species in the lab, and still gets the jolt when one moves through tall grass where a venomous lookalike could be. I want to say she isn't being irrational, that she can't switch the jolt off by deciding to, and that she'd be better off if it faded, and that things like exposure and attention are the kind of thing that could make it fade.
(5) Emotions toward fiction are real emotions, not a separate "quasi-" state: I'm properly afraid of the thing in the game and properly angry at the character who never existed.
(6) Animals and pre-verbal infants have emotions, and the dog's fear of the vacuum cleaner is the same mental kind in the respect that matters, even though the dog has no concept of danger.
(7) Emotions are our main route to knowing about value; someone with no affective life would be badly epistemically deprived about what matters. But I'm not a relativist: I want to say that the outrage people in other times and places felt at an exposed ankle was just wrong, not merely differently calibrated — while also not explaining all that variation away as a difference in their beliefs.

So: is this a package or a pile? What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Which pairs actually bite, ordered by how badly, and for each one what would have to change and what that costs.
