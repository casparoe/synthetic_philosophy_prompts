I run a fortnightly seminar for about eight first- and second-year machine learning PhD students — a whiteboard, seventy-five minutes, no slides, and no preparation expected of anyone — and next week's session is mine. The topic I agreed to cover is the Löbian obstacle: the difficulty a reasoner faces when it wants a general license to trust a successor whose reasoning is at least as strong as its own. The setup I plan to use is an agent that searches for proofs in a system at least as strong as Peano arithmetic and then does whatever the first proof it finds recommends, and that now has to decide whether to hand the remainder of its task to a modified copy of itself: same design, same values, same hardware, searching either for longer or in a slightly stronger theory. What the parent wants is a theorem of the form: for every action-relevant claim X, if the successor proves X then X is true, so I may act on X. Löb's theorem says that such a system proves that conditional for a given X only if it already proves X outright, so the general license is unavailable, and the standard fallback, proving the conditional in a stronger theory, has to be paid for again at every delegation. As I understand it, the obstacle bites even when the successor reasons in exactly the theory the parent reasons in; that the only licenses available force each successor to reason in a strictly weaker theory than its parent; and that this means the chain of delegations can only be continued finitely many times unless the tower of theories bottoms out, because the alternative, letting each successor be stronger, would require the original agent to have been stronger than every later member of the chain, which cannot be arranged from below. Please correct any of that which is wrong before I commit to it at a whiteboard. Two complications in the room: one student has read some blog posts on self-reference in artificial agents and will arrive with half-formed ideas, and a logic postdoc will be visiting, whose presence worries me for two opposite reasons.

What I want from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation. First, the order: what has to be in place before what, and why, given that nobody in the room knows any provability logic. In particular, should I state Löb's theorem as a black box and spend the time on what it does to the delegation decision, or is there a five- or ten-minute gesture at why it holds — a fixed point, a sentence that says of itself that its own provability implies its truth — that will make the result feel inevitable rather than imported, and if so, what exactly must that gesture contain in order to stay honest? Second, the carrying example. Is my parent-and-modified-copy scenario the right one to build the whole session around, and what must it have and lack? I am unsure about one feature: whether the successor should be stronger than the parent or merely faster. Making it stronger invites the question of why the parent was not simply built stronger to begin with; making it merely faster risks making the obstacle look like a fact about the cost of proof search rather than about self-reference. There is also a specific thing I do not want to get wrong: someone will propose that the parent restrict its trust to proofs shorter than some fixed bound and verify those itself, and I do not know whether that escapes the obstacle or merely relocates it.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order I should expect them, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, plus the ones I have not thought of. The ones I anticipate are: that this is just Gödel's second incompleteness theorem about self-consistency proofs under another name; that it is an uncomputability result like the halting problem; that the whole thing dissolves if the agent uses probabilities rather than proofs; that the parent can simply adopt the axiom schema saying that everything it proves is true; that the parent could test the successor on sample problems and trust it inductively; and that the issue is an empirical worry about the successor being corrupted or value-drifted, when it is supposed to bite for a successor that is exactly as trustworthy as the parent. Tell me which of these I should let them form and then break rather than pre-empt, since I suspect at least one is pedagogically useful. Fourth, the point I most want advice on: the student who has read the blog posts will probably raise some move whereby the parent makes the conditional vacuously true, resolving to delegate only if it has not proved that it will delegate, or something in that neighbourhood, and I have only half understood it. Should I raise it myself, where in the session, and what is its honest status: does it dissolve the obstacle, relocate it, or buy only a degenerate form of delegation? Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I am refusing; what to do with the logic postdoc so that they neither hijack the session into modal-logic notation nor sit silently through a version of their own field they find insulting; and one question I can put to the group at the end that would show me they have grasped the obstacle as a structural fact about delegation rather than as a general skeptical worry about trusting anything.
