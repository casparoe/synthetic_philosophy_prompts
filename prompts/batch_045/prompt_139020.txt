I got made redundant in March after eleven years as an in-house translator, and the part I can't stop chewing on is this: the clients now put our old jobs through a model and pay two freelancers a third of our rate to clean up the output. The model was trained on translations written by people like me. So my past work is literally the thing that replaced me.

Two of my friends read this in opposite Marxist directions. One says Marx would have been thrilled — automation is precisely what busts capitalism open, machines are the material precondition for a society where nobody has to sell their hours, and the only question is who owns them. The other says he'd talk about the reserve army: capital doesn't automate to liberate anyone, it automates to discipline whoever is left, and the result is more precarity, not less.

Where I get stuck is that none of the specifics have a 19th-century analogue. It's a machine that does the sort of thing he thought distinguished the worst architect from the best bee. It doesn't wear out, and it can be copied for nothing, so the link between the labour that went into it and it's price has basically come apart. It's built out of the recorded work of the people it displaces. And the political demand that's actually on the table — some kind of dividend or payout for training data — looks like compensation rather than ownership.

So: which of my friends is closer to what Marx would definately have said? Would he have backed a data dividend or called it a bribe to keep us quiet? Is there anything here worth fighting for beyond who owns the weights — and if he thought the fight was about ownership, what would that even mean for something that's non-rival and infinitely copyable?
