hey — I'm putting together the eval harness for a reasoning system (a research-assistant-ish thing with a big store of facts that does multi-step inference over them), and I keep wanting to say things like "it knows p" and "it does not know q, even though q follows from what it has stored." I've ben reading around the problem of logical omniscience and everyone just picks a formalism and defends it, so before I pick one I want the spec instead.

Write me the desiderata that any account of an agent's logical knowledge has to meet to be good enough for my purpose. The purpose matters, so here it is precisely: I need knowledge ascriptions to a system in order to (1) decide whether to trust an output without re-deriving it, (2) decide whether to spend more compute before answering, (3) decide when the honest thing to say is "I don't know," and (4) diagnose an error as an error rather than as a difference of opinion. If a requirement only matters for some other purpose — pure semantics, epistemic logic as mathematics, normative epistemology for humans — say so and demote it.

For each requirement, give the case that motivates it and why that case is a requirement rather than a preference. Please handle these at minimum, with better ones if you have them:

- the warehouse case: 10,000 stored facts, q follows by three joins, nothing has ever derived q. Any account that says the system already knows q is useless to me.
- the one-step oversight: the agent has every premise active, q follows in one obvious step, and it still doesn't have q — e.g. a player who misses a mate in one. I want to be able to say it *should* have known without saying it *did*, so whatever I'm defining has to leave room for a normative gap.
- the 200-variable tautology versus the three-variable one: both are guaranteed by logic, only one is checkable by anything I can build.
- the short-proof-hard-theorem case: some consequences follow by a proof of a dozen lines that nobody finds for three centuries. So "distance" between what's known and what follows cannot just be proof length or derivation depth — tell me what else the account has to be sensitive to (search shape? whether the trick is in the agent's repertoire? something else?).
- the certificate gap: a subsystem reliably outputs the right answer to a class of questions but can't produce a proof or witness for any individual one. Reliability without a certificate — is that knowledge for my purposes, and why or why not.
- the inconsistent spreadsheet: one contradictory pair buried among thousands of entries. The ascription must not explode; the system does not thereby count as knowing everything.
- the before/after case: same agent, same store, before and after it reads a proof. What exactly changed — the set of things known, the agent's access to them, or only what I'm willing to attribute? And the harder version: an agent whose own inference rules aren't classical (defaults, defeasible steps, a paraconsistent patch), so the theorist's consequence relation and the agent's come apart. Which one is the account even talking about.
- the cutoff problem: any fixed resource budget gives a cliff where one more second of compute flips the ascription from false to true. Does the account have to avoid the cliff, explain why the cliff is harmless, or go graded?

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste. In particular I suspect two of my own are fake and I want you to argue me into or out of them: that the ascription has to be computable/tractable (maybe I only need it well-defined, not checkable by me), and that it has to keep a flat "knows" operator rather than degrees (maybe degrees are fine and I can threshold them for the trust/spend-compute decisions).

Then the part I actually care about: which subset of the surviving requirements can't be jointly satisfied? My hunch is that these three can't all hold at once — closure under single obvious steps, non-closure for longer chains, and no arbitrary cutoff — and that you buy your way out only by going graded or by letting an interest/context parameter into the ascription. If that's not the real inconsistent triad, tell me which one is, and be explicit about what has to be dropped.

Finally, one or two lines each on how the standard moves do against your list — non-normal/impossible worlds, syntactic or awareness-style accounts, implicit-versus-explicit belief with resource bounds, and straight probabilism — and name the single requirement that eliminates the most of them. Keep that part short; the criteria are the deliverable, not the scorecard. A table is fine.
