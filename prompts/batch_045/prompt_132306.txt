Here is the answer I have to grade:

Searle's target is strong AI, the claim that a computer running the right program with the right inputs and outputs would thereby have a mind in the same sense that human beings have minds. The argument is essentially deductive. First, a computer program is formal: it specifies operations on symbols purely in terms of their shapes, not their meanings. Second, understanding a language requires semantics, i.e. meaning, and not just the shuffling of symbols. Third, therefore, implementing a program cannot by itself be sufficient for understanding. The point of the room is to isolate the formal element so that we can see it for what it is. The man in the room has exactly what any digital computer has, so if he does not understand Chinese on that basis, no digital computer understands it on that basis either. This is not the claim that machines are useless or that a machine could never behave intelligently. Searle is happy to grant that the room passes the test and is a perfect simulation; his point is that simulating understanding is not the same as having it.

The systems reply says that the man is the wrong unit to look at. The man is only one component of a bigger system that also contains the rulebook, the scratch paper and the filing cabinets, and it is the whole system that understands Chinese even though no individual part does. Searle's response is the internalization move. Suppose the man memorizes the entire rulebook and all the stored symbols and does the whole computation in his head, standing in a field with no equipment at all. Now the system and the man are one and the same thing, and there is still obviously no understanding of Chinese — just a man who has memorized a huge number of uninterpreted squiggles and rules for moving them around. If the system still does not understand when it is all packed into a single head, then adding more parts that are themselves nothing but symbol manipulators cannot manufacture understanding either. More syntax cannot get you semantics.

The robot reply concedes that pure symbol manipulation is not enough and says that what is missing is a causal connection to the world. Put the program in a robot with cameras and microphones so that its symbols get hooked up to the things they are about — the symbol for apple ends up causally linked to actual apples — and let native speakers train it up over years by correcting it. Searle's answer is that this only adds more syntax. What the robot receives through its sensors is more input, which is to say more formal symbols, still processed by formal rules. And the causal relations it has to apples are nothing special: photocells and bathroom scales have causal relations to apples without understanding anything. So grounding the symbols in perception does not add understanding; it just adds another layer of formal processing with the same problem.

This is why Searle's actual conclusion is that only biological brains can understand anything. Understanding requires the specific causal powers of the brain, and since silicon and transistors do not have those powers, no computer built out of them could ever understand Chinese, however it is programmed or trained. ROBO-ROOM is a computer, so the argument goes through against it. There is one respect, however, in which ROBO-ROOM really is different from the original room, and it is decisive. In the original room, the instructions were written down by a programmer who already understood Chinese, and the symbols had whatever meaning they had only because it trickled down from the programmer's understanding. In ROBO-ROOM nobody wrote the rules at all; the network learned them from a huge pile of data on its own. Searle's argument works only against systems whose rules are hand-coded and can be read off by a programmer, so it does not apply to ROBO-ROOM at all. And the fact that ROBO-ROOM passes a hard test after years of correction is at least some evidence that it understands.
