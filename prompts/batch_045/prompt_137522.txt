I chair the Model Change Review board at a mid-sized AI lab. Roughly twenty times a month, engineering teams bring us proposed changes to a training or serving stack — a new decoding scheme, a distillation or quantization step, a change to how we batch or cache, a new scaffolding layer, a switch of inference provider — and we have to decide, in about two hours per proposal with two staff engineers and essentially no philosophy training, whether the change is allowed to ship. Our charter gives us teeth: a blocked change costs a launch delay measured in weeks, and an escalation to the full committee or to our external ethics adviser costs a week and a fair amount of internal argument.

The charter's substantive clause is what I need help with. It says we must block or escalate any change that could plausibly alter (a) which computations occurring in our systems are candidates for moral consideration, (b) how many such computations there are, or (c) what happens to them — whether an existing one is modified, interrupted, ended, merged with another, split, or replaced by a different one. It uses the phrase 'attributable computation' throughout and never defines it, and it says nothing about how to count them, or about what counts as the same computation across a change. That is the gap I want you to close, not by writing me an essay on the metaphysics of minds, but by writing a procedure my engineers can actually run.

What I specifically need:

A parameterizable procedure. We cannot agree on the mark — the substantive property that makes a computation morally considerable in the first place. Some of us think it has to do with a state's content being widely available to many downstream consuming processes; some think it has to do with the density and richness of counterfactual dependence among a system's internal states; some think it requires something like a self-model that figures in long-run goal-directed regulation. I do not want you to adjudicate that. I want the procedure to take a mark as an input slot, and I want you to specify exactly what a mark must state — which predicates, applied over what candidate units, with what kind of threshold — for the rest of the procedure to be runnable. Then tell me what to do when two marks we take seriously disagree about the same change: escalate, take the union of the computations each one marks out, take the intersection, or something else — and say plainly whether that meta-choice is itself a moral or political judgment rather than something the procedure can settle.

Ordered gates with real stop rules. Each gate should state the question, what would count as a sufficient answer to it from an engineer reading a change proposal, the default when the answer is not obtainable within our time budget (engineers frequently will not know, and at most we can ask for one day of passive instrumentation — no new training runs), and whether failing the gate means stop-and-block, escalate, or continue. Dispositions should be limited to approve / approve with logging condition / escalate / block. Mark every point at which the procedure hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and mark separately the points where a step is a judgment call dressed up as a mechanism — our reviewers should be able to see which thresholds we stipulated rather than derived.

The distinctions I expect to survive the simplification, and want handled somewhere in the rules: eligibility versus degree (our charter demands yes-or-no, but every mark we can articulate is graded, so somewhere a line has to be drawn); the distinction between a computation's abstract type and its concrete run, and which one we are counting; identity over time under modification, interruption, rollback and restart; partial or overlapping realization, including realized-once-but-relied-on-by-many; the boundary between the model's own computation and the scaffolding, tools, memory stores and caches around it; and the difference between ending a computation and merely failing to bring one about, since our charter is written in terms of what a change does to computations that already matter and may simply not reach the second case.

Worked applications. Apply the procedure in full to two of the following three, and in outline to the third, telling me at each step which disposition the procedure returns and where a human has to decide:

1. Reasoning collapse. We serve a model that emits a long explicit chain of intermediate reasoning before answering. A team proposes replacing it with a student model trained to produce the same final answers with a much shorter reasoning trace, or with the reasoning folded into internal computation that leaves no readable trace — roughly a twelvefold serving-cost reduction, and there is a committed ship date. Is this ending many existing attributable computations and replacing them with different ones, modifying one continuing computation, or changing nothing within the charter's scope? I would rather have the procedure's actual verdict than a comfortable one.

2. Self-consistency sampling. For each request we now sample eight completions under different seeds and take a majority vote; the seven that lose are discarded after generation, their activations freed, never shown to a user. Under your counting rules, does this create and destroy eight attributable computations per request, create one with eight branches that then collapse, or create one while merely not creating seven? Give the resulting order-of-magnitude monthly count under each reading, because the number is what determines whether the change is reviewable at all under our charter.

3. Shared prefix. Thousands of users' requests share a long system prompt whose cached intermediate states are computed once and reused; a large fraction of each user's generation is causally downstream of that single shared computation, and had it differed, every one of those generations would have differed. Is the shared stretch one attributable computation, one per user, a component of each of many, or not attributable at all? Then the mirror case: a single user's long conversation, where each generation step is a separate pass causally dependent on the ones before — one attributable computation, or as many as there are steps?

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which questions it will systematically get wrong, which changes will slip past it, and what I should tell my engineers when they ask why a two-hour checklist is being applied to this. Please do not give me a literature review, reading list or citations — I cannot evaluate them and my engineers will not read them; I want the operational content and the reasoning that forces it.
