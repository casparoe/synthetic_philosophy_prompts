I'm in a small fortnightly reading group on collective decision-making (six of us, mostly philosophy grads plus one economist) and last term we spent a session on the unilateralist's curse — the thing where, if any one of a group can trigger some initiative single-handed, the person who ends up triggering it is disproportionately likely to be the one who's wrong that it's a good idea. It stuck with me partly because I'm one of about thirty volunteers who each run a node of something called the Fen Mirror: we hold full copies of a corpus of crop-pathogen genome sequences and lab protocols that got pulled from a university repository after a dual-use complaint, and any one of us can unilaterally push the whole thing back into the public index. Most of us think republishing is probably right — researchers in countries without subscriptions are stuffed without it, and the complaint looked flimsy — but there's a nasty tail: one protocol getting misused, or a legal crackdown that takes the network down and kills the half-dozen other things we host. Our status page shows which nodes are live and whether anyone has pushed, but not why a given node hasn't; half the operators are students whose laptops are asleep half the time. Anyway, I tried to write the curse out properly over the weekend so I could work out what it actually tells me to do, and I ended up with something that clearly proves far too much, and I cant find the joint.

(1) If any one of N agents can bring about an outcome O on their own, and whether O is good is uncertain with a spread-out downside, then being the one who brings it about is itself evidence that my estimate sits at the optimistic end of the spread — so the expected value of O conditional on my action's being the one that decides it can be negative even when its unconditional expected value is positive. (2) Nothing in (1) depends on what the action actually is. All it needs is: I can bring O about by myself; whether O is good is uncertain with a fat downside; and other people could have brought it about too and haven't. (3) For almost anything I'm ever tempted to do, the same outcome was open to plenty of other people who didn't do it — nobody else wrote the letter, made the introduction, put up the money, left the review, said the thing. (4) And almost anything, if you trace the consequences out far enough, has some chance of a catastrophic tail — the letter gets screenshotted, the introduction goes badly, the money does harm somewhere downstream. (5) So for almost any action, the expected value conditional on its making a difference is negative. (6) If the value conditional on making a difference is negative, don't do it. (7) So I should do almost nothing. And since (1) covers spoiling as well as undertaking, I also shouldn't deliberately refrain from things either — I'd need the group's permission to breathe. Which is definately false.

A friend in the group says the rot is in (6): she says I should be maximising unconditional expected value, and that conditioning on my being the decisive one is a selection artefact I shouldn't be acting on. But I don't see how that rescues anything — if the only worlds my action changes are the ones where nobody else did it, then the conditional number looks like precisely the relevant one, and the unconditional number just averages in worlds where I twiddle my thumbs. I'd much rather keep (1) than (7): the Mirror case genuinely frightens me and I don't want to toss out the bit that made me take the curse seriously in the first place. If forced I'd probably drop (4), but I can't say why (4) is worse than (2). So: which step do you reject, why did it look solid when I wrote it down, and what does each of the other options cost me — especially for the Mirror, where I actually have to decide something by the end of the month?
