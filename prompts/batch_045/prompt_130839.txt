So I got into a long argument with a friend last night about the 2018 Uber self-driving fatality in Tempe and we got nowhere, so I want a second opinion.

The facts as I remember them (correct me gently if I have any wrong): it's just before 10pm in March, a modified Volvo XC90 running Uber's prototype automated driving system is going north on Mill Avenue at somewhere in the low-to-mid 40s mph, a woman starts crossing outside the marked crosswalk pushing a bicycle loaded with shopping bags. The system detects something roughly 5.6 seconds before impact but never classifies it as a pedestrian — I think the classification goes unknown object, then vehicle, then bicycle, and each reclassification wipes the tracked history so the predicted path gets restarted — and by the time it decides a collision is imminent, it's too late. Emergency braking for collision mitigation was effectively switched off by design, with the safety driver expected to intervene; the safety driver was looking at her phone and looked up about a second before impact. The NTSB's probable cause was the operator's distraction, with Uber's safety culture and risk procedures as contributing factors. I also vaguely rememeber something about the design not really accounting for people crossing outside crosswalks, but I might be making that up.

My friend's claim: this is a textbook frame problem, and it vindicates the pessimistic line that relevance just can't be captured by an explicit representational system — the world outran the frame. My claim: this isn't the frame problem at all, it's the frame problem run backwards. The frame was deliberately shrunk: classification has to settle before action, hard braking was suppressed because of false positives, weird cases were out of scope. That's a system whose designers *solved* the relevance explosion by making the frame small on purpose and handing the leftovers to a human, and the human wasn't looking. Maybe we're both wrong and the real thing on display is the inverse problem — not 'what do I keep fixed when I act' but 'how do I stop the frame from filling up with junk'.

What I actually want:

1. A disciplined mapping. Who or what is the agent that holds a frame here — the driving system, the system-plus-driver pair, or Uber as an organization? What are its frame axioms, in the boring sense: what did it treat as staying the same across an action? Object classes, road geometry, where crosswalks are, how pedestrians move, what counts as a thing worth braking for. Which of those broke, and which broke in a way that mattered?

2. Does the frame-churn detail — reclassification resetting the tracked history — do any real work, or is it an incidental bug? My friend calls the general phenomenon 're-framing cost' and thinks it's the heart of the case: a system that re-asks what-it's-looking-at every fraction of a second destroys the very accumulated state that made the object relevant. Is that a genuine distinction with teeth, or just a name for a bug?

3. Is the interesting failure that the frame was too small (person-crossing-outside-a-crosswalk isn't in the ontology) or that the frame was uncommitting (detect but don't act until classification settles)? Those strike me as opposite diagnoses, and I can't tell which one the frame problem is even about.

4. What would solving it here look like? Piling on more frame axioms in a monotonic logic, nonmonotonic or default reasoning, the situated/embodied 'don't represent the world, just cope with it' move, or some relevance-realization / precision-weighting story where the system decides on the fly what matters. Which of those would actually have prevented this specific crash, and which just redescribe the failure in fancier words?

5. The direction I care about most: what does the case do to the theory? Does it support the pessimistic reading, support the boring reading (an engineering hazard that better world models simply fix), or show that the classic formulation is silent about something load-bearing — delegation (the frame was outsourced to a human who wasn't framed in), timing (frames have a refresh rate and a cost), organizations, and the gap between 'this is relevant' and 'I am permitted to act on it'?

Please don't spend your first three paragraphs explaining what the frame problem is; I can recite that part. And be honest about where it goes quiet on this case instead of forcing it to speak — if the truthful answer is 'this is a safety-culture failure with a philosophy gloss', I'd rather hear that.
