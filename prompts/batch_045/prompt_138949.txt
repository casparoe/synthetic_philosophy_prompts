I came to philosophy late, after a career in mineralogy, and I have become stuck on a phrase that does a great deal of work in the reading group I attend: that the sciences carve nature at its joints. It was deployed against me last week. I had observed that jade turned out, on analysis, to be two quite different minerals, and that this seemed to me to tell us something about how even respectable scientific categories can sit awkwardly on the world; I was told that it shows only that mineralogy had not yet located the real joints, and that the joints are there whether or not we find them. I cannot tell what claim the slogan is making in that reply, or whether it is strong enough to bear the weight put on it.

Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses the slogan might express, ordered from the near-trivial to the metaphysically demanding: for instance, a reading on which joint-carving merely means supporting induction, counterfactuals and explanation; one on which it means tracking clusters of causally co-operating properties; one on which it means being the partition that any method of investigation not sharing assumptions with the others would converge on, a test I have been entertaining myself; one on which the joints are the divisions that figure in exceptionless laws; one on which there is a single uniquely privileged taxonomy of the world; and a pluralist reading on which many cross-cutting partitions are equally legitimate. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and — this is what I mainly want — whether it would actually license my interlocutor's move of treating every failed category as our error rather than as evidence about the world's structure.

I would be grateful for a numbered list, one or two sentences of verdict per entry, and not more than 600 words in total; no references or suggested reading, please.
