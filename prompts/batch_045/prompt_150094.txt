Putting together my 'where I stand' notes for a reading group on the direction of time and I want someone to poke at them before I turn up. Here's the package:

(1) There's no arrow in the fundamental laws — every temporal asymmetry we care about comes from the entropy gradient.
(2) The gradient gets explained by a very low-entropy boundary condition at one temporal end of the universe plus ordinary stat-mech reasoning, nothing else.
(3) That boundary condition is a fine place to stop explaining; I don't think 'but why was it low?' needs an answer.
(4) Memory, records, and our sense that we can act on the future are all explained by (2), so nothing extra is needed for those.
(5) But I do want a real, non-epistemic asymmetry for deliberation. My own coinage, deliberation-dependence: what I can deliberate about is what counterfactually depends on what I decide, and I want that to be primitive rather than a shadow of the fact that I know less about the future.
(6) I rely on a typicality principle — reason as a random observer among those in your epistemic situation — which is how I wave off Boltzmann brains.
(7) I'm not bothered by the weak interaction failing to be T-symmetric; that feels like a red herring here.

Which of these actually fight each other? I genuinely can't tell whether (5) is just a less pretentious way of saying (4) or a straight contradiction of (1), and someone in the group mutters that (4) and (6) together pull the rug out from under (2), which I half see but can't spell out. Rank whatever tensions you find by how bad they are, say for each what I'd have to drop or weaken and what that costs me, and flag any that only look like tensions. I don't want a reading list — I want to know which one of my commitments is doing the damage.
