I have retired after a career in experimental physics (condensed matter, mostly, with a great deal of data analysis that I now realise was Bayesian without my having known the word) and I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group in the philosophy department on probability, induction and related matters. Last week we spent the whole session on Bertrand's chord problem from his 1889 probability textbook and got precisely nowhere, and I would like to arrive next week with something better than a restatement of the dispute. One thing I worked out on the train home, which I would like you to use as a constraint rather than answer directly: chord length is a monotone function of the distance d from the centre to the chord's midpoint, and the threshold in Bertrand's question (chord longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, i.e. longer than R times the square root of three) is exactly d = R/2. So the probability asked for is just the probability that d falls below R/2, and by choosing the distribution of d freely one can obtain any number in the interval (0,1) at all. The famous three answers one third, one half and one quarter are three arbitrary points in a continuum, not three rival solutions to a well-posed question, and it seems to me that any treatment which presents them as a menu of equal-status candidates has already misdescribed what is going on. What I want from you is the specification, not the answer.

Please write me the list of requirements that any adequate treatment of this family of problems has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and with each marked as either non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Among the candidates I would want you to rule on, explicitly: that the treatment must respect the distinction between chance and credence, so that when a genuine randomising apparatus is specified there is a fact of the matter and indifference has nothing to say, while when the chord is already fixed and merely unknown to us the burden falls entirely on indifference; that it must say which symmetry group is privileged and why, since rotational and reflectional symmetry about the circle's own centre leaves the answer completely open, and it is only when one adds invariance under translations of the circle relative to whatever is generating chords (roughly Jaynes's 1973 argument) that a unique distribution is picked out; that it must generalise beyond geometry to the other places indifference fails on continuous domains and to the finite cases such as dice where it plainly does not; and that it must be usable by a working scientist who has to put a distribution on a continuous parameter, where the maximum entropy recipe with a flat reference measure is famously coordinate-dependent. Tell me which pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, since I suspect the interesting result is in the list itself, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates.

To keep it honest, here is a case I constructed for the group which I do not think anyone has seen, and I would like your arithmetic checked as well as your verdict. Move the problem up one dimension: a ball of radius R, a chord of the ball chosen at random, and the question is the probability that the chord's midpoint lies within R/2 of the centre. As I compute it, four natural selection methods give three different answers: two independent uniform points on the surface, and the rigid-motion-invariant measure on lines conditioned on hitting the ball, both give the probability (c/R) squared at threshold c, hence one quarter at c = R/2; a midpoint uniform in the volume gives (c/R) cubed, hence one eighth; and a midpoint uniform along a randomly chosen radius gives c/R, hence one half. The coincidence I find striking is that in the plane the invariance argument lands on the radial method, whereas in the ball it lands instead on the two-random-surface-points method, so the invariance defence cannot be supported by pointing at whichever textbook method one likes. Say whether I have this right, and say what the coincidence shows. Alongside it I want two hard cases placed on the list: a chord generator that is known to be rotationally symmetric about the centre but whose radial profile is unknown, or equivalently a hoop whose position relative to a fixed throwing rig is unknown, where I suspect the translation-invariance demand is really an indifference assumption smuggled in; and the question whether specifying a finite measurement precision on chord length does anything to dissolve the original problem. Finish with a brief scorecard, no more than a paragraph each, of the three or four broad responses you see in the literature described as positions rather than as authors, and please give me no bibliography, citations or suggested reading, since I could not follow them up and would have to take them on trust.
