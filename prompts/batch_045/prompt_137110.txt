The specific thing I would like explained is this. Imagine two valleys on opposite banks of a river: same climate, same soil, same language and religion. In 1890 a colonial administration happens to build its railway station in the northern valley rather than the southern one, purely because the northern bank was a few metres flatter. A century later the northern valley has secure property rights, banks and a literate population, and the southern valley does not. I think this kind of comparison is what people call a natural experiment, and I think the worry about what it tells us about other places is called external validity — please correct me if I have those the wrong way round.

What I want to know is how far such a comparison really takes us. Even if it is clean, could its effect simply wash out, or even reverse, over two hundred years? The places that were richest in 1500 are often not the richest today, which makes me doubt that any single fixed cause can be the whole story. And are "institutions", "geography" and "culture" genuinely separate explanations at all? Good institutions look to me like one way of describing a culture, and both look like things that geography makes easier or harder at a given level of technology. Is that a real problem with the debate, or have I just confused myself?
