Long time lurker, first time poster. I've been chewing on al-Ghazālī's thing about causation for ages now and I keep going round in cirlces because I think there are two different claims getting run together, and I can't work out whether the difference does any work.

Set up as I understand it: he denies that there's any necessary connection between what we call a cause and what we call its effect. Fire touching cotton doesn't *have to* burn it, the connection is just something God has set up as a habit, and that's what leaves room for the miracle stories (the fire not burning Abraham, that sort of thing). Fine. But I want to separate out:

(1) Call this "modal inertness": no cause *necessitates* its effect. The conjunction is contingent all the way down.
(2) Call this "productive inertness": no created thing *brings anything about* at all. God is the only agent; creatures are occasions, not producers.

Now the bit I made up and can't find anyone discussing, which is why I'm asking here rather than googling. Imagine God, at creation, writes down a complete mapping from total world-state at one instant to total world-state at the next — or just a whole timeline, same difference — and then never does anything again. Call it the "scripted-habit" model. Every stage follows from the script, not from the previous stage's own powers; but there's also no moment-by-moment divine willing, no sustained re-creation, nothing that looks like ongoing action.

Things I'd like explained, argued out rather than reported:

- Is the scripted-habit model occasionalism or not? If your answer is "depends how you define occasionalism,", say what's actually at stake in where you draw the line. I want to know which definition is load-bearing for the arguments, not which one is standard.
- Which of (1) and (2) does the miracle argument actually need? Suppose he only had (1). Couldn't he already say the fire's not burning Abraham violates nothing, so (2) is doing extra work for free? Or does (1) leave a residue — don't we still want to say the fire was disposed to burn and got overridden?
- On (2), every event is equally a direct divine act. What work is the word "miracle" doing then? Can he keep a principled ordinary-vs-miraculous distinction, or does a miracle just become an event that's rare relative to our inductively learned expectation of God's policy — i.e. a regularity in the policy rather than a break in it? Is that basically a Humean regularity story about laws one level up, or is the analogy misleading? (I keep thinking of it as a prior over God's policy and I suspect that's me forcing a map/territory thing that doesn't belong.)
- In the interventionist sense of causation — set X, see if Y moves — what happens to intervention under (2)? If the fire isn't doing anything, "intervene on the fire" seems to either collapse or become shorthand for something about the script. Does that mean the causal-graph picture survives occasionalism as a mere compression of the policy, and if so can an occasionalist keep talking causally the way we keep talking agentially about a thermostat?

Steelman whichever of these you don't instinctively buy, and please flag which of my questions is really a verbal dispute.
