I work as a backend engineer and was recently put on my employer's five-person release-review board, which decides whether we keep using a third-party risk-scoring service in one of our products. Our charter is unusually explicit: the contract gets renewed if and only if two criteria both hold — P, the measured error rate on the validation set is within the agreed tolerance, and Q, the gap in error rates between the two customer segments we score is within the agreed tolerance. Every member accepts that rule, and every member votes consistently with it. At the last review the individual positions were: two members judged P met and Q not met and voted against renewal; one member judged both met and voted to renew; two judged P not met and Q met and voted against renewal. So P carried 3–2, Q carried 3–2, and renewal went down 4–1. Two other members, Ingrid and Marcus, then had an argument in the hallway that I could not referee, and I keep going back and forth myself.

Ingrid's opening move was that the board was charged with making a decision, not with holding a coherent belief set, so it should simply vote on renewal and stop: four people voted no, and that is the outcome. Marcus replied that the four-person majority against renewal is not really a coalition at all, since the two members who think Q fails and the two who think P fails each regard the other pair's reason as mistaken, so no member's actual position is what gets enacted; and he added that under our charter the board has to publish written reasons, and the only reasons it can honestly publish — majorities did endorse both P and Q — logically entail renewal. Ingrid's second move had three parts: first, that there is no group mind, so nothing is more or less rational here, only more or less to our taste; second, that Marcus was wrong that no shared view is enacted, because all four do accept the weaker proposition that at least one of the two criteria fails, which is exactly what a no vote records; and third, that premise-first aggregation is not a method at all but an artifact of which propositions someone chose to call premises — she pointed out that if you instead treat P and not-renew as the voted-on premises, the same five ballots logically yield not-Q, the opposite of what the majority said about Q. Marcus conceded the agenda point but said it is an argument for fixing the agenda in the charter in advance, which is precisely what we have done, since P and Q are the designated criteria and renewal is defined in terms of them. He then turned Ingrid's disjunction point against her: support for a conjunction is hard to assemble and support for its negation is easy, because diffuse disagreement is enough, so a conclusion-first rule is a status-quo machine that will manufacture no votes whenever the decision depends on more than one thing. And he proposed a fix he borrowed from reading about quorums in distributed systems: require each criterion to pass by more than three quarters of the board, since then the sets of members endorsing the criteria must overlap, and everyone in the overlap accepts both criteria and therefore accepts renewal — so the derived decision always has real majority support behind it from members whose reasons actually agree. He said the general requirement is that each of k criteria pass by more than 1 − 1/(2k) of the members, and he half-remembered an Arrow-flavoured impossibility theorem saying that no aggregation rule can have every property you'd naively want.

Ingrid's closing move was that the fix just relocates the problem, because someone still has to decide which propositions count as criteria rather than conclusions, and because the threshold degrades horribly as criteria multiply — with two criteria you need four of five, but with six you would need eleven of twelve — so the rule buys consistency by making action nearly impossible; even in our two-criterion case, she said, it converts a 4–1 landslide against renewal into a 3–2 squeaker for it. Marcus said that was just restating that no rule is perfect and that therefore her original demand — that the board's decision be read off a plain vote on the outcome — had no privilege.

Please adjudicate this move by move: who is right about which specific claim, where each of them overreaches, and what, if anything, both are missing. I would particularly like you to check the arithmetic rather than take it on trust — is the three-quarters threshold right for two criteria, does the 1 − 1/(2k) generalisation actually hold, and is Ingrid right that six criteria would require eleven of twelve? I'd also like you to say whether her agenda-relativity argument is sound as an objection to premise-first aggregation, whether Marcus's fix-the-agenda-in-advance reply actually answers it, and whether her status-quo-bias point survives Marcus's supermajority proposal. Finally: someone told me this setup has a name in philosophy and that there are impossibility results about it, but everything I could find was framed around appellate courts and legal doctrine, which doesn't map cleanly onto a five-person vendor review, so please say whether this really is the same phenomenon, whether Marcus is characterising the impossibility results correctly, and what a board in our position should actually take from them — no references or reading lists, just the substance.
