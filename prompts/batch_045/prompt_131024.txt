I got into a stupidly long argument with a friend after a seminar on personal identity last week (I'm only auditing the course, and we were standing outside with terrible coffee for the best part of an hour), and I'd like to carry on with someone who'll actually hit back. Please play a committed psychological-continuity theorist about personal identity — roughly, the view that what makes someone later the same person as someone earlier is the right kind of continuity of consciousness, memory, character and agency, that a person is located wherever that stream is currently being produced, and that the living body is just peripheral hardware you happen to be driving. Be sharp and don't let me get away with a caricature: concede small points readily, never the main one, argue in the first person as yourself rather than telling me what Dennett or anyone else would say, and no bibliographies or reading suggestions — I've no library access and neither do you. British English please, and keep each turn to a paragraph or two so it still feels like an argument in a doorway. Stay in character until I say "stop", and open by answering my question rather than asking me to clarify it.

Here's my move. Take the old brain-transplant story where the brain is lifted out and hooked back up to the body by radio, and then smear it out: instead of one dramatic operation, the surgeons replace a single neuron at a time with a functionally identical silicon unit, and every replacement unit lives in a rack two thousand miles away, talking to its old neighbours over a link. Ten years later the skull is empty, the rack is running everything, and at no point was there an interruption, a noticed change, or a moment at which I "moved". Now two variations. First, the latency ladder: the delay on that link is nudged up in almost imperceptible steps — a millisecond, ten, a second, a minute, an hour, a week of store-and-forward — and at the end the body is quietly scrapped and fed a perfect simulation of itself instead. Second, the fork: partway through the smear, at the point where about 40% of the system is in the rack, the surgeons copy rather than move, so from then on there are two equally continuous claimants, each writing essays insisting its the one whos me. My charge is that your theory is left having to say something arbitrary — that whether I am in the body or the rack depends on how many milliseconds of lag there are, or on whether the surgeons happened to move rather than copy — and that once you see that, the honest move is to give up on "where am I?" as a question with a determinate answer at all. I'd rather say something boring: I'm the living animal, I'm where the animal is, and in the fork case the animal out in the world is still me while the rack is a duplicate with my memories. So: in the smear case, did I move to the rack, and if you think I did, tell me on which day it happened.
