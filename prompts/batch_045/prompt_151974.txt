I'm in an undergraduate seminar on comparative epistemology, and we've spent the last two sessions on Jain anekāntavāda. As I've come to understand it, the view holds that reality is many-sided, that ordinary claims hold only relative to a standpoint or way of taking something (a naya), and that the properly cautious form of assertion is therefore a conditioned predication — the syādvāda scheme, paradigmatically the sevenfold one, in which a thing can be said to be, not to be, both, indescribable, or various combinations of those depending on the respect in which it's being described. We read the blind men and the elephant as an illustration. Our professor has insisted several times that anekāntavāda is not relativism, but he hasn't yet said what the difference is supposed to consist in.

The line I keep running into, in the readings and in discussion, is "reality is many-sided," or in its fuller form, "every claim is true only from a standpoint, and no single standpoint gives the whole truth."

Here is why this matters to me. In our last session two students got into a genuine disagreement about whether consciousness is a fundamental feature of reality: one defended a physicalist picture, the other argued for something like the Jain position that each living thing has a jīva. They shared all the same empirical evidence and all the same background arguments; they simply weighed the considerations differently and came out on opposite sides. Our TA closed the exchange by saying that, given anekāntavāda, both of them were right — each from his own standpoint. That struck me as dissolving the disagreement rather than settling anything, but I can't say why it's wrong, and I'd like to know whether any careful version of the view really does license what he said.

So I'd like a disambiguation, not an introduction. Please set out the distinct precise theses that "every claim is true only from a standpoint" could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or something false or degenerate — and, crucially, which readings would actually support the TA's move and which would leave the disagreement exactly where it was. In working through the menu, a few things I'd like addressed specifically:

First, the scope of the qualifier. Is "in some respect" operating on the whole claim, so that what's true is something like "relative to standpoint S, p," or is it narrowing the predicate, so that the claim is "x is F in respect R"? Does the choice between these change whether the two students' assertions are genuinely compatible, or merely appear to be?

Second, whether the partial accounts are supposed to be combinable. If the many standpoints can in principle be conjoined into one complete account, that seems close to the platitude that inquiry is perspectival but reality isn't; if they are incommensurable and no single account unifies them, the view looks much stronger. Which reading does the TA need?

Third, the reflexivity problem: is "reality is many-sided" itself only true from a standpoint, and is that a fatal problem or a feature the tradition can absorb?

Fourth, the role of omniscience. Our professor mentioned in passing that the tradition recognizes a standpoint that isn't a standpoint — the omniscient one, which grasps the whole. If such a standpoint exists and delivers the unqualified truth, doesn't the view collapse into the unremarkable claim that human knowledge is limited? And if it doesn't, what exactly is the "whole" that no standpoint captures?

Finally, whether the view has any teeth. If every claim comes out true from some standpoint, is there anything anekāntavāda rules out, and what would count against it? I'm inclined to think the TA's use of it was empty, but I'd rather know which precise version of the claim is doing the work before I say so in section.
