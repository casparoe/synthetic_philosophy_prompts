I have no academic background — twenty-three years in medical billing — but for the past year, since I knew this toast was coming, I have been reading about the thermodynamics of information in the evenings, and I would like you to help me with two things.

My father retires at the end of the month. He has spent forty-one years as the custodian of the magnetic tape archive at a government research laboratory — the vault, as everyone there calls it. He is not a scientist; he was hired as a technician and never left. He never deleted anything. He fought the retention-schedule meetings for four decades and won most of them. For thirty of those years he wrote the vault temperature into a bound logbook by hand, twice a day, in pencil, and the logbooks now fill eleven boxes in his garage.

The dinner is about sixty people: half his colleagues — physicists, storage engineers, the men who ran the refrigeration plant — and half family, including four grandchildren between six and eleven. He dislikes being praised for things he does not think he did, and the one thing he has said in plain words for as long as I can remember is that a bit is not a thought and does not float; it is a physical state in a physical place, and keeping it costs electricity, floor space, and somebody showing up. Nothing is preserved, he would say; things are maintained.

Last month the lab announced that the vault will be decommissioned in fourteen months. Everyone from his work knows; my mother does not, and he has asked me not to raise it. I do not think I can honour both of those at once, and I would rather handle it than pretend.

Three things I need straight before I write, because half the room will know if I fudge them and I would rather be corrected now than from the floor:

1. The version of the demon I keep coming back to is the one-molecule engine: a partition in a box, the demon notes which side the molecule is on, and gets roughly kT ln 2 of work out of the cycle. The standard exorcism I keep finding says the cost does not come from finding out, it comes from clearing the demon's memory — one bit reset, the same kT ln 2 paid back. So I invented a demon that never clears anything: it writes each result to a tape that grows by one bit per cycle and keeps every one. As far as I can tell this demon runs at a profit indefinitely and the cost is merely deferred. Is that right, and if the cost is deferred, deferred onto what exactly — the tape's own upkeep, the unavailability of an infinite tape, or something else? I would also like to know whether the measurement itself is genuinely free: I have read both that the demon must pay to see the molecule and that seeing can in principle be done reversibly, and I cannot tell which of those is the settled answer.

2. My brother-in-law's argument, made at two family dinners: run the vault colder. The Landauer cost per bit is proportional to temperature, he says, so an archive at a few kelvin is a hundred-odd times cheaper per bit, and you can pay for the refrigeration out of the savings and keep the difference. I think this is a perpetual motion machine with extra steps. My attempt at the reason: erasing a bit at the cold temperature dumps kT ln 2 of heat into the cold reservoir, and pumping that heat out to the room costs at least that heat times the quantity room temperature divided by cold temperature, minus one — which works out to k ln 2 times the gap between the two temperatures, so that dissipation plus pumping totals k ln 2 times room temperature, exactly what you would have paid by erasing at room temperature in the first place, and the cooling buys nothing at all. Is that right? Is the cancellation exact or only an inequality one way? Where does his version fail if you let him hand-wave the refrigerator?

3. The one that decides what I can actually say on stage. My whole plan was going to be: every day for forty-one years he paid the cost of keeping those bits, and paying a cost is what love looks like. Now I doubt the premise. Is it true that a bit sitting untouched on a tape costs nothing at all — that the bound applies to erasing and overwriting, not to leaving a record alone — and that the real cost is episodic, arriving only when somebody reads it, checks it, copies it onto new stock, or repairs the errors that have quietly accumulated? If so, the honest sentence is not that he paid daily but that the tape was going quietly and for free the whole time and he kept showing up to pay in lumps; which I think is both truer and harder to say kindly. Tell me which version a physicist in that room would accept.

Then the toast itself: four to five minutes, no equations, nothing more technical than the word bit, and nothing that requires the grandchildren to be told what entropy is. It cannot say his life's work is safe, or that nothing is ever really lost, or that he can now wipe the slate clean — all three are things he would consider false, and the last one I take to be false for a reason I would like confirmed: deletion is a physical operation with the same kind of price tag, not an absolution. It has to give my mother something without telling her the vault is closing, and give his colleagues something without becoming a lecture. If the resolution I sketched above — we will keep paying, and the paying is the tribute — is itself just sentiment with a thermodynamic vocabulary stapled to it, say so and give me a better one. Flag any line in the toast you write that one of his colleagues would wince at, and give me the version they would not.
