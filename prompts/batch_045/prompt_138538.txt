I write historical fiction, and I have been asked to run a ninety-minute workshop at a writers' festival next month. Twelve to fifteen participants, all adults, all novelists or would-be novelists with a project set in the past; a room with a whiteboard and tables, no other materials. I want to build the session around Braudel's distinction between the nearly immobile history of geography, climate, and inherited social structure and the short, loud history of events and individuals. I want them to arrive at it by doing something, not by being told.

Design the exercise for me.

Constraints on the design:

- The phrase longue durée must not be spoken in the first seventy-five minutes, and there must be no framing lecture. Anything they are going to understand should be built into the rules, the roles, or the scoring.
- I do not want the exercise to reward arcane knowledge. Invent the setting — a coastline, a river delta, a gap in a range of hills, a crop, an inheritance custom, a route that exists only because of where the water and the prevailing wind happen to be. The structures embedded in it should be of the kind Braudel actually cared about, but nobody should be able to win by knowing what grain cost in a given decade.
- Participants are writers, so short writing is allowed, but no burst longer than three minutes and nobody should have to read aloud more than two sentences.

What I would like the rules to force: that a vivid individual's decision inside a single scene has almost no power to alter what happens over the following three hundred years, while some dumb, unchosen feature of the ground — a silting harbour, a wind that blows one way, a soil that will grow only one grain — quietly settles who eats, who leaves, and who inherits; and that the participants themselves will keep trying to smuggle agency back in through a great man or a decisive battle, because that is what a plot is made of.

Please give me: the materials; the roles or stations and how participants rotate through them; a timeline in minutes; a scoring or elimination mechanic that needs no money and can be tallied on the whiteboard; the moment in the session where the frustration is meant to peak and what I should do if it does not; and a debrief that maps specific things that will have happened in the room onto the parts of the distinction — the near-immobile structure, the intermediate rhythm of a generation or a price cycle, and the event that is real but whose significance is lent to it. I want them to leave not with a definition but with a craft problem: how to render a structure as a constraint on choice rather than as décor.

Then be frank about two things. First, what will predictably go wrong: the player who refuses to play and narrates a siege instead; the group that colludes so that every event matters; the participant who concludes the exercise is about setting and walks out with a nicer description of a harbour. Second, and more important to me: what the exercise cannot reproduce. My worry is that the longue durée is precisely what no character can perceive from inside a single lifetime, so the feature that makes it true is also the feature that makes it undramatizable. Tell me what I will have to supply in the last ten minutes that the game could not, and how to supply it without the session collapsing back into a lecture.
