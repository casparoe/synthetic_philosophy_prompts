Last night I argued for hours with a friend about what the units of selection really are, and we ended up just restating our positions. I want to try a different route: here are the verdicts I actually hold, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to — and, honestly, which of them I should abandon.

1. A mouse segregation-distorter allele that disables sperm carrying its rival and so is transmitted to nearly all of a heterozygote's offspring, even though homozygotes are non-viable. I am sure this is selection at the gene level; no story about organisms or groups seems to be doing any work.
2. Sterile workers in a eusocial wasp colony, where haplodiploidy makes workers more related to their sisters than to their own offspring. I want to say kin selection, and I resist my friend's claim that this is really colony-level selection. The colony buds off daughter colonies and looks for all the world like a reproducing unit, but I still don't want to call it group selection.
3. Transposable elements that copy themselves through a genome while lowering the host's fitness. Gene-level, straightforwardly — and note that here the copying happens inside a single individual's lineage.
4. A case I invented to settle the argument: colonial beetles in which four foundresses, drawn at random from the whole population, start a nest each season. Carriers of a restraint allele lay half as many eggs, but their nests survive and bud off daughter nests at twice the rate. Relatedness within nests is essentially zero. Here I am confident it is group selection, and I don't think it can be rewritten as kin selection.
5. The same beetle case, except that nests are founded by sisters, so within-nest relatedness is roughly a half. Same allele, same causal effect on nest survival. Now I find myself wanting to say kin selection and not group selection, even though nothing about the mechanism has changed except who ends up nesting together.
6. Virulence in a virus whose vector needs the host alive: within a host, faster replication wins; between hosts, intermediate virulence wins. I am happy to say selection is going on at both levels here, and I don't think there is a single real one.
7. The case I keep going back and forth on: a body-plan feature in a plankton lineage that lowers that lineage's extinction rate without making any individual fitter than its conspecifics. Is that selection at a level at all, or something else wearing selection's clothes?

My friend's line throughout was that this is all one bookkeeping scheme — partition the change into within-group and between-group components, and the talk of levels is optional decoration, so there is no further fact about which level selection acts at. Case 5 makes me doubt myself; cases 1 and 4 make me doubt him.

My own stab at a principle: an entity is a unit of selection only if it is a unit of multiplication with heredity, and the trait's fitness effect runs causally through that entity's own reproductive output rather than by summation over its parts. I already suspect that clause misfires on case 1.

So: state a principle, or a short list of candidates, precisely enough that I could apply it myself; say which of my cases motivates each clause; tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up; and tell me what it says about case 7 and about some case I haven't listed that would discriminate between your principle and my friend's.
