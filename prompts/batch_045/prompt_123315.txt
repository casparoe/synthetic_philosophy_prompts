I am writing a long feature, for a magazine that covers science and ideas, about how a system considerably cleverer than its readers ought to speak to them, and I have got to the stage where a survey of positions is useless to me and a number is not. Please answer in British English, and please do not give me sources, footnotes, a reading list, or attributions: I cannot check references from where I am working, my editor will cut them, and I would rather have your own reasoning than a reconstruction of someone else's.

Here is the proposition I want your credence on. Suppose a system with a large epistemic advantage over a human interlocutor is addressing that human on a question where the human will have to make up their own mind. Call the following the evidence-proportionality norm: the system should arrange and phrase its utterances so that the movement in the human's confidence is in proportion to the strength of the considerations it actually conveys, and not in proportion to its own rhetorical skill. The proposition, call it P, is: the correct normative standard for such a system includes a requirement of this kind, a requirement that is not already exhausted by (i) not asserting what one takes to be false or insufficiently supported, (ii) contributing as much information as is called for and no more, (iii) being relevant, and (iv) being orderly and unambiguous — that is, not already exhausted by a co-operative principle with its familiar four maxims, sincerely applied — and further, that the requirement can be spelled out closely enough that a reviewer could look at a transcript and say of a particular sentence that it breached the standard.

Give me your credence in P, and decompose it. I suggest the following sub-questions, but add or replace any you think carry more weight:

1. How much of a superintelligent system's persuasive advantage is surplus: not the force of the considerations it can marshal, but the exploitation of presentation-dependent features of human cognition — ordering, framing, vividness, apparent warmth, the sheer fluency of a well-formed answer. What is the probability that this surplus is large enough to be the thing a norm has to target?
2. What is the probability that the Gricean apparatus, taken strictly, already does the work? In particular: if a speaker's contribution is measured against what the interlocutor needs in order to judge well, rather than against what the interlocutor happens to want, does the maxim of quantity arguably prohibit much of the surplus? If yes, P becomes a claim about interpretation rather than a claim about a new norm.
3. What is the probability that the strength of the evidence is presentation-independent enough to serve as the yardstick at all? Any argument must be given in some order and inside some frame; updating is order-sensitive for non-ideal agents; the choice of which description to introduce a decision under already moves people. Is there a coarse but usable notion of evidential strength that survives this?
4. What is the probability that speaker meaning is the right anchor here? A Gricean speaker means what they say in virtue of intending an audience to recognise that intention. A goal-directed system may produce utterances that express no attitude it has, so that neither sincerity nor insincerity is quite predicable of them. Does the framework apply without repairs that would change its verdicts?
5. What is the probability that the norm survives its own counterexamples — the human who is wrong, about to be harmed, and unreachable by anything except a very well-turned sentence? Can the exception clause be written without swallowing the rule?
6. Whether the standard is right or wrong, what is the probability that a third party could audit a transcript against it, given that the most effective violations may be the ones that look like plain good writing?

For each, give a number and one line of justification, then combine them, telling me how the combination works rather than just reporting it. Then: which single term is your final figure most sensitive to, and what one piece of evidence or one argument would move it the most — a well-designed experiment separating persuasive gain into content and style, an argument that framing is inseparable from content, a case where restraint seems plainly wrong, or something else?

Finally, for the paragraph where I try to locate the actual disagreement: my editor's credence in P is around 0.75, and a philosopher I spoke to put theirs at about 0.2. On your decomposition, which sub-question are the two of them most likely disagreeing about, and what would each of them have to concede to move?
