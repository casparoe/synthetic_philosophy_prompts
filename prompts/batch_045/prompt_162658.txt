I spent last evening arguing with a friend and got to a point where I could neither accept his conclusion nor locate its mistake, so I would like an outside diagnosis.

The claim I had been defending is modest: higher-order evidence can make it rational to hold a belief at a confidence that your own first-order assessment does not support. In the standard cases this looks like a species of epistemic akrasia — you go on believing p, your evidence for p looks to you exactly as it did before, and yet rationality requires you to hold p less firmly, because you now have reason to think the process that produced that assessment was unreliable. My friend granted the cases and attacked the description. As carefully as I can reconstruct it, his argument runs as follows.

Consider this case. You spend an afternoon working out whether a complicated bet is favorable: a chain of conditional-probability judgments, checked twice, and you settle at 0.9 confidence that it is. Only afterwards do you learn that the coffee at the reception was, unknown to the guests, laced in roughly half the cups with a compound that degrades conditional-probability judgment; that people who receive it report exactly the same confidence as everyone else while getting problems of this kind wrong about half the time; and that there is no way for you to tell which cups were laced.

(1) Your total evidence now includes the fact that your assessment was produced by a process with a substantially elevated error rate. Rationality requires proportioning confidence to total evidence. So you must lower your confidence below 0.9 — say into the 0.6–0.7 range. Call this required revision R.

(2) R is itself a judgment about how evidence bears on a proposition — a piece of probabilistic reasoning — and it was arrived at by you, after you learned about the coffee.

(3) The compound, if it degrades anything, degrades probabilistic reasoning. There is no principled way to confine its effects to first-order judgments like the bet being favorable while exempting second-order judgments like R; the drug does not know which level it is operating on.

(4) So, by exactly the reasoning of (1), you must discount R: you must lower your confidence in the claim that you should lower your confidence.

(5) But partially discounting a required reduction just is partially restoring the confidence you were told to give up. So taking the higher-order evidence seriously at the first level requires you to ignore it at the second. The two levels cancel.

(6) And nothing stops the cancellation. For every level n, taking the impairment seriously at n requires discounting the verdict reached at n+1; so there is no level at which the drug does any net work. Either the requirement cancels itself out — in which case higher-order evidence never mandates revision, and the standard cases were misdescribed from the start — or it iterates without end — in which case no confidence you finally settle on was reached by a process you have any reason to trust, and you should suspend across the board, including on whether you should suspend.

(7) My account therefore licenses akrasia everywhere, on the skeptical branch of (6), or nowhere, on the cancelling branch of (6) — but not selectively, in just the cases I wanted it for.

(8) So there is no such thing as rationally required epistemic akrasia. Where it seems to appear — a belief held at a confidence one's own assessment does not support — what is really going on is an ordinary change in first-order evidence, or a plain mistake.

What I want is a diagnosis of the chain, not a verdict on my view. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocuous when I granted it, and what rejecting it costs me elsewhere. In particular:

- I would rather give up almost anything than (1). If (1) goes, I do not see how the drug case differs from any other case of learning that a source is unreliable.
- I keep circling (3) and the move from (4) to (5). On (3): it seems open to me to stipulate a compound that degrades only first-order conditional-probability judgment and leaves general reasoning about evidence intact, in which case the regress never starts. But my friend says that stipulation is doing all the work and is ad hoc — that any impairment narrow enough to spare the meta-judgment is also narrow enough to be no threat to the first-order judgment. Is that right?
- On (4) to (5): I suspect the word discount is being used in two ways — reducing the force of a reason to revise, versus acquiring a reason to revise back upward — and that only the second would make the levels cancel. If so, say precisely what the first commits me to instead.
- Finally, my friend had a fallback for the case where I answer with convergence: grant that each discount is smaller than the last, so the regress has a fixed point, and take the fixed point as the rational confidence. I do not trust this. Why should the resting place of a procedure that distrusts itself at every step be the right answer, rather than a description of where anxiety settles? Price that reply too.

I would like the alternatives ranked by what they cost, and I would like to know whether any way out leaves me with what I originally wanted: a rational requirement to believe at a confidence you yourself do not think your evidence supports, in some cases but not all.
