hey — I want a diagnosis, not a verdict, and definitely not a tour of the positions.

Four of us run a population axiology reading group (two philosophers, one economist who answers everything with "expected utility", and me). We've been going at the repugnant conclusion for about eighteen months and I'm fairly sure we stopped moving somewhere around month three. Here's the board. One camp says the vast population of lives barely worth living really is better than the small population of excellent lives, and that the repugnance is a predictable mistake — scope insensitivity, loss aversion, the fact that we swallow the two endpoints in one comparison instead of going step by step when every step looks fine. The other camp says that verdict is a reductio, not a cost: a theory that will always trade quality for headcount has lost the plot, because there is nobody for whom the trade is an improvement, and the stepwise argument only looks compelling if you already grant that "worse for no one" is enough. Round and round. There's also a third position in the room — the critical-level, threshold-style views — which everyone concedes is ad hoc and nobody can agree is actually *worse* than ad hoc, and there are impossibility results floating around saying you can't have all the conditions people want at once. That hasn't helped either.

My hypothesis, which I want stress-tested rather than confirmed: a lot of the standoff is downstream of a question nobody in the room has made explicit, namely what the currency is. "A life barely worth living" only makes sense if welfare is a single scalar thing with a zero point on it. So I invented a case to poke at it — call it the flatlands case. The extra people in the huge population aren't suffering at all: no pain, no boredom, decent health, warm relationships, projects that go well, and nothing they want goes unmet, because what they want is small and gets met. On a hedonic reading they're barely above neutral. On a desire-based reading they're thriving. On an objective-list reading they score fine on every item. So: is the flatlands population a population of lives barely worth living? I cannot get my group to answer this, and what I notice is that whichever way you answer seems to *decide* which side of the repugnant-conclusion fight you end up on, without anybody having argued about aggregation at all.

So what kind of stalemate is this, actually? Is it verbal, or near-verbal — two sides using "welfare" and "worth living" for different things and therefore never really contradicting each other? Is it a genuine bedrock clash about aggregation that my currency observation merely decorates? Is it a meta-disagreement about what counts as data — whether pairwise intuitive judgments are evidence for an axiology the way perceptual judgments are evidence for a theory of vision, or whether they're noise to be explained away? Or is the question itself ill-posed, so that "better" isn't the same relation once the population size varies and there's no single thing being disputed? Tell me which, or tell me it's a mix and how the parts hang together, with reasons grounded in what each side actually says — I've read the positions, I don't need them listed back.

Then the part I care about more: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Would settling the currency question first dissolve any of it, or does the aggregation fight come through the currency question untouched? Is there a distinction, a formal result, or a change of question that would move us? And if you think nothing will move it, say that and say why.

One last thing: if the flatlands case collapses into something already familiar under another name, say so, and say which condition it's really pressing on — I'd rather find that out than reinvent it badly.
