I am trying to work out what I actually believe about reparations for historical injustice, and I want the principle rather than the political position. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases, recorded as I feel them rather than as I think they ought to come out. Some of them pull against each other, and I would like to know what I would have to give up.

1. Survivors of a wartime internment program carried out by their own government, compensated while still living. Yes, unhesitatingly: individual payments plus an official acknowledgment.
2. A painting taken from a family in 1938, bought in good faith at auction sixty years later by someone who had no way to know its provenance. Yes: it goes back to the heirs, and the buyer gets nothing from them.
3. Territory ceded under a treaty signed under duress a hundred and eighty years ago. The present occupants are mostly descendants of settlers who purchased title in good faith from a state that no longer exists; there are cities, farms and infrastructure on the land; the dispossessed community still exists and is measurably worse off in every social statistic. Yes, but a weaker, messier yes than in case 2, and not a yes to simply handing the land back.
4. Slavery in the United States. Wrongdoers and victims long dead, gains enormous and untraceable, and two wrinkles I want preserved: many of the people who would pay are themselves descendants of enslaved people, and many of the recipients are, through other lines of descent, descendants of owners; and most recipients exist at all only because the injustice happened. Neither wrinkle moves me. What does move me is form: I want this as a programmatic, group-level redress, not as a tort payout to each proven heir.
5. A former colony that, on the best available economic history, is richer now than it would have been if the colonizer had never arrived, but which was governed without its consent and had mineral wealth extracted. Yes. But the amount feels as though it should track the extraction and the denial of self-government, not any overall welfare comparison. I also reject the offsetting arguments entirely: the railways, the schools, and the fact that the same colonizer later abolished the slave trade at real cost to itself do not, to me, count against the debt.
6. The wrongdoer's gains were real but have since been destroyed: the wealth was spent on a war the wrongdoer then lost, and its society is now poorer than the descendants of those it wronged. Weak yes: something symbolic and institutional, considerably less than in case 5.
7. A religious order or a company that still exists, still owns the buildings, and abused children in its care fifty years ago. Strongest yes of the whole list.
8. A community wronged three centuries ago that no longer exists as a community: its descendants are scattered, identify with the nation that wronged them, and nobody is claiming anything. No. But I am uneasy, and I suspect my no is driven by there being no one to pay and no one to organize a claim, not by justice.
9. The harmed group's own rulers sold co-ethnics into the trade and profited from it. Does not defeat the claim, and barely reduces it: the buyer's wrong is not erased by the seller's. Yet I notice I reach for something like a clean-hands instinct in other contexts, and I cannot say why I withhold it here.
10. Two family cases, which I include because the contrast is doing something I cannot name. My grandfather underpaid workers in a factory he owned: no, I owe nothing, and what remains dissolves into general obligations and taxation. My mother defrauded a named person fifteen years ago: yes, if she cannot pay, I should.

Cases 2 and 3 are the pair I cannot reconcile. Both involve a present holder who acquired in good faith; in one I return the specific thing, in the other I do not. I don't know whether that difference is principled or just a concession to cost and upheaval.

The principle I have been toying with is a version of unjust enrichment rather than harm: reparations are owed by whoever presently holds the gains of a wrong, to whoever presently occupies the position of the wronged party, in the amount of the gain rather than the amount of the harm, structured to reverse the gain rather than to compensate a loss. It handles 7 and 5 nicely, but it goes quiet on 6, where no gain remains and I still want something; it makes my verdict in 8 look like an administrative accident; it has no obvious way to treat 9; and it does not tell me why the painting travels and the land does not.

Please do the following. Test that principle against the cases, repair it if it can be repaired, and put beside it one or two genuinely different candidates — a harm- or worsening-based one, a relational or civic-standing one, whatever you think actually competes. For each candidate, state it precisely enough that it returns a verdict on a case I have not described, tie each clause to the specific case that motivates it, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon and which of my cases is carrying more weight than it can bear. I would also like verdicts on three cases I have deliberately left out: a wrong that was entirely lawful under the law of the place and time it was done; a case where fortunes have reversed, so that the victims' descendants are now the richer group and the wrongdoers' descendants the poorer; and a case in which the disparity is produced by no discrete act and no identifiable wrongdoer at all, but by a long accumulation of ordinary private decisions. Finally, say something about whether form — cash, land, apology, institutional change — is settled by the same principle or is a separate question, and who, if the claimant community is divided about what to accept, has standing to settle it.
