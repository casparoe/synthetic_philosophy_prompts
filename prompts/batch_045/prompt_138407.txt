I chair the Novel Organisms Committee at a large biomedical research institute, and I need a decision procedure we can actually run in a meeting.

Our committee meets monthly and sees ten to fifteen proposals a year. We have forty minutes per proposal. The six of us are two molecular biologists, a clinical microbiologist, an occupational hygienist, a lay member, and me; I am a cell biologist by training and the only philosophy any of us has is an undergraduate course I took years ago. Our decisions bind principal investigators, and anything contested goes to the Director. The hygienist has to be able to follow the reasoning.

What we are asked to decide is whether a proposal involves a living organism, or a replicating biological entity. Our national rules regulate work with living modified organisms and with biological agents, and the Institute's own containment schedule is stricter and covers anything we judge to be a replicating entity. Neither the regulations nor our schedule says what living or replicating means at the margins, and that is now where most of our time goes. The proposals arriving on my desk include: a bacterium whose genome has been reduced to a minimal set and which grows only in a medium we formulate for it; a pair of engineered strains, each unable to synthesise a metabolite the other supplies, so that neither is viable alone while the pair is self-sustaining and evolves; a self-replicating RNA in a cell-free extract, housed in lipid vesicles we make for it, where the RNA evolves but cannot make its own container; an organism of reversed molecular chirality, should anyone propose one; prions and viroids; a seed and tardigrade collection proposed for a lower containment class on the grounds that nothing in it is metabolising; and a bio-hybrid device in which engineered tissue drives a small machine that assembles copies of itself from parts we supply.

Please write me the procedure. I want ordered questions, each with a statement of what would count as a sufficient answer to it, explicit stop points at which the answer is no and the proposal is returned, and explicit hand-over points at which the question is one of values rather than fact and a named person must decide. It should be grounded in whatever is actually established about how definitions work — the difference between listing properties and picking out a single marker, cluster and family-resemblance concepts, stipulative versus descriptive definitions, purpose-relativity, borderline cases and continua — but translated so that no one in the room needs the theory to use it.

Three things I want handled explicitly, because they are where we keep coming apart. First, entities that are metabolically inert but revivable by apparatus we control: if you need a term, call it latent viability, and tell me whether it is life and why. Second, entities whose self-maintenance depends on a niche or a component supplied by us or by another organism: does a relational or niche-relative answer change the classification, or should we insist on counterfactual independence? Third, cases where a pair or consortium satisfies whatever test you propose but no member of it does: is the system alive and the parts not, and what does the procedure return for the proposal in front of us?

Then apply the procedure to three cases: the two-strain consortium, the dormant seed and tardigrade archive proposed for downgrading, and the self-assembling bio-hybrid device. At least one of these will, I suspect, come out against the committee's instincts; say plainly which one, and what I should do about it rather than how I should feel about it.

Please write in British English, do not cite or point me to any literature, and keep the whole thing under about 2,000 words. Structure it in this order: Purpose and scope (around 120 words); The procedure, as numbered steps with sufficiency conditions, stop points and hand-over points (around 700 words); What each step is buying us, giving the conceptual grounding in plain terms (around 350 words); Where the procedure hands the decision to a person (around 200 words); Worked applications, one section per case (around 600 words in total); Where the procedure misfires (around 250 words); and, finally, What the procedure cannot do (around 150 words).
