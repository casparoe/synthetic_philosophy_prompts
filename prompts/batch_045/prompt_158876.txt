I write a column on science and ideas, and I keep tripping over a piece of vocabulary. The entire AI industry now calls a confident false output a hallucination or confabulation, logs it in incident reports and evaluation write-ups as an error, and scores systems on accuracy against a ground truth — while the same engineers' model cards insist that the system is only predicting the next token and does not really believe, mean or refer to anything. Nobody says a random number generator hallucinated a seven, and nobody logs a bland or awkward but true answer as an error, even though the training procedure penalised it just as heavily. I would like you to reconstruct the theory of representational content that this practice presupposes.

As I understand it, the natural candidate is teleosemantics: an output's content is fixed by what the system was selected or designed to do, and an error is malfunction with a function — deviation from a proper function, not merely an unusual causal chain. Two worries I want addressed. First, these systems have a peculiar selection history: the final training stage optimises for human approval rather than for truth, and most deployed questions fall outside the historical conditions in which whatever function they have was selected. Does strict teleosemantics actually deliver the verdict the practice wants, that confabulation is an error, or does it make confident confabulation the system working as designed? Second, how much does the practice strictly presuppose: would an informational or covariational theory of content, or a broadly interpretive one on which we merely adopt the intentional stance, license exactly the same vocabulary, or does talk of hallucination really commit us to something like proper function?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits well with, and say what would change in how labs and regulators talk if the teleosemantic underpinning were dropped. British English, about 600 words.
