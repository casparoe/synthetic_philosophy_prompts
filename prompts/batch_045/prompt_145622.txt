I do exhibit and signage design at a science museum, and I've been reading a bit about Otto Neurath and his isotype picture language — the Vienna method of pictorial statistics, Gerd Arntz's little figures — and now I can't look at airport signage the same way. The part that got me: from what I can gather Neurath wasn't naive about pictures at all. He thought they have to be taught (he wanted visual education in schools), that a picture language can't do what a word language does when it comes to exactness — conditions, negation, argument — and that he never meant isotype to replace words, only to work next to them. Which sits oddly with him pushing an *international* picture language and with that line of his about words dividing and pictures uniting.

Here's the practice I keep turning over: safety and wayfinding signs are built to carry their whole message with no words attached, the symbols get standardised across countries, and yet the standards bodies test candidate symbols on panels of people and bin the ones too many misread, and travellers then basically learn the rest by repetition. So we design as if pictures are read by anyone anywhere, and we test as if they're a convention you have to be trained into. What does that practice actually presuppose about pictures and meaning? Does it strictly need the naive view (pictures mean naturally, words only by convention) that Neurath seems to reject, or is it compatible with his, or just underdetermined between them? Does the wordlessness buy any of the neutrality he gestured at, or is a standardised stick figure its own politics? And what would have to change in how we build signage if we took his view seriously — or dropped it?

Also flag anything I've got wrong about Neurath, and separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on him; I definately don't want a contested reading handed to me as the consensus one.
