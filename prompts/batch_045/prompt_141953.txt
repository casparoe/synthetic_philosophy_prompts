I'm in the fourth year of a PhD in philosophy, working on a chapter that tries to say what privacy *is* before it says anything about what it's worth, and I've gotten stuck in a way that suggests I should stop theorizing top-down and instead admit what I think about cases. So here are my verdicts, given as honestly as I can, including the ones that don't sit well together. My physician learning something intimate about me in the course of treating me: no violation at all. The clinic clerk who pulls my chart out of pure curiosity and reads the very same line: a clear violation. A stranger who reads the diary I left on a park bench: a violation, but a noticeably milder one than someone who breaks into my encrypted journal and reads the same pages — and I can't tell whether that difference belongs to the notion of privacy or only to how bad the violation is. A supermarket's system that infers a pregnancy from my purchases and mails me coupons, where no employee ever sees the inference and no human ever forms a belief about me: a violation. A weather app that infers where I am in order to forecast: not a violation, even though the inference is the same kind of thing and the data is arguably more sensitive. A statistics agency publishing counts by district: fine. The same agency publishing two such tables a year apart, from which a neighbor's new medical condition can be recovered purely by differencing: a violation, though nothing individual-level was released and nobody ever singled her out — and this is the pair I find hardest to reconcile with the previous one, since in both cases the agency disclosed only aggregates. Donating my own genetic data to a research database with full consent: no violation of *my* privacy; the police identifying my brother from it: a violation of *his*, even though nothing of his was ever collected or read. My ex-partner simply remembering intimate things: no violation. My ex-partner publishing them in a memoir: a violation, though no new information changes hands and the access is years old.

The case I keep going back and forth on, and that I'd like the principle to settle, is this: a lab trains a model on my email in order to study something unrelated, the model becomes a near-perfect predictor of what I'll say next, it is never queried about me, no output about me is ever generated or read, and it is deleted. Something in me says this is the most disturbing case on the list and something else says nothing happened to me at all.

My tentative principle, such as it is: a person's privacy is set back when some agent comes to occupy, with respect to that person, an informational position — attending to, storing, inferring, or being in a position to derive — that the norms of the practice in which the relevant traces were generated do not license, regardless of whether anyone forms a belief or grasps a proposition, and regardless of whether the person consented or had control. Please test that against my cases and repair it rather than merely endorsing it: tell me which clause does which work and which case motivates it, whether it over-generates on the weather app or under-generates on the differencing case, and whether it can say anything at all about my grandmother's letters, which I'd object to a stranger reading thirty years after her death without wanting to say that *her* privacy is being violated. Then give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed — a stranger filming me in a country whose language and signs he cannot read; a "statistical twin," a synthetic profile tuned to respond exactly as I do and used to test products on someone who doesn't exist; and the case where I livestream my entire life so that no access is unauthorized, but a system builds a profile that predicts my movements better than I can. Where my verdicts can't all be saved, say which one has to go and why the resulting principle is worth the loss; and if my whole pattern is better explained by starting from the value of privacy — something about being manageable, or legible, or subject to another's will — than from its nature, say that too.
