me and my friend got into a three hour argument last night about Bertrand's paradox and I still don't know who won. Not about which answer is right — we both agree that "a chord drawn at random is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle" comes out 1/3, 1/2 or 1/4 depending on whether you randomize the endpoints, the distance of the midpoint from the center, or the midpoint in the disk. We were arguing about why that can't be settled. His line: there's a uniqueness argument, demand that the distribution not change when you move or resize the circle and exactly one of the three survives, so this was basically solved and the people still writing about it are redoing homework. My line: that just relocates the choice, you picked which transformations count as symmetries of "random chord," and picking them is the same kind of stipulation as picking a parameterization in the first place. Then he threw a variant at me and it made it worse: same circle, but the chords come out of a sealed machine and we get to watch a thousand of them before answering. He says that collapses the whole thing into ordinary inference about a chance process, with a fact of the matter. I say the original question is what you should say when all you've been told is "a chord was drawn at random," before any data, and that question is still sitting there untouched for the next machine. My hypothesis is that it's two questions under one name — a measure theory exercise and a question about rational credence under total ignorance — but he thinks it's one question with a real answer and people keep dodging it. What is this stalemate actually made of, and what would count as progress on it? Answer in exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up.
