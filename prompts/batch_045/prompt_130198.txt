**Question.** (a) Distinguish God's natural knowledge (*scientia naturalis*), middle knowledge (*scientia media*) and free knowledge (*scientia libera*) by their objects and by their relation to God's decree. (b) Explain how middle knowledge is supposed to permit meticulous providence without divine determination of creaturely choices. (c) In seminar we called the following the "redundancy objection": a conditional about what a creature would freely do either concerns the circumstances that creature actually faces — in which case knowing it looks like nothing more than knowing a future contingent, and middle knowledge adds nothing to free knowledge — or concerns circumstances that never in fact obtain — in which case it is unclear both what makes it true and how it could guide the arrangement of the actual world. Say how well Molinism answers this, and in doing so state and assess the grounding objection. (Terminology: God *strongly actualizes* a state of affairs when he brings it about directly; he *weakly actualizes* it when he strongly actualizes some circumstances and the rest follows from what creatures would freely do. Call a possible world *feasible* if God can weakly actualize it in this way.)

**Student's answer.**

Molina's threefold division sorts God's knowledge both by what it is about and by whether it depends on his will. Natural knowledge has as its object all necessary truths and all possibilities, and it is pre-volitional, since God does not choose what is possible. Free knowledge has as its object the contingent truths about the actual creation, and it is post-volitional, since which world is actual depends on God's creative decree. Middle knowledge is intermediate in both respects: like natural knowledge it is pre-volitional, but like free knowledge its object is contingent. Its objects are counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — propositions of the form *if creature S were placed in circumstances C, S would freely do A*, where C is a set of circumstances S never in fact faces.

Because the truth of these conditionals does not depend on God's decree, God can survey them before decreeing anything and then select which circumstances to bring about. Providence is reconciled with freedom as follows: God knew, prior to any decree, that if Peter were in the courtyard he would deny Christ, and by strongly actualizing the courtyard circumstances God weakly actualizes the denial without causing it. The will is untouched: God's knowledge is not causally efficacious with respect to a free act, and Peter could have done otherwise in exactly those circumstances. This is the advantage of Molinism over the Dominican view, on which God's concurrence with a creaturely act settles which act the creature performs, and over mere simple foreknowledge, which gives God no way to arrange which free acts occur. And since God can strongly actualize any circumstances he pleases, the conditionals tell him what would follow, so he can weakly actualize whatever world he wants.

The first horn of the redundancy objection turns easily. What God knows pre-volitionally is not that Peter will deny Christ but the conditional, and a conditional is not a future contingent, since its truth does not require the circumstances to obtain. The second horn is where the real difficulty lies: it is the grounding objection. It is hard to see what in reality makes a counterfactual of freedom true, given that neither any actual decision nor any divine decree settles it. Molinists standardly reply that these conditionals are true because God knows them: his knowledge cannot fail, so the conditionals hold necessarily, and that is exactly what makes them available to providence. This is not circular, since the claim concerns what grounds the truth of the conditionals rather than how God comes to know them. Molinism therefore survives both horns, at the cost of allowing that the truths of middle knowledge depend on God's intellect rather than on the creatures.
