writing the framework chapter of my thesis right now (philosophy of biology, but I'm embedded in a behavioural ecology lab and sit in on their lab meetings, so I get it from both sides) and I want someone to audit where I've landed before my committee does it for me. Here's roughly the position, no particular order:

(1) the spandrels critique is basically right, and I mean it as a methodological claim: an adaptationist story about a trait shouldn't be accepted just because it's plausible, elegant, and nobody has offered a better one.
(2) but I don't accept it as an empirical claim. for the vast majority of complex, functionally organised traits I think drift and constraint aren't real competitors — nobody has ever actually written down a drift hypothesis for something like the camera eye that generates a testable prediction and won.
(3) selection is the only process around that can produce the appearance of design, so "this thing looks engineered for a task" is genuine evidence for selection, not a circular inference.
(4) the right account of function is etiological: a trait's function is what it was selected for, not what it currently does.
(5) exaptation is real and probably common — plenty of traits start as byproducts and get recruited into a current role.
(6) the method I actually want to defend is reverse engineering plus optimality modelling: posit the design problem, solve for the optimum, test the prediction, keep it or bin it. that's what's made the field progress and it's the bit I'd least like to lose.
(7) the architectural analogy in the 1979 paper is doing more work than it deserves: the bits of the building they point at are forced by the geometry of arches plus a dome, whereas most of the biological cases aren't forced by anything in that sense, they're contingent byproducts.
(8) i also accept the anti-atomisation complaint in that paper — organisms are integrated wholes, you can't just carve them into independent traits and optimise each one.

And then there's this thing I keep circling and don't know how to file. I've started calling it the retrospective design signature problem: once a co-opted trait has been under selection for long enough — say a signalling structure that began as an allometric byproduct of body size and got recruited for display — every operational test we have for design returns "adaptation". fit to an optimality model, complexity-and-fit-to-a-task, phylogenetic correlation with the relevant ecology, knockouts that wreck the current role. the critique is right that the origin was a byproduct, but there's no empirical signature left that would show it. and if that's right, then either the critique is undecidable in exactly the cases where it matters most, or "adaptation" was never a claim about origins in the first place.
