I somehow ended up on the aquisitions committee at the natural history museum — I write about science and ideas for a living, which I think is why they asked, and I said yes before I'd thought about it. Here's the problem. A lab has offered us, on long-term loan, a flask of something they call Marrow-4, and they'd like it in the Origins gallery labelled "the first living thing made by humans". I've read their write-up and I genuinely can't tell. Marrow-4 keeps itself going chemically and holds itself together inside a boundary of its own making; it grows until you split it, both halves carry on, and over a few hundred splits the population gets measurably better at things — faster growth, tolerating much saltier conditions. But it has no genome: nothing in it is copied and passed on as a template. What the daughter inherits is roughly half the parent's mixture, and the continuity runs through that. It also can't be frozen down and revived the way a bacterium can; a line dies if nobody splits it for a week or so. So one half of the committee says that's plainly life — bounded, self-maintaining, evolving, and DNA is just the version that took off here on Earth. The other half says we'd be deceiving the school parties: it never reproduces itself, a technician reproduces it, and what looks like inheritance is closer to the offspring being reconstituted from whatever happened to be in the parent, so it's a self-sustaining chemical process in a bag — marvellous, and not an organism. There's a consistency wrinkle too: two cases along in the same gallery we already tell visitors that viruses aren't alive, and a mule obviously is despite being sterile, so whatever line we take has to sit reasonably beside those. Draft the memo I'll circulate before the next meeting. I need a definite recomendation on whether we take the flask and, if we do, what the label should actually say; reasons the rest of the committee can argue with; and a fair hearing for whichever of the two positions you're rejecting rather than a summary of it. If there's a formulation that's honest for nine-year-olds and doesn't just collapse into "scientists disagree", give me that as well.
