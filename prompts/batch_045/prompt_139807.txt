So I run a final-year seminar on philosophy of science, and tomorrow's the last session — we're closing on Lakatos, methodology of scientific research programmes, and we've spent two weeks on the difference between a progressive and a degenerating problem shift. I always finish the term with a short poem read aloud instead of a summary, and this year I want a villanelle. The running example I've used all term is one I made up (at least, I *think* I made it up — please don't fact-check the science, it's a teaching toy): a nineteenth-century research programme whose hard core is that the aurora borealis is sunlight reflected off the Arctic ice, so the whole display up there is a sort of mirror-show hanging over the pole. Over the weeks we watched it take hits — the light isn't polarised the way reflected light should be, the spectrum has lines in it that aren't in the sun, the height measurements put it miles above any ice, the magnetic storms that arrive along with it — and every time, one more auxiliary hypotheses gets bolted onto the protective belt: a second reflecting layer, a luminous haze lying over the ice, a correction to the correction. What I want is for the form to do the machinery's work. Two refrains: one is the hard core, stated flatly and beautifully, the kind of sentence that made people sign up for the programme in the first place; the other is the repair, the belt doing its job. They should come back verbatim each time, but I want each return to pick up one more bracketed qualification or epicycle, so the words never change while more and more of the world gets fenced off from them — and the final quatrain should leave the two of them side by side, so that by the end you genuinely can't tell which one is the discovery and which one is the patch.

A few things I care about actually being right rather than name-dropped, because half the room wrote their essays on this. First, the slide from progressive to degenerating shouldn't just be a change of mood: in the early stanzas the belt should be predicting something new and getting it right, and later only mopping up after the fact, so someone who knows the distinction can point at a stanza and say, that's where it stopped being a progressive problem shift. Second, the negative heuristic — the arrows must not be pointed at the hard core — I'd love that to show up as the programme's instructions for where to look degrading into instructions for where *not* to look. Third, there's a wrinkle that derails us every year and I want it in the poem: one of the late rescues isn't ad hoc at all, it predicts something novel, it's corroborated, and it's completely trivial, so by Lakatos's own machinery the programme still counts as progressive while everyone in the room can feel it's dead. Please don't resolve that. And don't have the poem tell anyone to abandon the programme — there's no instant rationality and no rival programme on offer yet — I'd rather it ended with the question properly open. If you can manage it, put the rhyme families to work too: one family the language of light and prediction, the other the language of patching and repair (mend, amend, defend, pretend, suspend…), so the ear can hear which way the thing is moving. Someone who's never heard of Lakatos should still just hear a poem about a beautiful wrong idea about the sky; someone who has should be able to tell which line is which, and why they end up together.
