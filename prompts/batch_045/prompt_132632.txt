I organise a philosophy-and-film evening at the public library where I work, and the next session is on 12 Angry Men, which I want to build around the lottery and preface paradoxes rather than around civic virtue or group dynamics. For the uninitiated half of the room I'll state them roughly like this: in a large fair lottery I seem justified in believing of each ticket that it will lose, yet I know one wins, so justification (at any threshold short of certainty) is not closed under conjunction; and the careful historian is justified in believing each of the hundreds of claims in her book while also justified in believing that at least one is false. Please don't send me to the literature — no bibliographies, reading lists or citations; I want a reading I can deliver aloud, and it has to work from the film itself and from the two puzzles as I've set them out. Assume I know the plot but not the epistemology in any depth.

Here is the shape I want you to work through. The prosecution's case in that film is plainly conjunctive — motive, the shout heard through the wall, the old man's fifteen-second dash to the door, the woman who saw the killing through the windows of a passing train, the downward stab wound, the switchblade that was supposed to be one of a kind — while Davis never claims the boy is innocent, only that he can't vote to convict. So my first question is whether his stance is best modelled as a lottery-paradox-shaped belief state at all, or as something more like acceptance for action: a refusal to treat a high probability as sufficient given how lopsided the costs of the two errors are. And I've been toying with a distinction I'd like you to sharpen or reject: call it dismantled-case doubt. Most of Davis's victories are not rebuttals but undercutters — producing an identical knife doesn't show that this knife wasn't the murder weapon, it shows only that the knife no longer discriminates; the business with the eyeglasses shows that the witness across the street could not have seen what she said she saw, without showing that the killing didn't happen. If a case is taken apart item by item, and every item is shown merely to be less reliable than it looked, then rigour itself starts manufacturing doubt, and I can't tell whether the film thinks the surviving items are strengthened by having survived the stress test, or whether it is quietly committed to the bleaker view that a procedure thorough enough to test everything will always leave you short of a threshold.

The part I'm least able to see clearly is the collective layer, and it's the part I most want. Because the defence only needs one undefeated item, the case for acquittal is disjunctive where the prosecution's is conjunctive, and a disjunction of individually weak doubts can be quite strong — which, if it works, would be the film's answer to both paradoxes, since the paradoxes get their bite from assuming that justification aggregates conjunctively. But the eleven men who end up voting not guilty don't converge on a shared disjunct: by the final vote they seem to hold overlapping but different sets of doubts, and the last holdout is converted by something that isn't evidence at all. So I want you to say what the film is claiming about what a verdict is: whether unanimity here is a genuine group judgement, an aggregation of separate reasons, or a moral act of a kind that the belief-first framing of both paradoxes can't accommodate. Be faithful in both directions — don't turn the film into a dramatised syllogism, and don't bend the paradoxes until they fit — and tell me where the film resists the reading you're giving, especially in its closing minutes. If you think there's something the film shows about aggregating doubt across people that the standard way of posing these two puzzles leaves out, end on that.
