I'm setting this year's PhD comps in political philosophy and I need one 90-minute question on luck egalitarianism that can't be crushed by reciting the brute/option luck distinction and then gesturing at the harshness objection. Heres the wrinkle I want the question built around: plenty of people who suffer unmistakable brute-luck misfortune — a disability, a childhood illness, a freak accident — end up affirming it. They say it made them who they are, they wouldn't trade it back, and they find the offer of compensation somewhere between baffling and insulting. Call it the problem of endorsed misfortune.

The question should force candidates to say what luck egalitarianism is actually trying to do — neutralize brute luck's effect on people's shares? hold people responsible only for what they chose? give them roughly what they'd have insured for behind an ex ante veil? something else — and then to say whether, given that aim, endorsed misfortune is a genuine counterexample, a red herring, or just a case where compensation is owed and may be waived. Ideally they also have to say something about whether an ex ante insurance framing even has the resources to describe this case, or whether it just changes the subject.

Deliverables: the question itself, a model answer of about 700 words that commits to a verdict instead of hedging, and a marking scheme out of 20 — five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and one line on how candidates usualy get each one wrong.
