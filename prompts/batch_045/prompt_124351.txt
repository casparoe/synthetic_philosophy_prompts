**1. Extract from the lecture handout**

Devlin distinguishes an extreme thesis — that a society is entitled to use the criminal law against any conduct its members regard as immoral — from a moderate thesis: that a society is entitled to do so only where toleration of the conduct would, in the judgement of the ordinary reasonable member of that society, pass the limits of toleration and so endanger the existence of the society itself. On this version of the test the question is not simply whether the conduct is immoral but whether its toleration would be destructive, and the standard is the reaction of the reasonable member rather than a headcount of majority disapproval. The empirical premise — that a common morality is as necessary to the existence of a society as an organised government, so that a society which tolerated certain conduct would not survive it — we called the disintegration thesis. Legal moralism, the view that conduct may be criminalised on the ground that it is immoral or that tolerating it would corrupt the common morality, is not to be confused with legal paternalism, the view that conduct may be criminalised in order to protect the agent from himself.

Hart's reply had three strands: first, that the disintegration thesis is an empirical claim for which Devlin offers no evidence; second, that the "morality" Devlin is protecting is positive morality — the morality actually accepted in a given society — and that Devlin gives no reason why its coercive maintenance should be immune to critical moral scrutiny, or why the moral reformer and the martyr must be counted enemies of the society they seek to change; third, that on Mill's harm principle the bare fact that a practice is condemned by positive morality is not a reason for criminalising it where it causes no harm to others. Hart's legal positivism is not the basis of his objection to Devlin, and candidates were warned against deriving the one from the other.

**2. The question**

*Question 4.* In Aetolia, a small, stable and prosperous jurisdiction, the following obtain.

(i) A practice, vellin-use, is engaged in only by informed and competent adults, in private, with the consent of all participants. It is known to carry no risk to non-participants and no measurable effect on the conduct of participants towards others.

(ii) The practice is regarded with intense revulsion by the overwhelming majority of Aetolians. The revulsion is not a mere matter of taste; it is bound up with a belief, which is false, that vellin is excreted in a form that contaminates the shared water supply.

(iii) Aetolia has no common first-order moral code; its citizens disagree profoundly about how to live. What holds Aetolia together is a second-order convention: nearly all Aetolians accept that what the criminal law declares is the authoritative settlement of moral questions, and they defer to it for that reason. Aetolia's sociologists agree that a well-publicised decision to leave vellin-use lawful would substantially weaken this convention; they disagree among themselves about whether Aetolia would survive the weakening.

A bill is before the Aetolian assembly to criminalise vellin-use. Its stated ground is that to leave the practice lawful would be to reveal the law as one opinion among others and so to undo the only thing holding Aetolian society together.

(a) In no more than three sentences, state the difference between Devlin's moderate and extreme theses and say which of them the Aetolian case would require. [4]

(b) Does Devlin's argument, as stated in the handout, support the proposed prohibition? Your answer must explain what work, if any, is done by (i) the fact that Aetolia's cohesion rests on a convention of deference to the criminal law rather than on a common moral code, and (ii) the limits-of-toleration standard. [8]

(c) Does Hart's reply dispose of the case? Your answer must address (i) the status of the disintegration thesis and (ii) the fact that the Aetolians' revulsion rests in part on a false belief. [8]

Candidates were told to take the stipulations as given and not to argue with them. No credit is available for a general restatement of the Hart–Devlin debate.

**3. Candidate 4471's answer**

(a) Devlin's extreme thesis is that a society is entitled to use the criminal law against any conduct its members regard as immoral; his moderate thesis is that it is entitled to do so only where enforcement is necessary to preserve the society's existence. The moderate thesis is the one Devlin defends, and it is the one the Aetolian case would require, since the bill does not appeal to the bare immorality of vellin-use but to the claim that tolerating it would dissolve the bond that holds Aetolia together.

(b) The case gives Devlin nothing. His argument is that a common morality — a body of shared first-order judgements about how to live — is the cement of society, and that is exactly what Aetolia lacks. What Aetolia has instead is a habit of deference to the criminal law, and a habit of deference is not a morality; nor is it the sort of thing the criminal law can be said to protect, since if the law changed tomorrow the habit would simply reattach itself to the new content. Nothing in Aetolia's cohesion is therefore at stake in this decision, and the limits-of-toleration standard does no work: the Aetolians' reaction to vellin-use is intense, but intensity is all it is, and described neutrally it is majority disapproval and nothing more. Devlin expressly declines to rest anything on majority disapproval. His moderate thesis cannot reach the case, and the bill would be an instance of the extreme thesis he disowns.

(c) Hart's reply does dispose of the case, for two independent reasons. First, the disintegration thesis is an empirical claim about what happens to societies that cease to enforce a moral code, and the comparative evidence is clear: jurisdictions comparable to Aetolia have decriminalised comparable practices without any of the collapse Devlin predicts. Once that premise is falsified, Devlin's whole position collapses with it, since it is the only thing distinguishing his conclusion from the bare assertion that immorality may be punished. Second, the Aetolians' revulsion rests on a factual error, and Devlin's standard is fact-sensitive: a reasonable person who knew that vellin does not contaminate the water supply would feel no revulsion at all, so the bill fails even on Devlin's own terms. That leaves the harm principle to settle the matter. Vellin-use harms no one but the user; the state has no business coercing self-regarding conduct; and a liberal state must remain neutral between competing conceptions of the good life. To criminalise it would be to impose the morality of the majority on a minority for no better reason than that the majority dislikes it — pure legal moralism, that is to say, the view that the law may coerce people for their own good. On the facts as stipulated, the correct answer is that vellin-use should remain lawful.
