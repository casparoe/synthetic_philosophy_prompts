Hey — I run a weekly first-year reading group on interpretation, 16 students, 75 minutes, and this has gotten personal lately: I published a short personal essay a few months back and readers keep telling me what it "really" says — one person wrote a long thread about how it was secretly about my father, which I never once thought about while writing it. I dont want to lecture about intentionalism versus anti-intentionalism, and I dont want a bull session of opinions either.

So: design a session where they run into the problem before anyone names it. I'm imagining students writing very short texts (a few lines of fable-ish prose) with a private intended meaning recorded somewhere, the texts anonymized and swapped, readers writing down what they take the text to mean, and then some kind of confrontation between the two. Two things I'd like built in, because they're the bits I keep chewing on: (1) some texts should be co-written by pairs who each record their own intention privately and never see the other's, so "the author's intention" may not be a single thing to begin with; (2) after authors see how they were read, they write down what they'd now say the text means, and the group has to decide whether the intention at the moment of writing or the later one is the one that settles meaning — if either does.

What I want them walking out with is the sense that "what I meant" and "what the text says" are two things that can come apart, and that it isn't obvious which one wins. I need: the actual rules and roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, what to hand out, a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the specific moves people make (including the ones who just insist the readings are wrong), what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on which part of the real dispute this setup can't reproduce and where I'll just have to talk at them. No naming of positions until the end, please.
