I'm due to give a short "where I stand" paper at a graduate workshop on higher-order evidence, and before I commit the position to slides I want to know whether the package I've assembled is actually stable. Please audit the set rather than explain the topic back to me: assume I know the standard cases (the drunk, the exhausted driver, the peer who disagrees, the surprise exam) and that I'm using the following terms in their ordinary senses. *Higher-order evidence*: evidence about the reliability of my own belief-forming process, rather than about the subject matter. *Level-splitting*: holding a first-order belief while separately judging that the belief was formed unreliably. *Uniqueness*: the denial of permissivism — for any total body of evidence, exactly one doxastic attitude toward a given proposition is rationally permitted. *Conciliationism*: the view that acknowledged peer disagreement requires substantial movement toward the peer's view.

Here is where I've landed.

1. **Uniqueness.** I reject permissivism. When people say the evidence leaves it open, I think they are unsure what their own total evidence supports, not reporting a genuine latitude in what rationality permits.

2. **No level-splitting.** Epistemic akrasia is never rationally permissible. If I have strong evidence that the process which produced my belief that P is unreliable, holding P at the same confidence is not an option: the higher-order evidence has to move the first-order attitude, not sit alongside it in a separate metacognitive register.

3. **Nothing special about higher-order evidence.** It is just more evidence, and the right response is to conditionalize on my total evidence, which now includes facts about my own condition. There is no separate norm requiring me to give weight to my calibration, and no distinct attitude of taking oneself to be unreliable. I hold this partly because I cannot see how a special norm would avoid being either redundant with Bayesian updating or in conflict with it.

4. **The asymmetry clause: reliability attenuates but never amplifies.** Evidence that I was tired, biased, or in bad conditions should lower my confidence. Evidence that I was at my best — rested, in the flow, right about this kind of case nine times out of ten — should not raise it above what my first-order evidence already supports. I cannot bootstrap my way to a higher confidence by learning that I am reliable. I concede that if I ran the numbers naively, positive higher-order evidence would look like it should push me up; I want the clause anyway.

5. **Conciliationism, roughly to the midpoint** — and I hold this even in the case where my peer has seen all of my evidence and all of my reasoning and I cannot locate a single step at which we part company. Relatedly, I think 5 applies to disagreement about 5. My closest interlocutor is a steadfastness theorist, she is my peer on the epistemology of disagreement as well, and if I treat her dissent as higher-order evidence about my reliability on *that* question too, then my confidence in conciliationism falls, which seems to weaken how much I should conciliate on the first-order matter. I cannot tell whether this settles at a stable midpoint or collapses — into steadfastness on one side, or into discounting everything on the other.

6. **Fallibilism is not akrasia.** I can rationally believe P while granting that I would very likely abandon P if I looked harder or if further evidence arrived, and I take that to be compatible with 2 rather than an instance of what 2 forbids.

7. **Two verdicts I am not sure my own commitments deliver.** (a) *The fatigue memo*: I read a scan, conclude that the lesion is malignant, confidence around 0.9; I then learn that I am at hour fourteen of a sixteen-hour shift and that readers at hour fourteen misclassify roughly a quarter of cases of this type. My verdict is that I should come down to about 0.8, not to the 0.75 the raw reliability figure suggests, because this particular scan is unusually clear and the statistics are dominated by borderline images. I am suspicious of how convenient that stopping point is: it keeps me above the level at which I would act on the diagnosis. Tell me whether 1–5 actually deliver 0.8, or whether I am smuggling in a pragmatic floor and calling it epistemology. (b) *The deferred result*: the lab result arrives tomorrow, but I know I will be reading it at four in the morning after a night shift, in exactly the condition that 4 says attenuates my confidence. My verdict is that today I should set my credence to what I would expect it to be after a well-rested reading, not to my actual expected-tomorrow credence. Is that consistent with the diachronic constraint that current credence should equal the expectation of future credence, or have I quietly built in an exception?

I don't know which of these is at fault and I don't want the most popular combination on offer; I want to know what commits me to what. Go through the set and tell me which pairs pull against each other and how badly, which ones only look like they do, and what the least costly revision is. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each one say precisely which commitment would have to give and what I lose by giving it up.
