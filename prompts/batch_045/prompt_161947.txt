I work near the computational neuroscience–philosophy boundary, and a reading group I've been part of for about eight months has stalled completely on the question of whether the brain is a computer. The positions have crystallized into three, and everyone can state the others' views well enough that I no longer think anyone is confused about the facts. One camp holds that the question has a trivially affirmative answer and is therefore the wrong question: any physical system implements some computation or other under some mapping of its states onto symbols, so the only substantive question is which computation, and the answer to that is whatever the best working model in systems neuroscience happens to say. A second camp holds that "the brain computes" is a real empirical hypothesis with content — that neural activity is governed by rules defined over representations whose identity doesn't depend on the physical medium, and that this medium-independence is exactly what licenses the style of explanation we get from the old three-level scheme (computational theory, algorithm, implementation), and what makes a model fitted to neural data portable onto a completely different substrate. A third camp says "computes" is an ascription we make rather than a property systems have, so the dispute resembles asking whether a stomach really digests or whether we merely find it useful to describe it that way. Nobody has moved, and nobody has moved while agreeing about everything empirical: spike timing, population coding, the predictive-processing framework, the fact that artificial nets trained on neural data predict neural responses, the fact that a working model can be burned onto a neuromorphic chip. Each side treats the others' arguments as beside the point, which I take to be the signature of something other than a factual disagreement. One detail that struck me: when I asked the group whether a whole-brain simulation that reproduced someone's behavior down to the last report would settle the question, several people said yes and several said it would be irrelevant — and that split cut across the three camps rather than along them.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. My own hypothesis is that the argument is really about what a good explanation in neuroscience has to look like — whether a mechanistic account bottoming out in ions, receptors and anatomy is complete, or whether it must be supplemented by a level of description whose individuation conditions are independent of the physical details — and that the vocabulary of "computer" is a proxy war over that methodological question. But I can see three alternatives worth taking seriously: that it is a verbal dispute over a word with a formal-mathematical sense and an engineering sense; that it is two questions sharing a heading, a classificatory one about whether brains fall under the concept and an explanatory one about whether computational models earn their keep in practice; and that it is empirical after all, but about a hidden question nobody has stated cleanly — something like whether the causal structure of neural tissue is recoverable from the tissue alone or has to be read off the task the tissue is performing. Tell me which diagnosis you think is right, argue for it against the others on the basis of what each side actually commits itself to, and say what it would take to be wrong about it.

Then tell me what would count as progress under your diagnosis. In particular I want your judgment on a way out I've been toying with, which I'll call the blind-compilation test: hand an engineer the complete wiring diagram and component dynamics of a system, with the output variables left unlabeled and no information about what the system is for; if the task description can be recovered from structure and dynamics alone, the system is computing that task in a substantive sense, and if it cannot — if the mapping has to be supplied by someone who already knows what the system does — then "it computes X" is our description rather than its property. I like the test but I don't trust it, because it seems to give answers I want to reject in cases where I have firm intuitions: a pocket calculator clearly passes, and yet on some readings a weather system passes too, which suggests either that the test is too weak or that my intuitions about the marginal cases are doing no real work. Does the test dissolve the dispute, relocate it one level down, or merely restate the triviality camp's position in new vocabulary? And if it fails, is there anything — a formal result about implementation, a distinction I'm missing, an experiment, or a change of question — that could actually end this, or should the group expect to be exactly here in eight more months?
