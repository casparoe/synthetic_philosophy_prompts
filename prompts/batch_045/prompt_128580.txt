I've drifted into supervaluationism about vagueness and I'm now writing it up as a thesis chapter, which is awkward because my supervisor is a card-carrying epistemicist who keeps asking me what would change my mind, and I don't have a good answer beyond "nothing much, really." (I also got into an argument about it at the pub with two philosophers from the maths department who were convinced the whole thing is just fuzzy logic with extra steps, which didn't help.) So: help me build a map of how exposed the view actually is. The position as I hold it: vagueness is semantic indecision — for a vague predicate there's a set of admissible precisifications, constrained by nothing over and above our usage, and a sentence is true simpliciter iff true on all of them, false iff false on all of them, and gappy otherwise. The payoffs I care about are (1) we keep classical logic in the object language, since every instance of excluded middle comes out supertrue, while still explaining why the sorites feels compelling — each conditional "if that's a heap then so is that plus one grain" is supertrue, but the argument they compose isn't truth-preserving in whatever the relevant global sense is; I think I have this right but tell me if the standard version is different, (2) we explain the distinctive epistemic profile of borderline cases without positing an unknowable fact, and (3) higher-order vagueness is supposed to be handled by letting admissibility itself go vague one level up.

What would count against it? I want defeaters ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and — the part I can't do myself — I want to know which ones a supervaluationist would concede actually bite, as opposed to being dialectical noise manufactured by the other side. Here's my starter list, such as it is: the objection that I've only relocated the sharp boundary upstairs into the set of admissible precisifications; the worry that on my account a sincere borderline assertion isn't true, so ordinary speakers are systematically in error, unless I tell some story about assertion that I suspect quietly collapses into the epistemicist's; the "penumbral connections" problem — I'm never sure the view can really explain why each conditional sounds obligatory while the chain of them can't be used; and the feeling that my third payoff is a cheat, because "admissible precisification" isn't sharply defined either, so I've explained vagueness by helping myself to more of it.

Two cases I invented because I couldn't find anything quite like them, and I want your read on how much weight they carry. First: imagine a speech community — call them the Kesh — whose meta-level is sharp. For any proposed sharp cutoff of their word for heap-ish, every competent speaker says immediately and without hedging whether it's admissible, and they all agree, including about cutoffs right at the edge of admissibility; no higher-order vagueness anywhere in the language. My instinct was that this is good news for me, since all the vagueness is then in one place, the multiplicity of sharpenings. But it also seems to make determinacy perfectly knowable, so that every competent Kesh speaker knows her own borderline utterance is neither true nor false — which is just the implausible consequence the epistemicist says I'm stuck with anyway. Does the case cut for me or against me, and what would settle which? Second, a made-up experiment I keep using as a foil: you force speakers to pick a cutoff for a vague term, in a hundred unrelated languages, with real money riding on accuracy. The picks are all over the place; the same person picks differently a week later; and — the bit I think matters — when you show someone the group mean they move toward it while insisting it's a decision rather than a correction, and they never treat a wild outlier as having made a mistake, only as having chosen differently. I read that as evidence there's nothing there to be right about. My supervisor reads it as precisely what you'd predict if there were an unknowable fact plus a coordination problem: of course nobody treats deviants as mistaken, since nobody can tell who's right. Who's got the better of that, or is the experiment simply badly designed to separate the two readings? And if it is badly designed, what's the version that would separate them, and why?

Then the meta-question, which is the one my supervisor really presses: is the view insulated? It sometimes feels to me like supervaluationism isn't a theory of vagueness at all but a framework for implementing any story about semantic indecision — choose your admissible precisifications, choose your consequence relation, and you can model more or less anything you like. If that's right, is there any observation, intuition, formal result, or consequence for some other area (the semantics of "determinately," the logic of conditionals, what we say about disagreement between speakers with different sharpenings) that could still refute it? And should I be worried about that insulation in the way I'd be worried about an unfalsifiable claim in physics, or is it fine — even appropriate — for a semantic theory to be more like a language for stating theories than a theory? Please be blunt about where I'm most exposed, and end by telling me which single thing you'd personally find most convincing against me and why.
