I have no background in philosophy or law — I've just been turning this over on my own since I moved to Germany a couple of years ago — and there's one institution here I can't stop puzzling at.

If you are registered as Catholic or Protestant, your employer deducts a church tax along with your income tax: a surcharge of roughly eight or nine percent of your income tax bill, depending on the state, which the state collects and passes on to the church. Your religion gets recorded when you register your address. A great many people were entered into that register as infants, by baptism, decades before they were in any position to agree to anything. If you later decide you don't belong and don't want to pay, you can't just stop; you have to make a formal declaration at a government office, in most states for a fee, and only then do the deductions stop. At which point the church treats you as having cut yourself off — no sacraments, no church wedding, and so on. I also gather that the state decides which religious communities get to use this machinery at all: it isn't automatic, some groups have had to fight for it and some have been refused.

The thing I keep circling is that the same country has no established church and insists that religion is a private matter and the state neutral among faiths — yet here the state is the church's billing and enforcement arm, and keeps an official register of who belongs to which church. And the amount owed has nothing to do with believing, attending, or caring: a wealthy person who hasn't been inside a church since childhood pays far more than a poor one who goes every week. So membership is treated as a single binary legal status, entered once by something done to you as a baby and exited only by a deliberate act at a government counter, while the money owed behaves like a tax on income with a religious trigger attached.

What I'd like is the implicit theory behind that arrangement. What view of a religious community must someone hold for this to make sense — a voluntary association of people who opt in, or something more like a public corporation, or a community you belong to by ascription whether or not you chose it? What view of membership: a status conferred once and held until revoked, or something maintained by practice and so capable of fading? What view of state neutrality: even-handedness among recognized communities, or the state keeping its hands off altogether? And what view of religion itself, given that the state has to decide which communities qualify — is any group organized around a comprehensive worldview eligible, or does the arrangement quietly presuppose something specific about what a religion is? One way to test that: if a group of people organized around an avowedly secular worldview asked for exactly the same machinery, would the practice's own logic have to grant it?

Please also tell me which of these the arrangement strictly presupposes and which it merely sits well with, and where it's genuinely underdetermined — where several different theories would license the same institution, so that it may just be a historical compromise rather than the expression of any view. And if the underlying view were replaced by a voluntarist one, or by a strict separationist one, what concretely would have to change?

Don't just lay out the options for me to choose between: commit to a reading of what this practice really presupposes, give your reasons, and say plainly whether you think the arrangement is coherent or merely stable — and if you're unsure at some point, say where and why.
