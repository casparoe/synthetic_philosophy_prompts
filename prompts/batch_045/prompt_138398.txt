So I TA for a first-year seminar on rational choice and we did a session on Hofstadter's superrationality — the idea that in a symmetric game you should pick the move you would want everyone else to pick, because the other superrational players are running the same reasoning you are and will land on the same answer. I wrote up what I thought was a knockdown objection to it and now I think the argument proves too much, but I cant see where it breaks. Here it is, more or less as I wrote it:

(1) The core superrational move: your choice and your counterpart's are not independent, because both are outputs of the same piece of reasoning. So you should choose the strategy that, if everyone chose it, gives everyone the best result.

(2) In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against someone who reasons exactly as you do, and who you know reasons exactly as you do, this gives cooperate. That is the result that makes the view attractive and I still think it is right.

(3) But nothing causal is happening in (1). My cooperating does not cause my counterpart to cooperate, any more than my defecting causes them to defect. What (1) is really trading on is that my act is evidence about what they will do. So superrational choice ranks options by what they tell you, not by what they bring about.

(4) And if that is what does the work, it should not matter where the correlation comes from. Evidence is evidence, whether it comes from shared deliberation or from a common cause.

(5) So take the dessert case. There is a gene that both makes people crave a particular dessert and causes a nasty illness, and you dont know whether you have it. Reaching for the dessert is strong evidence you have the gene; eating it does not cause the illness and skipping it will not prevent it. By (4), you should push the plate away.

(6) That is absurd. Passing on dessert makes you no healthier, and a decision theory that tells you to manage the news like that has gone wrong somewhere.

(7) So superrationality is refuted — except that (1) and (2) still look right to me, and if I give up (1) I lose the only argument I have for cooperating in the twin case, which was the whole point.

My gut says the trouble sits in (3) or (4) and that correlation is doing two different jobs there, but I cant say what the principled difference is supposed to be, or what it costs to insist on one. My professor also said (1) as I have stated it is incomplete anyway: it only covers everyone making the same move, and the usual solution to the puzzle where twenty people each get a letter offering a billion dollars to whoever replies, but only if exactly one person replies, needs each of them to roll a die and reply with some small probability so that they come apart. If superrationalists are supposed to be perfectly correlated, how are they supposed to roll independently? Is that fatal or just a patch?

Please tell me which step you would reject and what rejecting it costs, and price the alternatives — I would rather keep (2) than (6) if I have to choose. Also be explicit about which bits are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, because I dont want to walk into seminar and present a contested reading as though its the consensus. And American English, please — I am handing this in to a US department.
