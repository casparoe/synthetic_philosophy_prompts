Hi — I'm writing the answer key for next week's problem set in a confirmation-theory seminar and I really don't want to put words in the professor's mouth, so I'm trying to work out what rule she's actually running before I commit to anything.

Her running example is H = all ravens are black, and she rates observations on a five-point scale: nothing / negligible / weak / moderate / strong. Here are the verdicts I've managed to collect from her old handouts and from things she's said in section:

1. An expert spots a bird in the wild and identifies it as a black raven: moderate.
2. Her own white sneaker, which she's owned for five years, sitting in her hallway: nothing at all — she says it was never a test of anything.
3. A blind draw from a warehouse bin labelled non-black objects, roughly two million items in it; the item turns out to be a red toy truck: somewhere between negligible and weak.
4. The same kind of draw, but from a bin with only about thirty non-black items, and again it comes up a non-raven: she says this is clearly stronger than 3, though still below 1.
5. A blind draw from a bin labelled objects that are not ravens; it comes up a white coffee mug: nothing.
6. She nets one bird out of a mixed flock of ravens and crows that she's been watching for an hour, so she already knows every bird in that flock is black; the only open question is raven-or-crow. It's a crow: nothing.
7. A bird that a DNA test says is a raven, glimpsed in bad light and judged probably black but not certainly: weak.
8. A white raven: falsifies, and she insists that holds no matter how the thing was selected and no matter what anyone expected.

The two cheap rules are obviously out: she rejects the plain-vanilla Nicod-style rule that any instance of the hypothesis confirms it, together with the equivalence condition (that would make 2 confirm), and she also rejects the view that only black ravens can ever do any confirming (3 and 4 do confirm for her).
