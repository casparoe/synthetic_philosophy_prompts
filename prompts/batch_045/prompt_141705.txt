I'm in a tiny reading group working through the meta-problem of consciousness — not the hard problem itself but the question of why we end up making hard-problem-ish claims at all: that there's something it's like, that explanation runs out somewhere, that you could have all the functions and still lack something. We got frustrated last week because every account we looked at seemed to be answering a different question. One was explaining why we *say* "conscious", another why we *believe* it, another why the intuition resists functional explanation, and no two of them had the same job list. So before I read any further I want the spec written down: what does an account have to do to count as having solved the meta-problem?

Fix the purpose so adequacy isn't up for grabs: I want an account good enough to tell me whether solving the meta-problem should move my credence on the hard problem at all — whether a complete explanation of the machinery that produces our phenomenal talk is itself a reason to think the hard problem is real, a reason to think it isn't, or basically inert. Score the desiderata against that. Here's my scratch list, please fix it rather than just agreeing: (i) it has to explain the *fragility* as well as the near-universality of the intuition — most people have it, but it's the sort of thing that dissolves on reading a good argument, so does a theory have to explain the losing as well as the having, or is the "recovering illusionist" just noise to be explained away; (ii) the modality gradient — the intuition is vivid for colour and for pain, thin for proprioception and for the sense of knowing, and basically absent for whatever it is that sets pupil dilation; a congenitally blind friend of mine reports the puzzle vividly about timbre and not at all about colour, which feels like the wrong shape of data for any account that treats the intuition as one single faculty; (iii) the attribution gradient — we hand out "something it's like" to a chatbot after twenty minutes of conversation and to a thermostat almost never, and to a cat long before a crab, and it looks like the same mechanism producing first-person verdicts and third-person verdicts, so say whether those have to be unified; (iv) an engineered case I made up because I don't think anyone puts it this way: suppose we build an agent with a separate self-monitoring subsystem trained to emit verdicts about its own states in the vocabulary of phenomenality ("this one feels like something", "that one doesn't"), where we can inspect the training signal and see exactly what it was rewarded for. What should a solution to the meta-problem predict about that system — that it emits such verdicts, that we'd be tempted to take them seriously, and does the prediction change if the reward was social (match other people's phenomenal talk) rather than structural (flag states that are globally broadcast, attention-grabbing, or report-independent)? If a theory can't say anything determinate about this case I don't see how it's a theory rather than a story. Plus the distinctions I think it must not collapse: making a phenomenal claim vs. having a phenomenal state; the intuition's content vs. its causes; explaining why we're confident vs. explaining whether we're right.

For each desideratum give me the case or consideration behind it, and mark it non-negotiable or as a prejudice of one camp — "must not entail that our reports are false", "must be mechanistic all the way down", and "must leave the hard problem exactly where it was" look to me like three demands that can't all be genuine. Then tell me which subset is jointly unsatisfiable, if any is: my suspicion is that anything rich enough to explain why functional explanation *feels* inadequate is also something that quietly settles the first-order question, which would make the whole "neutral" option unavailable. And tell me which single desideratum knocks out the most candidates. A short scorecard at the end is fine — illusionism, higher-order or representationalist accounts, and realist-but-functionally-explanatory views, two lines each — but keep the weight on the criteria, and no bibliographies or citations, we're reading offline and half of us are on small models that'd just make the references up anyway.
