THE QUESTION (20 marks: 6 for (a), 5 for (b)(i), 6 for (b)(ii), 3 for (b)(iii))

(a) State Adams's thesis about indicative conditionals. Explain what Lewis's triviality result shows and why it has been thought to threaten that thesis.

(b) In a hospital ward every patient is either severely ill or mildly ill, with prior probability one half each. Hospital policy is to administer a certain drug to a patient if and only if that patient is severely ill. Two further factors are relevant, independent of one another and of severity: a patient is either drug-resistant (probability one half) or not, and either vulnerable (probability one fifth) or not. Recovery is completely determined by severity, drug status, resistance and vulnerability, as follows:

- severe, given the drug: recovers if and only if not resistant
- severe, not given the drug: recovers if and only if not resistant and not vulnerable
- mild, given the drug: recovers
- mild, not given the drug: recovers if and only if not vulnerable

Ana knows all of the above — the policy, the probabilities, the deterministic outcome rule — but she does not know Dana's severity, her resistance status, whether she is vulnerable, whether she was given the drug, or whether she recovered.

(i) Compute the probability that Adams's thesis assigns to each of the two indicatives "If Dana was given the drug, she recovered" and "If Dana was not given the drug, she recovered". Then compute, for each of the two counterfactuals "If Dana had been given the drug, she would have recovered" and "If Dana had not been given the drug, she would have recovered", the credence Ana should have that it is true. For the counterfactuals, assume a Stalnaker–Lewis semantics in which the selection function is supplied by the causal structure: the closest worlds to any world w in which Dana receives the drug are those that differ from w at most in whether she receives it and in what causally depends on that, and similarly for the closest worlds in which she does not. State what, if anything, your numbers say about which treatment is better.

(ii) Say that a set of conditionals sharing a consequent C and differing only in whether their antecedent is A or A' is *crossed* when the indicatives and the counterfactuals disagree about which antecedent is more favourable to C: P(C|A) is greater than P(C|A'), while the counterfactual with antecedent A' is more probable than the counterfactual with antecedent A (or the reverse). Your results in (i) exhibit a crossed set. What, if anything, does the existence of crossed sets show about the semantics of conditionals? In particular, does it show that indicative and counterfactual conditionals differ in truth conditions, or that no single semantic treatment can cover both? Explain, and be explicit about any assumption your argument requires.

(iii) Suppose Ana now learns that Dana was not given the drug and that she recovered. Re-evaluate: what happens to the indicative and to the counterfactual, and why? Be explicit about anything Adams's thesis is unable to say in this situation.

THE ANSWER

(a) Adams's thesis is that the probability — and hence, for Adams, the assertability — of an indicative conditional "if A, then C" for a speaker is the conditional probability P(C|A) evaluated on that speaker's credence function, rather than the probability of any proposition A ⊃ C. Lewis's triviality result is the central objection. What it shows is that no conditional connective can satisfy the equation P(A → C) = P(C|A): given the equation, conditionalizing on A and on not-A must each leave the probability of A → C unchanged, so the conditional must be probabilistically independent of its own antecedent, and a conditional whose probability never varies with its antecedent is no conditional at all. The result is completely general, since it uses nothing beyond the equation and the probability calculus, and so it does not rest on any contestable assumption about what conditionals are: even for a single speaker at a single time, the equation is unsustainable. It follows that Adams's thesis is false, and that the assertability of an indicative cannot simply be a conditional probability. The Adamsian reply — that indicatives do not express propositions, and so have no truth conditions to be trivialised — is expensive, since it leaves the behaviour of conditionals in embedded positions, and our practice of ascribing them to other speakers, unexplained.

(b)(i) Given that Dana was given the drug, Ana learns that she is severely ill, since the drug goes exactly to the severely ill. So the probability of the first indicative is P(recovers | severe, drug) = P(not resistant) = 0.5. Given that she was not given the drug, Ana learns that she is mildly ill, so the probability of the second indicative is P(not vulnerable) = 0.8. For the counterfactuals, the closest worlds in which Dana receives the drug hold her severity, her resistance and her vulnerability fixed. If she is severely ill she recovers just in case she is not resistant — probability 0.5, on a case of weight 0.5. If she is mildly ill she recovers whatever else is true of her — weight 0.5. So the first counterfactual has probability 0.5 × 0.5 + 0.5 × 1 = 0.75. For the second: if severe, she recovers if and only if not resistant and not vulnerable, i.e. 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4, with weight 0.5; if mild, she recovers if and only if not vulnerable, i.e. 0.8, with weight 0.5. Total 0.2 + 0.4 = 0.6. So the indicatives rank no-drug above drug (0.8 against 0.5) while the counterfactuals rank drug above no-drug (0.75 against 0.6). The drug helps in both strata — 0.5 against 0.4 among the severely ill, 1.0 against 0.8 among the mildly ill — so it is the counterfactuals, not the indicatives, that say what Ana should act on: the drug should be given. The indicatives mislead only because the policy makes administration of the drug a symptom of severity.

(b)(ii) This is a crossed set, and it is not a pragmatic curiosity. All four sentences are built from the same antecedent material and the same consequent, and all four are assessed by Ana on the same evidence; the only systematic difference is the mood of the conditional. Since the probability of a sentence is a function of its truth conditions together with the evidence, two sentences that differ in probability while the evidence is held fixed must differ in truth conditions. So crossed sets show that the conditional construction is semantically ambiguous: indicative and counterfactual conditionals do not have the same truth conditions, and the difference is carried by the conditional construction itself. This is just what the similarity semantics predicts, moreover, since on that account the counterfactual takes the intervention reading automatically — the closest worlds in which Dana receives the drug are of course the ones that hold her severity fixed — whereas the indicative is left with the evidential reading. Nor does the restrictor account offer a way out: on Kratzer's version of it the indicative/counterfactual contrast is carried by the conditional construction, that is by the mood of the if-clause, rather than by the conversational background, so the reversal in (i) would have to be located in the operator, which is exactly what I have argued.

(b)(iii) Once Ana learns that Dana was not given the drug and that she recovered, she can infer that Dana is mildly ill and not vulnerable. The counterfactual "If Dana had been given the drug, she would have recovered" is now certainly true: the closest drug-worlds hold her mildness fixed, and mild patients who receive the drug recover whatever else is true of them. Its probability is therefore 1. The indicative is in a worse position: its antecedent now has probability zero on Ana's credence function, and Adams's thesis has nothing to say about conditionals whose antecedents have zero probability. So the indicative drops out entirely and the counterfactual carries the whole content. That is the right result, and it confirms that the counterfactual is tracking the causal structure while the indicative is only tracking the evidence.
