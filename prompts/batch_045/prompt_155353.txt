I'm convening a graduate reading group next term on the politics of naming atrocities — why the legal category of genocide gets invoked, withheld, and fought over — and for the first session I want to open with a constructed case rather than a canonical historical one, because everyone arrives with a settled verdict about the standard examples and the discussion just replays it. So I need a scenario built to order, and I'd rather you engineered it against my specifications than produced a plausible-sounding illustration. The setting should be a modern provincial administration in a fictional country dealing with a pastoralist minority whose members are tracked in the administrative record by grazing-permit and census category rather than by name. Constraints: no camps, no executions, no shooting; the group's physical destruction, such as it is, has to run entirely through the clause in the treaty's list of prohibited acts that covers deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to bring about destruction in whole or in part — grazing commons reclassified, veterinary cordons, ration allocation keyed to registered residence, schooling and resettlement in district towns — so that the deaths, births forgone, and dispersal accumulate without anyone ordering anyone killed. There must be no architect: no minister, committee, or doctrine that ever adopts the disappearance of the group as an objective, and each official's own mandate must be genuinely administrative and genuinely his reason for acting. Include one mid-level coordinator who can see the four streams at once and can see where they are heading, and one official who foresees the outcome, mentions it in a memo as regrettable, privately welcomes it, and does nothing beyond his ordinary duties. No individual's contribution should be such that removing it alone would have averted the outcome. Keep the scenario to one paragraph, keep it realistic, and — importantly — make the factual record undisputed: memos, minutes, and metadata say what they say, and the three readings I want run on it must disagree only about what intent is and where it lives, never about what happened.

The three readings, and the verdicts I want: (i) a purposive reading, on which the dolus specialis requires that destruction of the group as such be the actor's conscious aim or purpose in acting; (ii) a knowledge-based reading, on which it is enough that the actor knew his conduct was part of a pattern whose outcome was the group's destruction and participated anyway; (iii) a structural reading, on which the intent is attributed to the apparatus rather than to persons, or is simply inferred from the systematic and patterned character of the acts themselves, with no individual mens rea required. On my rough estimate (i) acquits everyone, including the official who privately welcomes the result — his purpose in acting is administrative, and desire without purpose is not intent — (ii) convicts the coordinator and the welcomer but not the officials confined to their own stream, and (iii) convicts the enterprise without needing to settle anything about individuals; but I'd rather you derived that than echoed it, and if the case comes out differently I want to know why. Give me the verdict for each named individual under each reading, with the elements of the definition doing the work in each case.

Then the machinery, which is the part I actually care about. Tell me which single structural feature of the case generates the divergence — I think it is the absence of an intender combined with the fact that no one's contribution is individually necessary, but if it is something else, isolate that instead and don't smear the effect across two variables. Then run the removal test: rewrite the case so that a coordinating committee somewhere adopts the disappearance of the group as a problem with a solution, change nothing else, and show me whether the three readings collapse toward agreement via ordinary imputation — if they do, I've learned that my case was only ever a joint-criminal-enterprise case with the principal deleted, which is a finding I want stated plainly. Finally, give me the strongest attack a defender of the purposive reading would make on the case, and be honest in sorting the objections into two piles: those that say the case is rigged — that it has redescribed negligence or foresight as intent, or let an emergent outcome fix the mens rea, or built a genocide with no perpetrator — and those that concede the case and argue instead that the concept simply has limits, and that a word whose political work depends on identifying perpetrators should be kept narrow even at the cost of leaving this pattern unnamed. The point of the exercise, which should govern what counts as cheating: I want to know whether the disagreement about the intent requirement is really a disagreement in the metaphysics of action about what it takes for an agent to intend a result, or a downstream disagreement about what we want the word genocide to be for. If you can't get the three readings to come apart without stipulating an intender somewhere, say so — that failure is itself the answer, and I'd rather have it than a case that just stipulates the verdicts.
