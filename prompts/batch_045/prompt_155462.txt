Our reading group is working through the Aunt Hillary material — Dennett's ant colony and the dialogue Hofstadter builds on it — and I want a one-page flowchart to hand out: a decision tree that starts from a commitment nobody in the group disputes (a mature colony is a genuine biological individual, with colony-level traits that selection acts on) and ends at a named position on whether the colony has a mind.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of isms: give five to seven branching questions first, say which combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, and mark the empty combinations as incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended. Every node should be answerable by someone who doesn't know where it leads, and each should be anchored in a concrete case: nest-site choice by quorum sensing, where no ant compares the candidate sites; repletes used as colony-level storage no individual ant tracks; the removal of dead and infected nestmates with nothing in charge; a colony that shifts its soldier-to-worker ratio over weeks in response to rivals next door — a state about a part of itself that no part is in a position to be in; a colony that dies in a thunderstorm although none of its ants does; and a colony that reproduces by fission, leaving two viable colonies where there was one.

Leaves must include: mere aggregate; intentional system with no what-it's-likeness; a real colony-level mind with ants playing the role of neurons; nothing over and above the ants' minds; cognition spread across nest and trails; and no determinate answer because subject boundaries are stipulated. If two leaves can't be separated, say so rather than inventing a distinction, and tell me which fork you're least confident in and what would make you rebuild the tree.
