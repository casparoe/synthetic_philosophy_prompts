I am writing a term paper for a seminar on early modern philosophy of mind, on Descartes' treatment of animals, and I keep running into one short sentence doing a great deal of unexamined work: "animals are machines" — or, in the version that came up last week, "they're just automata."

The occasion was a departmental reading group on animal sentience. A psychologist had presented work on nociception and pain-like behaviour in fish and crustaceans: responses that are modulated by anaesthetics, that persist and generalise, and that do not look like bare reflexes. A philosopher in the audience said, in effect, that this is where we get back to Descartes: if animals are machines, then the question of what it is like to be a fish is empty, and in any case there is nothing anyone could ever appeal to in order to answer it. The discussion stopped there, which satisfied nobody, and I now want to say precisely what could and could not be meant by the sentence that stopped it.

What I need is a disambiguation, not an exegesis and not a bibliography: please treat the sentence as a piece of language and lay out, as a menu ordered from the near-trivial to the radical, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. For each one give a verdict — platitude, substantive and defensible, contentious and probably false, or straightforwardly false on what we now know — together with a note on what would settle it.

Then the part I actually care about: for each reading, say whether it would do the work my colleague wanted it to do. Which readings would entitle someone to treat the fish's inner life as a non-question? Which would only shift the burden of proof onto the person claiming that the fish feels something? And can the evidential reading (nothing could ever settle it) be held together with the metaphysical one (there is nothing there), given that he seemed to be leaning on both at once and they appear to pull in opposite directions?

Here are the distinctions I have been going back and forth on; use them or replace them with better ones, but do address the underlying questions.

1. What is being denied: an immaterial and immortal soul; reason or intellect; thought in the full Cartesian sense, i.e. the sort of general-purpose, language-involving responsiveness that no mechanism could produce; mere sensation or feeling; or moral standing. These come apart, and it looks to me as though some can be denied without the others.

2. What "machine" is doing: fully explicable in mechanical terms; deterministic; a mere artifact, made for a purpose and belonging to someone; devoid of interiority; or simply very complicated but not thinking. My suspicion is that one of these is a platitude, one is a theological claim doing more work than it appears to be doing, and only one is strong enough to carry the conclusion — say which.

3. The two tests Descartes is usually read as offering, the language test and the test of flexible adaptability across novel situations, do not look like the same kind of test, and I think the argument needs them to be: one seems to be about expression, the other about underlying capacity. Parrots and magpies are the case that makes the trouble, since they produce speech sounds without, on this view, ever saying anything that shows they understand what they say. Does the failure-to-express reading generalise in a way that also catches pre-linguistic human infants and people temporarily unable to speak, and if so what is the principled reply — and what happens to the argument if the reply is that the capacity is present even when unexercised, since capacities of that kind are not settled by the behaviour in front of us?

4. Descartes sometimes writes as though animals do have something like sensation, describing them as expressing fear, hunger or anger, while denying that they think. If a reading that separates phenomenal feeling from thought is available, say whether it is coherent, whether it is Descartes', and what it would do to the argument about the fish: it seems to me that the question whether there can be the feeling without the thought matters more here than anything about souls.

Finally, mark clearly which reading you take to be the one people who repeat the slogan generally mean, and state which single reading, if any, is both defensible and strong enough to carry the conclusion that was drawn from it. If the honest answer is that no reading is both, say so plainly and identify the weakest step in the argument that was made.
