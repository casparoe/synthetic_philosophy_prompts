I'll add the citations myself so please don't put any in, and don't give me a separate literature review section.

The bit of her view I'm starting from (correct me if I'm mangling it): in the Truth and Politics essay and the later Pentagon Papers one, she argues that factual truth is politically more fragile than rational or philosophical truth, because a fact is just what happened and there's no reason it had to be that way — so a liar can always substitute a tidier and more coherent story, and facts survive only if they're publicly attested and propped up by institutions without an axe to grind, courts and a free press and universities. Lying is a form of action in a way that truth-telling isn't, and systematic lying doesn't merely conceal facts, it erodes the shared world that facts were supposed to be facts about.

What I cant get past is this: her model of a fact looks like the witnessed one. Somebody was there, or a document records it, and the political struggle is over whether to believe them. But most of the facts that drive political argument now are witnessed by nobody. Unemployment rates, temperature records, vote totals, casualty counts, crowd sizes — these are outputs of measurement procedures aggregating things no individual person saw. So the question I want is: is the fragility she describes a feature of factual truth as such, or is it an artifact of the eyewitness model? And if it's the latter, which I suspect, then the contemporary version of the problem isn't that the fact gets denied but that the procedure gets discredited, and the reply that you should just go check with the witnesses does no work at all, because there aren't any. Call that the witness-structure problem.

My half-formed idea: the unit worth analysing isn't the fact but what I'd call its witness structure, and we need at least four kinds — witnessed by someone present; witnessed but surviving only in records; derived by aggregation from many unwitnessed inputs; and model-generated, meaning counterfactual or projected, like sea level in 2100 or deaths averted by a policy. The claim I'd want to end up defending is that her institutional remedy has to change shape as you go down that list, and that at the lower end what's needed is not publicity but something like followable procedure: making the route from inputs to output traceable by people who will never see the inputs. If that's right it's an amendment to her account, not just an application of it. There's also a regress worry I want kept in view, since defending a derived fact seems to require further unwitnessed facts about how the measurement was conducted, which might be the principled reason the bottom of the list is the most fragile.
