I'm in eleventh grade, and my family is hosting Aiko, an exchange student from Osaka, for three weeks. She speaks careful, polite English and, as far as I can tell, says what she thinks the moment calls for.

On Sunday my grandmother served her apple pie and asked, in front of the whole table, whether she liked it. Aiko said, "It is very interesting — I have never tasted anything like it." My grandmother took that as delight: she has bought a month's worth of apples, plans a pie every weekend, has told her neighbors that her Japanese guest loved her cooking, and is entering the pie in the county fair. In fact Aiko told me afterward that it was much too sweet for her, and that being asked that way, in front of everyone, was almost unbearable; she asked me not to pass it on. What I keep coming back to is that Aiko would have said something very close to the same thing even if she had loved the pie — and my grandmother, of course, has no way of knowing that.

A Dutch friend of mine, Bram, was served the same pie at the same table last month and said, "No, I don't like it, it's too sweet — is there anything else?" Grandma was quiet for about an hour and then laughed about it, and she has never tried to give him pie since.

I can't work out where the fault lies or what I owe each of them. Was Aiko's answer a lie at all, or was it the kind of remark that nobody hearing it is supposed to take at face value — and if it was, is it my grandmother's job to know that, or Aiko's job to say something a person from here would read correctly? Is it fair to ask a guest to give up the way she was raised in order to be understandable to us? And for my own part: I can stay quiet; I can say something true that I know will lead my grandmother to a false conclusion, such as "Aiko isn't used to American desserts"; or I can be straight with her and break Aiko's confidence in the process. Bram's bluntness is starting to look like the kindest of these, since it bought one hour of awkwardness instead of a month of pies — but is that the real argument for his way of answering, or is there something his answer gives up that Aiko's does not?
