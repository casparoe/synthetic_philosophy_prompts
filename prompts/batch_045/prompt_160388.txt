Here's where I've got to. As I understand the Rietdijk–Putnam argument: take the usual sort of example, two people ambling past each other in opposite directions on a high street. Because their "now" slices through spacetime at slightly different angles, the slice that for one of them contains the pavement right now also contains, for the other, some event in a far-off galaxy that for the first person still lies in the future. Since there's no fact of the matter about whose slice is the real present, and since anything that's simultaneous with me in some frame must be real, everything — the whole past and the whole future — comes out equally real: the block universe. My supervisor says plenty of philosophers don't accept this and that I'm smuggling something in, but I can't see what; as far as I can tell its a straight consequence of special relativity, which is about as well-confirmed as physics gets, so the physics seems to be doing the metaphysics for us. I've even tried to strengthen it by adding what I call the chaining step: events in my own future are timelike-separated from me, so they can never be simultaneous with me in any frame, but I can hop from my here-now to a distant "now", from that distant event to another one further along, and so on down a chain of spacelike hops until I arrive at my own death — and since co-reality looks transitive, the whole chain collapses into one big co-real block. With that in place I really can't find the gap.

So this is what I actually want to ask. If the block picture is right, in what sense is my death not already the case? I realise "already" might be a tensed word smuggled back in — or maybe that just is the mistake — but I can't see the difference between "every event exists tenselessly" and "everything is happening at once", and my supervisor only says the second is a howler without explaining why. And connected to that: if my death is as real as my birth and there's already a settled truth about every choice I'll ever make, what exactly am I doing when I deliberate over, say, whether to apply for a PhD? It feels as if the decision is sitting there in the block, that I couldn't do otherwise, and so that whether I bother deliberating can't make any difference to how things go — which is fatalism, and I gather that's meant to be a seperate thesis that the block universe doesn't hand you, but I can't see why not. And as a kind of coda: if nothing really passes, then my experience of time flowing has to be an illusion that science explains away, presumably via the thermodynamic gradient — is that the standard eternalist line, or is there a way of keeping the block and still taking the experience of passage seriously?

Basically: is the block universe as forced by the physics as I think it is? Does my chaining step actually close the gap my supervisor keeps pointing at, or does it smuggle in the very thing under dispute? And once you grant the block, how much of what I've piled on top — fatalism, deliberation being pointless, passage as illusion — really follows, as opposed to being extra assumptions I brought along myself? I'd like to know which bit is the load-bearing mistake.
