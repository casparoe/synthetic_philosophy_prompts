I hold (and have been defending) a threshold-sensitive version of the asymmetry about coming into existence that is associated with David Benatar, and I would like a stress-test of it: a clear-eyed map of what would have to be true for it to be false.

The classical asymmetry, as I formulate it, contains four claims. (1) The presence of pain is bad. (2) The presence of pleasure is good. (3) The absence of pain is good even when there is no subject who enjoys that absence. (4) The absence of pleasure is not bad even when there is no subject who is deprived by it. These claims are standardly taken to imply that coming into existence is always a harm relative to never existing: any actual life contains some pain, which is bad; never existing contains no pain, which by (3) is good; and the pleasures of existence do not offset any badness in never existing, because by (4) their absence is not bad.

My threshold-sensitive version restricts clauses (3) and (4). I distinguish significant from trivial sensations along the lines of intensity and duration, and I apply (3) and (4) only to significant pains and pleasures. For sub-threshold sensations, I restore ordinary symmetry: the absence of a trivial pleasure is bad for the possible person who never enjoys it, and the absence of a trivial pain is not good. The upshot is that the antinatalist conclusion does not follow universally. A life whose only discomforts are mild and brief, amid rich and sustained happiness, can be better than never existing, because those discomforts are sub-threshold and so never existing does not gain the asymmetric good of their absence. By contrast, a life containing any significant pain remains worse than never existing on my view, since never existing secures the significant good of that pain's absence while never existing is not made worse by missing the life's significant pleasures.

Please tell me what would count against this position. Treat it as my considered view, and do not limit the answer to objections that a critic might raise; I want the view's own exposure. In particular, please address:

- Which observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, or conceptual developments would be decisive against the view, and which would merely be awkward or call for minor repair rather than abandonment? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
- What the position predicts that its rivals do not, and how those predictions could in principle fail.
- Which commitments a defender of the view should concede as genuine defeaters, as opposed to objections that beg the question or merely restate rival assumptions.
- Whether the view is at risk of being insulated by its own qualifications. If the threshold between trivial and significant can be adjusted to accommodate any counterexample, does the view become unfalsifiable, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind? Please be willing to conclude that the view is well insulated and say whether that is or is not a problem.
- What would personally move me. I am most swayed by cases in which a happy person sincerely judges that they were not harmed by being created, and by the worry that any threshold restores symmetry only by smuggling in person-affecting intuitions that the classical asymmetry was supposed to discard. Please rank these as defeaters and indicate which of them a defender can absorb.

Please answer in American English.
