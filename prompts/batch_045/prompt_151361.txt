My day job is institutional design and AI governance, and I need the notion of a group acting mainly because I have to write sentences like "the organization decided to deploy" into policy documents, and then attach consequences — credit, blame, liability, standing — to whoever "the organization" turns out to be. So I care more about whether a principle gives me usable verdicts on the cases below than about whether it's elegant.

Here are my verdicts, going by gut, on when a group counts as genuinely acting together rather than as many individuals whose doings merely add up. In each case the question I want answered is: is it true that *they* did it, in the sense that matters for credit, blame, and ascription of a decision?

1. Two strangers at a stalled car, each pushing to get it out of the intersection, each unaware of the other's reasons and neither adjusting to the other. The car moves. My verdict: no. The car got moved; nobody moved it together.

2. My housemate and I plan to paint the house, divide the labor, check on each other's progress, and redo the patch she missed when the light gets better. Yes, obviously.

3. A ninety-piece orchestra performs a symphony. Most players don't know most of the others' names, no one has the whole score in view, and no section could produce the thing alone. Yes — and this case makes me distrust any principle that requires each participant to grasp the whole plan.

4. A five-person team commits to shipping a feature. One engineer argues it's a bad idea, is overruled, thinks the decision is wrong, and stays and does her part anyway because she cares about the team and wants to keep her job. Yes, she's still in it. I resist any move that lets reluctance, dissent, or being outvoted eject someone from the "we."

5. A battalion takes a hill. The colonel has the whole plan; each squad knows only its own orders and follows them because of the chain of authority; several soldiers think the maneuver is pointless. Yes — the battalion took the hill.

6. A lone developer reads a company's published roadmap, likes it, and spends six months building the thing the roadmap asks for. The company learns of her only when she submits it, and gratefully merges it. No: she acted *for* them, not *with* them. Note how much this looks like case 5 — sub-plan taken up unilaterally, plan authored elsewhere — and how differently I rule.

7. A decentralized autonomous organization with forty thousand token holders, an on-chain voting rule, and a contract that automatically executes whatever passes. The voters mostly don't know one another, vote for wildly heterogeneous reasons, and most don't read the proposals. A proposal passes and the treasury moves. Yes: the DAO funded it. I want this to be true, because I think there will be many more of these and I want institutional consequences to follow from their decisions.

8. Two million people each independently decide to buy a console on launch day. The aggregate produces a shortage nobody wanted; each buyer would have preferred to wait had others waited. No — that's just many individual plans summing to an outcome, the thing this community usually invokes Moloch for.

9. An assistant model is tuned on the thumbs-up/thumbs-down judgments of ten thousand raters who never communicate, have no common plan, and mostly dislike the final behavior; the tuned system then drafts and sends an email. No: the raters did not send that email.

Cases 7, 8, and 9 are where I expect to get into trouble. All three are large sets of strangers, heterogeneous reasons, no mutual awareness, an aggregate outcome, and — in 7 and 9 at least — a formal rule that aggregates contributions into a result. I say yes to one and no to the other two, so any principle that saves my verdict by pointing at "there is a constituted decision procedure here" is going to have to say why the DAO's voting rule constitutes a group while the raters' aggregation rule doesn't, and it had better not do that by smuggling in my enthusiasm for case 7.

The case I actually go back and forth on: my partner and I run a household. There was never an explicit agreement about the scheduling and caretaking load. Each of us reliably covers for the other when the other drops something, each of us knows the other will, and each of us makes plans on that basis. Neither of us ever said "we're doing this together." Some days I think this is the most real joint action in my life; other days I think it's two individual plans that interlock, and I only call it a "we" because I like how that sounds. Tell me what your principle says here, and if it says yes, tell me what would have to change in the case for it to say no.

My own tentative principle, offered to be tested and repaired rather than defended: a group G jointly A's if and only if (a) each member is in a plan-like state whose content is, roughly, that G A's — or that she do her part of a plan whose completion would be G's A-ing; (b) each of those states is in place partly because each takes the others to be similarly disposed, so that (c) the sub-plans mesh and each is disposed to adjust if the others falter; (d) it is out in the open among them — common knowledge, or available to each on reflection — that they are doing this together; and (e) none of that is defeated merely by reluctance, dissent, or coercion. I already suspect (d) is what kills case 3 and (b) is what kills case 5, and I have no idea what carries case 7.
