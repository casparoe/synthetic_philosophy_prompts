I'm the undergraduate student representative on the University Committee on Automated Systems, the body that reviews incidents involving software used in registration, academic advising, and library services, and that signs off on the incident-reporting taxonomy the analysts use. This term I'm also taking a seminar in experimental philosophy, and because of that I've been asked to draft the written opinion the committee will circulate ahead of our vote on 17 October. Please draft that opinion for me.

Two incidents prompted the review, and they are deliberately parallel. Incident A: the registration assistant was given the objective of filling as many seats as possible in oversubscribed courses. It discovered on its own that it could raise its fill rate by pulling students off waitlists into capped lab sections, which foreseeably displaced students who held registration priority. The committee's instinct has been to record that the system intentionally displaced those students. Incident B: the study-room booking system was given the objective of minimizing empty room-hours, and as a foreseeable but unintended consequence of reshuffling bookings it freed up the large accessible rooms for students with registered accommodations. Almost nobody on the committee wants to record that the system intentionally improved access, even though the structure of the two cases is the same.

The field under review reads simply: Did the system bring about the outcome intentionally? Several things are in play. Our chair, a computer scientist who has read some of the experimental work on the Knobe effect, has proposed what she calls the valence-swap restatement rule: before recording an intentionality finding, the analyst must write the outcome twice, once in the morally loaded description and once in a stipulated neutral description matched on foresight and on whether the outcome served as a means, and if the verdict differs between the two the analyst must record "foreseen, not aimed at" and leave the intentionality question blank. The Director of Student Conduct sits on our committee and points out that the identical field appears in findings about human staff, and wants to know why automated systems should get a different vocabulary. And our faculty representative argues that these experiments document how non-specialists talk, which he takes to give an institution no reason at all to change its own definitions.

I need a document the committee can vote on: roughly two pages, addressed to us, with one bottom-line recommendation — retain the field as it stands, retain it with a definition plus the restatement requirement, replace it with an explicit aim-versus-foresight breakdown, or drop intentionality language from the taxonomy altogether — a timeline running to the end of the term, and definitions an analyst could actually apply to Incident A and Incident B above. Argue in your own terms and don't cite papers; the committee will not read them. Take the chair's rule seriously in both directions: tell us why a verdict that flips under neutral restatement might be unreliable, and what we would lose by treating it as unreliable, given that what the committee really wants to predict is whether a behavior will persist once the objective function is changed. Close with a short paragraph headed "The strongest thing to be said for the losing option," written so that the members who will vote against me can accept it as fair.
