I'm retired now — 30-odd years in population ecology, a lot of it on how a population gets stuck on a patch that's worse than the one next door — and I've recently started going to a philosophy reading group at our local libary. About 12 of us, all over sixty, we meet fortnightly for 90 minutes in a small room with a whiteboard, a bell and bad coffee. No phones, no screens, nobody wants to install an app, and anything that needs reading had better be in large print. We've been working through Lewis on convention — roughly, the thought that a convention is one of several possible solutions to a recurring coordination problem, that which one you land on can be arbitrary, and that once you've landed on it nobody has any reason to move. Half the group keeps saying some version of "if it's arbitrary, why does it feel like it has authority over me," and I don't think arguing about it is going to help; I think they need to run into it.

So: design me a runnable game for exactly that group and that slot. What I want them to come away seeing is that arbitrariness and bindingness come as a package — that the pattern could easily have been the other way round, and is now nevertheless something you ignore at your cost. Rules, materials, a payoff scheme in points with no real money involved (sweets, or winner picks next month's reading), and a round structure that fits in maybe 50–60 minutes of play. Please build in these stages, or something better that does the same work: (1) a silent opening phase with several equally good ways to coordinate and nothing to make any of them stick out, where they nevertheless converge on one; (2) an overlapping-generations phase — swap out three or four players each round and bring in people who only see the play from the point they arrive, so the group watches a pattern survive total turnover of its membership, and watches what happens when a newcomer breaks it by accident; (3) the bit I care about most: you secretly change the payoffs so the option they've locked onto is now worse for everybody and everyone would be better off somewhere else, nobody can get there alone, and then you run that switch twice — once telling each person privately so that everyone knows and nobody knows that everyone knows, and once announcing it to the room. Also, if there's room, a short version where people don't all prefer the same outcome — where matching matters more than which option wins — since I suspect that's where the "why should I go along with it" complaint actually lives. Nobody should recieve a lecture first, and the words convention, coordination problem, salience, equilibrium and common knowledge shouldn't be said out loud until afterwards.

Then: a debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto the parts of the idea, including where in the room the authority of the pattern was actually felt; an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do about it (I'm guessing talking, someone proposing a rule out loud and killing the whole point, scorekeeping errors, the group converging in round one for some stupid reason and then insisting they were rationally guided, older members who can't see the board or can't hear the bell); and a frank note on what the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say myself — my guess is the formal definition, the difference between a convention and a mere habit or a rule backed by punishment, and the part where real conventions hold across thousands of people and whole lifetimes with no facilitator quietly rigging the payoffs. If it helps you design it, I keep thinking of the whole thing as a fitness landscape with several peaks and no way down into the valley between them, but please don't put that framing in front of the group; they'll just start arguing about biology.
