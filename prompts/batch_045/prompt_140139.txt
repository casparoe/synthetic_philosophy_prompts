I'm not a philosopher, I write short speculative fiction for a small online magazine, and I've got a story kicking around where the philosophical bit in the middle refuses to do any work. Maybe you can tell me whether the setup is even coherent before I write another bad draft.

The background as I understand it: anekantavada — real things have more aspects than any single statement can capture, so an honest claim is only true from a standpoint (a naya, I think the word is). Then the sevenfold scheme built on it: for a given claim you can say, in some respect yes, in some respect no, in some respect both, in some respect neither can be said, and then combinations of those, seven in total. The bit I actually care about is that the qualification is not a shrug. Saying in some respect is supposed to be informative: you owe people some account of which respect, or at least what kind of respect, and they're supposed to be able to check you on it.

The failure mode I want to write about I've started calling standpoint laundering, and I dont know if it has a real name. It goes like this: a claim is true only from a standpoint, it gets repeated without the standpoint, everybody downstream reasons as if they'd recieved an unqualified truth, and the thing that got dropped is exactly the thing that made it true. If there's an established term for this in the tradition or in epistemology I'd like the word, but please no reading list, no citations, no links — I just want to know if the idea holds up as I've described it. What makes it more than sloppy quoting for me is that it usually happens on purpose and for a decent reason: a properly sevenfold statement gives nobody a reason to do anything. If the flood barrier is in some respect sound and in some respect not sound, nobody signs the work order. Somebody has to drop the qualifier to get anyone to move, and they aren't being cynical, they're making the claim actionable.

The setting I've got: a city where any public body issuing a factual claim has to file a standpoint tag alongside it, and there's a small office of people whose job is to review the tags. Protagonist is a reviewer, late thirties, good at it, tired of being the person who adds qualifiers. The case that breaks her: a claim about drinking water safety after a contamination scare, filed correctly with its standpoint — something like safe by the municipal testing protocol's standard for a thirty day exposure window — which gets recirculated within hours with the qualifier stripped. The stripped version isn't false, exactly. It's just unmoored. And the person who stripped it argues, pretty plausibly, that the tagged version would have gotten people hurt and the stripped version got them to act.

Where I want you to take a side in the story instead of hedging: is there any legitimate way to act on a standpoint-relative claim without laundering it? One character should say yes — you commit from a standpoint and you name it, I'm ordering this on the hydrologist's reading and not the ecologist's and here's why I'm picking that one. Another should say that's laundering with extra steps, because the moment the order goes out the standpoint falls off anyway and all anyone hears is that the water is safe. I don't want a wise elder explaining that both are partly right. If there's a resolution, I want it to be a decision somebody pays for. Also skip the blind men and the elephant, I've read it a hundred times and it turns the doctrine into a joke about perspective.
