I'm writing the final for a third-year seminar, "Formal Systems, Incompleteness, and Mechanism," and I need one multipart question on Hofstadter's MU-puzzle. My students have all read Chapter I of Gödel, Escher, Bach and nearly all of them have memorized the takeaway — count the I's, notice it's never a multiple of 3, conclude MU is unreachable, moral: you have to jump out of the system. I want a question that separates the ones who understand what that argument is and what it is not from the ones who can recite it. We've also covered Gödel's first incompleteness theorem and the Lucas–Penrose style anti-mechanism argument, and I want the question to force students to place the MU-puzzle relative to both.

The system, as I'll reprint it on the exam: alphabet {M, I, U}; single axiom MI; rules (i) xI ⊢ xIU, (ii) Mx ⊢ Mxx, (iii) xIIIy ⊢ xUy, (iv) xUUy ⊢ xy.

Constraints: three parts, answerable in about 50 minutes, 30 marks, closed book, no calculators. Here is what I need each part to do.

Part (a), 10 marks: force a proper statement of the invariant argument as a claim about every derivable string, proved by induction on the length of a derivation, with each of the four rules checked. In particular it must not let students wave at rule (ii): doubling preserves "number of I's not divisible by 3" only because 2 is invertible mod 3, and that has to be said out loud. It should also require them to explain why this settles the puzzle when no amount of searching inside the system could — derivations are unbounded, so failure to find one after any finite search is compatible with one existing later.

Part (b), 14 marks: hand them a student answer to diagnose. The answer I have in mind is something like Dana's: "MIU is a formal system, so Gödel's incompleteness theorem applies to it, and like every formal system there are things true in it that it can't prove. MU is one of them — it's a perfectly well-formed string the system can never derive. The way to see this is to stop playing the game and jump out of the system: once you're outside, you reason about the rules instead of following them, and you can prove the number of I's never becomes a multiple of 3. That's the real moral: mathematical insight can't be mechanized, because a machine is stuck following rules inside the system the way we are, so no machine could have solved this." I want the question to require students to untangle at least these: the scope error about incompleteness (weak systems can be complete; MIU has no arithmetic, no negation, and no way to express anything about itself); the category error of calling MU a truth (MIU has no semantics, no truth predicate, only derivability); the slide from "the argument is a metatheoretic proof" to "the argument is unmechanizable" (the invariant proof itself is formalizable and machine-checkable, so the MU-puzzle gives no support for the anti-mechanist conclusion, whatever one thinks of Lucas–Penrose); and the ambiguity in "jump out," which can mean proving a theorem about the system from a metatheory, or abandoning or revising the framework — only the first is needed here.

Part (c), 6 marks: redefine rule (ii) as tripling, Mx ⊢ Mxxx, keeping the other three rules, and ask what becomes of the mod-3 argument. In this variant MU is a two-step theorem (MI ⊢ MIII ⊢ MU), so students have to identify exactly which clause the original proof depended on and say whether "MU is unreachable" is a fact about the string MU or about a system. I'd like a closing sentence asking what it would take to establish non-derivability in a system where no handy invariant presents itself.

Please write: the question exactly as I'd hand it to students, with the system reprinted, the three parts, and marks shown; then a model answer of about 700–800 words; then a marking scheme giving, per part, the moves an A answer must make, the single move that separates a B from an A, and one line on how students typically get that part wrong. Bonus credit in the scheme for an answer that notes MIU-derivability is decidable by a simple criterion rather than merely undecided by the puzzle.
