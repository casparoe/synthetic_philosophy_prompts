Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about the chapter of The Prince that takes up fortune, and I want to put the disagreement to someone rather than keep circling it. My friend's claim was that the chapter quietly re-enchants politics: once you grant that "Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less," you have let in a causal power that then has to be read, humoured, or forced, and the famous comparison of fortune to a flooded river that one should have prepared dikes and canals against in fair weather becomes advice about propitiating something rather than about engineering. I defended a deflationary reading: that fortuna is best understood as a name for whatever an agent in a given situation cannot foresee or control — a pre-statistical notion of residual variance — so that the dikes are simply exposure-reduction and the chapter is compatible with there being no such power at all. What neither of us could settle is whether the chapter's own examples bear this out, especially since its central precept, "he will be successful who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times," is immediately qualified by the observation that a man rarely can, "because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to" — which looks like advice that cannot be followed by exactly the people who need it.

The occasion is the second session of a seminar series I run in which a specialist agrees to answer strictly in character as a historical author, constrained to what that author actually wrote and to nothing after his own lifetime. Our guest will be answering as Niccolò Machiavelli, drawing only on The Prince and the Discourses — no later commentary, no secondary literature, no hindsight. Please draft eight to ten interview questions for him, each with a short note on what it is meant to force and a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give (and, where relevant, flag the dodge he is apt to use — for instance that a deflationary reading mistakes a metaphor for a mechanism, or that any counterexample is simply "the times").

Questions I specifically want covered: whether fortuna is a cause or a placeholder for the agent's own explanatory limits, and whether the dike image reduces risk or merely relocates it; whether the "conform to the times" precept is self-undermining given the fixity of natural inclination, and whether the higher-order choice between being a dike-builder and being impetuous is itself a bet on which kind of era one is in, giving a second-order regress; whether virtù is in danger of functioning as a merely retrospective success-word, in which case the "half and half" claim becomes unfalsifiable — press this by asking what outcome would count against the account, using his own hero Cesare Borgia, who did everything the book recommends and still lost, and whose catastrophe he attributes in one place to a specific political error and in another to an extreme malignity of fortune; whether the closing image of fortune as a woman who must be beaten and who yields to the audacity of the young actually recommends a strategy inconsistent with the hydraulic image of patient preparation; whether, in the set-piece case of Pope Julius II's impetuous march on Bologna, what is called fortune is really just the unanticipated reactions of other powers, so that fortuna reduces to strategic opacity and virtù to a social skill rather than a disposition; how all of this transfers to the Discourses, where he asks whether Rome owed its empire to virtù or to fortune (if partly to fortune, what sense does "imitate Rome" make?) and where necessity, hunger, and bad laws seem to manufacture virtù, making fortune not the adversary of virtù but its source; and whether the real moral of the dikes is institutional — that the only durable defence against fortune is a constitution rather than a ruler's dispositions — in which case the advice to a new prince looks like a second-best. I would also like one closing question that turns the framework on his own career after 1512, including the fact that The Prince was in part a bid for Medici favour: ask him how his own account distributes the blame in a case where the agent is himself, and be ready for the answer that this is precisely what the framework predicts. Avoid anything introductory or biographical-for-its-own-sake; every question should require him to cash out the machinery of the view rather than restate it.
