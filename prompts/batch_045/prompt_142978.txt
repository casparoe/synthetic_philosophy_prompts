I recently joined the program committee of a mid-sized science-funding foundation, and this cycle I have a real vote on whether AI safety becomes one of our three decade-long funding themes. I've been asked to draft the section of the recommendation that answers the internal dissent, and I've realized I can't do it honestly because the disagreement turns on a proverb nobody will define.

At our last meeting, one of the ML people on the committee argued: no one has died, no system has escaped, no regulator has found a runaway — so until someone produces actual evidence that current or near-future systems are dangerous, treating existential risk as a serious hypothesis is speculative, and we shouldn't reorganize a portfolio around it. A second member answered with "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," the room treated it as decisive, and the discussion moved on. I want to hand the committee something better than that. My own attempt at splitting the claim gives four cases, and I suspect the four behave completely differently: 'guaranteed silence,' where the hypothesis itself predicts that no one will be around to register the event, so the observation window is screened off by the very thing at issue; 'unexecuted search,' where the evidence might exist but is proprietary, classified, or simply nobody ran the study; 'insensitive search,' where we did look but our instruments wouldn't have caught the thing — public benchmarks and red-teaming wouldn't reveal a system that behaves well while it is being watched; and 'evidence that arrives too late,' where the discriminating observation only comes after the decision has become irreversible. I also can't tell what to do with near-misses: a near-miss is observable without anyone dying, so the absence of near-misses looks like the one piece of silence that isn't observation-screened — except that a strategically deceptive system would suppress its own near-misses, which seems to make that silence evidential only conditional on the systems not being deceptive, i.e. conditional on roughly what's in dispute.

So: give me a menu of six to eight precise theses that proverb could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the strong, where each entry states the claim as a claim about how much a specified piece of silence should move a specified prior, rather than as another slogan. For each one, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false (I have a nagging sense that on at least one natural Bayesian precisification the proverb is just false, and that someone who says it is relying on our not asking how sensitive the search was), and then say whether that reading actually rebuts my colleague's inference or only looks as though it does. Please also take the reverse side seriously: in a field with many thousands of researchers, strong career incentives to publish alarming results, and several years of lived experience with deployed systems, is there a defensible precisification on which the silence we have observed is genuine evidence and he is entitled to his conclusion — and if so, does the proverb, on any reading, block it? For each reading, add one line on what observation would flip it, since I'd like to propose what we'd have to see in the next three years to change either side's mind. No citations or reading lists, please — this goes to a committee of a biologist, two ML researchers, a philosopher of science and someone from finance, and I need it to work from first principles.
