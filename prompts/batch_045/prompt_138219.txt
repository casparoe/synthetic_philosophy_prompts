I'm retired now (was a lab chemist, mostly solid state, nothing glamourous) and four of us from the old department have been running a reading group for a couple of years — we did time, then free will, and this term we've got stuck on why there is anything at all rather than nothing. My first instinct was that physics had answered it decades ago: the vacuum isn't empty, contributions to the energy cancel, fluctuations happen, end of story. The group talked me out of that fairly quickly — apparently that only pushes the question back a step, or changes it into why there is this rather than that, which is a different question — and I can see that now.

What we ended up arguing about instead is a rule my friend Derek proposed, which he calls the no-borrowing condition: a genuine answer to the question is not allowed to help itself to anything that already exists or already holds — no laws of nature, no modality, no logic or mathematics, no possible worlds, no necessary being, no principle of sufficient reason, nothing — because each of those items is itself part of the something that was supposed to be explained, so an answer assembled out of them is circular. We spent a whole session trying to break the rule and couldn't. Everything anyone proposed smuggled something in, and even the cleanest-looking moves fail it: if you say that nothing was never really possible, you've borrowed modal facts, and if you say that some being exists of necessity, you've borrowed a thing. Derek's conclusion, which I've more or less gone along with, is that no-borrowing is close to self-evident and that it settles the matter — the question admits no answer, and a question that admits no answer even in principle isn't a real question at all, it's just a bit of grammar that looks like one, rather like asking what is north of the north pole. My grandson is in his second year reading philosophy and when I explained this to him he got a strange look and said we'd walked into something people genuinely fight about, but he refused to tell me what it's called.

So is Derek right? Granting no-borrowing, or granting whatever weakened version of it survives scrutiny, is giving up on the question the only honest option left — and if you don't think so, what is actually left to appeal to that doesn't cheat? I'd rather be told which premise of ours is wrong than be humoured, and if the verdict is that the condition is fine but the conclusion doesn't follow, I'd like to know exactly which step fails and why.
