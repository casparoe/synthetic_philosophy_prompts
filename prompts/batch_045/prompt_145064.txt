I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on the foundations of rational choice, and I would be grateful for a careful adjudication of an exchange that took place in our decision-theory reading group last week. The two participants were Marcus, a postdoc in economics, and Nadia, a fifth-year in my department. I was chairing the session, contributed nothing of substance, and came away genuinely unsure who had the better of it; nothing below should be read as my having taken a side.

The topic was the Allais pairs, set out in the usual way: A pays $1M for sure, while B pays $1M with probability 0.89, $5M with probability 0.10, and nothing with probability 0.01; C pays $1M with probability 0.11 and nothing with probability 0.89, while D pays $5M with probability 0.10 and nothing with probability 0.90. The familiar pattern is A preferred to B and D preferred to C, and since the two pairs differ only in that a 0.89-probability branch pays $1M in the first case and nothing in the second, independence requires the two choices to go the same way.

(1) Marcus: this has not been shown to be a violation of independence at all. Rewrite all four options as two-stage lotteries: a first coin lands heads with probability 0.89, on which you receive $1M (in A and B) or nothing (in C and D); on tails you proceed to a second-stage prospect that is either $1M for sure (in A and C) or a 10/11 chance at $5M (in B and D). On that description the two pairs pose exactly the same question at the second stage, and the Allais pattern consists in the answer to that question varying with what the first-stage branch would have paid. What the data therefore show is a failure of what I will call branch-independence — insensitivity to what happens off the path you actually end up on — not a defect in the comparison of risks. Nor is branch-dependence incoherent: there is no money pump for it, because in any genuinely sequential version of the problem you would be told which branch you were in and would then choose consistently.

(2) Nadia: the relocation is fair but it does no work, for two reasons. First, the two-stage description buys its parallelism by assuming part of what is contested: to write A as a lottery that pays $1M whether the coin lands heads or tails, you must assume that $1M for sure is equivalent to a coin flip that pays $1M either way, and that is the reduction principle for compound lotteries, which is precisely one of the things in dispute. Second, even granting the description, branch-independence is no more optional than independence. If the first coin can be tossed and revealed in time to act on it, then in the heads branch the members of each pair are indistinguishable, so the whole decision collapses onto the second-stage comparison — and that comparison is the same comparison in both pairs. To reject independence is to say that your preference between $1M for sure and a 10/11 shot at $5M reverses depending on what you would have received in a branch you now know you are not in.

(3) Marcus: that is consequentialism asserted rather than argued, and consequentialism is not a constraint of rationality. Agents may care about what might have been: regret and disappointment are real costs, and models that price them are coherent alternatives to expected utility rather than descriptions of a malfunction. Your sequential argument also assumes that being told which branch you are in leaves your desires fixed. For an agent with counterfactual emotions it does not. Learning that you are in the tails branch of a lottery whose heads branch paid $1M is itself a change in your situation, and it can rationally change what you want.

(4) Nadia: then test the mechanism you propose against the case. Compare the options state by state in the two-stage form, as regret-style models do. In both pairs the two options agree on the heads branch; on a 0.10 slice you get $5M instead of $1M, and on a 0.01 slice you get nothing instead of $1M. The statewise comparison is therefore identical across the two pairs, and any view that prices your outcome against the outcome of the option you declined must treat them alike: it cannot generate A over B together with D over C. So regret about the road not taken is not available as the explanation. The only remaining story of that kind is disappointment measured against your own prior expectation, which is much lower in the second pair — and that is a poor basis for a normative view, since it makes your preferences hostage to how much hope you were encouraged to feel before choosing, and exploitable by anyone who can move your expectations first.

(5) Marcus: that is an objection to one causal story, not to the coherence of branch-dependence; and the exploitability charge is one that expected-utility theory itself escapes only by stipulating which sequences of trades count as pumps. The defensible conclusion from the data is more modest: people have a marked taste for certainty, and it shows up wherever a sure outcome is on offer. If the whole departure from independence is a premium attached to probability 1, then it is local, stable and arguably reasonable — a feature of our attitudes to risk rather than a failure of means–end coherence — and what looks bad is the axiom that refuses to let it count.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered moves, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and why; flag cases where a move succeeds against the objection it is answering but fails against one that was never made, and where one of them concedes something they did not need to concede. Then say what both of them are missing, whether there is any reformulation of the case that would discriminate between the competing diagnoses on offer (and if none would, why not), and who is more right overall.
