I'm in a reading group on interpretability and we keep going round in circles about whether neural nets really have representations or whether that's just convenient shorthand, so I want to try and force the question with a concrete case. Take Anthropic's Golden Gate Claude demo from mid-2024: as I remember it they trained sparse autoencoders on Claude 3 Sonnet's internal activations, got a big dictionary of features, found one that lit up on mentions and pictures of the Golden Gate Bridge, and then cranked it up so the model started dragging the bridge into answers to unrelated questions — how to spend ten dollars, write a love story — and when asked what it imagines it looks like, it said it imagines it looks like the bridge. (If I've got details wrong, correct me in passing, but dont make that the main event.) Our group has sort of converged on three buckets: the realist, who says the SAE discovered a representation that was already in there and the intervention just reveals it; the illusionist, who says we projected the concept onto whatever direction fell out of our optimizer and the model has a bridge representation in about the sense a cloud has a face; and the instrumentalist, who says representation-talk is fine but its entire cash value is: here is a lever, pushing it does this. I want you to work all three and be disciplined about the mapping — what exactly is the thing doing the representing, what makes some direction in vector space about the bridge rather than about orange metal or San Francisco tourism, and what would count as evidence for each of them.

Three things I especially want chewed on. First, the bridge feature isn't a neuron; it's a direction in a dictionary learned by a second model trained on the first model's activations, and as far as I can tell two SAEs trained with different seeds or different dictionary sizes give you different features with no clean one-to-one match between them. Does that make representation something chosen rather than discovered, or is it just the usual messiness of an imperfect instrument? Second, one friend insists the real test isn't can you intervene but what the intervention does: if the model genuinely represents the bridge, turning the feature up should change behavior in bridge-appropriate ways — the toll, the fog, the traffic — rather than just spraying the word bridge across every answer. She calls it tracking versus spraying and says a purely causal story can't even state the difference; another friend says she's smuggling in exactly the content that realism was supposed to explain. Third, the model tells you in words that it imagines it is the bridge. Does the self-report add anything over the behavioral effects, and should it matter that the self-report is itself produced by mechanisms the intervention is now controlling?

Then run it the other direction. Does this episode confirm the realist, embarrass her, or is it a case nobody in the debate anticipated — a case where we have a precise causal handle on something and still don't know whether to call it content? Should it change how we think about the older single-unit demos, where people found one neuron that tracked something and forced it on and off, or is the scaled-up dictionary version a different animal entirely? If illusionism is right, what exactly are interpretability researchers doing when they publish a feature, and why is it worth doing? And where a frame goes silent — where it has nothing to say about which of these questions even makes sense — say so instead of wringing an answer out of it; I'd rather have the silences marked than a tidy verdict.
