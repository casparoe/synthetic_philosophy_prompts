so i help run a monthly philosophy-and-beer night at the hackerspace downtown (mix of backend devs, two philosophy grad students, and whoever wanders in), and last month the simulation thing came up and we spent two hours going in circles. Three different people said "yeah I think we're in a simulation" and meant three completely different things: one meant the universe is computational in some boring sense, one meant the base-rate argument about astronomically many ancestor-simulations goes through, and one meant that if its true nothing we do matters. So for next month I want a handout that sorts people by case rather than by label — roughly 8 to 10 questions, each a concrete scenario with two or three options to pick between, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto ten or so named positions, small enough to print on two sides of A4 and work through in a 90 minute session.

The angle I care about is that the tree has to force separate commitments on three things we kept collapsing: whether a simulated mind is even possible (substrate-independence / gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement with functionally identical hardware, that kind of case), the self-location step (given that many sims exist, does that make it likely that *you* specifically are in one — how you handle a billion penny-cost copies of the same century, or a sim where only you are fully simulated and everyone else is a cheap behavioural model), and the significance question (does it change anything if its true — e.g. the sim that gets shut off after ten simulated minutes because the researchers got their data, or the civilisation that decides running sims full of suffering would be monstrous, or finding a lattice artifact in high-energy cosmic rays). Leaves I want to be reachable: the full package that takes the famous trilemma from Bostrom's 2003 Philosophical Quarterly paper and adds the indifference step to get "almost certainly simulated"; the person who accepts the disjunction but refuses to pick a disjunct; someone who denies simulated minds have experiences at all; someone who says posthuman civs couldn't or wouldn't run very many; someone who says sims are common but ancestor-sims aren't, so we'd be in a research sim or a game instead; someone for whom "is it a simulation?" collapses into "the universe just is a computation"; someone who rejects the move from many sims to probably-me on reference-class or sampling grounds; someone who says fine, probably, but nothing follows; and someone who thinks the whole question isn't well-formed enough to have a view about. Build it from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they allow — then locate the real positions in it, and flag any coherent combination that nobody actually occupies with a one-line guess at why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Also flag any two leaves your tree provably can't separate, and whether some further case could pull them apart. Every question should be answerable cold by someone who's never heard the labels, and no question should just be "do you think the universe is a computer" — if a branch only makes sense to someone who already knows the view it leads to, rewrite it. Each leaf gets a name (invent one, or just say "unnamed" if nobody defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please don't tack on a reading list or footnotes, nobody in this group is going to do homework.
