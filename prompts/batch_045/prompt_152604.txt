Setup so we're using the same words: by prioritarianism I mean the view that outcomes are ranked by the sum of a concave, increasing transform of each person's welfare — take f(w) = √w as the working example, so an outcome's value is Σ √wᵢ. Everyone knows the selling point: unlike egalitarianism, prioritarianism never says a levelling down is an improvement, because any increase in anyone's welfare raises the sum.

Here's the argument (mine mostly, though a friend in the departent helped me get step 7 into shape):

(1) No-levelling-down: an outcome A isn't better than an outcome B if nobody is better off in A than in B and someone is worse off in A.
(2) That's the levelling-down objection, and it's what sinks egalitarianism: if equality has intrinsic value, then moving from (12, 8) to (8, 8) — the better-off person down to 8, the worse-off person unchanged — can be an improvement overall, even though nobody gains and somebody loses. That's unacceptable, so egalitarianism is out.
(3) Prioritarianism escapes this. Since f is increasing, levelling down always lowers Σ f(wᵢ). No levelling down is ever an improvement. That's supposed to be the whole point: you keep what's attractive aboutegalitarianismwithout paying for it.
(4) But we don't only rank sure outcomes; we rank prospects, i.e. choices under uncertainty. And the natural extension of (1) to prospects is: a prospect A isn't better than B if nobody is better off under A and someone is worse off — where being better off under a prospect just is having higher expected welfare, since that's what it is for an uncertain option to be better for you.
(5) Now the case that breaks it. Two people, Ann and Ben, √ weights. P: (4, 4) for sure. Q: a fair coin, heads (10, 0), tails (0, 10). Under Q each person's expected welfare is 5; under P it's 4. So by (4) nobody is better off under P and both are worse off. But the prioritarian values are P = √4 + √4 = 4 and Q = ½(√10 + 0) + ½(0 + √10) = √10 ≈ 3.16. Prioritarianism prefers P: a prospect that is worse for everyone.
(6) So the escape in (3) only works for sure outcomes. And essentially every real policy choice involves uncertainty, so the escape covers almost nothing.
(7) Worse: if the thought behind (1) is that betterness has to be traceable to somebody's being better off, and you hold onto that in full generality — outcomes and prospects — then combined with expected utility you get pushed the whole way to utilitarianism (the Harsanyi-style aggregation result: respect everyone's preferences under risk, add the usual rationality constraints, and social value is a weighted sum of expected utilities). So the levelling-down objection doesn't take you from egalitarianism to prioritarianism; run honestly it takes you to plain utilitarianism, which is where we started and which none of us want.

I dont accept the conclusion, and I'd rather keep (3) than (4) — the sure-outcome escape feels like the entire reason to be a prioritarian. But I can't say which step is doing the illicit work, and I'm scared that rejecting (4) loses me (1) as well, in which case I've got no objection left against the egalitarian.
