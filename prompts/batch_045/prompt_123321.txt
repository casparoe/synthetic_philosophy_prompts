The case they agreed to work through is ours, and the numbers are ones I've simplified and partly invented, so treat them as stipulated:

1. In 2022 our district estimated the heritability of a sixth-grade reading-comprehension composite at about 0.51, from a twin comparison, at a time when the nine elementary schools differed wildly in funding and class size.
2. The district then ran three years of free one-on-one tutoring for every struggling reader. Average scores rose by roughly 0.7 standard deviations, the spread of scores shrank by about 30 percent, and when the estimate was recomputed on a comparable sample it came out near 0.66 — higher than before, even though reading improved for almost everyone.
3. Someone also computed a heritability for the gain itself, meaning the difference between each child's 2022 and 2025 scores, and got roughly 0.40.
4. A district committee then proposed tracking a new composite it calls reading resilience: scoring in the top quartile of readers despite being in the bottom quartile of a home-literacy questionnaire. They want a heritability estimate for that too.
5. Finally, my own contribution is a thought experiment I made up, which I want them to address directly: the double-shuffle test. Imagine two impossible experiments. In one, you hold every child's environment exactly as it is and randomly reshuffle which genome goes with which family and school. In the other, you hold every genome in place and randomly reshuffle which child gets which school, tutoring slot, neighborhood, and peer group. Ask whether heritability is supposed to predict the size of the first effect, the second, both, or neither.
