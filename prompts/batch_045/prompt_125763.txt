I am a retired experimental physicist — nearly forty years in solid-state chemistry, most of them at a single institute — and for the last two years I have been going to a philosophy reading group at the local adult-education centre, largely because I wanted to understand what my colleagues thought they were doing when they argued about priority. We are working slowly through Nietzsche's 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, with its three ways of relating to the past — the monumental, the antiquarian and the critical — and I came to it because of something I had noticed in my own field for decades and never had a word for. Two episodes, which I will describe loosely, since I have no wish to start an argument with former colleagues. First: my subfield holds an annual lecture named after the man who made the measurement that started everything, and every speaker retells the story as the moment the field became quantitative; the original paper, which I read as a student, reported something much narrower and was in part wrong, and it is now routinely cited as support for a claim its authors explicitly declined to make. Nobody in that room experiences the mismatch as falsification. It is experienced as fidelity, and the story visibly does the work of making new graduate students feel that great work is still possible here. Second: the founding model of the field was known to be quantitatively wrong by the mid-1980s, is still taught in the first lecture of the introductory course, and still functions as the thing people measure themselves against; and when, every decade or so, some of us point out that the founder held views we now reject, the monument is not damaged by the criticism — it comes back shinier, the criticism having somehow been absorbed as part of what makes him interesting.

What I cannot fit is the shape of the change over time. The monument is not stable. In the 1970s the founding figure of the field was the theorist who built the model; by the 1990s it was the experimentalist who killed it; now it is a younger contemporary of theirs whose neglected paper is presented as the true origin — and each generation's textbooks and after-dinner speeches present their own selection as though it had always been the selection, so that the field reveres a past it continuously rewrites and has no recollection of having rewritten. Three details seem to me to matter and to pull in different directions. The veneration is not credence: nobody believes the founding model, so what is being preserved is a use rather than a truth. The monument can be built out of a known failure, which seems to rule out the progress-to-the-present narrative that my one historian friend immediately suggested under the name of Whig history. And the critical moment, the periodic condemnation, does not break the past the way I expected from Nietzsche's third category; it renovates it. One member of my group said this is simply monumental history and that Nietzsche had already described it; another said it is not, because the point of the monument there is to spur emulation by presenting greatness as something that was once possible and might therefore be possible again, whereas what actually gets transmitted in my field is the bare shape — there was a founder — with the person filling it essentially interchangeable.

So my question is a carving question rather than a naming question, though I would like a name if a good one exists. Is this configuration something that already has an established concept, and if so which one, and how exactly does my case sit with respect to it and to its nearest neighbours? If several established notions are candidates, tell me for each what it would explain here and where it goes wrong, and which fits best and why. If nothing established fits properly, say so plainly and give me a term with a definition tight enough to do some work, together with the boundary cases: is it the same phenomenon when a committee changes the story deliberately for funding or political reasons as when it drifts unnoticed over coffee; does it require a whole discipline, or can one laboratory's oral tradition have it; and what would not count — I take it that revising a story simply because somebody found the letters in an attic is a different thing, even though the visible change looks identical. I would also like to know whether any part of my reading group's disagreement is merely verbal, and if so which part and why. Please do not answer with a reading list or references: my library access is a small town library with slow interloan, and I would much rather have the distinctions, the misfits and the edges than a set of things to go and look up.
