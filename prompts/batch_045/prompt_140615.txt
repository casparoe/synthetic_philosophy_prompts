I write fiction for a living, and I am fluent in the vocabulary of my craft — point of view, focalization, the difference between who speaks and who sees, free indirect style, the implied author, the seam, dramatic irony, voice, the unreliable narrator, the reader's construction of a world from a text. I have no training in philosophy of mind, and the jargon of that field means nothing to me. But I have a professional stake in a question that seems to belong to it, and I would like the question translated into terms I can actually work with.

The stake is this. For two years I have been completing a novel begun by a collaborator who died halfway through. He wrote the odd chapters, I wrote the even ones; we never conferred about the plot, and his manuscript breaks off in the middle of a scene I had to finish without knowing where he meant it to go. A copy editor smoothed the joins. Readers now report a single narrative voice, and several have praised the book's unity of vision. I know that this unity is an artifact, and I also know that I cannot say exactly whose book this is. So: does the finished volume have one narrator or two? I do not want the legal or the publishing answer. I want the answer that bears on the following case.

In patients who have had the bundle of fibres connecting the cerebral hemispheres surgically severed, information presented to one side of the visual field reaches only the hemisphere on the opposite side. Language is housed in the left hemisphere in most people, so an object shown to the right hemisphere cannot be named aloud, though the left hand can often pick it out from a set of hidden alternatives. More strikingly, when the right hemisphere initiates an action, the speaking left hemisphere frequently supplies a rationale for it that is invented on the spot and that the patient appears to believe. Philosophers have drawn very different conclusions from this. As I understand them, the main ones are:

(1) There are now two conscious subjects in one skull, where before the operation there was one.
(2) There remains a single subject, but that subject's conscious field has come apart; or, in a variant, a single subject alternates between the two hemispheres, switching rather than splitting.
(3) Nothing multiplied and nothing came apart, because the unity was never there: the brain was always a competition of parallel processes, and what we called a single stream of consciousness was the after-the-fact story told by whichever system has access to speech. On this view the operation only removes the conditions under which the story is continuously maintained.
(4) The question "one subject or two?" has no determinate answer. Integration comes in degrees, there is no sharp line to be drawn, and there may be no fact of the matter where the line falls.
(5) Even granting all of the above, the subject's own report is what it is: what matters for responsibility, for testimony, for what we owe the patient, is what the person says and does, not a metaphysical headcount.

Please translate each of these into the vocabulary of fiction, one at a time, as faithfully as you can: narrators, focalization, voice, the implied author, embedded narration, the seam, the unreliable narrator, the reader. For each one, tell me three things: what translates cleanly, what translates only if you distort it, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. I want the translation to be honest. If a claim only survives because my vocabulary quietly supplies it with an author, a reader, or a binding, say so rather than presenting it as a translation — that is exactly the kind of smuggling I am trying to detect.

Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the interesting part. I have some specific suspicions I would like you to settle. In my vocabulary the unity of a text is a relational matter — it depends on a reader, or on an editor's decision, or on a convention about what counts as one book — whereas the unity of a consciousness is supposed to be intrinsic, not conferred from outside; is that difference load-bearing or is it something my vocabulary simply cannot see? Fictional unreliability presupposes a truth that the narrator misreports; is there such a truth when a hemisphere confabulates, or does the analogy smuggle in a fact of the matter that the case lacks? Who, if anyone, plays the role of the reader inside a skull — the experimenter, the speaking hemisphere, nobody? Which of these positions has no counterpart at all in a craft vocabulary built around artefacts that somebody made and somebody else reads?

Finally, the verdict. Two things I would like to keep if I can: that there is a real difference between a book that is unified and a book that merely reads as unified, since my entire craft rests on that difference not being a convention; and that the chapters my collaborator wrote are genuinely his and not mine, which is what makes the question of the book's voice more than a puzzle about printing. Tell me whether the residue you have found is a defect in the vocabulary of fiction — something it cannot say and ought to be able to — or a sign that the philosophical question was less sharp than its practitioners take it to be. And if anything in the translation can be carried back into my own work, tell me what it licenses me to say about the book I have finished.
