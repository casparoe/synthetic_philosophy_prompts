Our interdisciplinary reading group — historians, a couple of political theorists, two people who build long-run historical indicators, and me — has spent about two years on a single question: whether the world is becoming less violent. We have not moved. I want a diagnosis of why, not a verdict on the question, and I want it aimed at the shape of the disagreement rather than at its content.

Here is where we are. One side points to per-capita measures: homicide rates per 100,000 per year, battle deaths as a share of world population, the disappearance of judicial torture and of lethal crowd punishment, the long-run fall in interpersonal killing within state societies. They insist that absolute body counts mislead, because the twentieth century had more people in it and better weapons, and that any honest normalization shows decline. The other side replies that the category has been gerrymandered: the series track the kinds of violence that states record and that look like one person striking another, while excluding what they variously call structural, slow, administrative, colonial, and domestic violence, along with violence against animals. They add that the data for small-scale and non-state societies are thin enough that the baseline from which decline is measured is largely a construction, and that choosing per-capita normalization in the first place is itself a moral decision, not a neutral one. When the critics raise structural violence they are told that this is a different topic. When the declinists produce a normalized series they are told that they picked the denominator. Each side thinks the other has changed the subject, which is usually the signature of a dispute whose structure nobody has described.

My own hypothesis, which I offer only as one: the live disagreement is not really about data, and not merely about the word 'violence' either. It is (a) a disagreement about which harms are admissible into a narrative of moral progress, which is substantive but rests on commitments neither side argues for, plus (b) a genuinely formal question about normalization and aggregation that nobody has isolated, and which may be doing most of the work.

To test that, and to take the empirical question off the table so we can see what remains, I want you to work with the following device. Suppose we had a complete audited ledger: for every person alive at every date, every harm event they undergo, with its mechanism, whether any agent intended it, its duration, its severity in some interpersonally comparable unit, and the counterfactual baseline for that person at that time. No sampling error, no missing centuries, no coding disputes. In that situation, please tell me:

1. Is there a fact of the matter about whether violence declined? If yes, what fixes it; if no, where exactly does the indeterminacy enter?
2. Separate the remaining disagreement into its parts — the extension of the category, the choice of measure, and the normative reading of the trend — and say for each whether it is verbal, substantive-but-arguable, or bedrock. In particular, isolate the formal question about measures. I have noticed that our group collapses several quantities that can diverge: events per person-year; the probability that a person is ever a victim in a lifetime, which can rise when lifespans lengthen even as the annual rate falls; the share of deaths that are violent, which moves when competing causes of death move; total harm-weighted years, which trades off against the falling lethality of assaults given better medicine; and rates per unit of exposure or interaction rather than per head. Work out which of these can come apart, and say how much of the apparent empirical dispute is really a choice among them.
3. Consider a case the ledger might contain: intentional interpersonal violence falls to nearly zero, while deliberate, foreseeable harm of equal magnitude is administered through administrative channels — no one strikes anyone, no one is shot, and the harm-years are unchanged. Does the decline thesis come out true there? Answering yes or no, and saying why, seems to me to locate the real fault line more sharply than another round of data.
4. Tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. One of our number proposes that we stop asking for a single scalar and instead test for componentwise dominance across a fixed vector of harm types, reporting exactly where the ranking flips depending on weights. Is that progress, or capitulation? Would a decomposition, a theorem about the divergence of the measures in (2), a redefinition, or an argument that the question is ill-posed do better? And if you think no progress should be expected, say what kind of question it is and why stalemate is the rational endpoint.

Please argue for the diagnosis rather than surveying positions, and press on my hypothesis where it is wrong. There is no need to attribute views to named authors, to reconstruct the literature, or to cite anything: I want the structure of the impasse, and a reason to think it has the structure you say it has.
