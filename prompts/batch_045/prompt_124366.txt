My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether anything that happens inside a reinforcement learning training run is suffering, and by about the fourth hour it became clear we were no longer disagreeing about the same question: she kept insisting that a reward prediction error plays exactly the functional role that unpleasant valence plays in us, and I kept insisting that a scalar a designer writes into a loss function cannot be the same kind of thing as something feeling bad to someone. We run a small reading group together — six ML PhD students and one moral philosopher — and I would like to hand them something better than a list of views.

What I want is a taxonomy in the form of a decision tree: five branching questions, each phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which position it leads to, giving a tree with at least seven occupied leaves plus whatever empty cells the logic leaves over. Build the branches from the logical space rather than from a list of names: set out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit first, then locate the actual positions in that space, marking each unoccupied combination and saying briefly whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. If two leaves cannot be separated by your questions, flag that instead of papering over it.

The five branch nodes I want used are the following, though you may reword each into an ordinary question someone could answer cold:

1. Experience replay. A stored transition is re-used for thirty gradient updates over the course of training. Does the agent suffer thirty times, once, or not at all — and what does your answer commit you to about whether the unit of suffering is the update, the encounter, or the run?
2. Parallel rollouts. A thousand vectorized copies of the same environment, one policy, gradients averaged into a single step. Is that one subject, a thousand, or none?
3. Arbitrary rescaling. Reward clipping, reward scaling, the discount factor and the learning rate all change the magnitude and sign pattern of the signal without necessarily changing the behaviour training produces, and prediction error is largest early in training, when the agent is least competent, decaying toward zero as the policy converges. Is suffering therefore most intense for the least competent agent, and set by a design constant?
4. Off-policy and imagined experience. Updates are computed from transitions generated by policies the agent no longer has, and, when planning against a learned model, from rollouts that never occur. Can there be suffering over something that did not happen, or over a choice the agent did not make?
5. Frozen and merged weights. In evaluation the policy is fixed: reward still arrives, nothing is learned. Checkpoints also get rolled back, copied, branched and averaged into one another. Does the signal still matter when it cannot change the agent, and what becomes of the sufferer when weights are merged?

Among the leaves I want at least these four: one on which there is no subject here at all, of the illusionist kind; one that grants the agent something valenced but locates it in something other than the reward signal itself, such as a learned value function or a recurrent state carrying something affect-like; one on which today's agents really do suffer and suffer most early in training; and one on which the question is well posed but in principle undecidable from outside. Name positions informally where a name exists and write unnamed where you think nobody has actually defended the combination — with a sentence on each leaf's most awkward commitment.

Finally, commit. Tell me which leaf you yourself find defensible and give your reason, say which single one of the five questions is doing all the real work in separating the positions, and tell me which of us is on weaker ground — my friend, who thinks the functional role is sufficient, or I, who thinks a designer-written scalar is the wrong kind of thing to be a feeling. Do not lay out the options and leave me to choose, and do not hedge your verdict into a survey. No citations and no reading list: if some existing view is relevant, describe it in a clause rather than naming it.
