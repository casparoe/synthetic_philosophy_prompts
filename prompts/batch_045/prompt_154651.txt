I write real-time control software, and my team has now had the same argument perhaps a dozen times in different guises. I would like to know whether the thing we keep arguing about has a settled name and an established way of being carved up, or whether we are reinventing, badly, something that already exists. I am not after a reading list; I am after the carving.

The recurring shape is this. We have a module with a precise specification: it takes inputs of a certain kind and returns outputs, and the specification fixes the correct answer for every input, including inputs the device will never in fact receive. Then someone proposes a substitute, and we argue about whether the substitute "does the same thing" or merely "produces the same answers." Four cases, with the verdicts my team actually reached:

(1) A clean reimplementation in a different language, using entirely different internal steps and intermediate quantities, correct across the whole specified range including values we never test. Verdict, uncontested: yes, it does the same thing.

(2) A learned approximator fitted to the original's behavior. It matches within tolerance across the operating range, and it also generalizes correctly to inputs far outside anything it was fitted on, including inputs we construct only in order to stress it. Nothing inside it corresponds to any intermediate quantity named in the specification. Verdict, contested: I say yes; a colleague says it computes nothing at all, it estimates.

(3) A precomputed table covering every input the device will encounter during its service life, plus a fallback that behaves safely outside that set. It agrees with the original on every input that will ever actually occur. Verdict, uncontested the other way: no, it is playing back answers.

(4) A table like (3) together with a small routine that detects an off-table input and, on those occasions, derives a new entry and caches it. Verdict: we cannot agree, and we cannot say what would settle it. A related variant: a system containing a real copy of the original, buried under a great deal of idle machinery, which consults that copy whenever queried.

What I think I see is that our verdict tracks neither agreement on actual inputs — (3) agrees perfectly and still loses — nor correspondence between the internal steps and the specification's steps, since (2) has none and often wins. What it seems to track is how the substitute would behave on inputs that have not occurred and will not occur, and on worlds slightly unlike ours in ways that do not change the function itself. Two further features bother me. First, the verdict is retrospective: we only learn whether a substitute really did the thing when an input arrives from outside the range we designed for, and until then we are guessing. Second, the verdict seems to shift with what we want it for — certification, assigning blame after a failure, explaining the device to a regulator — even when the box on the bench is unchanged.

So my questions. Does the distinction we are reaching for have an established name? People have gestured me toward two neighbouring debates — one about whether a system that reproduces a process's effects thereby does the thing itself or only imitates it, and one about whether any physical system can be said to realize any formal description you like — and neither seems to be quite ours, though I cannot say precisely why. If one of them is ours, say which and say where the fit is imperfect; if ours sits between them, say that. If nothing established fits, coin a term and define it.

Either way, I want more than a label. I want to know: what exactly the property is; how it differs from mere agreement on all actual inputs, from robustness, and from generalizing to new cases; whether it is a property of a system alone or of a system together with a range of inputs and a purpose; and how it should classify cases (4) and the buried-copy variant, which I offer as boundary cases. If part of our disagreement turns out to be only about words, tell me which part.

One reason I care. The same move appears when people argue about brains: someone claims that if each neuron were replaced by a small device that does what that neuron does, the result would still think, and someone else replies that the replacement would only be a playback of what a brain does. That exchange seems to me to turn on exactly our distinction, and I would like to know whether there is a real distinction there or only our own unclearness, projected onto a bigger question.
