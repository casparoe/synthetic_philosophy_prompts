I convene a small departmental podcast on punishment and moral responsibility. Last month's episode was with a free will skeptic who argued that, since no one is ever deserving in the basic desert-entailing sense, the only response to criminal behavior the state can justify is something like the public health–quarantine model: containment, treatment, and prevention licensed by a right of self-defense or harm-prevention, never by the claim that the offender deserves to suffer. Next month's guest is a compatibilist in the broadly Dennettian tradition who thinks this throws away something worth keeping: that agents who act for reasons, are responsive to information and to the expectations of their community, and could have done otherwise in the sense that matters are genuinely deserving of blame and punishment, and that a practice of holding them responsible is not merely a useful regulatory device but a fitting response to what they did. He is formidably good at batting away objections with the reply that they presuppose an incoherent folk picture of ultimate self-origination that nobody should want, and I would like a set of questions that makes that deflection progressively harder to use rather than letting him restate the view.

The questions I want should press at the joints where this dispute actually bites, and I have a few devices of my own I would like built into them. First, the control question: I want him to say what exactly the information-sensitive, reasons-responsive control he appeals to adds beyond a forward-looking story about shaping future behavior — why is it desert-supporting rather than merely excuse-blocking — and what he makes of the fact that the skeptic can grant all of it while denying that it closes the gap (that an agent's being sensitive to reasons still seems to be something that happens to him, courtesy of heredity, upbringing, and circumstance). Second, a case I have been using in my seminar: a jurisdiction adopts rolling review, in which every sentence is continuously revised downward the moment an offender's predicted dangerousness drops, with no regard to how serious the original offense was. A desert theorist cannot accept this, since desert is fixed at the time of the act; I want a question that forces him to say whether that reveals desert as doing genuine independent work or merely as a device for blocking leniency, together with a follow-up for when he says the two come apart only rarely. Third, a paired-institutions case: two facilities with identical deprivations and identical lengths of confinement, one labeled punishment and one labeled quarantine, each justified solely by containment. What, if anything, morally distinguishes them, and can his account explain why conditions may not be made harsher than containment requires — with the follow-up that if it cannot, the quarantine model constrains cruelty less well than retributivism does, and if it can, it seems to be covertly relying on a desert-free notion of proportionality. I would also like one question on the evidential trade involved in swapping act-based desert for risk-based prediction, given that actuarial risk instruments are imperfect and demographically skewed, and one on the asymmetry of praise: skeptics tend to argue about blame, but giving up desert seems to cost credit, esteem, and supererogatory merit too, and I want him to say whether desert is symmetric for him and what we lose if it is surrendered on the credit side. Finally, one methodological question: if ordinary verdicts about responsibility are soaked in the very desert assumptions in dispute, whose verdict settles whether desert exists — the folk concept, the best revisionary reconstruction, or something else — and why.

Please give me eight to ten questions in the order you would ask them over roughly forty-five minutes, each with a brief note (a sentence or two) on the answer he is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer, escalating so that the later questions close off the escape routes opened by the earlier ones. The last question should ask what kind of evidence — empirical findings about lay attitudes, cross-cultural variation, a society raised from birth on quarantine-model institutions, or something more conceptual — could actually count against his position, and what he would say if such a society turned out to have no more crime and no erosion of interpersonal trust. Please keep everything self-contained: no questions that depend on his knowing a bibliography, no citation or reading-list prompts, and nothing biographical or generic; I want questions he can answer on the spot, from the content of the view itself.
