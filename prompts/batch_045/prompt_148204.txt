I'm taking a seminar on the metaphysics of virtual worlds right now and I keep feeling like the positions we've been given are talking past each other, so I want to run them on a case I actually know something about: the EVE Online heist everybody calls Black Sunday (I think that's the name) from the mid-2000s. The facts as I remember them, and please just grant them even if I've mangled a detail: a group of players founded a small corporation and spent something like ten months infiltrating a much bigger, richer, mostly-industrial player corp — running mining ops, being useful and chatty, getting one of their own voted into a director role with hangar access — and then on one coordinated evening they ganked the target corp's CEO, cleaned out the corp wallets and hangars, resigned en masse, and made off with in-game assets that were reportedly worth a five-figure sum in real money on grey-market currency sites at the time. As I understand it the devs ruled it legitimate gameplay and nobody was banned, while the game's own terms of service say everything in the game belongs to the company and selling it for real cash is forbidden. Take all of that as given.

We've been handed three accounts of what a virtual object even is: (a) code-reductionism, on which talk of a stolen battleship is just shorthand for patterns in a database plus what players were disposed to do; (b) institutionalism, on which virtual objects are status objects like money or chess positions — real, but constituted by rules plus uptake, so nothing "over and above" the practice yet not eliminable either; (c) fictionalism/pretense, on which the assets are props in a game of make-believe and the only real facts are facts about what the players imagined and felt. I want a careful mapping, not a vibe: on each account, was there an object that changed hands, and was there a theft? What exactly did the people who'd sunk ten months of real evenings into that corp lose — and does it matter that EVE losses are permanent in a way that, say, a respawning death in WoW isn't? Then the bit I can't get straight: whose institution is doing the constituting? The EULA says the stuff is CCP's; the grey market priced it in dollars; the player community plainly treated the hangar as the corp's. Does institutionalism just fracture into competing institutions here, or does that show it was the wrong kind of account? And on harms, two of my classmates have tests I half-invented in my notes: the substitution test — if the identical year of trust-building and betrayal happened over email in a volunteer organisation, we'd call it a real harm, so what work is "it's a game" doing? — and the permission defence — the game's rules permit the move, permitted moves don't wrong the player, as when I take your queen.

A couple more things, then I'll shut up. First, the reverse direction: which of the three accounts does this episode actually confirm, which does it embarrass, and which does it simply not speak to — say so explicitly where a theory is silent rather than squeezing an answer out of it. Second, settle an argument: my study buddy says the harm was obviously real because the victims were genuinely upset, and I think that's too quick, since people get genuinely upset about novels and about their football team losing, and nobody concludes that fictional characters were wronged. Which way does the distress datum actually cut, and does your verdict change for a five-minute gank versus a ten-month con, or for a world where the ships come back?
