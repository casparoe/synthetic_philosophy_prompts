Q4. Outside mathematics, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem is often reported as showing (i) that no formal system can capture all of arithmetic, and (ii) that the human mind can do something no machine can.

(a) State the first incompleteness theorem as precisely as you can, being explicit about what a theory must be like for it to apply.

(b) Assess claim (ii).

(c) A physicist says to you: "My field is after a theory of everything — a finite list of laws, stated precisely, from which every physical fact follows. Gödel shows that any such list is either inconsistent or leaves some question unsettled. So the dream of a complete physics is refuted by pure logic." Is that a fair use of the theorem?

The students had one lecture on the coding argument for the first theorem and one on the second theorem (roughly: a theory of the relevant strength, if consistent, cannot prove the sentence that expresses its own consistency). Here is the answer, typed up as written:

---

Gödel's first theorem says that any formal theory satisfying three conditions is incomplete. The conditions are: the theory is consistent; it is strong enough to represent a decent amount of arithmetic, so that it can code up its own syntax and handle the primitive recursive functions; and its axioms are effectively listable, meaning there is a mechanical procedure for telling whether a given string is an axiom, so that all the proofs could in principle be enumerated. Given those three, there is a sentence G in the language of the theory such that neither G nor its negation is provable.

The construction uses Gödel numbering: symbols, formulas and proofs all get code numbers, and the theory can then express facts about its own proof relation. By a diagonal construction you get a sentence that is equivalent, in the theory, to the claim that this very sentence has no proof in the theory. If the theory proved G, then, since it can verify any actual proof, it would also prove that G is provable, contradicting what G says — so it would be inconsistent. If it proved the negation of G, it would be asserting that there is a proof of G when there isn't one; Gödel's own version needed ω-consistency to rule this out, but Rosser showed that plain consistency is enough. So if the theory is consistent, G is undecidable by it. And since G says that it is unprovable, and it is unprovable, G is true. That is claim (i), more or less: no single formal system settles every question of arithmetic.

On (ii): I think the theorem does support something close to this. Given any machine, or any formal system, we can construct its Gödel sentence, step outside the system, and see that the sentence is true, while the system itself cannot prove it. So there is at least one arithmetical truth that we can know and that the machine cannot derive. And we can then do the same thing again for the system plus that sentence, and again, so that however the machine is built, the mind can always outrun it. The mechanist might reply that we don't know for certain that the machine's axioms are consistent, but that seems like scepticism about everything: if we cannot trust our belief that a system is consistent, we cannot do mathematics at all.

On (c): the physicist is right that a theory of everything would have to be an effectively axiomatized formal theory, and that if it is strong enough to encode arithmetic then the theorem applies to it: it is either inconsistent, or there is a sentence in its language that it cannot settle. There is one qualification, which is that the theory has to be rich enough to encode arithmetic. A list of laws that did not quantify over infinitely many objects, or that could not express addition and multiplication, would not be touched by the theorem. But a theory of everything will certainly have the natural numbers and arithmetic in it somewhere, so the consequence really does follow: some arithmetical statement is left unsettled by physics. The physicist is right that the dream of a complete physics is refuted, though she should really say "there is some sentence in the language of the theory that the theory cannot decide" rather than "some physical fact is unpredictable", since in general we cannot pick out in advance which sentences those are.
