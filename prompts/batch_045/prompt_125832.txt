Me and two other people on our town's volunteer broadband committee have to decide whether to put $50k of the town's money into a community fiber co-op, and before we vote we each have to write down our own probability that it'll still be running and covering its costs in ten years. My number is 40%. Dan's is 70%. Dan is definately not an idiot — he's an actuary.

Here's the thing. We've been at this for three months. We've shared every document, every spreadsheet, every phone call with the consultant. Last week we literally sat down and swapped files: he read everything I had, I read everything he had. Then we said our numbers out loud, and said them again after hearing each other. Mine moved from 35 to 40. His moved from 75 to 70. And there it stuck.

Dan says this is impossible. He says he read somewhere that it's been proven — like, mathematically proven — that two honest, rational people who've shared all their information can't end up with different numbers, and that the fact that we're still apart is itself proof that one of us is being irrational or stubborn or is secretly weighing something he won't put on the table. And since he can't find anything wrong with his own reasoning, he thinks the someone is me. He wants us to just split the difference and put 55% in the report. He also keeps saying "we both want the same thing and we're looking at the same world" as if that closes it, and I dunno, it feels like he's smuggling something in there but I can't say what.

A few things make me doubt him, but I can't put them together.

One: our numbers come from different places. He's reasoning from a pile of case studies about municipal broadband projects in other towns. I'm reasoning from his pile plus what I know about how our town actually does things — nobody here paid their water district bills for years, and we had to dissolve the volunteer fire company's fundraising arm because two people couldn't stand each other. I don't think that's "evidence he hasn't seen." He's lived here his whole life too. He just thinks that stuff is noise and I think it's the whole ballgame.

Two: we disagree about what kind of thing this is. He talks about it like it's one more draw from a bucket of similar projects. I think it's more like — it's this town, this particular group of people, one shot, and the bucket stuff is only mildly informative. He says that's not a real distinction and I should say which bucket I think it's in instead. I don't have one.

Three: Priya, the third person (she's abstaining on the number but she runs our meetings), had us try something she called "the ladder." You write your number, then write what you think the other person's number is, then you read those out, then you revise, and you keep going until nothing changes. We did three rungs and it stopped moving: me 38, him 58. Dan says the ladder proves his point and if we'd done ten rungs we'd have met in the middle. I don't see why it has to meet — it stopped, didn't it? Does "it stopped" actually mean something, or did we just give up too early?

Four, and this is the part that matters for my wallet: Dan's offered to bet me $200 at his odds, on top of whatever the committee decides. Every instinct says take it — I'm getting great odds. But there's a voice in my head saying that if Dan is right that I'm the irrational one here, then betting against the better reasoner is exactly how you lose money, and the fact that he's the one offering is itself a bad sign. Is that voice right? Is his offer evidence against me?

So: am I rationally required to move my number, and if so, to what? Is "split the difference" something I should agree to for the report even if I don't believe it? Is it genuinely possible for two honest people who've shared everything to disagree about a probability, or is Dan right that it can't happen? And should I take the bet?
