I convene the communication-skills training half-day for senior clinicians at my hospital — around eighteen people per session, mostly consultants and registrars with a couple of specialist nurses and a chaplain — and I have a seventy-five-minute slot at the end of the afternoon, which is the worst slot of the day: everyone is tired, they have been doing scripted role-play since nine, and they are professionally allergic to anything that looks like being graded on how they talk to patients. Last year I filled that slot with a straight discussion of whether deceiving a relative by what you imply is morally any different from deceiving them by what you say outright, and it went badly. The group split at once into people for whom the difference was obvious and people for whom it was a lawyer's evasion; nobody moved; and it became clear at the end that the two camps were disagreeing about something we had never isolated, because in every example someone produced, the misleading option also happened to be gentler, or vaguer, or less likely to be found out. So this year I want them to run into the question rather than discuss it, and I would be grateful for a design.

What I want them to come away having *noticed* — not been told — is whether the difference they feel between the two kinds of deception tracks anything at all once you hold the downstream effects fixed, or whether it is really a difference in what the speaker did, in what the hearer was entitled to, or in how much of the falsehood any single person has to own. Three structural devices occur to me, and you should feel free to improve on or discard them, but the design has to include something doing comparable work. First, a matched-outcome round: pairs get a clinical scenario in which the relative must end up with a specific false belief and act on it, and the speaker is assigned one of three vehicles for producing it — asserting the false proposition outright, asserting only true things that generate it as an obvious implication, or staying silent about it in a context where silence is conspicuous, because the relative has just asked directly. The point of the round is that by construction the relative ends up with the same belief and takes the same action in all three conditions, so any residual difference in how the speaker feels, or in how the room judges the speaker, cannot be a difference in outcome. Second, a transcript device: immediately after each exchange the relative writes down the single sentence they would now use to summarise what they were told, and the speaker separately writes down the single sentence they would be willing to say out loud to the room — and the pair compares the two before anything is discussed. I have found that clinicians will happily imply things in the moment that they refuse to write down afterwards, and I want that gap to be the data rather than my assertion that it exists. Third, an accumulation round: a relay in which a false belief is produced in the relative over five short exchanges, each one handled by a different participant and each one individually unimpeachable, so that no single speaker ever asserted anything false and nobody intended the final belief — and then the question of who, if anyone, deceived the relative is put to the whole group while they are still holding the individual exchanges in mind. Constraints: no real money and no prizes beyond something trivial and slightly embarrassing; the stakes have to be genuine enough that participants actually try to succeed at the communication task, because if they are indifferent to whether the relative forms the belief, every round collapses into people announcing that they would simply tell the truth; the words for the distinction should not appear on any handout or from my mouth until after the last round, so the clinical framing has to carry it; and the whole thing has to survive participants who game the scoring, refuse the premise, or try to turn it into a discussion of consent law and documentation.

If any of my framing smuggles in an answer — if, say, the matched-outcome design begs the question against the thought that the entire moral point of the distinction just is the effect on the hearer, or against the thought that there is no difference here to be felt and the activity can only manufacture one — please say so plainly instead of handing me a design that quietly presupposes what I asked it to test, and tell me where exactly your design settles something I wanted left open. I would also like a note on which readings of the distinction your activity quietly takes a stand on, since I will be running it with people who hold several of them.
