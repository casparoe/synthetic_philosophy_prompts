I'm the TA for an upper-level seminar on free will and I'm putting together the problem set for the week on agency and mechanism, and I need one case that I can't find in the usual places. We started from the old entomology anecdote behind the word "sphexishness": the digger wasp drags its paralysed prey to the burrow, sets it down on the threshold, goes inside to inspect, comes back out and hauls it in; if while it's inside you move the prey a few inches, it comes out, drags it back to the threshold, and goes in to inspect again, and you can keep it looping like that more or less indefinately. The moral everyone draws is that rigidity — the failure to take in feedback and update — is the signature of the mechanical, so flexibility and fine-grained sensitivity to how things stand look like the signature of a real agent. But my roomate, who's also in the seminar, pointed out that the behaviour we admire most in people is often rigid in exactly that sense: the nurse who doesn't recalculate whether to falsify a chart, the referee who won't reopen a decision once the crowd starts shouting. So "she just does the same thing every time no matter what you do to the setup" can't settle anything by itself, and I want a case that shows that rather than one that just asserts it.

Here's what I need you to build. One scenario, realistic — a workplace, a hospital, a courtroom, a family, whatever; no neurosugeons, no implants, no chips, no demons, no science fiction at all. Two agents (or one agent at two times, if you'd rather) performing the same kind of action, held equal in every respect that could otherwise explain the difference: same information, same competence, comparable upbringing and incentives, neither of them coerced, addicted, phobic, brainwashed, exhausted or self-deceived. The only difference should be the one thing your case is built to isolate. And the two verdicts have to cross:

- A flexibility / anti-sphexishness criterion — roughly, freedom and responsibility track how much the agent's behaviour varies appropriately across nearby counterfactual circumstances, so the more the behaviour is a fixed loop that can't take in feedback, the less free the agent is — has to say that A is free and responsible and B is not.
- A source or ownership criterion — roughly, the behaviour has to be the agent's own in the right way, flowing from a will or a character or a practical identity that is theirs rather than merely from a mechanism that happens to be running in them — has to say the reverse: B is free and responsible, A is not.
- Neither verdict may be stipulated in the description. For each criterion I want to see the actual test applied to the actual details: which counterfactual variations the flexible agent tracks and which the rigid one fails to register, and what it is about the rigid agent's loop that makes it theirs rather than something merely happening to them.
