You are a professional philosopher who also knows how measurement actually works in practice — someone who's read the literature on indicators, incentives and social epistemology, and who has also been in the room when a target gets set. Take your time with this one and think carefully: before you write any procedure, spend a few hundred words brainstorming on what actually goes wrong when a number stops being used to find something out and starts being used to hold someone to account, and only then commit to an answer. I'd rather have you reason first than produce something tidy. And please write the whole thing in British English.

Some background so you know where I'm coming from. I'm 71 and retired; I spent about thirty-five years as a field biologist, freshwater invertebrates mostly, and then a lot of years on survey design, which is how I first started thinking about this. Since retiring I've got into philosophy properly, going through things on my own, and I ended up on the board of a small wildlife trust. We manage maybe 900 hectares across a handful of sites, a tiny staff, and a lot of volunteers who do most of the recording. Our board meets quarterly, we set targets once a year in a half-day workshop, and whatever we come up with has to be signed off by trustees who are mostly not technical and have about two hours of patience. We have spreadsheets and no statistician. Every number we produce is produced by the same volunteers whose behaviour the number is meant to change, which I think is the actual crux of our problem and possibly the thing that makes our situation different from the standard textbook case.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my board could actually run on a proposed target, before we adopt it, to work out whether the number is going to get gamed and what to do about it. Concretely: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question given that we have no statistician and a couple of hours; the point at which the procedure says stop, don't adopt this as a target at all; and the point at which it has to hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is about values rather than facts. Please also include a redesign step — how to respecify a target that fails, rather than only a veto — and be explicit about which of your steps are real checks and which are judgement dressed up as procedure.

I've been muddling towards some distinctions of my own and I'd like you to use them or tell me which of them are muddled and should be dropped:

- the cheap-move test: could a competent insider move the number in an afternoon without changing anything about the thing we care about?
- audit surface: how visible the production of the number is to us, and how few people it depends on. I suspect a narrow audit surface is the real predictor and that a lot of the rest is downstream of it.
- cliff versus slope: whether the target is a threshold or a continuous gradient. I think thresholds concentrate effort at the margin and also concentrate dishonesty at the margin.
- orthogonal anchor: is there any second number that is correlated with what we want but hard for the people being measured to influence? If there isn't, I think we're stuck.
- shape-watching rather than level-watching: looking at the distribution of the underlying records — suspicious heaping just past the line, oddly round numbers, variance collapsing — rather than at the headline figure.
- proxy drift: cases where the number comes apart from the thing not because anyone responded to it but because the world moved underneath it. My instinct is that this is a genuinely different failure from gaming and needs a different fix, but I'm not confident, and I'd like you to say if the two really come apart or if it's one phenomenon with two stories about it.
- spec churn: how often you can change the definition before everyone stops believing the number means anything. I worry that the obvious anti-gaming move, which is to keep re-specifying, has a cost I never see discussed.

Then run the procedure on three of our actual cases.

1. A payments-for-results scheme we're considering: landowners get paid per confirmed breeding pair of a target wader on their land, confirmed by a photograph with a date and a grid reference. The money is real and the landowners are not hostile, they're just busy.

2. Our headline "species richness" figure: the number of species recorded on a site in a year, compiled largely by volunteers, published in the annual report, and — this is the part I'm uneasy about — used to decide how next year's volunteer budget is split between sites.

3. The one I'll dislike: "hectares of habitat under favourable management", which is the number we report to our main funder. I invented it about fifteen years ago, it's the thing I'm known for locally, and my strong prior is that it's fine. Please apply the procedure to it honestly rather than flattering me, and if the procedure says it's the worst of the three, say so and say what I'd have to give up to fix it.

Two more things. First, tell me where the Goodhart framing is the wrong frame altogether — where "when a measure becomes a target it stops measuring the thing" is not what's happening, including the awkward case where gaming and doing the actual thing coincide, so that the cheapest way to move the number just is to do the work. Second, tell me what the procedure cannot do: what it will never tell us, and where we should just accept a bad-but-cheap number and stop worrying.
