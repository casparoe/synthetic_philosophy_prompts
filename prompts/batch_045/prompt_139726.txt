I have been reading, and occasionally writing for, the rationalist blogosphere for well over a decade, and I am drafting a long essay on Iago as a case study for something that corner of the internet argues about endlessly and never settles: whether there is a genuinely moral difference between lying and merely misleading. I would like your help with the middle section of the essay, which is a reading of Shakespeare's Othello through that distinction.

I do not need a literature review or any citations — I know the broad shape of the philosophical argument: on one side, the claim that assertion is governed by a distinctive norm, so that saying what you believe to be false is a special abuse of a shared practice, a way of exploiting someone's warranted reliance on your word, whereas arranging for them to come to a false belief by innuendo, omission, emphasis or staging is a lesser wrong (or at least a wrong of a different kind, with a different remedy: clarification rather than retraction, suspicion rather than breach of trust). On the other side, the claim that this is moral bookkeeping over a mechanism: what I do when I mislead you is intentionally engineer a false belief in you for my own purposes, and the fact that I did it with true sentences rather than a false one is a fact about my choice of instrument, not about what I owe you. There is also a hybrid view I find attractive and want tested: that the interesting difference is not moral but epistemic and forensic — lies leave a hostage to fortune, because a false assertion can be checked, contradicted and pinned on the speaker, whereas insinuation leaves the hearer holding a belief he built for himself, with no one to blame but his own inference.

To make the reading precise I want to use a small taxonomy of my own, rather than the usual two-way split. Call a deception deniable when every sentence the deceiver utters is something he could in principle defend as true, and the false belief is left to the hearer to construct. Call it staged when no assertion does the work at all: the deceiver arranges the world — props, timing, overheard fragments — so that the hearer's own senses testify against him. Call it asserted when the deceiver comes out with a flat false claim that he must stand behind. And there is a fourth posture I want included, the shrug: the point at which the deceiver, having implied everything, declines to say anything further and lets the hearer's imagination finish the job.

My questions, then, are these. First, map Iago's campaign onto this: how much of the temptation scene is deniable, how much is staged, and where, if anywhere, does he cross into assertion? My impression is that he crosses late and under pressure, when insinuation stops working on a man who keeps demanding that he speak plainly, and that what he offers at that point is not really proof but a puppet show. Second, and more important: does the play treat the line as morally load-bearing at all, or merely as prudent? Iago takes considerable trouble not to cross it, and in an early soliloquy he congratulates himself that the counsel he gives is free, honest and merely probable. Is the play showing us a man with a scruple, or a man with a good lawyer — someone avoiding assertion because assertion is falsifiable, and because he has watched what happens to claims that can be contradicted? The final scene is where I want you to press hardest: Emilia can refute a claim, but she cannot refute a shrug, and Iago's last move is to refuse to speak at all.

Third, the handkerchief. Desdemona's inability to produce it, and her insistence that it is not lost when she simply does not have it, looks like a near-perfect case of a true-but-misleading utterance received as a lie, with the tragedy turning on the reception rather than the utterance. Does that suggest the distinction is, in practice, a matter of what the hearer can hold the speaker to, rather than of what the speaker did?

Fourth, the epistemics. Othello demands ocular proof and is handed a staged performance he then interprets to his own destruction; he repeatedly begs Iago to hand over his thoughts, and Iago hands him a hypothesis and lets him do the rest. The rationalist reading writes itself: a demand for certainty is satisfied by a theatre, and the supplier is merely meeting a demand. I want to know whether that reading survives, and whether it changes the philosophical upshot. If the vice the play is really anatomising is Othello's — his appetite for certainty, his willingness to outsource his beliefs, his refusal to sit with doubt — then the lying-versus-misleading literature may be arguing about the wrong party, and the interesting normative question is what a hearer owes by way of resistance.

Fifth, be honest about where the reading fails. The play's own vocabulary is about seeming versus being, and about a man called honest by everyone who is not; that is an axis of hypocrisy and performance, not of assertion versus implicature, and the fit may be worse than I think. Iago's motive is notoriously thin, which makes any claim about what the play thinks risky. And a tragedy in which nearly everyone dies may be a poor venue for fine-grained moral distinctions: the play may not care about the difference between a lie and a dodge at all.

Finally, say what the play adds that the arguments do not. My own guess is that it adds the cost of deniability: that a deceiver who never asserts never has to retract, and that this foreclosure — the loss of the possibility of being set right — is the real damage, and one that the philosophical literature, with its tidy pairs of cases, tends to miss. Argue me out of that if it is wrong.

A test you might find useful, or might want to reject: imagine Iago had simply said outright, at the start, what he wanted Othello to believe. If our moral verdict on him would be unchanged, then in this case the distinction is doing no work; if it would change, it is doing work, and I want to know which scenes carry that weight and why.

Please write in British English, and do not trouble yourself with bibliography, further reading or citations of any kind: I want the reading, worked through in the scenes, and I want it to say plainly where Othello resists the lens I am bringing to it.
