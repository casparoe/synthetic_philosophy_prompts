At work we built an internal LLM tool that writes most of our boilerplate and a decent chunk of the actual logic, and my team is 8 people now than it used to be 12. My director keeps saying "look, it's just a tool — a power loom didn't put weavers out of work, it made cloth cheap and everyone ended up better off, and a spreadsheet didn't kill accounting." He uses that to argue nobody should worry and we shouldn't slow the rollout.

A friend of mine who's into Marx uses the exact same analogy and gets the opposite conclusion. He says that's basically Marx's point about machinery: under capitalism a machine isn't a neutral tool, it's capital, and the same machine that makes a worker more productive also makes him disposable, pushes down what his labour is worth, and turns him into someone who tends the process instead of driving it. So "it's just a tool, like the loom" apparently supports both "relax" and "this is the mechanism by which you become redundant."

Can you audit it? I want the mapping table first — loom, weaver, cloth, mill owner, the mill itself, new kinds of jobs — for our case, then where the mapping breaks. My guesses at the disanalogies that matter: a loom can't copy itself for free, a loom doesn't get better by watching the weaver, demand for code isn't like demand for cloth, and the people who own the model aren't the people who own the company I work for. But I bet some of those are irrelevant to the argument and I'm missing the one that matters, and I want to know which features of the loom case the analogy quietly drags in that our situation doesn't have. Then: does either conclusion actually survive, or was "it's just a tool" doing all the work and the loom just making it feel historical?
