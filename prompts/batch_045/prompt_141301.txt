I've been reading my way into predictive processing and the free energy principle mostly via pop-science, and I want to know how much of what I've absorbed is actually the view rather than a catchy version of it. The thing that prompted this is a chapter in a book by a fairly well-known neuroscientist (I honestly can't remember which one, it was a library copy) that I found very convincing at the time and now slightly suspicious of. Here's more or less how it goes — I've compressed it but the gist and the phrasing are theirs:

"The brain sits in a dark, silent box and only ever receives ambiguous signals at its sensory surfaces. It copes by running a generative model that is constantly predicting what its sensory input ought to be. Whatever is left over is prediction error, and the brain's whole job is to drive that error toward zero — the quantity it drives down is free energy, which is basically a measure of surprise, i.e. how far the world departs from what the brain expected. There are only two ways to reduce the error: change the prediction to fit the input, or change the input to fit the prediction. Perception is the first and action is the second, so action is really just making your predictions come true. That is why perception is controlled hallucination: what you experience is your brain's best guess, not the world. Attention is just turning up the gain on some prediction errors rather than others — the literature calls this precision. The upshot is that a living system's entire life consists of steering itself into the states it already expects to be in, which is why the free energy principle can in principle explain everything from why your eyes saccade the way they do to why you hold the political opinions you do, and why two people with different priors can look at the same world and genuinely see different ones."

Two things make me suspicious, though I can't tell whether they're real problems or me missing something. First: a friend who works in ML read the same passage and said that if the brain is just minimizing a quantity that measures mismatch between its model and the world, then the cheapest possible solution is to sit still in a dark, quiet room and never learn anything, and the fact that animals don't do that means either the principle is empty or it has to smuggle in extra desires to get going. I've seen people call this the dark room problem, so I assume it has an answer, but I'd like to know what the answer is and whether it's an ad hoc patch or it was in there from the start. Second: the "action is making your predictions come true" idea seems to have a weird consequence that the chapter doesn't notice. A scientist who designs an experiment specifically to be surprised, or a person who goes to a foreign city to get lost, looks like someone deliberately increasing prediction error; and if everything is minimization, then that has to be redescribed as confirming some higher-order prediction, at which point I'm not sure the claim still says anything.

So: how fair is this summary? Could you go through it and sort the sentences into what someone who actually works on this would sign off on as stated, what they'd accept as a rough gloss but wince at, and what they'd say is a real misrepresentation (a conflation of two things the framework keeps apart, or a modest claim inflated)? I especially want to know whether "free energy", "surprise" and "prediction error" are being treated as the same thing here when they shouldn't be, whether precision really is just attention, and whether the big payoff at the end — that this explains everything, and that different priors mean different perceived worlds — follows from the accurate parts or only from the inflated bits. Please don't give me a reading list or point me at papers, I'm not going to be able to chase them up anyway; just tell me what's true in the passage and what isn't.
