I spent thirty-eight years in experimental physics, most of them building and calibrating detectors, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the university's lifelong-learning programme. We are seven people, none of us philosophers, and we have come to a standstill over introspection. The trouble, as I see it from my own background, is that we keep asking whether introspection is reliable as though reliability were one number. In my working life an instrument had a specification sheet: discriminability distinct from criterion, dynamic range with saturation at both ends, hysteresis, drift within a session, bandwidth, and whether the act of measuring perturbs the thing measured. I have found it useful to write out the same sheet for introspection, and I would now like to turn that sheet into something the group can actually use.

What I want is a routing instrument: a decision tree whose internal nodes are questions and whose leaves are named positions on the reliability and authority of introspection. Concretely: eight to ten branch nodes, mostly binary but ternary where three answers genuinely differ, each phrased as a concrete question about a case or a claim that a retired engineer or a retired judge could answer without knowing which position it leads to; no more than eight leaves; then a routing key that maps answer vectors to leaves, and a short note for each leaf on what it is committed to.

I would like the tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: begin from the distinctions and the answer combinations they permit, then place the actual views in the cells. Where a combination is unoccupied, say whether that is because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unoccupied. Where two positions cannot be separated by any question on your nodes, say so plainly rather than inventing a discriminator.

The distinctions that matter for our purposes, and which I would like to see doing the branching work, are these. First, which channel is under discussion: gross presence or absence of a current experience; fine-grained character or degree; comparative judgements across an interval; self-ascription of standing attitudes and beliefs; causal explanation of one's own recent choice; and access to subpersonal mechanism. Second, what failure mode is alleged: poor discriminability, a badly set response criterion rather than a weak signal, drift or hysteresis, saturation, measurement disturbance, or confabulation in the sense of a report generated without any corresponding signal at all. These two axes are where I think the group's disagreements really live, and a tree that collapses them will not settle anything.

The leaves should include, at minimum: an incorrigibilist position that restricts authority to gross phenomenal presence; an inner-sense or perceptual-model position treating introspection as a fallible quasi-perceptual faculty; a transparency or agentialist position on which first-person authority over one's attitudes is not a matter of detection at all; a self-interpretation or confabulation position on which reports about the causes of one's behaviour are inferences from self-observation and frequently wrong; and a global pessimist position on which even judgements about whether one is in pain right now can misfire. Add at least one leaf labelled unnamed for a cell nobody occupies. Cross-cutting distinctions such as immunity to error through misidentification should be handled where they genuinely discriminate and set aside where they do not.

I would like the following cases to appear as nodes, because they are the ones my group actually argued about. One: a faint sensation at the edge of attention which becomes vivid as soon as one attends to it, so that the question is whether the report describes the pre-attentive state, the amplified state, or neither. Two: a subject who is shown a physiological readout and then asked to report again, where the interesting result is the difference between feedback improving accuracy and feedback merely shifting the reporting threshold. Three: someone who acquires a fine sensory vocabulary through professional training, as a taster or a clinician does, and thereby discriminates what she previously could not. Four: being asked why one chose the item one chose, and answering with a reason that subsequent evidence makes doubtful. Five: someone who reports constant pain for years and yet, absorbed in a demanding task, reports none.

On the epistemic side: I would like each leaf and each branch to carry a marker for how contested it is, and I would like you to say where the instrument as a whole rests on an assumption a specialist would dispute, particularly my assumption that the channels form separable domains and that unreliability can be parameterised at all. I am aware that my detector vocabulary may be importing distinctions that do not survive the translation, and I would rather be told so than accommodated. If the task as I have posed it cannot be done well, if the tree collapses, if some requested leaf does not fit the logical space, or if the framing itself is confused, please say that plainly at the top and give me the nearest instrument that can be built instead, rather than producing something that looks tidy and is not. Where you are unsure whether a question discriminates, flag it rather than guessing.
