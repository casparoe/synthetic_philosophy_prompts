I need help writing something, and the constraints are stranger than they sound. I'm a philosophy professor (early modern mostly, but I teach the aesthetics seminar every other year) and I somehow ended up on the board of the Alder Arts Council in the mill town I grew up in — the gallery is on the second floor of the old Dunning Mill, and every spring we give the Alder Prize to one work in the juried show: $2,500, and the piece gets bought into the town's permanent collection, which in practice means it hangs in the library atrium forever. I was one of three jurors this year. The winner was a twelve-foot triptych, 'Last Shift', by a self-taught painter named Darlene somebody-or-other: the mill at golden hour, very soft, very polished, and hand-stitched into the canvas in red thread are the names of about eleven hundred people who worked there, including my grandmother's. The public vote went to it by something like four to one. People cry in front of it; they bring their kids and point at a name. I voted against it. My reasons were exactly the reasons you'd expect from someone who just spent thirteen weeks teaching sentimentality and the standard of taste — the golden light is doing all the work, the stitches are doing the rest of it, and if you pulled the two apart you'd have a bad painting and a list of names. My problem is that I don't trust my own verdict. On the theory I teach, taste isn't just whatever anyone happens to feel, there is a standard, but the standard just is the settled verdict of critics with the right qualities: strong sense, delicacy of sentiment, lots of practice, wide comparison, freedom from prejudice. Measured against that list I am the least qualified person in the room — least practice, least comparison, conspicuously prejudiced because I've been marinating in this material all semester, and the most personally invested, since my grandmother's name is literally sewn into it. I cried the first time I saw it, which I'm still a little annoyed about. I've sat with it eleven times now and it does something different to me almost every time, and I cant tell anymore whether that's the object or me. I also keep thinking that my verdict can only be right if the thing is trying to be a painting, and maybe it isn't — maybe it's a memorial with a picture on it, in which case all my training is arriving at the wrong angle.

Anyway. Darlene is sixty-eight, this is her first show, and on Friday I have to stand in front of roughly two hundred people — her family, three generations of mill families, the donors who funded the purchase, a reporter from the local paper, and the two other jurors who know perfectly well how I voted — and hand her the prize in about four minutes. I cant say it's a good painting and I can't imply it's a bad one. I can't say beauty is just in the eye of the beholder, because I think that's false and it would be condescending to Darlene and to everyone in that room whose aunt is stitched into it. I can't say the jury has spoken and the matter is settled, because everyone knows I was the dissenting vote. I can't say anything that makes the donors feel the town blew two and a half grand on a wall of names. And I can't turn it into a four-minute lecture on Hume with the phrase 'ideal critic' in it, because nobody in that room has any idea who Hume is and it would land as a philosophy professor explaining a mill town to itself in front of a painting of the mill. What's left, as far as I can tell, is to say something true about what a thing like this asks of us to look at it well — that a verdict of taste is the kind of thing you can be wrong about and can also be corrected on, that being moved is not the same as being managed, that the people who weep in front of it and the people who are suspicious of it are doing the same thing badly or well depending on how much practice they've had at it — and I genuinely don't know whether that's a speech or a hedge. Can you write it? Around 500 words, the way a person actually talks, no quotations from anyone and nothing that would sound like a citation if you read it aloud, and it has to give Darlene something she can keep. Then, after the speech, tell me in a paragraph or two which sentences were load-bearing for you and what you had to give up to get the thing to stand up.
