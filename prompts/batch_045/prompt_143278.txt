I work in computational economics, and I keep walking into the same argument at workshops. One side says the socialist calculation debate was settled decades ago — by Mises and Hayek, or by the collapse of actually existing socialism in 1989, depending on who is speaking. The other side says it was never settled, only interrupted, and that the interesting version of the question is being asked again now, in new vocabulary, about algorithmic planning, platform logistics, and large-scale machine learning. I cannot tell what kind of deadlock this is, and a diagnosis is what I want, not a verdict.

Here is the dispute as I carry it around in my head. Mises's original claim: without private ownership of the means of production there are no market prices for higher-order goods, hence no common denominator in which to compare heterogeneous inputs, hence no economic calculation — and the difficulty is not computational effort but the absence of the very magnitudes any computation would have to use. Hayek's 1945 restatement: the problem is not solving a given system of simultaneous equations, because the data of that system are not given to anyone; the relevant knowledge is knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place, local, transient, often not articulable by the person who has it; prices work as a system for communicating the upshot of that dispersed knowledge without anyone needing to possess it; and the equilibrium construction a planning board would simulate already presupposes what was to be discovered. Lange's reply: the board need not solve the whole system. It announces parametric prices; enterprise managers follow two rules — produce at the output where price equals marginal cost, and choose the input combination that minimizes average cost; the board then raises the price of any good in excess demand and lowers it for any good in excess supply. This trial-and-error process converges to the competitive allocation, and distribution can be settled separately, so the allocational and the distributional functions of markets come apart.

My hypothesis is that three separable complaints are bundled under the single word calculation, and that most of the apparent deadlock is the bundle. Call them specifiability: can the problem be written down at all, by anyone, at any cost. Solvability: given that it is written down, can it be solved, and does the iterative process converge. Maintainability and incentive-compatibility: can the solution be kept current as conditions change, and do the agents inside the mechanism have reasons to report their costs and capacities truthfully and to act on the instructions they are given. On this reading Lange's construction answers the second question under idealizing assumptions, assumes away the first, and has no vocabulary for the third — mechanism design and the formal treatment of strategic misreporting arrived decades later — which is why objections about recalcitrant managers and about conditions changing faster than reports arrive got folded into the first question, where they then looked like rhetorical points rather than arguments.

That hypothesis also predicts something about the modern version. The contemporary claim is that abundant data and modern computation settle the first two questions at once: everything is logged, sensed, and embedded in learned representations, and optimization is cheap. My suspicion is that these are precisely the two complaints the older argument identified as not binding, so that the modern version re-runs the original dispute under new labels while both sides believe something has changed. I want that suspicion tested rather than confirmed.

So, concretely: how much of this impasse is verbal, turning on different senses of calculation, rational, or possible; how much is a clash of standards of evidence, where one side takes a formal existence or convergence result as the relevant exhibit and the other takes an argument about what any real agent could come to know; how much is a genuine substantive disagreement about how economies actually are — whether dispersed knowledge is mostly articulable or mostly not, whether the relevant choice sets are convex enough for the convergence story, whether the economy is well described as a system with a definite state that a tâtonnement could home in on, whether very large firms that plan internally are evidence that the thing works at scale. Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing most of the work, and is that question in principle answerable? And what, on your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction, a convergence or impossibility result with its assumptions stated, an empirical proxy that is not simply 1989, a reframing of the question, or nothing — perhaps the episode was decided by an unplanned experiment and what remains is an argument about counterfactuals that no evidence could touch.

Two constraints on the answer. Engage the positions as I have stated them rather than surveying the literature, and give me no reading list: I am not asking who won, and I am not asking what to read next. Where a distinction is carrying weight, state it in a sentence I could go off and apply to the algorithmic-planning version myself.
