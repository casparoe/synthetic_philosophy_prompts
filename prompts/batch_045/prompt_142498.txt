I'm a literary translator, and in the spring I'm giving the closing reading at a small, slightly old-fashioned colloquium on translation under formal constraint — the tradition that runs from Hofstadter's 1997 book on rendering one short French lyric dozens of ways, where every version keeps the rhyme scheme and the syllable count and therefore ends up saying something the others don't. I'd rather close my own paper with a poem than with another page of argument, and for once I'd like it to be someone else's poem rather than my own.

Here is the situation the poem should grow out of. For two years I've been working on a cycle of short love lyrics in a language I'll call Selven — the name and the language are my own invention, but the grammatical problem is one I actually face, and I'd rather the poem work through a made-up case than a real one that everyone at the colloquium will have opinions about. In Selven every finite verb has to carry an evidential: the speaker must mark each clause as witnessed, reported, or inferred, and the marking is a single syllable fixed to the verb. English can express all three, but never in one unobtrusive syllable — I saw her go, they say she went, she must have gone are different lengths, different rhythms, and different claims about who knows what. So a translator determined to keep Selven's meter and its rhyme scheme has to decide, line by line, whether the lovers actually saw each other or are only said to have, and either the line breaks or the epistemics go.

What I want is a sestina: thirty-nine lines, six stanzas of six, then a three-line envoi, with the usual rotation — the word that ends line 6 of one stanza ends line 1 of the next, and so on around the cycle, with the envoi taking up all six again, two to a line, one of them tucked inside the line rather than at the end. I'd like the six end-words to be letter, measure, faithful, tongue, seen, and told. The point of the form, for me, is that those six words keep returning in new grammatical and emotional company and each time they land a little differently: the words stay in place and the sense moves, which is exactly the claim I want the poem to make rather than state. Somewhere around the fourth or fifth stanza I want the turn to be the moment the reader realizes the choice was never between preserving form and preserving meaning — it's that saving the measure forces a decision, line by line, about how anyone knows what the poem says, and that a prose crib, which is free to write out in full that she says she saw him, quietly turns a lyric into hearsay. I'd rather the poem not contain a translator-figure who explains all this in the closing lines, and I'd rather it avoid the usual currency of the subject: nothing about things being lost, nothing about untranslatability, no prison-houses of language.

Two more requests. First, I'd like the poem to be right, not merely allusive — someone who actually does this work should find the formal mechanics and the evidential problem handled correctly, and someone who doesn't should still find a good poem. Second, after the poem, give me one paragraph of prose in which you take a definite position: say what your poem claims about form-preserving translation — that the version that keeps the meter is the more faithful one, or that it is a different poem wearing the same clothes, or something sharper I haven't thought of — say which of the three goods (the sense, the music, the marking of who witnessed what) your version deliberately gave up in order to keep the others, and name the strongest objection a translator who disagrees with you would make. I don't want a survey of the available positions with the poem floating above them; I want the poem and the paragraph to commit to one answer and defend it.
