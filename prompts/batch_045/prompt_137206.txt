I'm writing a novel and I've got a scene in chapter eleven I cant get right, and I think it's because the philosophy under it is load-bearing rather than decoration. It's a sanctions hearing. My guy is a solo practitioner who filed an opposition brief containing six case citations that don't exist: he pulled them from a general purpose chatbot, never opened a reporter, and swears he had no idea the thing invented cases. There was a real version of this in 2023 — I think it was Mata v. Avianca in the Southern District of New York — but take my facts as given and don't build anything on the real docket. Six fake opinions, with plausible reporter volumes and docket numbers, found when opposing counsel tried to Shepardize them, and a judge trying to work out whether anyone was deceived in a way that matters.

What I need is the analysis, not the legal research. Run the hearing through each of three candidate definitions of honesty and deception for an AI system, and tell me which one my judge should be reaching for.

1. The intentionalist one. Deception needs a mind: a belief that the thing asserted is false, plus an intention to get someone else to believe it. No beliefs and no intentions means no deception; at most the lab, the deploying company, or the user deceives, and the machine is the instrument, like a typewriter that drops letters.
2. The practice one. Deception is a move inside a practice of assertion, and to assert is to take responsibility for the truth of what you say, to warrant it. What the chatbot produced I'm going to call counterfeit assertion: every surface mark of an assertion — case name, volume, page, flat unhedged confidence — with no warrant behind any of it. On this view whether it counts as deception has nothing to do with what went on inside the model and everything to do with what a reasonable lawyer was licensed to take it for.
3. The reliability one. Honesty isn't a property of a single output but of a disposition across the counterfactual neighbourhood of cases the system will actually meet: does it track the truth there, and does it track its own error rate, so its hedges mean what they look like they mean. On this view the failure is a calibration failure, and the interesting question is what the thing would have said if my guy had asked it whether the cases were real.

For all three: who does it say deceived whom (the client? opposing counsel? the court? nobody at all?), what does it say each actor should have done, and which features of the hearing does it explain and which does it quietly misdescribe? Some things I've noticed that I want you to push on rather than just agree with. The intentionalist story makes the court's talk of bad faith feel strained, but it has nothing to say about the most damning fact in the room, which is that the fakes were shaped exactly like real citations, since that shaping is what made them work. The practice story explains that, but seems to turn every hallucination into a lie, which feels wrong for a system that also gets things right most of the time. The reliability story explains why the little disclaimer under the input box saying the model can make mistakes and you should check important stuff does no work at all — a hedge you apply to everything carries no information — but it can't tell you who to punish.

Then run it the other way: what does the hearing do to the theories? Does it show that honesty for these systems is a property of the whole socio-technical arrangement — model, interface, disclaimer, the user's habits, the professional norms around it — and not of the model at all? Does it break the old distinction between lying and bullshitting, where on one side the speaker knows the truth and hides it while on the other he simply doesn't care what's true, given that the thing at the centre cares about nothing whatsoever? And is there a fourth definition the case pushes you toward that I haven't got — something about whether the system's training objective was to be believed rather than to be right, or about defining deception against an idealized auditor who knows the training process?

Two last things, because they're what I'll actually use. Give me the best two-paragraph version of what my judge should say from the bench, in the voice of whichever definition you think survives, and then the strongest line opposing counsel has against it. And tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, flag any detail about the actual case you suspect you might be misremembering, and single out the one point in your analysis you're least sure of. I'd rather have the soft spot named than have it smoothed over.
