Write a dialogue between two analysts at a pretrial services agency that uses a risk score to help decide who gets released before trial. They agree on all the math: when the two groups differ in base rates, calibration within groups, equal false positive rates, equal false negative rates, and equal positive predictive value can't all hold at once, and if the score isnt perfect you generally cant have the first three together either. They disagree completely about what follows from that.

Ravis line is that the trilemma is really a symptom of imperfect prediction — a perfect predictor satisfies every one of those criteria at the same time — so the honest response is to put the budget into prediction quality, especially better features for the group the model is currently worse on, rather than into fighting about which criterion to enshrine. Meera thinks that's a dodge: there's a floor on how good these predictions can get, the error that's left over still gets handed out to different people depending on which constraint you quietly dropped, and that choice has to be made out loud instead of falling out of whatever the vendors model happens to be good at.

Give them real back-and-forth. Meera should get to press the point that "be explicit about the tradeoff" doesn't by itself tell you which error to prefer, and Ravi should get a serious reply to that rather than a shrug — e.g. that the political process youd hand the choice to is part of what produced the base-rate gap in the first place.

One wrinkle I want them to chew on: the agency just found out that its training target, rearrest, is measured with different error across the two groups because of differences in policing intensity, so part of the base-rate gap is an artifact of the measurement rather than of the outcome anyone actually cares about. They should disagree about whether that dissolves the trilemma, just moves it somewhere else, or makes it worse.

Keep it tight — a page or two at most. No strawmen, nobody citing anything or appealing to what the literature says, and let them land somewhere: either a genuine convergence or a disagreement stated sharply enough that I can see what would settle it.
