I'm a backend engineer, moved to the UK from Poland about four years ago for work, and I've got no philosophy background beyond arguing with my partner's family at Sunday lunch. Last week we ended up watching the State Opening of Parliament on the telly at her parents' house and got into an argument I can't settle, and I keep turning it over in my head. The King sits in the Lords chamber and reads out, in the first person, the government's whole legislative programme — her dad says he has no hand in writing it and probably wouldn't pick any of it if he did — and then MPs spend days debating a message thanking him for it, and her dad also claims that voting it down is treated as no confidence in the government. Meanwhile the actual job description seems to be: have no opinions. Non-partisan, never says what he thinks about anything political, ever. Except at the last couple of national emergencies, when he's the one who gives the country the moral address, and everyone — including people who couldn't care less about the royals — thinks that's exactly right, and that an elected politician doing the same thing would be a bit off. And my colleague at work swears the monarch is required to be in communion with the Church of England and that a Catholic can't take the throne, which if it's true is the one opinion you're apparently required to hold. Two of our new hires who just got citizenship had to swear allegiance to him personally, which was genuinely strange to me as someone who grew up pledging to a flag or a constitution.

So what I actually want to know is: what view of political authority, or of legitimacy, or of what a state even is, does this arrangement presuppose? That's several questions at once so take them in whatever order helps.

- First, the powers nobody uses. He formally appoints the PM and signs every bill, and everyone behaves as if those are real acts that make things happen legally. But the cleverest thing anyone said was from her uncle: if he ever actually refused, that wouldn't prove the power was there, it would prove it wasn't — the power only exists because it's never exercised, and one use would destroy it and probably the monarchy with it. What kind of thing is that? It's not like a break-glass credential at work, real but hopefully never needed. It's closer to a write permission that deletes itself on the first write. What is the practice doing by keeping such a thing in the system at all, if it can never be exercised?
- Does that work require a person? Could a rule, or a court, or a random citizen with a signing key do it? Or does it only make sense because there's a body, a voice and a family line — and if so, what does that say about what a state is, that it needs one?
- And is the whole outfit consistent with itself? It says authority comes from somewhere other than current voters — continuity, tradition, whatever — and also that the person embodying it must be silent about what to do with it. It bans him from politics while demanding he be the nation's moral voice. It demands he have no opinions while requiring one specific religious one. Is there a coherent picture there, or is it three different centuries bolted together?

My flatmate thinks the question is a trap: he says nothing is presupposed at all, the monarchy is tourism and soft power and brand management, and there's no theory underneath to extract. I think there must be, because the setup is so wierdly specific — if you were designing it from scratch to do a job you'd want to know what the job was. So: reconstruct the implicit theory for me, tell me which parts the practice strictly requires and which it merely goes along with, and tell me what would actually have to change if we pulled the person out of that slot and put something else there — a rule, an elected figurehead, a ceremonial president, whatever the real alternative is. And please don't give me books or a reading list, I won't read them; I'd rather you just reason it out from the practice itself.
