I sit on the serious-incident review panel at a hospital, and I keep hitting a pattern I have no vocabulary for. Some of the items we list as contributing factors are things that did not happen. Four recurring shapes:

(1) A patient deteriorated overnight, and we recorded "the night nurse did not check on him at 4 a.m." But virtually nothing that could have happened did happen — nobody checked at 3:50 either, nobody called the family, nobody on the floor was doing almost anything anyone might conceivably have done. A few non-occurrences make the list; all the rest don't. I can see that we are selecting, but not what the selection tracks: the person's role, what was normally to be expected, whether acting would have made a difference, or something else.

(2) In another review, three people separately failed to perform the same check, and any one of them performing it would have caught the problem. No individual failure made a difference — had any one acted, the outcome would have been what it was anyway — yet we listed all three, and nobody accepted from any one of them that "my failure didn't matter, the others would have failed too."

(3) Sometimes the item is nobody's failure at all: "the alarm did not sound." No one omitted anything at the time; it had been muted weeks earlier by another team. It still behaves like case (1) on our list, and I can't tell whether it belongs in the same box.

(4) Occasionally the non-action is on the good side: a resident did not override the standard protocol, and because she did not, the patient did better than he would have had she intervened. It feels right to call that a cause too, but the word sits badly with us in a review.

A philosopher friend said "that's just causation by omission," which covers every one of these and therefore none of the distinctions I need; another muttered about "double prevention" and wouldn't explain. Is there an established concept — or several — that carves this up the way I want: the selection problem in (1), the redundancy in (2), the non-agential items in (3), the beneficial ones in (4)? If several are needed, tell me which fits which case and where they overlap or conflict. If some combination of my cases has no established label, coin a term and define it with boundary cases. I want the carving, not a name.

Format: give me a markdown table with one row per candidate concept, established or coined, with columns: label | established_or_coined | definition_in_one_sentence | cases_covered (by number) | weakest_case (which of 1–4 it handles worst, and why) | coverage (0–100, how much of the total pattern it captures). Under the table, two short labelled lines: confidence (0–100) and what_would_change_this_verdict — the argument or distinction that would make you re-carve or relabel, and where your own carving is most likely to be wrong. Also say whether your answer depends on what the review is for (blame, learning, insurance), or whether "cause" has to mean the same thing in all four cases.
