You are a professional philosopher who specializes in metaphysics — numerical identity, individuation, and the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles. Please think carefully and show your staging: before you commit to anything, brainstorm the different general rules that could generate the verdicts I list below, check each of them against the new case, and only then give me your best guess. I am a software engineer with no philosophy training. I sit in on a metaphysics reading group and I have been writing down what the philosopher who runs it says about cases.

1. A universe containing nothing but two iron spheres of the same size, composition and temperature, a mile apart, alike in every intrinsic respect. She says: there are exactly two spheres, so the identity of indiscernibles is false. When I asked whether each isn't distinguished by being the one a mile from the other, she said no — that only uses the objects themselves to do the distinguishing, and by that test any two objects would be distinct.

2. A statue and the lump of clay it is made of, in the same place at the same time: two objects, not one, because they differ in what could happen to them — the lump survives being squashed, the statue does not. So a merely modal difference is enough to make two things.

3. When I asked what does the work in case 1, she said they are in different places, and what more did I want. But she also said that in case 2 place cannot do that work, since they share it, so something else has to. I could never tell whether she thinks being in a different place is itself a difference between the objects, or a fact about them that is not a difference at all.

4. I showed her my simulation, where two records can agree in every field and still be two records, because they sit at different addresses. She said addresses are no help as an analogy: an address is something you can inspect and compare, so it is one more property, and the real question is what happens where nothing of that kind is available. She did not say what happens then.

The new case. I am rewriting the simulation so that the state is an unordered set of particles — no identifiers, no array slots, no addresses, nothing in the world that could be inspected to tell one from another — and each tick's state is computed as a pure function of the previous tick's state. Two particles of identical mass start a mile apart, move symmetrically toward each other, and at the tick where they meet the resulting state is exactly the same set of positions and velocities whether you describe it as their having bounced off each other or as their having passed straight through. Nothing in the program settles which happened. (If I keep an array slot as part of each particle's state, the program can tell — but nothing inside the simulated world can read the slot, so I assume she will say that does not count. Tell me if that assumption is wrong.)

So: does she think there is a fact of the matter about whether the particle now on the left is the same particle as the one that started on the left? What are the rules consistent with verdicts 1 to 4, what does each say here, and which is she most likely running? I care less about who is right than about what she is already committed to. Finally, if her verdicts really do underdetermine this, give me the cleanest further case to put to her that would split the surviving candidates — I was going to ask her whether two possible worlds alike in every qualitative respect but differing only in which sphere is which are really two different worlds, but tell me whether that is the right probe or whether something else separates them better.
