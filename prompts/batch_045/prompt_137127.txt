I'm 71 and retired — 35 years as an experimental physical chemist, mostly calorimetry, which means I spent a career sticking error bars on things — and I've started sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university. They're working through a book about knowledge and its limits. I cant follow the book, so I rebuilt one of the arguments in my own terms, and I want to know whether my version is really the same argument and, if it is, where it cheats. Please write in British English, and go easy on notation — I don't mind technical philosophy, I just read symbols very slowly.

Here is my setup. A subject has her hand in a water bath whose temperature is ramped from 20.0 °C to 30.0 °C in 1000 steps of 0.01 °C; consecutive cases differ by far less than anyone can feel (I'm told the discrimination threshold for warmth is an order of magnitude bigger, and I'd believe it). Call the cases α0 to α1000. Let p say the water is at or above 25.0 °C. Her only evidence is how the water feels. At α0 she clearly knows ¬p; at α1000 she clearly knows p. "In a position to know q in a case" means: could come to know q in that case without any new evidence, just by attending to how things are with her. The group calls it luminosity when being in some condition itself puts you in a position to know you're in it.

(1) Luminosity for experience: for any mental condition M — feeling warm, being in pain, seeming to see red — if M obtains in a case, then in that case the subject is in a position to know that M obtains.

(2) KK, in the weak dispositional form: if a subject knows p in a case, then in that case she is in a position to know that she knows p. The group says this is just (1) with M set equal to knowing that p, but I don't see how, since knowing depends on how the world is and not only on how things seem to her.

(3) Margin for error: if a subject is in a position to know q in case α, then q is true in every case relevantly similar to α — similar, that is, with respect to her total evidence. Consecutive cases in my series are similar in that respect by construction.

(4) At α0 she knows ¬p.

(5) So by (2) she is in a position to know that she knows ¬p at α0; so by (3) she knows ¬p at α1 as well; so by (2) again at α1, and so on up the series.

(6) Hence she knows ¬p at α1000, where the water is at 30.0 °C. But knowledge is factive, so ¬p would be true there. Contradiction with the setup.

(7) The same run with (1) in place of (2): take M to be feeling cool. At α0 she feels cool; by (1) and (3) she feels cool at α1; by (1) again at α1 she feels cool at α2; and so on; so she feels cool at α1000, where by construction she feels warm and does not feel cool. Contradiction again — and nothing in the run depends on its being warmth rather than pain, or colour, or anything else we'd have thought was self-intimating.

(8) So nothing is luminous: not knowing, not feeling cool, not being in pain. In particular KK is false. (And if even the weak dispositional version fails, the strict version — knowing p implies knowing that you know p — fails with it, surely, since knowing something is one way of being in a position to know it. Or is that too quick?)

That conclusion is what makes me think the machine is rigged somewhere, because I don't believe for a second that a woman with her hand in near-freezing water might not be in a position to tell that it feels cool. But I can't find the rigged bit, and I want to know what I'm buying.

Three things I've tried. First: to be in a position to know by attending, she has to actually make a judgement, and a judgement takes a few hundred milliseconds, during which the bath has moved on. If I run the ramp fast enough — say the whole 10 degrees in 200 milliseconds — then there is no case at which she can exercise the disposition before the case is over. Doesn't that break the induction, and doesn't it show that "in a position to know" needs a time index the argument doesn't have? Second: similarity isn't transitive. α0 resembles α1 and α1 resembles α2, but α0 needn't resemble α2 much at all. Does the argument quietly assume it is? Third, and this is where I'd put my money: (3) is doing work it has no right to do. Error bars constrain measurements, not dispositions to measure. I can believe that if you *know* q, then q doesn't fail in a case you couldn't tell apart; I don't see why that constrains a mere ability to come to know. But when I say that, the group says the disposition is safe or reliable only if the same holds of it, and I can't tell whether that's an answer or a restatement.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (3) — an indistinguishable case where I'd be wrong is exactly the sort of sloppiness I spent my career stamping out — and to keep that we sometimes know things and sometimes know how things feel. I'm happy to drop (2) if that's cheapest, but I want to know the bill: does dropping it lose me the right to say "I don't know, and I know that I don't know"? That seems to be what I use whenever I decide to go and look something up. Is there any way to keep luminosity for experience while dropping it for knowledge, or does (7) show that if (1) and (3) stay, both go? And if instead I should give up (1) but keep (3), does it follow that whether someone is in a position to know isn't fixed by her evidence — because two cases indistinguishable from the inside can differ? As a natural scientist that repels me, though my son points out the water really is at a different temperature in the two cases, so maybe nothing repugnant follows at all and I'm just muddled. Tell me where the argument goes wrong, or tell me it doesn't and I have to swallow (8). Please write in British English.
