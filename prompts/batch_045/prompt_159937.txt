You are a philosopher who specialises in the metaphysics of virtual worlds and who has also sat on game-moderation appeals boards. Before you commit yourself, brainstorm the different rules our past decisions are severally compatible with, and only then choose between them.

I chair the appeals board of a large persistent multiplayer survival game. Four features of the game matter here: player-built structures that take tens of hours to raise; tamable creatures that die permanently on our server; a cash shop that sells licensed prank items explicitly advertised as part of the fun; and NPC companions whose dialogue model is fine-tuned by a single player's interactions over time, so that each companion develops quirks no other player's has.

Our precedents: (1) a player burned down another's forty-hour hall, using fire the physics allows but no server rule licenses — real harm, thirty-day ban. (2) A player pickpocketed another's coin using a thief class whose entire design is theft — not a harm, that is the game. (3) A player lured another's tamed wolf into lava on the permadeath server, in a case where the admins could in principle have restored it from backup — real harm, no sanction reduced on appeal. (4) A player followed another around for weeks saying nothing, standing close enough to block her camera, destroying nothing and breaking no written rule — an initial dismissal on the ground that nothing was destroyed was overturned, and we ruled it a real harm, reasoning that the game exists for the players' projects and that conduct which predictably ruins someone's experience with no game-sanctioned point is a harm even when nothing is deleted.

The case before us now: X used a shop-bought prank item, Rewit, on Y's companion, resetting its learned model to factory state. Y had spent some two hundred hours with that companion; the model's update history is not versioned server-side, so no restore brings back what it had become; the game's own code, however, shows that the companion stored nothing, and its apparent memories were generic continuations generated on the fly. Y is devastated; X says the item was sold for exactly this.

What will we most likely decide, which rule are we implicitly running, and which precedent is the closest analog — and where does that analogy strain? Whichever way we go, what principle about virtual objects and virtual harms would we be adopting, and would it commit us to saying the companion was real in some sense? Finally, name one further case that would best tell the rules apart, and state how confident you are in your prediction, singling out the claim you are least sure of.
