I'm in a tiny angel syndicate — eight of us, we all get the same pitch deck and then say yes or no one at a time in a shared channel, fixed order, everyone sees what everyone before them said. This spring we all piled into a deal that turned out to be a disaster: the two people who spoke first were both wrong and the rest of us just followed. Someone in the group said "that's an information cascade, it's rational, nothing to fix," and I've been chewing on that ever since, partly because we're about to vote on switching to simultaneous blind commitments next quarter and I want to know whether I'm voting for something real.

I tried to write out why the "it's rational" line bothers me and ended up with this, which I think proves too much but I can't find the bad step:

(1) More information never hurts. If your private signal is correlated with the true state, then a decision rule that conditions on it can't have lower expected payoff than one that ignores it. So the best available rule uses your private signal alongside everything public.

(2) Your signal isn't redundant with the public record: everyone else's signal is conditionally independent of yours given the true state, so the history doesn't already contain what you know.

(3) So your optimal move is whichever option maximizes expected payoff given your posterior after pooling the history with your own signal.

(4) Holding the history fixed, that posterior moves strictly with your signal — more favorable signal, higher posterior. So which option maximizes it has to come out different depending on what your signal said, at least sometimes. Your own evidence has to be able to flip your action.

(5) Herding = going with the crowd even when your own signal points the other way.

(6) By (4), herding means not maximizing expected payoff given everything you know. So herding is never optimal, and a rational agent never does it.

(7) And if nobody ever herds, then every action in the sequence tells you something new about that person's private signal, so the public record keeps improving and the group keeps getting more accurate the bigger it is.

(8) So: no lock-in, no fragility, no waste, ten people strictly better than three, and a group our size should get calls like this one right almost always.

(9) Which is absurd. Our eight behaved like three. I'm also fairly sure the standard version of this model says a cascade can settle on the wrong option with a probability that stays bounded away from zero however large the group gets, and that it starts once one option is ahead by two in the running tally. And (8) seems to prove too much elsewhere too — it would show I should never let a specialist's judgment override my own read of the evidence, which can't be right.

So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? If forced I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than anything that turns on how fine-grained a yes/no vote is, because (1) and (2) are the part that matches my actual complaint: my private doubt counted for nothing. But I can't tell whether I've definately built a proof that herding is irrational or just a proof that I've misread the model, and it matters for the vote — if the argument works then blind simultaneous commitments are the fix, and if it doesn't then either the fix is something else or there's nothing to fix.

What I'd like: name the step, say why it looked fine, and if there's more than one candidate, price them against each other — what am I committed to on each? Tell me how confident you are in what you're saying overall and flag the single point you're least sure about. And if the practical upshot for our procedure is different from what I think it is, say so plainly.
