I'm a senior at a large public high school and I convene our school's philosophy society: roughly fourteen members, mostly 16 to 18, a handful who read well beyond class and the rest who showed up because a friend did. We meet for 75 minutes on Thursday afternoons in a room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, and whatever I can carry in my bag — index cards, string, scissors, masking tape, a bag of counters. No projector, and I don't want a lecture: last term I opened a session with ten minutes of explanation and watched everyone's attention collapse, so I'd rather the structure do the teaching and have them run into the problem before anyone names it.

The session I can't get right is on the self as a center of narrative gravity — the view, as I met it in a popular essay (I believe the phrase is Dennett's, though I haven't been able to find the original), that a person's self isn't an extra item tucked inside them but is more like the center of gravity of a body: a point you can locate, reason about, and use to predict how the thing will behave, which is nowhere an additional part of it, and which in awkward cases falls in empty space rather than in any of the matter. What I want them to feel rather than be told is that this posit does real work — it can settle who gets praised or blamed, what someone will do next, whose story this is — while not being one more ingredient in the world; and then, crucially, that a single life admits more than one narration, that different narrations put the center in different places, and that the quarrel over which narration is the true one is exactly where the view gets tested rather than a question the view dissolves.

Could you design the session: rules, roles, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline, built around something they physically do with small but real stakes? I'd imagined a warm-up in which they find the actual center of gravity of an irregular cardboard shape and discover that for a hoop or a boomerang the point hangs in mid-air, followed by a staged case in which they assemble a character out of fragments I hand out and have to place a marker on the floor where that character's center lies, and then a scoring round where the payoff comes from predicting what the character does next, so the posit has to earn its keep instead of being asserted. I'd also like two late twists built in: one where two narratives of the same life predict equally well yet locate the center differently, and one where the narration that does the locating belongs to other people — a family's version of the person, a reputation, a community's story — so that the center seems to fall outside the person altogether. Please keep the label and the word fiction out of the room until the activity is finished. Then give me a debrief that maps each moment onto the part of the view it was meant to make vivid, an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (someone will insist the real self is just the brain; someone will decide the whole thing is obvious and stop participating) along with how to handle each, and a frank note on what the activity cannot stage and I will have to state out loud afterwards — my guess is that it can show the posit is useful and contested but not the metaphysical claim about what there is, though tell me if I've drawn the limit in the wrong place.
