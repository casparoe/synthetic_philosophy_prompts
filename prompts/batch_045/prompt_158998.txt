Longtime lurker, been reading the rationalist blogs and forums since roughly 2014, work on the ops/strategy side at a mid-sized AI lab — and there's an argument I keep having at my local EA meetup that I can't get straight, so I figured I'd try it out here. It's about the competitiveness constraint and whether the alignment tax is ever actually worth paying. My gut on individual cases is pretty confident; I just don't think my verdicts hang together, and I want to know whether there's a principle behind them or whether one of them has to go.

The cases, roughly as I argued them last week: burning a quarter of our training compute on interpretability and eval infrastructure that we reuse every generation — fine, barely a tax at all, it pays for itself within two cycles. Refusing to open-weight a mid-capability model because of misuse risk, knowing another lab will release something comparable within a year — fine, but I'm uneasy about it in a way I'm not about the first one. Unilaterally sitting on a finished frontier model for eight months to do safety work that produces nothing reusable — not fine. Funding a large internal safety org that mostly writes position papers and staffs committees, no reusable artifacts — not fine, and honestly it feels worse to me than the eight-month delay even though the delay costs way more, which definately seems wrong somehow. Taking a huge commercial contract that pays for our compute cluster but hands deployment decisions to the customer — not fine, feels like selling the thing the race was even for. Two rival labs merging specifically to end a race between them — fine, even though it looks exactly like the collusion everyone says is only fine in theory. Signing on to a competitor's safety standard because it's cheap and buys regulatory goodwill while changing nothing about how we build — not fine, pure safety-washing. Publishing an eval result showing a genuinely dangerous new capability, which accelerates everyone including the reckless labs — fine. Releasing the weights of the model that has that capability — not fine. That last pair is the one i keep circling: both hand capability-relevant information to people who will use it badly, and I still come out opposite ways.

What I keep reaching for is a three- or four-way distinction rather than one principle: a tax that amortizes, where you pay once and keep the artifact; a tax that's pure rent, where you pay every cycle and own nothing at the end; a cost whose benefit mostly accrues to whoever moves next rather than to you, which I've been calling "leakage"; and a fourth case where a rent that's tolerable each time compounds until you lose the race outright, at which point you have no influence over anything at all, "the cliff". Is there a single principle that gets all of my verdicts, or close to it? If not, tell me which verdict I should overturn and why, state the principle precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself, say what it predicts about some case I haven't listed, and what it says about the one I genuinely go back and forth on: a conditional slowdown, where we publicly commit to slowing down if and only if at least two of the other three frontier labs do the same, with third-party verification. Please don't just tell me it depends — commit to something. And no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables: continuous prose, like you're writing me an email back.
