I write long novels with an omniscient narrator, and in the course of revising I keep needing a distinction I have no name for. I am not asking for a reading list or for references; I want the distinction itself, stated sharply enough that I can use it.

Here is the pattern. Once the draft is finished, I go back to chapter two, where the narrator says of my protagonist that she would not see him again for eleven years. When I first wrote that sentence I had not decided the ending; when I had decided it, the sentence became true, and nothing inside the scene of chapter two changed to make it so. It now looks like a fact located at an early moment in the story but settled by an event much later in it. Compare a neighbouring sentence in the same paragraph: either she sees him again within eleven years or she does not. That one was never up to me, and no choice of ending could have made it false. So among the narrator's forward-pointing sentences there seem to be two kinds, and the difference between them is not explained by anyone's ignorance, since the narrator is omniscient in both cases, and not by whether the event takes place, since on either ending it takes place or it fails to take place.

A second thing I have noticed: the sentences of the first kind nearly always have a particular grammatical shape in my drafts, a past tense wrapped around a future, was going to, would later, was about to, as if the phenomenon were a feature of the language rather than of what the language is about. I do not trust that, but I do not know how to test it.

What I am fairly confident this is not: it is not the puzzle about whether an infallible forecaster deprives my characters of their freedom. The narrator's knowledge is not the variable here, since she knows the ending whatever it turns out to be, and she could assert the eleven-years sentence in chapter two of a story in which the parting is a free and undetermined act. And it is not, I think, simply the observation that truth is timeless, because that observation seems to erase the difference I am trying to draw rather than explain it.

So: is there an established concept for facts that are about one time but settled, or not settled, at another, or for the corresponding gap between a statement's being already true and its being already settled? I gather there is a very old puzzle somewhere nearby about a naval battle tomorrow, but as I understand it that puzzle asks whether such statements have a truth value at all, which is not quite my question. If my phenomenon has a name, tell me the name and then do the harder work: say what it excludes, how it differs from the neighbouring notions I have gestured at, and where exactly its boundary falls, because I want to know which of my narrator's forward-pointing sentences quietly take something away from my characters and which do not. If it has no name, coin one, define it, and give me the cases that would test the definition.
