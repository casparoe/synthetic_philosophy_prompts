I have been teaching myself some machine learning in the evenings and drifted into the theory side, where a popular book I picked up presents Solomonoff induction as, in effect, the perfect learner: the formal version of Occam's razor that any real algorithm is a rough approximation to. I do not have the mathematics to check the book myself, but several of its claims sound too clean, and I would rather know which ones to distrust than carry them forward. The passage goes roughly like this:

Solomonoff induction considers every possible computer program that could have generated the data you have seen, and it predicts by backing the shortest one, since the shortest program is the simplest explanation and simplicity is a guide to truth. The procedure is uncomputable, so nobody can actually run it, but this hardly matters in practice: any good file compressor approximates it, because compressing a file just is finding the short program that produces it. Its great virtue is that it contains no adjustable parameters and no subjective prior that anyone has to supply. It has been proved that its predictions converge to the true distribution as the data grows, with total error before convergence bounded by the complexity of the true source, so given enough data it settles on the correct theory and cannot be badly misled for long.

My own suspicion is about the no-subjective-choices claim, because I have seen it said that the probabilities depend on which universal machine or programming language you start from; the book's answer is only that this shifts things by a fixed constant that washes out as the data grows, which sounds like it sidesteps the question rather than answering it. That is the one place where I have a guess, and I suspect there are problems I cannot see.

Could you go through the passage claim by claim and sort it into what someone who actually works on this would accept as stated, what is a loose popularization they would wince at but let pass in spirit, and what is simply wrong? In particular I would like to know whether the book's closing moral survives correction: that Solomonoff induction gives us a principled, assumption-free explanation of why simpler theories tend to predict better, and thus settles the old worry about why induction works at all.
