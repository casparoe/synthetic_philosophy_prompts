I sit on the allocation committee of a small pooled giving fund, and for the past two years we have scored candidate causes on the three-factor framework that circulates in effective altruism: importance (how much value there would be if the problem were solved), tractability (how much progress a marginal dollar buys), and neglectedness (roughly, how little resource the rest of the world is directing at the problem relative to its scale). We multiply the three. A quant who joined our committee this year has written up an argument that the framework, taken literally, is incoherent, and I cannot find the seam. I reproduce it as he gave it.

(1) The three factors are meant to be independent axes, a cause's priority is their product, and you should give where the product is largest.
(2) Neglectedness is a function of how much resource is already going to a cause. The more that is already going in, the less neglected it is, and full crowding means zero neglectedness.
(3) Since the product contains neglectedness as a factor, a cause that ceases to be neglected has priority zero — however enormous the good still available in it, and however cheaply a dollar still buys that good.
(4) But our own giving changes how much resource is going in. So the framework has this dynamic consequence: back a cause, watch its neglectedness fall, watch its score fall below some other cause's, and move. Repeat. The framework therefore never licenses sustained investment in anything; its output is permanent hopping between causes. And if our fund is large relative to a field, we can exhaust a cause's neglectedness in a single cycle and be told to walk away from work we have just begun.
(5) It also has a static consequence that is arguably worse. Take two causes identical in importance and identical in what a marginal dollar does in them, but suppose one already has billions flowing to it and the other has nothing. The framework ranks the billion-dollar cause at zero and sends you to the empty one. That cannot be right: if the marginal dollar does the same thing in both places, the history of who funded what before you arrived is irrelevant to where your dollar should go.
(6) Everyone on the committee behaves as if (4) is a caricature and (5) is straightforwardly false. Importance and tractability are plainly doing real work in our deliberations, and I want to keep them. So the culprit must be neglectedness: it is not a third independent factor at all. Its only real content is that crowded fields have had their cheapest opportunities picked off, which is just tractability at the margin under another name. Multiplying it in double-counts, and the honest framework is importance times tractability — plain cost-effectiveness, with neglectedness demoted to a search heuristic.

I half want to accept (6), because I have never been able to say what neglectedness contributes beyond crowding. But (6) also says that everyone who has used a three-factor scorecard for years has been double-counting, which is a lot to swallow; and the quant's step from (1) and (2) to (4) has the smell of a scope error that I cannot locate.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look sound? If the flaw is in (2), tell me what neglectedness is actually a function of, and why the reading that generates (4) and (5) was the natural one to reach for. If the flaw is in the move from (1) and (2) to (4), say what allocation problem the framework is really solving — I suspect something about all-or-nothing versus marginal allocation is doing hidden work, and I want it made explicit. Then price the alternatives: what does it cost to keep neglectedness as a genuine third factor, what does it cost to demote it to a heuristic, and is there any reading on which both my committee's actual practice and the quant's (5) come out right?
