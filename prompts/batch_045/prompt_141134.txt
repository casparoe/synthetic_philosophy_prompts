I'm in a tiny reading group — me, a retired civil engineer, a postdoc, and one guy who mostly reads blogs — and we've been grinding through some contemporary metaphysics on grounding and fundamentality. Last month we got completly stuck on the whole-is-fundamental view (I think it's called priority monism?) because nobody could say what "fundamental" is even supposed to be a property of, and since then the blog guy has been pushing an analogy that I genuinely can't tell is illuminating or whether its doing all the work. He says: the fundamental facts are like the axioms of a theory and the derivative facts are like the theorems. The base is whatever everything else follows from and which itself follows from nothing, and "in virtue of" is just derivability read metaphysically instead of logically. He's using it to argue that fundamentality is cheap: axiomatizations are never unique (the same body of mathematics can be derived from lots of different starting points, and there's no fact of the matter about which set is really the axioms), so there's no fact of the matter about which facts are really fundamental either, and half the arguments about physicalism and its rivals are bookkeeping disputes in disguise.

I want to audit it properly instead of just trading intuitions, so: give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the theory, of the language, of the axioms, of a theorem, of a proof, and of two axiom sets having the same theorems — and then where it breaks. The mismatches I'm suspicious of: (1) entailment between sentences is one thing, and grounding is supposed to be hyperintensional and to relate facts, so "follows from" can't literally be the same relation; (2) theorems don't hold *because of* the axioms in any constitutive or causal sense, and the axiom/theorem split looks like an ordering we impose for epistemic reasons — what's obvious, what's provable from what — whereas fundamentality is supposed to be out there in the world whether or not anyone knows it; (3) the non-uniqueness point seems to cut both ways, since I vaguely recall that some theories can be axiomatized with a single axiom while others need infinitely many, and I have no idea what that's supposed to correspond to metaphysically; (4) another member prefers a totally different picture, the foundation-of-a-building one, where the base has to hold up the rest, and axioms don't obviously hold anything up.

Then the part I actually care about: which of those mismatches — or others I haven't thought of — are load-bearing for his conclusion that fundamentality is cheap and the big disputes are verbal, and which are just cosmetic weirdness in the mapping? Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the axiom picture carrying the whole thing? And if it doesn't survive, is there a better analogy: the group also threw out "fundamental facts are like the generators of a group" and "they're like the parts of a machine," and I couldn't tell those apart either, including what each of them quietly assumes about whether the fundamental things have to be the small things.
