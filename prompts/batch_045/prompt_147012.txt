Two rival accounts of what makes a given mark a token of the letter 'a' are on the table.

According to the intrinsic, or shape-cluster, account, a mark is an 'a' in virtue of where it falls in a space of perceived forms: it is an 'a' to the degree that it resembles the stock of learned exemplars, so 'a'-hood is graded, and a mark can be genuinely borderline between 'a' and something else.

According to the relational, or contrast-role, account, a mark is an 'a' in virtue of the job it does inside a writing system: it is the mark that a reader must be able to tell apart from the marks for 'o', 'e', 'd', and 'u', and whose substitution for those marks would yield different words. Shape matters only as whatever happens to secure those contrasts, so nothing about the form is essential in itself.

Compare the two accounts by testing them against three cases: (i) hands in which 'a' and 'd' consist of the same bowl plus a stem differing only in how far it rises, with no sharp break anywhere along that continuum, alongside the coexistence of single-storey and double-storey 'a's; (ii) scripts in which one and the same shape is an 'a' in one alphabet and an entirely different letter in another; (iii) a community that shares a language and an alphabet but in which every writer invents a private repertoire of marks, keeping them discriminable only within their own hand, while readers rely on a mapping from one hand to another.

Which of the two gives the better account of the essence of a letterform? Where exactly do the accounts diverge, and can either of them accommodate all three cases? Finally, on whichever account you defend, what becomes of the intuition that some renderings of 'a' capture the letter better than others — that a clumsy or stylistically wrong 'a' is still an 'a', but a poor one?
