On thursday I'm presenting at a joint econ-theory / philosophy workshop on the foundations of strategic reasoning. 25 minutes plus 15 for questions, and I'm more worried about the questions than the talk. Paper draft is done, slides are not.

My thesis: textbooks sell iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies *in mixed strategies* (call it IEMDS) as just the more powerful version of plain iterated strict dominance, but in games with three or more players it quietly imports an independence assumption that pure elimination doesn't have, and the correlation-robust version of the procedure gives strictly larger predictions. Sketch of the argument I'll give:

- A single round of mixed dominance is checked pointwise against pure opponent profiles, so at that step correlation doesn't enter at all.
- The step that does care is "eliminate a_i because it isn't a best response to any belief over the others' surviving strategies." My payoff isn't additively separable in the other two players' actions, so my expected payoff against a joint distribution over their profiles isn't the same as my expected payoff against that distribution's marginals. With three players, correlated conjectures genuinely enlarge the best-response set.
- With two players there's only one opponent, so a joint distribution over S_-i just *is* a mixed strategy of his, there's no distinction to draw, and IEMDS coincides with the correlation-friendly procedure (and hence with rationalizability). In three-player games the two procedures come apart.
- Rationality and common belief in rationality don't give you independence of beliefs for free; independence is an extra assumption about the type structure, so the textbook procedure is assuming more than the textbooks say it is.

The object I define: keep, at each round, exactly the strategies that are best responses to *some* arbitrary joint distribution over the others' surviving strategy profiles. I claim this is order-independent because the survivor operator is monotone. Second half of the paper introduces a variant I'm calling best-response-anchored elimination: you may only eliminate a_i in favour of a mixture that is itself still alive (not itself eliminable) at the next round — a hybrid of mixed dominance and iterated admissibility. I conjecture it's order-independent even though iterated weak dominance isn't; I have a proof for two players and a brute-force check over 3x3x3 games.

The room: two epistemic game theorists who think exclusively in type structures, belief hierarchies and RCBR-style characterizations, and who will treat a bare definition as unfinished business; one decision theorist who will want to know what a mixed strategy even is on my account (an act I randomize over, or a belief you hold about me?) and whether "dominance" is doing normative work; two behavioral/experimental people whose default is level-k and who will ask how many rounds of reasoning this needs and whether any subject has ever been shown to represent two opponents' actions as correlated; and one political scientist who builds applied models and will ask, reasonably, why anyone outside foundations should care.
