I'm a reporter who covers science and ideas, and I keep coming back to one source: a retired state-agency economist who sits on a technical review panel that has to sign off on quantitative models before anybody official is allowed to act on them. He is not a philosopher and would deny having a methodology at all, but over about four years I've watched him wave some submissions through and kill others, and I've kept notes. Now there's a genuinely nasty one in front of him and I want to know which way he goes — and, more than that, what rule he's been running this whole time.

His record, as I've got it written down:

- Frictionless planes, point masses, and massless cables in the model the state uses to certify a grandstand: approved in about ninety seconds, no discussion.
- Zero transaction costs in a cap-and-trade model used to forecast allowance prices after the cap tightened: approved.
- Firms modeled as perfectly informed profit maximizers, defended by a survival argument — competition selects for maximizers, so surviving firms behave as if they'd solved the optimization — used to predict which plants shut after a tariff: approved, though he spent the whole meeting asking whether the selection story had any teeth.
- A labor market treated as one instant-clearing auction, used to forecast the jobs effect of a small tweak to a state tax credit. It beat three rival models on out-of-sample error. Approved, and I wrote down his joke: the assumption was about as false as an assumption can be, and that was the point.
- An ocean treated as infinitely deep in a wave-height model for harbor design: approved, but only after he made them show him the range of wavelengths where it had been checked.
- Homogeneous mixing in a flu model fitted after the fact and used to explain why cases collapsed in March: killed.
- A single stable exponential discount rate in a model used to justify auto-enrolling people into a retirement plan: killed.
- A credit-spread model that was very accurate on the calm years it was calibrated on, used to size an emergency lending facility in the middle of a panic: killed.

The new submission: a state transportation department wants to use a model to pick which of two bridges to close for a two-year rebuild, and to decide how the detour gets signed. The model assumes every driver knows the entire road network and always takes the shortest path, that nobody learns anything from yesterday's commute, and that nobody uses a navigation app — rerouting is computed as though the whole network solved one global optimization all at once. Its track record is good: on three earlier closures in two other states it predicted average cross-town trip times to within six percent, better than the department's old model and better than a consulting firm's machine-learning version.

Here's the wrinkle. A university team rebuilt it with realistic driver knowledge, day-to-day learning, and roughly a third of drivers on routing apps. Replayed on those same three closures it fits a little worse — around eight percent error, and most of the gap comes from one of the three — but it ranks the two candidate bridges in the opposite order. It only flips for this particular pair; for most other pairs of bridges the two models agree.

So: what does he do? My philosopher friend says he's basically channeling Friedman — I'm paraphrasing, but the claim is that a theory's assumptions don't need to be realistic, and that the most significant theories tend to have the least realistic ones; she calls it the F-twist. But half his verdicts look like they're driven by something else entirely, and I can't tell whether the F-twist is the rule or just the thing he says at lunch. I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the machinery behind it.

Give me, with these sections in this order:

- "The rule he seems to be running" — about 150 words, one rule that fits all eight verdicts.
- "Other rules that fit just as well" — about 200 words, at least two more rules that his track record doesn't rule out.
- "What each one says about the bridges" — about 250 words, including where the rules actually diverge and why.
- "Your call" — about 150 words, your best guess at his verdict and the reason you're going with it.
- "The case that would settle it" — about 150 words, one further submission you'd want in front of him to separate the surviving rules.

Keep it plain English, no citations or reading lists — I can't chase down references on deadline and he'd deny every word of it anyway.
