Last week we worked through Clifford's essay on the ethics of belief and then James's reply to it, and I thought I understood the shape of the fight: Clifford says, in the sentence everyone quotes, that "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence," and he judges a belief by how you came by it rather than by how it turned out, so that the shipowner is guilty even if the ship makes port; James answers that when an option is forced, live, and momentous and the evidence can't settle it, our passional nature is entitled to decide, because withholding belief can cost you a truth that only the belief itself could have reached. The problem is that our discussion kept circling the same two examples — Clifford's shipowner and the religious hypothesis — and, since half the club is religious and half isn't, the second one always collapses into an argument about religion rather than about evidence. Here is the kind of case I'm after, and I'd like you to build one rather than recall one. It should have three features at once. Third, James's permission should look as though it applies, even though the point of the belief is not that the believer thereby gains access to a truth he would otherwise miss, since he isn't the one who benefits. What interests me is the inversion this produces: Clifford is the one with the social argument — that bad believing corrupts the habits of inquiry everyone depends on and wrongs the community — whereas James's permission looks like it concerns what I may wager for my own sake; yet in this sort of case Clifford's own social reasoning seems to pull toward the belief and James's individual one seems to have nothing to say.
