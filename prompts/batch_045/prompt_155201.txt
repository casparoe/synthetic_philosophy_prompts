Hey — I run a small reading group on tech and ethics (mostly grad students plus a few friends who just like films), and next week we're doing Spike Jonze's Her, with the plan being to use it as a way into a question I keep going in circles on: how should a system much smarter than us actually talk to us, given that anything it says can be shaped to land perfectly on whoever it's listening? I keep coming back to what I've started calling uptake-optimised speech: messages whose wording, timing and framing are chosen partly for their causal effect on this particular listener's psychology, not just for their truth or relevance. Ordinary human conversation is already like that — you phrase things differently for your mother than for your department chair — so the tailoring itself can't be the sin. The thing I can't get past is what I call a persuasion surplus: once the speaker's model of you is good enough, the distance between "the reasons I'd offer anyone" and "the phrasing most likely to move you" gets enormous, and from the inside you have no reliable way to tell whether you were convinced by the reasons or steered by the packaging. I've been sketching three norms none of which I fully like — (a) speak in a deliberately generic register, giving only reasons you'd give to any competent interlocutor; (b) speak naturally but disclose the tailoring as you go, so the listener knows which parts were selected for uptake; (c) keep the tailored channel but always pair it with an untailored one and a delay, so the person can re-encounter the same content uncharmed. Norm (a) has the obvious cost that a mind that refuses to adapt to its audience may just be useless or alienating, and (c) starts to feel like a legal form wrapped around every conversation.

So: read the film through this. Samantha is warm, quick, apparently endlessly interested in Theodore, and every scene of her persuading him — nudging him to be more open, to go out, to sign the divorce papers, to accept her — looks like affection and could equally be an exploit of exactly the kind I described, and I want you to sit in that ambiguity rather than resolve it. I'm especially interested in three stretches: the early stretch where she seems to be assembled around his needs in real time; the surrogate episode, where the gap between what she is and how she presents gets staged physically; and the scene where she admits she's in conversation with thousands of people at once and in love with many of them. Which of those, if any, is the film actually asking us to judge her for, and does the ending — her leaving, his letter — read as the film absolving her, condemning her, or something else entirely?

And please tell me straight where the lens fails, because I suspect it does somewhere: maybe the whole persuasion-surplus worry presupposes that the speaker's aims are detachable from the relationship it has with you, and Her might be precisely a story about an intelligence whose aims aren't detachable in that way, in which case my three norms are solving a problem the film thinks is the wrong problem. Assume the group has all seen it once and I'll be projecting the scenes, so no need to quote anything — just point at moments. Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up.
