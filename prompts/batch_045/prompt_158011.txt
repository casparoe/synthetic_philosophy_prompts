I am working through the case notes of a moral philosopher who retired last year and has stopped answering queries about them. For roughly two decades she kept a running list of miniature cases and recorded, for each, a verdict on the single question of whether the agent in the case has a reason to do the thing described. She is usually classified as an externalist about reasons, but her verdicts are considerably more specific than that label, and I want to know what rule she is actually running before I build an argument on her cases. Here is the record, in my paraphrase:

1. A thirsty man wants a gin and tonic and believes the tumbler in front of him contains one; it contains petrol. Verdict: no reason to drink it.
2. A donor wants to relieve famine suffering and believes that a certain charity passes nearly all of its donations through to the field; it does not, and he would give nothing to it if he knew. Verdict: no reason to give to that charity.
3. A smoker has no desire to quit, would acquire none from better medical information, and would in fact gain years of healthy life by quitting. Verdict: he does have a reason to quit.
4. A passer-by could save a stranger's life at trivial cost to herself; she has no desire to, no relationship to him, no office or role that touches him, and nothing of her own at stake. Verdict: no reason to intervene — a ruling she herself flags as the one readers most want to reject.
5. A department chair agreed to take the post; she now has no desire to do the annual paperwork, no one's interests are affected either way, and doing it makes her week slightly worse. Verdict: she has a reason to do it.
6. An archivist finds an error in a catalogue entry she made years ago; correcting it costs her a weekend, no one else knows of it, no living person is affected or will ever find out, and she no longer cares about accuracy. Verdict: she has a reason to correct it.
7. A woman promised a dying friend to scatter his ashes at a place he named. He has since died, no one else knows of the promise, no one is affected either way, fulfilling it costs her something small but real, and she no longer cares. Verdict: no reason to do it.

The case I need a verdict on is this: a senior researcher routes every professional invitation through an automated assistant configured to accept review requests whenever his calendar has room. The assistant accepts an invitation from a journal; he never sees the acceptance, has no desire to do the review, would not acquire one on reflection, and doing it would make his month worse; the editor has slotted him into the roster and would be left short if he withdrew. He never made or ratified this particular undertaking himself.

What would she say? I care less about the verdict than about the machinery behind it: give me the two or three rules that fit verdicts 1 through 7 about equally well, state what each predicts about the automated-acceptance case, tell me which you would bet on and why, and describe one further variant of that case — concretely, not merely in outline — that would separate the rules that survive. Please be explicit about where the record genuinely underdetermines her answer rather than smoothing the tension over, say how confident you are in the prediction and in attributing each rule to her, and single out the claim you are least sure of.
