I'm a PhD student working on the norms of inquiry (my thesis is on epistemic permissiveness in peer disagreement, so this is adjacent rather than central), and I've been running a reading group with two other grads and one faculty member on classical pragmatism. We've been on Peirce versus James on truth for about five sessions now and I think we've hit the point where everyone has said their piece and nothing is landing anymore, so I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

Here's where we are. Peirce's line, roughly: truth is the opinion that inquiry carried on indefinitely would settle on, the ideal limit the community of investigators would converge to, and crucially the real is independent of what you or I or any finite bunch of us happens to think — it's supposed to be a realist view, not an idealist one. James: truth is what it is good, or expedient, to believe; truth is a species of the good; the cash value of an idea is the difference it makes to somebody's actual life; truth is something that happens to an idea rather than a standing relation it has. And then the familiar moves: the objection that the expedient and the true come apart, since it can be useful for me to believe something false; James's reply that he means expedient in the long run and in the widest sense, which starts to look like it collapses back into Peirce; and Peirce eventually renaming his own view partly out of irritation at what had happened to the shared label.

So the received state of play is: either James is Peirce said badly with a more individualist emphasis, or James is doing something entirely different and the shared name is a historical accident. Both readings have been defended, everyone in the room knows every move, and nobody has moved in five weeks.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: I don't think they're arguing about the same thing. Peirce looks to me like he's asking what the property of truth consists in, and his data are the long-run practice of the sciences. James looks like he's asking what the concept of truth is for, and his data are the cases where the long-run story has nothing to say to the person who has to decide now — religious belief, ethical commitment, anything where inquiry can't settle things inside the agent's lifetime. On that reading the debate is two questions under one heading and the arguments don't engage because they aren't addressed to each other. But I'm not confident. It might be that they're both answering the same question and differ on something deeper: which failure mode each is trying to rule out (Peirce: relativism; James: a notion of truth that does no work for anyone), or what even counts as evidence about truth.

What made me think there's a substantive disagreement hiding in there is a case I made up to poke at it, which nobody in the group had a clean answer to. Call it the filtered archive. Take a community of investigators over an indefinite future, using methods that are flawless by their own lights, but suppose every piece of evidence they get passes through a single channel that silently drops one whole class of result. Nobody in the community can detect the loss, because the dropped results leave no trace they have access to. They converge, stably and permanently, on a theory that is in fact false. Variant: there are two such communities whose evidence streams diverge for good, each converges on a theory incompatible with the other's, and neither is epistemically blameworthy or has any available reason to suspect it's the one with the lossy channel.

What I want is what each of them is committed to saying about that case, and what the shape of the disagreement looks like once you see it. Does Peirce have to bite the bullet and call the false theory true, or does he have resources — the independence of the real, his would-be's, whatever daylight he leaves between the final opinion and truth itself — to say no? Does James have to say each community's belief is true for them, and is that a reductio of his view or closer to what he was after all along? Does the fact that they'd answer differently here show the dispute is doing real work, or does it just restate the standoff in new vocabulary?

And then the part I actually need: given whatever you think the diagnosis is, what would count as progress? Is there a distinction that dissolves it — a theory of the truth property versus a theory of the concept's point, or an ideal-limit reading of convergence versus a counterfactual-methods reading? Is there a formal question doing the work in the background, something about whether convergence is even guaranteed when evidence streams branch, or about how to aggregate the credences of agents who can't see each other's evidence, that we could actually settle and that would shift the philosophical argument? Or is the question as posed just not well formed and we should stop meeting about it?

Don't walk me through the two theories — I've read enough of both that a summary is just noise. I want the diagnosis, tied to what each of them actually commits himself to, and an honest verdict on whether this is resolvable or whether we should spend our remaining sessions on something else.
