I do interpretive planning at a mid-sized regional history museum — basically I'm the person who reads every exhibit script before it goes upstairs, which is a dozen or so a year, and each one is packed with "why did this happen" claims: wall text, panel copy, audio tour stops, the seven-minute intro video. We have no way of telling the difference between a script that actually explains something and one that just tells a story that happens to stop where the thing we're explaining starts. And I get maybe forty minutes per script, no one on my team has any philosophy training, and whatever comes out of the review has to survive a meeting with a director who has opinions, a community advisory board that has more, and eventually a docent saying it out loud to a fourth grader and a donor in the same breath.

I've read a little of this literature — the old covering-law picture where you're supposed to subsume the event under a general law, and then the people who say no, narrative explains in its own right and doesn't need a law bolted underneath. Please turn whatever in that fight is actually usable into a procedure I can run on one single claim: the questions in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the real disagreement is about what's worth saying rather than about what happened.

Two things specific to us that I'd like the procedure to either use or explicitly throw out:

- Our house rule, which came from a funder: every causal claim has to be one of three things — a mechanism ("the mill closed because the embargo tripled its fuel costs and it ran on fuel oil"), a counterfactual ("even without the levee, the '27 flood still would have taken the east bank"), or what they call a "legibility" claim, one that just makes the sequence graspable. I suspect the third bucket is where all the dead weight is hiding.
- A test one of our board members keeps pushing: tell the story backwards. If the chain still holds up in reverse there's real law-backed structure under it; if it collapses, it was only ever narrative. I half believe this and half think its nonsense, and I can't say which half is right.

I also need to know how the procedure handles claims that are only true because of how things later turned out — "the seeds of the '67 riot were sown when the streetcar tracks came up in '52" type sentences — and flat inevitability claims like "it was only a matter of time before the mine shut." Those are the ones our director loves and I have no vocabulary to push back on.

Then run it on three claims from the script on my desk right now: (1) the mill-closure-with-fuel-costs line above; (2) the streetcar/seeds line; (3) a ten-panel chronology of a mayor's two terms, 1948 to 1956, that a donor wants captioned "How the modern city got built." One of those should definately come out badly for me and I'd rather hear it from you than in the meeting.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether a usable procedure exists here and what its spine is — then the procedure, then the three applications, then what the procedure can't do for me. No preamble and don't restate the question back at me.
