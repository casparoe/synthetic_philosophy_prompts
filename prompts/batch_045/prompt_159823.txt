You are a philosopher of cognitive science who has followed the predictive processing and free energy principle literature closely and who is also at home in the philosophy of science literature on explanation, models, and theory structure. Please think carefully about what follows: before you commit to a verdict, spend a few paragraphs sketching the candidate diagnoses and saying what evidence in the debate would discriminate between them, and only then give me the diagnosis you think is best and tell me why the runners-up fail. Argue from the structure of the positions, not from citations — I have no database access where I'm writing this, and a reading list is the one thing that would be useless to me.

The situation: I'm a philosophy of science PhD student working on mechanistic explanation, and I have now twice watched a graduate reading group on predictive processing collapse into the same unmoving argument. The pattern is always the same. The defenders present the framework as a genuinely unifying principle: perception, action, attention, and learning all fall out of one quantity being minimized, the boundary between agent and world gets drawn formally via Markov blankets, and the analogy offered is to something like the principle of least action — a constraint on how any persisting system can be described, which organizes a research program rather than competing with mechanism-level stories. The critics then run four objections that I have come to think cannot all be doing the same work. First, the vacuity charge: any system that maintains its boundaries over time can be redescribed as minimizing free energy, so the principle has no empirical content and cannot lose. Second, the falsity-or-unsupportedness charge: the specific neural story — hierarchical cortical circuits passing precision-weighted prediction errors upward and predictions downward, with neuromodulators encoding precision — is not established, and some of the signature phenomena have alternative explanations. Third, the old-news charge: at the level of computation this is hierarchical Bayesian inference, which we already had, plus familiar ideas from constructivist and analysis-by-synthesis theories of perception; at the level of implementation it is a relabeling of known circuitry. Fourth, the ideology charge: the framework smuggles in a passive, brain-bound, representational picture of the mind, and the more situated and enactivist critics think this is not an empirical slip but a metaphysical commitment disguised as one. A framework cannot simultaneously be contentless, false, and trivially equivalent to what we already believed. So my first question is what that instability in the critical case tells us: is the critical side united by anything other than dislike of the framework?

Here is my own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis. I think the debate is stalled because the two sides are answering different questions under one name, and because the framework's defenders slide between three things that never get separated: a mathematical claim about systems with Markov blankets; a process theory about cortical message-passing; and a modeling language or heuristic for building task-specific models. When the first is challenged as empty, the defender retreats to "it was only ever a normative principle." When the second is challenged as unsupported, the defender retreats to "the principle doesn't commit you to an implementation." When the third is accused of being unfalsifiable, the defender points to the genuinely risky commitments of the second. I have started calling this the retreat cycle, and I want to know whether that is a fair description of the literature's dialectic or whether I have built an uncharitable caricature out of two or three exchanges. In particular: is the sliding between levels a legitimate feature of a research program at this stage — a principle constraining a space of process theories without entailing one — or is it the signature of a framework whose content moves as needed?

Second hypothesis I can't shake: part of this is verbal. "Prediction," "inference," "model," and "representation" each have at least three senses in play — a temporal one, as in predicting the next input; a statistical one, as in inferring hidden causes; an ordinary one, as in forecasting a distal event; and "model" hovers between a probability density over causes and a decoupled internal simulacrum. It looks to me as though enactivist critics and internalist defenders are using these words differently and then treating the disagreement as empirical. But I don't trust myself on how much of the impasse this actually explains, versus how much is real disagreement about what a theory in cognitive science is even for.

So, concretely, what I want: a diagnosis of the disagreement, with the sub-debates separated — I suspect the dark room objection, the vacuity objection, and the enactivist objection are three different kinds of thing, and I want to know which is verbal, which is a genuine empirical question, which is a clash of standards of explanation, and which is bedrock methodological commitment that neither side can argue for. Then: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? I have my own candidates — a formal result about which systems admit a Markov blanket description at all, or about what the principle rules out; a decisive neural result about layer-specific prediction error signaling that the framework could not absorb; an explicit taxonomy that fixes the framework to one level of description and forbids the slide; or a change of question, from "is it true" to "what does modeling this way buy you." Tell me which of these would actually move things, whether I've missed a better one, and be honest if your diagnosis implies that no progress should be expected and that the literature should be read as a research program rather than a dispute with a resolution.
