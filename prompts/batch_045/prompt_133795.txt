Hi — I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm finally doing the piece I've been threatening to write on how you'd actually build a science of consciousness. A philosopher friend made me write down my methodological profile first so she can poke holes in it, and looking at it now it's mostly things I absorbed by hanging around labs rather than anything I've checked. Here it is:

(1) First-person report — verbal or otherwise — is the only evidence that can settle whether a subject was conscious of something. If they can't report it, no experiment, however fancy, shows they experienced it.

(2) But I also think demanding report wrecks the measurement: asking someone to report seems to change what they experience and what turns up in the data, which is why I'm sympathetic to the no-report paradigms poeple keep arguing about, and I think the field should be weaning itself off report as a read-out.

(3) I like that Dennett-style heterophenomenology is sold as metaphysically neutral — it brackets whether the experiences it attributes are really there, so a realist about qualia and someone who thinks there's nothing extra can both use it.

(4) I also think that once you adopt it, the hard problem more or less evaporates: fix every disposition to report in every circumstance and there's no further question left to ask.

(5) I think the covert awareness findings — patients who look completely unresponsive but seem to follow commands with their brain activity — really do establish that some of them are conscious. That's a fact about what it's like for them, arrived at without anything that looks like a report.

(6) I like that the method takes subjects at face value instead of overruling them about their own experience; that's most of why I prefer it to the harder-nosed stuff.

(7) Last one, and it's the bit I want to put in the piece: I think you could legitimately run the method on a language model's first-person outputs, and I think what you'd get is a fiction — wich I take to be what you always get anyway.

So: does this hang together? Which pairs actually pull against each other and which only look like they do? Rank the real tensions by how bad they are and tell me for each what I'd have to give up and what that would cost me — I honestly can't see where the problem is and I don't know which one I'd drop. And since some of this is going to end up in print, be clear about which claims are settled among people who actually work on this and which are contested, and don't let a contested one come out sounding like the consensus view.
