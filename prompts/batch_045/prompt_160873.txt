Hi — I'm one of three people on the committee that hands out my workplace's "make work better" fund. It's tiny: £4,000 a year, and anyone can ask for anything as long as they can say with a straight face that it improves their working life. We've recieved four requests this round, about £2,600 left after the earlier ones, and I keep going in circles.

Mo wants £1,100 for an adjustable desk and a particular kind of chair because of a back thing — he can't sit in a normal office chair for more than an hour or so without it flaring up. Everyone agrees that's what the fund is for.

Jules wants £900 for a proper espresso machine and grinder for the kitchen. He got into coffee during lockdown and now says a bad coffee genuinely ruins his afternoon while a good one is the best part of his day, and its not like he's being ironic about it.

Sam asked for £160 to fix her bike so she can cycle in. She grew up with not much money, says cycling is the thing that makes her week work, and she seems to get more out of that £160 than anyone gets out of anything else on the list.

Rhys — the third person on the committee — says we should just split what's left equally, £650 each, spend it on whatever you like. His argument is that the moment we start ranking how much people actually enjoy things, we're judging whose pleasures count, and that it's both a bit creepy and totally gameable: whoever moans loudest about their coffee wins.

But then he said something I can't get out of my head, which is that if we go by "who gets the most out of the money", we end up giving the least to the person who's easiest to please and the most to someone who has trained himself to need £900 of equipment to have a decent afternoon. And he pointed out that Sam's £160 might not be a fact about her at all — it might just be that she's used to wanting less.

My gut says: fund the chair, fund the bike, don't fund the coffee machine, and feel slightly bad about it. But I can't actually say why the coffee machine is the odd one out, or why "she's used to wanting less" should change what we spend rather than being a reason to spend the same. Can you set out what's really going on here — what the different ways of deciding are, where each of them runs into trouble, and whether my gut survives scrutiny? Is there anything in Rhys's equal-shares idea or is it just a way of ducking the question? Last thing: Mo reckons that if we buy the chair we should count it against his share of the fund rather than treating it as an extra on top, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's fair or whether it just penalises him for being the one with the back problem.

Please answer in British English, and in plain language — I'm an accountant, not a philosopher.
