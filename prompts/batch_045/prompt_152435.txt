I run model risk for a vendor that ships churn and collections scoring models to around forty community banks and credit unions. About four times a year we re-fit each model, and before a re-fit ships, an analyst has to sign off on the feature set: roughly sixty candidate features a quarter, each reviewed by someone with SQL and applied statistics but no philosophy, in twenty to thirty minutes, on a one-page internal form that ends in one of four dispositions — admit; admit with mandatory post-deployment drift monitoring; admit only if re-derived from base fields; reject — plus three lines of rationale that I countersign and that compliance reads for fair-lending exposure. Right now my analysts justify a feature almost entirely by its lift on an out-of-time holdout, and I have come to think that check is nearly circular for exactly the features that worry me. A grue-style predicate — say, one that applies to accounts opened before our 2021 onboarding redesign and to accounts opened after it only if they satisfy some unrelated condition — fits any finite run of past data exactly as well as the honest predicate does, so a holdout carved out of the same pre-build window cannot separate them, and the difference only appears after deployment, when we have already shipped and the banks have already acted on the scores.

What I want from you is a written procedure my analysts can run per feature, grounded in whatever is actually usable from the philosophical literature on the new riddle of induction — Goodman's idea that projectibility tracks the entrenchment of a predicate in a vocabulary that has a real history of being projected, the point that grue and green are interdefinable so that no purely syntactic or definitional test can adjudicate between them, and the role of higher-level overhypotheses about kinds. It has to survive translation to people who will not read any of that: give the steps in the order they should be run, say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, say what to do when a step cannot be answered from the data and documentation we actually have (including what an analyst should use as a working proxy for entrenchment — e.g. whether the predicate's family shows up in previously shipped models, in monitoring reports, or in how the bank's own staff describe their customers), and mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and where the call has to be handed to me or to compliance because it is about values and cost asymmetry rather than facts. It also has to handle the cases that break the simple picture: features anchored to a date or to the scoring run itself; features defined by a threshold the bank sets and periodically changes, so their extension silently shifts; features defined by reference to another model's output, so that the predicate's extension moves when we act on the prediction; and features we cannot state in words at all, such as a thirty-two-dimensional embedding trained on transaction text, where the question of what predicate is being projected may not be answerable in the vocabulary of the form.

Please apply the finished procedure to three features from our current build and show the filled-in reasoning, not just the verdict. First, legacy_cohort: true of an account if and only if it was opened before the 2021 onboarding redesign; it is in the champion collections model and accounts for about eight percent of its lift. Second, referred_by_legacy_rules: whether the account was flagged for collections under the rule set we retired in 2022 — it is the single highest-gain feature in the new build, and its extension was produced by a system we no longer run. Third, a thirty-two-dimensional embedding of transaction description strings from a pretrained model, which we have reason to think encodes the transaction month because the raw strings contain month tokens; here I want to know what the procedure even asks, given that no one can write down the predicate. If your procedure implies that the out-of-time holdout result is close to worthless for features of the first two kinds, say so plainly rather than softening it, even though that is the evidence my team has been signing off on for years. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: what it has to say about a genuinely new kind — we are about to score behaviour on a payment product that has existed for eleven months, so no predicate for it has any projection history — and how it handles the tension between entrenchment and mechanism, since we usually know a causal story for the features we trust and entrenchment as such seems indifferent to whether there is one.
