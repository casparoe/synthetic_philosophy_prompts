I run a 90-minute session for about eighteen students in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on concepts and categories — mostly philosophy and cognitive science majors, none of them typographers. I want them to feel, before they are told anything, what is at stake in the question of what makes every 'a' an 'a': the problem behind Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project, where a letterform's identity seems to resist any checklist and to depend in part on the other letters in the same font. Design a hands-on activity using only paper, gridded worksheets and markers, in three movements. First, students work in groups to state an explicit rule that decides whether any figure drawn on the grid is an 'a', and then one student is assigned the role of adversarial collaborator and must draw a figure that satisfies their rule while being something no one would accept as an 'a'. Second, in pairs, each student draws three or four lowercase letters in a self-consistent style, swaps the partial set, and must both extend the other's half-finished alphabet and identify which of two continuations is their own — with a payoff scheme in which successful imitation and successful detection both score. Third, students judge the very same letterform twice, once presented alone and once presented alongside the other letters of the font it was drawn for, so that they experience its identity shifting with its siblings. Give me the grid, the rules, the scoring, a timeline, and how to run the adversarial round without its collapsing into a joke. Then a debrief that maps each movement onto a different kind of answer — a rule-based one, a similarity- or prototype-based one, and one in which a letter's essence is defined only relative to the whole alphabet and its neighbours — saying which of these the activity genuinely makes vivid and which the participants will simply have to be told about. Finally, be honest about what will go wrong: the artists in the room, students who refuse to judge, forgeries that are trivially easy or impossible, and the risk of the whole thing turning into a drawing contest.
