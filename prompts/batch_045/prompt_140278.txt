I'm in a Sunday reading group — four physicists and one philosopher of science — working through Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, and I've discovered that I have very firm, and possibly inconsistent, intuitions about which historical programmes were *degenerating* at which moments. I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them. Here are my verdicts. The phlogiston programme after the weight-gain-on-calcination anomaly, when its belt was absorbing the anomaly by attributing negative weight to phlogiston and invoking buoyancy effects: degenerating, and I want to say it was already degenerating in the 1770s even though work done inside the programme by people who believed in phlogiston produced genuine discoveries (various new gases, including what we now call oxygen) — those discoveries, I feel, belong to the rival oxygen programme and shouldn't be credited to phlogiston. The Lorentzian aether programme around the time it absorbed the null result of the Michelson–Morley experiment by postulating that moving bodies contract along their direction of motion: I want to call that move degenerating, and I want to call Einstein, a decade later, progressive — and then I get uncomfortable, because the contraction and time-dilation relations that came out of saving the aether turned out to be right, were independently confirmed later (muon decay in the atmosphere, the spectroscopic measurements of the late 1930s), and were carried over essentially unchanged into relativity. So my verdict is that the aether programme was degenerating *and* that its belt contained a truth that outlived it, and I can't say what that combination amounts to. Wegener's continental drift in the interwar decades: no confirmed novel predictions for thirty years, a mechanism that everyone had refuted, and near-universal rejection by geologists — and I refuse to call it degenerating. It seems to me to have been sitting on a pile of otherwise-unexplained facts about fossil and glacial distributions and the fit of the coastlines, and no rival then on offer explained any of them, so the honest verdict is not "degenerating" but something like "not yet due a verdict."

Now the case that breaks me. String theory and the supersymmetric-dark-matter programme of the last forty years: enormous internal theoretical development, no confirmed novel prediction, a target domain that no rival programme covers, and an anomaly (reconciling gravity with quantum field theory) that nobody else is making progress on either. My gut says degenerating, and I say it confidently — but my gut also said *not* degenerating about continental drift in 1935, and I can't point to a difference that isn't just my dislike of the one and my affection for the other. A friend in the group has proposed that degeneration is a three-place relation: a programme degenerates at a time only if a rival programme has by then accounted for whatever successes the first one had, so that its protective belt is growing without anything left to protect. That fits Wegener beautifully, and it fits phlogiston (the oxygen programme had taken over phlogiston's explaining by 1785 or so), but it would force me to give up my verdict on string theory — and it would say something odd about the gravitational-wave case, where decades of apparatus-building produced no new confirmations at all until the detectors finally reached sensitivity, and where I want to say, again, that no degenerating was going on: the prediction had been stated in advance, in a fixed form, and the delay was instrumental rather than theoretical. I also have two cases I've made up, where I have no verdict and want the principle to supply one. Call the first *confirmation-without-novelty*: every belt hypothesis a programme has added in thirty years has been independently testable and independently confirmed, and the programme accommodates every datum in its domain, but not one of those hypotheses has ever predicted a fact that wasn't already known when the hypothesis was proposed. Call the second *definitional retreat*: a programme whose hard core is partly definitional, and which answers each anomaly by reinterpreting its central terms in a way that is individually well-motivated and published in advance of the anomaly, so that nothing is ever risked but nothing is ever ad hoc either — the case I keep going back and forth on, and the one I actually care about.

So: give me the criterion you think I'm groping toward, stated precisely enough to apply, with each clause tied to the case that forces it. I want your verdicts on the two cases I invented and on a case I haven't thought of. I want to know which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and whether the cost of accommodating them is worth paying — I'd rather have a two-clause principle that overturns one of my verdicts than a seven-clause one that saves all of them, but I want to see both. And one more thing: the philosopher in my group says that on Lakatos's own view these verdicts are only available retrospectively, which strikes me as convenient for the philosopher and useless to me — so tell me what, if anything, your criterion licenses a working scientist to do *at the time*, when deciding where to put a graduate student or whether to keep funding a collider.
