I am preparing to lead a graduate reading group through Molyneux's problem, and what is blocking me is not the secondary debate but a cluster of difficulties in Locke's own statement of the case, which get worse the more closely I look at them. I would be grateful for your help with the passage below. It comes from the eighth section of the chapter "Of Perception" in the second book of An Essay Concerning Human Understanding — the section headed "Sensations often changed by the judgment," which opens by arguing that when a uniformly coloured globe is set before our eyes the idea actually imprinted is of a flat circle variously shadowed, and that judgment, by habitual custom, converts that appearance into the perception of a convex body. The blind man is introduced at the end of that section, and the passage runs as follows:

I shall here insert a problem of that very ingenious and studious promoter of real knowledge, the learned and worthy Mr. Molineux, which he was pleased to send me in a letter some months since; and it is this:—"Suppose a man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a cube and a sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and the other, which is the cube, which the sphere. Suppose then the cube and sphere placed on a table, and the blind man be made to see: quaere, whether by his sight, before he touched them, he could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube?" To which the acute and judicious proposer answers, "Not. For, though he has obtained the experience of how a globe, how a cube affects his touch, yet he has not yet obtained the experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so; or that a protuberant angle in the cube, that pressed his hand unequally, shall appear to his eye as it does in the cube."—I agree with this thinking gentleman, whom I am proud to call my friend, in his answer to this problem; and am of opinion that the blind man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say which was the globe, which the cube, whilst he only saw them; though he could unerringly name them by his touch, and certainly distinguish them by the difference of their figures felt.

The first thing I cannot settle is what the question is. The phrase "distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube" seems to me to conflate three demands I want kept apart: concurrent matching, where he holds one object in his hand while seeing both and indicates which visible thing is the one he is holding; deferred naming, where he has felt both beforehand, sets them down, and is then shown them and asked which is the cube; and bare visual discrimination, where he need only sort the two visible objects as same or different without applying either name. Tell me which of these the wording requires, which of them Molyneux's stated reason actually rules out, and whether any one of them could be answered affirmatively while granting that reason in full. My suspicion is that the reason is powerless against the third, and I want to know whether the text can bear that reading or whether "distinguish" and "tell" are meant as a single achievement.

My second difficulty is the gap between Molyneux's flat "Not" and Locke's endorsement of it, which is qualified three times over — "at first sight," "with certainty," "whilst he only saw them" — none of which appear in the answer Locke says he accepts. Do those qualifiers weaken the claim? Specifically, does "with certainty" leave room for a man who is right better than chance but without confidence? If it does, then given Locke's own stated reason, that the man has not yet obtained the experience, some degree of success should be expected as soon as the experience is obtained, which on the set-up described might be within the first minute of sight; and "at first sight" may then be doing less work than it appears to, since the question as posed already stipulates "before he touched them." I want your view on whether the passage is answering a question about the first instant of vision, or a question about whether the tactile idea suffices by itself for recognition, and on how much turns on that difference.

Third, there is the word "must" in "must affect his sight so or so." Is it asserting a connection the man has yet to grasp, or loose for "will, as a matter of course"? I take Locke to hold elsewhere in the Essay that no necessary connection between an idea of touch and an idea of sight is perceivable a priori, so that the tie between them has to be learned by repeated experience and held as a habit; if I have that wrong, say so, and if I have it right, then the interesting claim becomes one about how quickly such a habit forms, and a flat negative about the very first moment is close to trivial. Tell me whether the passage can be read as committed to the stronger claim, and where in the wording the commitment would have to live.

Fourth, the clause about the protuberant angle gives the tactile item as unequal pressure on the hand and then says that the angle "shall appear to his eye as it does in the cube," which seems to me to help itself to the very visual appearance whose absence is supposed to be the point. Is that a slip, or is it doing something I am missing? Fifth and least: the controls "of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness" look imperfect to me, since a cube of roughly the same overall size as a sphere holds substantially more metal and so weighs more, leaving the man a route to the right answer that runs through touch rather than sight. Does that matter to what the passage asks, or does "by his sight" dispose of it?

What I want from you: open with a sentence or two giving your overall reading of what the passage is doing and where its argumentative weight lies; then take my difficulties in the order I have raised them, saying for each which reading the wording favours and why, and being explicit where you think the text simply does not determine the matter; finish by telling me which formulation of the question the passage is best read as asking, what an experiment conducted on its own terms would have to hold fixed in order to answer it, and which of these ambiguities you think cannot be resolved from the wording alone. Please do not point me to the secondary literature or quote other passages at me: I have no library access where I am writing this, and what I want is your reading of these sentences. Write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in a letter, and aim for under nine hundred words.
