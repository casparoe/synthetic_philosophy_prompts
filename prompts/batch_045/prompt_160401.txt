I've been chewing over Sor Juana — the seventeenth-century Mexican nun who wrote in defence of women's learning and who, near the end, was made to sell her books after being criticised by a bishop. I'm not asking for a summary of her views; I'm trying to work out what I actually think now, definately. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases. A twelve-year-old girl secretly teaches herself Latin from her brother's schoolbooks because her parents would say it isn't for girls: fine, and they shouldn't stop her. A grown woman who taught herself philosophy quietly tutors a few young women in her own home: fine. The same woman gives a public lecture on that philosophy to a mixed audience of adults at the local university: not fine. She writes a theology book and publishes it anonymously, so nobody knows a woman wrote it: fine, or at least nowhere near as bad. She publishes the very same book under her own name, and men read it: not fine. She runs a seperate little village school teaching reading and sums to a mixed class of children, boys included: fine, and this one feels clearly fine even though she's teaching boys. My tentative principle is that learning is always allowed, and teaching is allowed only when it doesn't set a woman up as an interpretive authority over adult men — so teaching women or children is fine, and publishing anonymously is fine because her name isn't attached to the authority. But the anonymous versus named book case bugs me: the only difference is a name on the cover, which seems like a mere social fact, not a moral one. Is there a principle that really gets all these verdicts, or do I have to drop one? Please state the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — a woman putting a recorded philosophy lecture online, where most listeners are men but she never meets them and uses only her first name — and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate.
