I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism is genuinely solved or merely domesticated. Her position, as I came to understand it, was this: once you allow that the state expressed by a normative sentence can itself become the object of a further, higher-order state, the validity of a two-premise argument with normative premises reduces to the impossibility of being in certain combinations of states at once; and negation is handled not by the absence of an attitude but by a genuine contrary to it — a specific positive stance such as tolerance or indifference, rather than mere failure to disapprove. I think she is right about every case either of us could think of at the time, and I have since noticed something that makes me doubt the appearance of completeness: every case we know how to handle is one in which the speaker has *some* first-order stance toward the act under discussion, so that the higher-order machinery has something to operate on, and the contrary-attitude reply to negation has a contrary that is actually available. What I want from you is a case where that comfort is gone, and I want it for a short paper whose thesis is that the surviving residue of the problem is not embedding as such, but negation taking scope *inside* an embedding, in a stretch of reasoning whose author has no stance toward the act at all. Call the second half of that unanchoredness, and note that my friend runs together two different versions of the view — the simple approval/disapproval-plus-higher-order-state account she was defending, and the norm-acceptance-as-planning version — and I want to know whether my case damages both or only one.

So build me this: a single realistic vignette of no more than five sentences, set in ordinary adult life (a workplace, a clinic, a family logistics problem — nothing invented, no thought-experimental machinery, no philosophers or students as characters), in which a piece of deductive reasoning is natural and unforced and has all of the following features. First, a normative sentence appears twice: once as a premise, and once unasserted, inside the antecedent of a conditional, with the negation *inside* the antecedent rather than outside the conditional — something of the shape "if it's not the case that such-and-such is wrong, then so-and-so is permitted" — and the inference should have the usual ponens shape (or tollens, if that is the only way you can make it honest). Second, the argument is valid, and would still be valid if you swapped the normative vocabulary for plain descriptive vocabulary throughout: I will run that substitution myself, so do not build the validity on anything peculiar to the normative words. Third, the conclusion is about a different act or a different agent than the act the negated sentence is about, so the argument cannot be reconstructed as mere coherence among attitudes directed at one thing. Fourth, and this is the part I care about, the speaker is unanchored with respect to that act: they have no first-order stance of the relevant kind toward it — not approval, not disapproval, not tolerance or indifference, not suspension of judgement pending some fact, and not a higher-order stance toward any of those — and yet they are not insincere, confused, or speaking loosely; they are simply working through what follows from a written rule in a professional role. If you think real life never gives you full unanchoredness, give me the closest case anyway and state precisely which anchor survives; do not quietly smuggle one in and call the case unanchored.

After the vignette, do the work and commit to something. Walk through each step of the inference twice, once for each of the two versions of the view I named, naming the state that each version has to ascribe to the speaker for that sentence, and say whether that ascription gets the right result or is merely strained. Then tell me which single feature of the vignette is carrying the weight, what happens to the argument if that feature is removed (does it collapse back into the textbook case my friend already handles?), and how she would attack the case at its weakest point — including the obvious line that unanchoredness is really just insincerity in a professional disguise. Finally, give me a definite verdict on the question I actually care about: is negation-under-an-embedding-plus-unanchoredness a distinct residue of the Frege–Geach problem, or have I only put the old complaint in a costume?
