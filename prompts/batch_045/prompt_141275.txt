I'm in the final year of a philosophy degree, currently taking a seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I've ended up defending a position on the question of which varieties of free will are worth wanting that I'd like stress-tested before I commit it to a term paper. I'd be grateful for your help in mapping out what would count against it.

The position, in four parts. First: the term 'free will', as it actually functions in the debate, does not pick out a single property with a discoverable essence. It is a plural and partly conventional designation whose job is practical — to mark the line between agents we may properly treat as answerable and agents we must excuse, manage, or therapeutically handle. On this reading, the interminable dispute about whether free will is compatible with determinism is not a dispute about one property but a dispute about how to weight the elements of a cluster.

Second, the cluster. The elements I want are: local reasons-responsiveness at the time of action; diachronic self-governance, by which I mean the capacity to form, revise and abide by long-term plans and to reshape one's own motivational structure over time; ownership, in the sense that the motivational structure issuing in action is the agent's own rather than covertly installed in them; and membership in a social practice of answerability, being someone to whom demands and explanations are addressed. These are, I claim, at least partly dissociable from one another, and that dissociability is precisely what generates the apparently endless supply of hard cases in the literature.

Third: it follows from the first two parts that neither the truth of determinism as such, nor any finding in the neuroscience of action preparation, could settle the matter by itself. What is left over once the empirical noise clears is a disagreement about weighting, plus a normative disagreement about what exactly we would lose if the practice of holding responsible were to wither.

Fourth, and this is the part I contributed rather than inherited: a test for cluster membership. To decide whether some candidate capacity belongs among the varieties worth wanting, imagine an agent who has all the other capacities but whose decisions are intercepted and infallibly repaired by a hidden mechanism, so that what happens is always what the agent herself would have arrived at under ideal reflection, and she never notices. Does such an agent lose anything we care about, and would she be wronged by the arrangement? If yes, the capacity being tested is in the cluster. I further claim that people's answers to this diverge in stable ways, and that the divergence is not mere conceptual confusion: it reflects the fact that what we want here is to some extent first-personal and optional. But I am least confident about the coda I want to attach — that the practice of holding responsible could not survive widespread belief in that optionality, which seems to imply that some core element is not optional after all, and I am not sure how that sits with the pluralism.

What I would like from you is a map of what would count against this package, and I have some specific desiderata.

Please rank the defeaters in tiers, from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and say why each lands where it does rather than simply listing objections. I am particularly interested in the distinction between what a critic of my view would say and what a defender of it should concede, given its own commitments. Note that I do not want the consequence argument or the manipulation argument merely rehearsed; if you invoke them, tell me what additional work they do against this specific package, and in particular against the ownership element, which I suspect is my weakest link. I would also like at least one defeater of your own construction that I have not gestured at above, including any that exploits the tension between the pluralism and the non-optional core I gesture at in part four.

Two further things. First: assess how much the view actually risks. It may be that a position which is partly normative and partly a claim about the semantics of a term has been insulated against counterexample by its own vagueness, so that anything I find awkward can be absorbed by reweighting the cluster. If you think that is what has happened, say so, and then tell me whether it is a genuine defect for a view of this kind or whether demanding testability here would be a category mistake. Second: state what this view predicts that its rivals do not — about which kinds of case would move ordinary judgements about responsibility, about what would happen to our responsibility practices if people came to believe the cluster story, and about what we should expect if the elements really do dissociate.

For what it is worth, the three things I expect would move me most are: a demonstration that the four elements are really species of one thing, such as a single kind of counterfactual sensitivity, which would make the pluralism idle; a case in which my displacement test delivers an answer that is clearly the wrong one; and evidence that ordinary thought about free will is far more determinate than my account can recover. But tell me if you think I have misidentified what ought to move me.

Please write in British English, use prose with a few clear headings rather than bullet-point fragments throughout, and aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words. I am not asking for references, citations or a reading list — I have the seminar handout and the library is closed this week — so please argue from the structure of the position rather than from what others have said about it.
