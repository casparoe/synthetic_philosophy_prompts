I'm a second-year PhD student in political economy and I need to get a two-page dissertation fellowship proposal together by the end of the month, and I want the proposal to be built around an idea I can't quite get into shape on my own. Here's where I'm at. The literature on why some countries are rich is usually presented as a three-way horse race: institutions (the colonial-origins story, settler mortality as a source of exogenous variation in institutional quality, the reversal-of-fortune pattern where places that were rich in 1500 are poor now), geography (disease ecology, latitude, distance from the coast, continental orientation and which crops and animals were domesticable), and culture (trust, work norms, religion, the persistence of things brought by settlers). What bugs me is that everyone argues about which of these has more explanatory power in a cross-country regression, when as far as I can tell they aren't independently assignable treatments at all — they're bundled together in the actual sample, and each of the designs people reach for identifies a different thing. The instrument identifies the effect of institutions given one particular colonial path and not for countries that were never colonies; the twin-country comparisons (the Korea split, the Germany split, ethnic groups cut in half by a colonial border living under different national institutions) have something like n = 1 and differ along many axes at once; the geography variables barely move over time and so can't explain a takeoff even if they explain a level. My rough suspicion is that the effective number of independent observations in the cross-national data is tiny — call it eight or ten rather than 150 — once you account for colonial-era common shocks and the fact that everything diffuses to its neighbors, and that a lot of the confidence in the literature comes from a sample size that isn't really there.

So the thing I want the proposal to actually do is change the question rather than add another identification strategy. Instead of asking which cause wins, I want to ask: for each pair of hypotheses, what is the minimal observation, natural experiment, or hypothetical intervention that would tell them apart, and does the historical record contain anything that comes close? Two moves I think matter and can't fully articulate. First, portability: institutions are in principle transferable, geography isn't, and culture is somewhere in between (it travels with people, via migration and diaspora), so two hypotheses that fit all existing data equally well can still disagree sharply about what would happen if you moved the same institutions to a different place, or moved a population to a different place, or moved both. I want that turned into something like a portability test, not as a metaphor but as an explicit statement of what each hypothesis predicts about a counterfactual relocation. Second, I think some of the talk-past-each-other is that different people are asking different questions without noticing: some want to know what explains the *level* of income, some what explains the *variance over time* or the timing of takeoffs, and a variable that is basically frozen can answer one and not the other. My advisor says this is a methodology project dressed up as a substantive one, which may be true, and that's part of what the proposal has to reckon with.

What I need from you is the proposal itself, in the shape a committee would expect: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, why the existing positions leave it open (I mean the positions, not the papers — I'll fill in all citations myself, so please don't spend effort on references or checking whether I've described specific papers correctly, take the designs roughly as I've sketched them), the method including what I'd actually be doing month by month across the fellowship year, what a deliverable would look like for someone making a real decision rather than for a seminar, and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing — including the unglamorous possibility that the answer is no design separates these hypotheses and no counterfactual question separates them either, in which case I need to say what's left worth writing. Also give me half a page I can use to reply to the committee member who will definately say this is just philosophers' hand-wringing about causal inference and that the right response is to stop worrying and go find a new natural experiment, and tell me if you think the whole thing is too big for a year and what the natural cut-down version is, because I suspect it is and I'd rather hear it now than recieve the rejection.
