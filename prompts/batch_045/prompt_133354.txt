I should say first how I came to this, because I suspect it shapes what I need. I spent a working life in experimental physical chemistry and have only in the last few years started reading philosophy properly; I now convene a small self-directed reading group — nine of us, mostly retired, a couple of former schoolteachers and a retired judge among them, so the numeracy in the room is very uneven — and we meet for ninety minutes once a month in a back room of the public library. We have a whiteboard, a pack of index cards, and no projector, and we will not pass real money around, though I can put a few euros into a coffee kitty or hand out chocolate. We have been circling, without quite getting straight, the family of arguments in which an agent who violates some coherence condition can be led through a sequence of trades each of which she prefers at the time and ends up strictly poorer. I keep finding three different things run together under that heading. One is the cycle case: preferences go around in a ring, someone offers to swap the current item for the next one for a small fee, and the agent can be walked round the ring indefinitely. A second is the case where the agent is indifferent between neighbouring items in a chain but prefers the ends of the chain, so that small payments for each step along it deliver her back roughly where she began, minus the fees. A third is the temporal case, where the sequence is not a set of simultaneous options but a series of decision points over time, and the agent's later self does not want what her earlier self arranged for. I would like an activity for one session that separates these three and makes the group feel what is distinctive about each, without my naming any of them beforehand, and certainly without anyone hearing the standard labels for them until the final fifteen minutes.

The reason I want this staged rather than explained is a point I have been worrying at and cannot settle, and I would like the activity to put the group in a position to worry at it too. It seems to me that the cycle argument and the temporal argument are arguments of quite different shape. In the cycle case the extraction is limited by how many goods are on the table, by how many rounds the operator can plausibly run, and by the fact that every swap costs a strictly positive minimum to arrange: if the smallest fee anyone will accept is a cent and the goods are three, there is a hard ceiling on what can be taken, and the agent's total loss is, in the worst case, small. By contrast the kind of argument where a book is made against someone who violates the probability axioms can be scaled arbitrarily — the same incoherence, multiplied stakes, unbounded loss. So I am not sure the cycle argument establishes irrationality at all, as opposed to establishing that there is a possible world in which a small bad thing happens to you, and I would like the group to run into that ceiling themselves, ideally by letting some participants act as operators with a fixed number of rounds and a fixed handling fee and then asking how much they actually managed to extract and what would have to change for them to extract more. The temporal case looks different again, and this is the part I care most about: there, the agent's loss depends on which procedure she is running. Someone who looks ahead and reasons backwards through her own future choices will quietly steer to a worse plan than someone who simply decides each time as it comes, and both can do worse than someone who forms a plan in advance and executes it even at a node where she would now rather not. So I want a second phase in which the same temporal structure is run by three participants assigned different instructions — decide on the spot without looking ahead; look ahead and reason backwards; choose a whole course of action now and stick to it even when you would prefer to deviate — and in which the group discovers that the ranking of the three procedures is not fixed but depends on the structure of the case, so that no one of them is simply the rational one. If there is a way to make the group notice that the third procedure is really the option of walking away from the game altogether, and that a game with a fixed rule set quietly stacks the deck against it, so much the better.

What I need from you beyond the design: a minute-by-minute shape for the ninety minutes including how the room is set up and what is on the cards; a scoring or payoff scheme in points or chocolate that does not require anyone to be comfortable with arithmetic; a debrief that maps specific moments we should have watched for onto the distinctions above, and that says plainly, for each of the three arguments, which participants are likely to see which point and which they will miss. Then an honest section on what will go wrong. I can already predict some of it — someone will spot the pattern early and refuse to trade out of stubbornness, someone else will treat it as a game to win against me, someone will announce that they do not care about the stakes and thereby destroy the very preference the exercise depends on, and someone will change their mind mid-sequence and accuse the operator of cheating, which is interesting if handled well and fatal if not. Tell me how to handle each. Finally, be frank about what an afternoon in a library room cannot reproduce: I take it that genuine diachronic agency — a real change of mind over days, real temptation, the actual experience of failing to do what one had planned — is not something a ninety-minute game can supply, and I would rather have that stated explicitly for the group than have us pretend otherwise. Please write it so that someone with no background in formal decision theory could run it from your description alone.
