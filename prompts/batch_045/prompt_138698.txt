I need a favour for a seminar I'm running next week. We're on the doing/allowing distinction (and, messier, killing vs. letting die), and I've got a problem with the cases in the reader: they all do too many jobs at once. In the standard pairs, the person who does harm is also the person who moves their body, and also the person who introduces a threat the victim wasn't already facing, and also the person whose victim would have been fine anyway. So you can't tell which of those features is carrying the intuition. I want a designed pair — not another illustration — and I want the design shown to work.

The spec: two cases, call them Hold and Cold. In each, someone dies as a result of what an agent does or doesn't do, and the only difference between them is this: in Hold, the agent's body was already, in the moments before the death, sustaining something — gripping a rope, keeping a valve shut, bracing a weight off someone's chest, whatever — and the death follows from their ceasing to sustain it; in Cold, the agent's body was at rest and the death follows from their not beginning to sustain it. Everything else has to be nailed down as identical: same victim, same death, same causal route from the agent's behaviour to the death, same amount of time to act, same effort and skill required either way, same certainty about the outcome, same motives and intentions (both agents want this person dead for the same boring reason), same prior relationship to whatever threatens the victim, same institutional role, same alternatives available.

What I want back:

1. The pair itself. Clinical, workplace or domestic — ordinary life please, no trolleys and no mad surgeons. One paragraph each.

2. A check that the pair actually meets the spec. Go down the list of held-fixed features and say, for each, how the case secures it — and flag honestly any where you had to fudge it, or where two of the features turn out not to be jointly satisfiable. If you conclude the pair can't be built, say that instead and tell me exactly which two features collide and why; for what I'm doing, a clean impossibility result would be the more useful answer, and I'd rather have it than a pair that smuggles in a confound.

3. Run the four accounts I give my students over both cases and tell me what each of them says: (a) the causal one — doing is causing the harm, allowing is failing to prevent it; (b) the threat one — doing is introducing a threat the victim wouldn't otherwise have faced, allowing is failing to remove a threat that's already there; (c) the bodily-movement one — doing requires a positive act, a willed movement, and omissions aren't acts at all; (d) the normative one — the distinction isn't in the physics, it's about whether the agent failed to discharge a duty they already had. Which of them splits Hold from Cold, and which gives both the same verdict? Don't just tell me intuitions vary — commit.

4. Which single feature of the setup is doing the work, and what happens if I delete it. Does the pair collapse back into an ordinary Smith-and-Jones-style contrast, or does something survive?

5. The bit I actually care about most: take whichever account comes out looking worst here and give me the two or three lines its defender would say to get out of it — a real objection to the case, not a restatement of their theory. If their best move is to say the pair trades on an unstable or gerrymandered description of what the agent is doing, tell me whether that move is genuinely available to them or whether it costs them more elsewhere.

Two more things. First, my own hobby-horse: I've been toying with a *description-swap* test, where you hold the physical sequence fixed and vary only what we'd say the agent was up to — for instance by varying whether the sustaining was something they took on voluntarily thirty seconds earlier or were manoeuvred into, or whether the "hold" is done by the agent's muscles or by a bracket they're steadying that's about to slip anyway. Does the doing/allowing distinction survive that test, or is it hostage to how we describe the behaviour? If you think it's a bad test, say so and tell me why. Second, tell me whether doing/allowing and killing/letting-die line up in your pair or pull apart — half my students assume they're one distinction with two names, and if your pair separates them I'd like to know which case does the separating.

For context, since it should fix what counts as cheating: the point is to show my students that the doctrine of doing and allowing — the claim that, other things equal, doing harm is worse than merely allowing it — has nothing left to bite on once the confounds are stripped away, or else to show them exactly what it does bite on. So a case that just stipulates "this one is a doing and that one is an allowing" is useless to me; the verdicts have to fall out of the accounts, not be written in. And write it so a smart undergraduate could follow it without me talking over it.
