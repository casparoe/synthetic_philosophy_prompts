Model letters as patterns of filled cells on a coarse grid — a 5-wide, 7-tall box in which each cell is either inked or empty, so that a whole font is a fixed grid plus an assignment of ink. Two accounts of what makes such a glyph an 'a' suggest themselves.

According to the part-role account, a glyph is an 'a' in virtue of realizing the right roles — a closed bowl, a stem, an aperture where the bowl opens toward the stem — while the geometry filling those roles varies freely: the bowl may be fat or thin, the stem may slant or shorten, and it is still an 'a'. According to the frame-invariant account, what matters is coarser: how many enclosed counters the inked region has, and which edges of the grid the ink touches (top, bottom, sides).

Give a concrete example that drives a wedge between these two accounts. Describe, as an explicit grid of inked and empty cells, a single letterform about which they genuinely disagree — one counting it as an 'a' and the other refusing to — and then say in two or three sentences why the disagreement is a real conceptual difficulty for the project of saying what the essence of a letterform is, rather than a quirk of one made-up example. The difficulty should be visible in the grid itself: do not appeal to how actual readers would identify the glyph, to the conventions of any real typeface, or to any historical script.

End with a line of the form \answer{...} naming, in four to six words, the conceptual issue your example isolates.
