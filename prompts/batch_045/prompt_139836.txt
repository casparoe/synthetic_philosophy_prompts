Two of us have spent two years arguing about what Russell's paradox actually establishes, and we have agreed to stop arguing past each other. Please draft the joint statement we would produce at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between our two camps.

Camp A, the naive (revisionary) theorist. Unrestricted comprehension is constitutive of the notion of set: every predicate has an extension, including the predicate "x is not a member of itself." The Russell derivation is valid. What it refutes is not the existence of the Russell collection but the classicality of the underlying logic. Contradictions are to be contained, not eliminated: some instances of excluded middle, disjunctive syllogism, or contraction fail. The costs are paid in logic, where they are local, surveyable, and uniform, rather than in mathematical content, where they would be open-ended.

Camp B, the restrictivist. Classical logic is not up for renegotiation. The Russell collection does not exist; the paradox is a reductio of naive comprehension, and the proper answer to it is a positive account of what sets are — either the iterative conception (sets arranged in cumulative stages, each stage formed only from what earlier stages have made available) or limitation of size (a collection is a set just in case it is not equinumerous with the universe; the oversized ones are proper classes). Costs are paid in ontology, where we can say exactly what has been given up.

Ground rules, which the document should visibly observe.

Neither camp may simply help itself to its preferred criterion of adequacy — consistency, serviceability for ordinary mathematics, fidelity to the intuitive notion of set, expressive strength. Each must say why its criterion is the right one, and each must name at least one result or argument it would count as evidence against its own side.

Camp B may speak with one voice, but where the iterative conception and limitation of size diverge — over whether there could be a universal set, over whether Replacement and Choice are intrinsic to the conception or adopted for extrinsic reasons — mark the divergence rather than paper over it.

Camp A must say exactly which inference it gives up, and what that costs outside set theory.

No citations, no named authors, no quotations, no history of the debate: write as though the two of us are the only parties in the room and the arguments must stand on their own. Assume we both know the standard derivations and the other classical size paradoxes; do not re-derive them except where a step is load-bearing. Define any symbol you introduce, and use plain English wherever symbols are not needed.

Four cruxes we want treated; you may add at most two more, but only if they are genuine cruxes and not restatements of these.

One. Triviality, not merely contradiction. For arbitrary P, take C_P = {x : (x ∈ x) → P}. Unrestricted comprehension plus the usual classical rules yields P outright — triviality, not a merely local inconsistency. So Camp A must surrender more than non-contradiction: contraction, or detachment in full generality, or the free intersubstitution of provable equivalents in the relevant contexts. State precisely which, and then ask whether what remains is still recognisably a conditional. Camp B's formulation of this challenge must be one Camp A accepts as the real challenge.

Two. A hybrid case, which neither of us has seen discussed and which we propose as a joint test. Extend the language with a naive truth predicate T governed by the full biconditional scheme T(⟨φ⟩) ↔ φ for every sentence φ, including sentences that themselves contain T. Now consider the collection H = {x : ¬T(⟨x ∈ x⟩)}, where ⟨x ∈ x⟩ is read as the result of substituting a name of x into the formula "y ∈ y." Is this a genuinely new difficulty for Camp A, or only Russell's paradox in another costume? If Camp A must restrict the truth scheme in order to contain it, has it bought containment of the set-theoretic contradiction at the price of the concept of truth? And for Camp B: does the hybrid force anything beyond the set-theoretic restriction — a separate hierarchy for truth — and if so, may the two hierarchies be permitted to interact, or must interaction be banned in a way that looks ad hoc? Record each camp's prediction and what each would count as disconfirmation.

Three. The stable-core recovery proposal, our second joint test. Call a collection stable when, for every x, the theory proves that it is not the case both that x belongs to it and that x does not belong to it — its membership is never glutty. Camp A proposes to recover classical mathematics by restricting attention to the stable collections with the inherited membership relation, and claims an interpretation of ZF, or of ZF without Replacement, or of some weaker classical theory — Camp A should say which, and why that is enough. Both camps: is this the right test of whether the naive theory can pay for itself? What does each predict about whether the recovery survives Replacement and Choice, about whether the naive theory can prove the recovered universe nontrivial, and about whether it can establish its own nontriviality without already assuming consistency?

Four. Escalation by expressive strength. The naive theory can formulate the collection of all collections that are not members of themselves, with the quantifier ranging over everything the theory admits — sets, classes, and whatever lies beyond. Camp B cannot so much as express it. Is the capacity to generate a fresh contradiction at every level a mark of expressive power or a mark of defect? What finding would settle the question, and what does each camp expect that finding to be?

Return the statement in the following five sections, in this order.

1. Agreed claims — eight to twelve numbered claims, one or two sentences each, that both camps can sign. They should be substantive rather than procedural: what the paradox shows, what an adequate reply to it must accomplish, what makes a restriction ad hoc, and so on. At least three of them should be mildly surprising coming from one camp or the other.

2. Where we disagree — a table with four columns: the disputed claim; Camp A's formulation, in wording Camp A would accept; Camp B's formulation, likewise; and a verdict per row of substantive, partly verbal, or verbal, with one sentence of justification. Six to eight rows.

3. Cruxes and joint tests — one block per crux (the four above plus any you add, six at most), each containing the crux in neutral wording, the argument or formal result both camps accept as bearing on it, Camp A's prediction, Camp B's prediction, and what each camp would count as disconfirmation of its own prediction. Roughly 120 to 180 words per crux.

4. Conceded costs — two paragraphs of about 120 words each, one in each camp's own first-person voice: what this camp gives up, why it judges that the cheaper loss, and the one point at which it feels its own case least.

5. What would move us — about 150 words on the findings, if any, that both camps agree would end or substantially reorient the dispute; the single finding each camp most expects never to see; and, if any part of the disagreement turns out to be about words, an explicit statement of which part and why.

Aim for 1,100 to 1,500 words in total, and add no preamble or closing commentary of your own.
