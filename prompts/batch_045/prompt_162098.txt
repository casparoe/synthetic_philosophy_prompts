I'm working on a piece about the tech-forecasting crowd suddenly talking like historians — half the people writing about AI timelines now lean on the longue durée — and I want to know wether I can trust the book I'd be quoting.

One of those big "why history repeats" bestsellers sums up Braudel roughly like this:

"Braudel's argument was that geography and climate are what really drive history. Coastlines, prevailing winds, disease regimes, the slow limits a society lives inside of — that's the longue durée, the almost motionless layer, and it decides what's possible for centuries. Set against it, events and individuals are only surface froth, foam on a wave: whether Philip II signed a paper one afternoon barely matters, because the Mediterranean would have looked much the same either way. Braudel and the Annales people therefore abandoned narrative history and biography in favour of statistics and long data series, and the upshot is that human agency is basically an illusion. The practical payoff is what the author calls Braudel's window: whatever hasn't changed in two hundred years will still be doing the work in fifty, so once you've identified the structure you can say where a region or an industry is headed decades out."

I'm inclined to believe most of it, but "agency is an illusion" sounds like more than a structural historian would sign up for, and I can't tell whether the forecasting bit at the end follows from anything Braudel actually claimed or is the author freelancing on his name.

Go through it and tell me what a historian working in that tradition would accept, what's a fair simplification, and what Braudel specifically wouldn't have said — and then whether "Braudel's window" survives once the summary is cleaned up. Also flag anything important the summary leaves out; I have a vague sense there's a middle layer he cared about that this skips.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of my question.
