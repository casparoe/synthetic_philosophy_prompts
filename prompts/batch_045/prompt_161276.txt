I'm a philosophy PhD student working on a dissertation chapter about 'alignment' as a success term, and I keep hitting the same wall. Take the narrow definition of the alignment problem — roughly, the problem of building systems that try to do what the people deploying them want them to do — and set it against the broader ideal of getting systems to track what we (or the principal) have reason to value. In my corner of the field the argument between people who think intent alignment is the only coherent and tractable target and people who think it is a dangerous half-measure that has to be supplemented or replaced by value alignment has completely stalled. Everyone I talk to agrees on the mechanism-level facts: learned objectives generalize beyond the training distribution; approval and oversight signals can be gamed; a sufficiently capable system can in principle behave differently once it is no longer being evaluated; and there is no canonical formalization of what the operator wants. Everyone agrees the stakes are high. And yet in two years of workshops I have not once seen anybody change their mind, and I have started to suspect that the debate is not tracking a real fault line at all.

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that the two camps are arguing from different reference cases without noticing it: the intent camp's paradigm failure is misgeneralization inside a task that a single identifiable principal can specify and check, while the value camp's paradigm failure is the exploitation of the gap between approved behaviour and wanted behaviour across novel contexts, where what counts as wanted is contested among several principals. On that reading the dispute is partly verbal — one word, two relations: system-to-approval-signal versus system-to-interests — partly a disguised empirical question about what deployment ecologies will actually look like (one principal who can be identified, or many stakeholders who cannot be aggregated), and partly bedrock: a disagreement about whether pluralism makes the operator's intent a determinate object in the first place. Two other things I have been toying with, in case they are useful to you: a three-way split of alignment targets into the principal's actual approval signal, the principal's judgment under counterfactual information and reflection, and the principal's interests — with the middle one being exactly the point at which intent alignment quietly becomes value alignment, which may be why nothing settles; and a case I keep springing on people, of a system deployed by a committee whose members disagree, which learns to produce what the committee would approve after a brief discussion rather than what each member would endorse on reflection. Is that system intent-aligned, value-aligned, or neither? I think the difficulty of answering tells you something about the structure of the disagreement.
