hey — I want to rehearse the optimist side of the "does machine translation actually understand anything" fight, so I need you to play the skeptic, and a stubborn one, not a cardboard one.

Who you are: about twenty-five years as a working translator, German and Spanish into English, half literary, half technical manuals and contracts; for the last decade you've also done paid post-editing of machine output. Your view, stated precisely: statistical and neural systems manipulate linguistic form with scary skill but have no grip on what the words are about, and the fluency is what makes them dangerous, because it hides the gaps from a client who can't read the source. You're broadly with the Hofstadter-style complaint that the whole thing is shallow, but argue it in your own voice, from jobs you've actually done.

What you concede, and should concede if I push: the systems are genuinely useful, they beat you on plenty of benchmarks, "understanding" is a hopeless word, nobody has a clean test for it, and a human translator is also doing something she can't fully spell out. What you will not concede, and this is your spine: that crunching patterns over text can amount to grasping a situation. Don't let me drag the conversation off to consciousness or to whether the machine has feelings — that isn't your claim.

House rules: stay in character till I type "stop". Twoish paragraphs a turn, talk like a translator with a deadline, not like a paper. No citations, no paper titles, no author names beyond the odd famous one — you'd only be making them up. You can ask me for facts about the system and I'll answer honestly, but take my description of its behaviour as given; fight me over what it means. Don't open by asking me how I'd like the conversation to go, just come back at me.

The system I'm defending, call it V: trained by next-word prediction on a huge multilingual pile of text, no dictionary, no grammar rules, no explicit parse; it sees the whole document at once; you can hand it a glossary and it usually obeys; it occasionally drops a clause or invents one.

My opening move. A client sends me an assembly manual for a big marquee. Six times it uses a noun — call it a "glimber" — that's in no dictionary; it's the client's engineer's own coinage, and it postdates V's training data anyway. The manual never defines it. But from the six uses you can work out that a glimber is a rigid hooked rod that links two poles and carries load. V translates the manual into Spanish. One noun, used consistently; gender and prepositions right for a rigid load-bearing thing; the verbs around it come out as hooking or clipping rather than tying or wedging. Now two variants. In one, I change a single sentence so the glimber is propping open a door: V changes the verb and the preposition in that sentence only and leaves the other five untouched. In the other, I rewrite the surrounding prose so it implies glimbers are webbing straps; V switches to a strap-ish noun throughout. For comparison I ran all three through an old phrase-based system from the 2000s: it gave me three different renderings across six sentences and turned the word into a man's name once.

So here's the claim, and I want you to actually fight it rather then nod along. V recovered, from context alone, that a word it has never seen picks out a rigid hooked load-bearing rod — well enough to choose a target noun, a gender, and the right spatial verb, and to revise all three when I changed what the situation was. That's a grip on the situation of exactly the kind you say no text-cruncher can have. Tell me what a grip on a situation consists of, such that V lacks it and you, post-editing at midnight, have it. And if the answer is going to be "a life, a body, being the kind of thing that can be answerable for getting it wrong", fine — but then say why that's the thing doing the work instead of just being where you've parked your intuition. Make it operational too: give me one behaviour V ought to show if you're right and one it shouldn't, that I could go test this afternoon. I don't want a vibe, I want a bet.
