Hey — I'm a PhD student working on a chapter about the duty to remember vs. the case for forgetting (Margalit's ethics of memory, Rieff's bit about culture as a system of renunciations, that whole literature), and I want to use Nietzsche as a foil. So: what would Nietzsche — the 1874 essay on history and life, active forgetting as a condition of being able to act at all — have said about large language models, i.e. systems that in a fairly literal sense cannot forget anything they were trained on?

I've got three cases and I'm guessing he'd treat them pretty diffrently, but I want your read:

1. The European-style right to be forgotten: someone gets old, embarrassing or damaging links about them delisted from search engines, while the underlying page still exists.
2. Mandatory permanent archives: the state requires atrocity testimony, trial recordings and the like to be preserved digitally forever, with no expiry.
3. Three technical ways people now try to make a model forget, which I want you to treat as genuinely different: (a) excluding material from the training corpus up front; (b) machine unlearning — post-hoc surgery on a trained model's weights to remove the influence of specific data, approximately but never exactly; (c) leaving the model alone and only filtering or refusing at output time, so it still knows but cannot say. Please rank 3a–3c by how much he'd approve. I suspect 3c is the one he'd find most interesting — it looks more like repression than forgetting — but tell me if I'm over-reading.

Also briefly: any room at all, for him, for a duty to remember, or is never forget just the antiquarian and monumental history he attacks?
