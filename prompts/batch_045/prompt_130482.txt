I teach a first-year PPE course and I've got one 75-minute session, about 22 students, most of whom have done no formal maths since school and none of whom have seen social choice theory. I want them to feel why there's no aggregation rule that meets a short list of conditions that each sound individually obvious — and I dont want to just tell them, they should spend most of the hour trying to build a rule that works and failing.

Here's the rough shape I have in mind, fix it or replace it: they're drafting a "constitution" for spending a real £200 class fund across three options (a guest speaker, a party, a donation — whatever). In teams of 4-5 they write down a rule that takes each person's ranking of the three options and outputs a group ranking, subject to four plain-English constraints on a handout: the group ranking has to be a genuine ordering (no cycles), it has to respect unanimity, the group's A-versus-B verdict can't depend on where anyone ranks C, and no single person gets their way regardless of what everyone else says. Then teams swap and go adversarial: each team hunts for a preference profile that makes another team's rule break one of the conditions. Scoreboard = conditions you survive plus profiles you break.

What I actually need from you: concrete rules and materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, the scoring, and two or three reserve profiles I can plant if the room doesn't generate them (the spoiler case and the cycle case at minimum). Then a debrief that maps moments in the room back onto each condition — and in particular onto the bit this kind of activity usually fails to convey: every rule in the room breaking on some profile is not the same as the claim that no rule could work. How do I make that universal quantifier land in the last ten minutes without turning it into a lecture with a proof on the board?

Also be honest about what will predictably go wrong: someone proposes a dictator as a joke, someone proposes ranking by intensity of preference rather than ordinal rankings, someone's rule survives every profile anyone thinks of in fifty minutes and the whole thing fizzles. And there's an ending problem I can't solve — they still genuinely have to decide how to spend the money, so what do I do with that?
