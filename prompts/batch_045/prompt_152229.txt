my study group has been going round in circles on this for about six months and I'm the one who has to write something up, so I'd rather produce a document than pick a winner. The reason it's not academic for me: my brother-in-law had a stroke last year and came out of it describing pain in a way I still can't get my head around. He'll say the pain is there, point at exactly where, track it getting stronger or weaker, and when the pain clinic has him fill in their two separate 0-10 lines - one for how strong the sensation is and one for how much it bothers him - he'll put something like 8 and 0. He doesn't try to get away from it, doesn't ask for anything, and gets irritated when the nurses push morphine at him. But he's not globally indifferent: he still hates being tickled, still can't stand one particular itchy hospital blanket, and he does complain that the pain stops him concentrating on his crosswords, which he says annoys him in a completely different way. One friend says this settles it: the badness of pain was never in the sensation, it's an attitude or a desire-to-stop bolted onto the sensation, and the stroke just cut the wire between them. The other friend says that's changing the subject - either he isn't really in pain and is just reporting something he recognises as pain, or pain is by its nature a bad experience and you can't have the real thing with the badness surgically removed, so whatever he's got is a loss of part of pain plus a lot of confusion. Both are extremely smug and neither will budge.

So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them. Call the sides, in wording each would accept for themselves, something like 'the badness is in the sensation itself' and 'the badness is just disliking plus wanting it to stop'. Please fix the purpose of the theory as clinical: what should a team actually do about reported pain the patient says doesn't bother him - treat it, count it as a harm at all when weighing up interventions, take the "it doesn't matter" report at face value, and what to do when the only badness left is instrumental (it wrecks his concentration). Format it as a numbered section of claims both of them could sign - including about what the case itself does and doesn't show, and about what the neurology would have to establish for it to be evidence about pain at all - followed by a table of the disagreements, each row phrased so each side would sign its own wording.

Then the cruxes, which is the part I actually want. The ones I suspect matter: whether his case really is full-blown intensity with the nastiness gone, or instead a loss of motivation with the nastiness intact; whether 'pain' in their argument means the whole nasty experience or just the sensory component, i.e. how much of their fight is verbal; and how to handle a mirror-image case I made up to shut them both up - a drug that wipes out the burning completely but leaves an overwhelming urge to withdraw the limb and a wave of loathing aimed at the stimulus, where the patient says 'that isn't pain, but I want it to stop more than anything I've ever felt'. I'd also like them to deal with the opposite direction: people who want their pain, like someone with phantom limb pain who doesn't want it treated because it's the last bit of the limb he has. For each crux give the case or piece of evidence both would agree is relevant to it, and write down in advance what each side predicts about it, plus what each would count as evidence against their own view. If you conclude that some chunk of the dispute dissolves rather than gets settled, say which chunk and why - and flag if you think any of the requirements the two sides are implicitly placing on a theory here can't be satisfied together.
