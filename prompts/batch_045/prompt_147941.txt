A friend of mine who works on the philosophy of pain has given me her verdicts on a series of cases, always on the same question: is the experience itself bad for the subject at the time — bad for them in the way a toothache is, as opposed to merely signalling something bad, or being bad only in its consequences?

Ordinary post-operative pain: bad for the subject, and bad because of what it is like, not because of the tissue damage.

Pain asymbolia — a patient who localises a stimulus correctly, identifies it as pain, and reports its full sensory character, but is entirely untroubled by it: not bad for him at all. The lesion is of course bad for him; the pain is not.

The familiar dissociation produced by strong analgesia or absorption, where intensity reports are unchanged and the patient says the pain is as severe as ever but that it does not bother him: not bad for him either.

Bereavement: grief that the mourner would not give up and does not want taken away, though it feels awful while it lasts: still bad for the mourner — a cost she bears willingly, not a good in disguise. When I pressed her on how that is consistent with the dissociation case, she said a person can want the pang she is now feeling to end without wanting the grief gone, and that the reports of not minding one's pain in the dissociation cases are the opposite of that.

Two further cases are on the table and she has not ruled on them. I would like to guess where she lands.

(A) A climber deep into a long, deliberate ordeal: the pain is reported as intensely unpleasant, every bit as bad-feeling as the post-operative case, but he has no desire at all for it to stop, no impulse to escape it, would refuse an analgesic if one were offered, and afterwards says he would not change that hour by a second.

(B) A partial dissociation in the other direction: the pain sensation is hedonically flat — he denies that it feels bad in any way and compares it to a neutral but attention-grabbing bodily sensation — yet he has a steady, unemotional desire to be rid of it. He schedules the procedure, takes the medication, pays for it, and counts the result as an improvement in his life, all while insisting that nothing about the sensation is unpleasant or distressing.

Please do three things. First, set out the rules about what makes an experience bad for its subject that fit her four verdicts — I suspect a pure valence view and some desire- or aversion-based view both fit, but say what the live options really are and how each accommodates the bereavement verdict. Second, say what each candidate rule predicts about (A) and (B), give your best guess at her verdict in each with your reasons for preferring it, and say whether any single rule could make both of those verdicts jointly consistent. Third, describe one further case, different from these, that would separate the surviving rules more cleanly than (A) and (B) do, and explain why it does better.

Finally, indicate how confident you are in each part of what you have said, and single out the claim you are least sure of.
