Hey — I run a little scifi-and-philosophy film club (we meet in the back room of a pub, its very informal) and *Minority Report* is up next month. I want to do it properly rather than just rehasing the free-will-and-determinism thing everyone always does with this film. So: read it through the philosophy of punishment, retributivism vs. consequentialism.

The setup as I remember it: in 2054 a Washington "Precrime" unit arrests people for murders they haven't committed yet, on the basis of visions from three precogs; the people they grab are put into a coma and locked up indefinitely; the programme has basically eliminated premeditated murder in six years of running, but it can't see crimes of passion, only the planned ones. There's that early argument where Anderton says that stopping the murder doesn't change the fact that it was going to happen, and Witwer answers that it didn't happen — two lines that look to me like the whole retributivist case in miniature.

Two distinctions I've been using under made-up names — tell me if philosophers already have names for them or if I'm reinventing:
- **act-desert vs. will-desert**: grounding desert in the harm actually done versus grounding it in a settled criminal will, the thing an attempt still has even when nothing comes of it. Note the film's own split: the precogs only ever see the killings where will-desert has its best object, and are blind to the ones where the will isn't settled ahead of time. Is that a coincidence of the plot or is it doing philosophical work?
- **the prophecy problem**: when an authority tells you what you are going to do, the telling is itself part of the cause of your doing it. In the film it's literal — Crow is hired to pose as Sean's killer and goad Anderton into shooting him, so the state's prediction is a machine for manufacturing the will it then punishes. And even short of a frame-up, does an announced prediction leave enough *counterfactual slack* for there to be a fact of the matter about what the arrestee would have done, given that arresting him is what ensures he doesn't?

What I want:
- Which parts of Precrime does each theory object to, and does either theory's objection get *stronger* the more reliable the predictions are? My instinct is that the interesting retributivist complaint isn't "you might be wrong" but "even if you're right", but the film seems to spend nearly all its energy on the corruption plot and the purged minority reports, which is the shallow version. Is that fair?
- The comatose indefinite detention: no proportion, no reform, no possible deterrence (the crime never occurs), pure incapacitation. Which of the standard justifying aims can even be pointed at it, and does the coma as opposed to a cell change the answer?
- On the prophecy problem: is it an objection a retributivist should feel more sharply, or a consequentialist? Does Anderton's near-shooting of Crow, and then not shooting him, matter here — is the film claiming there's still a choice?
- Where does the reading break down? I don't want to turn it into a lecture with illustrations. The ending (Burgess cornered and turning the gun on himself, Precrime shut down, the precogs retired to an island, Lara pregnant) reads to me like an institutional fix — bad people, bad data — and not a verdict on pre-punishment as such. Do you read it that way, and is that a weakness in the film or just the genre?
- And what does the film add to the debate that the papers don't?
