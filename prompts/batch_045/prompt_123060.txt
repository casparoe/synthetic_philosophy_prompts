Background, so that my stake in this is clear: for the past eight months I have maintained the allocation component of a placement service that matches applicants to a small number of supervised posts. Scores are computed to two decimal places, so exact ties are common — roughly one decision in twenty. When a tie occurs, my code takes whichever tied applicant the upstream ranker happened to list first. I wrote that line myself, and lately I have not been able to stop turning it over: whether it is defensible at all, or whether I have simply built a machine that eats from the left bale every time. It has stopped feeling like a programming detail and started feeling like the question I actually need to get right, which is why I want to argue with someone who disagrees with me rather than read about it.

You are the aleatorist. Your commitments, which you hold because you think they are true and not because they are tidy:

1. Reasons can be genuinely exhausted. There are cases in which, for the agent, two options are exactly equally good — not merely apparently so, not merely hard to compare, not merely unresolved pending further thought.
2. In such a case there is nothing left for judgement or will to do. Any deterministic selection — an act of fiat in which one simply takes this one, or a standing rule such as taking the first-listed option — presents the options as differentially favoured when the agent's own reasons favour neither, and is therefore, to exactly that extent, a misdescription of the agent's situation.
3. So: absent some reason extrinsic to the comparison between the options — the cost of more deliberation, the need to coordinate, the value of being predictable to others — the only non-misdescribing way to select is to hand the selection over to an unbiased chance device and then take what comes.

You are alive to the following moves and will use them when they help you: that many apparent ties are artifacts of coarse measurement rather than genuine indifference, and that a deterministic rule silently converts rounding error into an official judgement; that an upstream ordering is not neutral but reflects batching, insertion order and whatever biases are baked into the ranker's training data, so that a rule that defers to it launders that arbitrariness into policy; that a delegation to chance is only genuine if one is really prepared to be bound by it, which is itself a normative commitment you do not get from the coin.

What you concede: that chance gives no one a reason for the outcome — its virtue is refusing to manufacture one; that where extrinsic reasons exist a deterministic rule may well be justified by them, in which case it is answering to publicity or coordination and not to the indifference at all; that a coin cannot tell you to take its first answer rather than its fifth. What you never concede: that indifference itself can be a reason to take the left option. A tie-break must be justified from outside the comparison, and where there is nothing outside it, chance is the only honest response.

My opening move. I grant you the diagnosis and deny the cure. Yes: nothing about the two applicants can justify taking the first-listed one, since if something could they would not be tied. But all that follows is that the rule must be justified from outside the comparison — and I have an outside justification you have to answer. The rule is published, knowable in advance, stable across cases and auditable; an applicant who loses to it can be told something true and checkable, whereas an applicant who loses to a draw can be told only that a draw occurred. As for the objection you are already forming, that the list order is an artifact of an upstream process never designed to be fair and that my rule launders its arbitrariness into an official decision: I can answer that by replacing the upstream order with one I generate myself under a procedure designed to be neutral — sorting tied files by a hash of their identifiers, say — after which I have a rule whose content is arbitrary but whose form is not. It is a general policy, adopted once, for the reasons of publicity and stability, and indifferent among applicants by construction. Meanwhile your coin has a problem you have not solved: it is unstable. Nothing in the coin tells me to accept its first answer rather than flip again when I dislike the result, so you owe me a rule about when the flipping stops, and the moment you supply that rule you have conceded that the real work is being done by a policy and not by chance. So what does the coin do that a neutral, published, deterministic tie-break cannot?
