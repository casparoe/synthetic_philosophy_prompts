ok so I built this argument for a reading group on Moore and it now seems to eat its own tail, and I can't find the seam.

Background I was working from: Moore holds that the greatest goods we know are the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the pleasures of personal affection; that a thing's intrinsic value is what it would have if it existed entirely alone; that wholes are organic unities, so a part's contribution to a whole needn't equal its own isolated value and adding a good part to a good whole can lower the whole's value; and that a beautiful object no one ever perceives would be worth hardly anything.

(1) Intrinsic value is settled by the isolation test.
(2) Value is organic: contributions aren't read off isolated values.
(3) The great goods are consciousness of beauty and personal affection, not objects or people as such.
(4) Unbeheld beauty has near-zero intrinsic value.
(5) So inside the whole [person + real object + veridical appreciation], the object contributes basically nothing; the value all sits in the conscious state.
(6) Take two subjects whose conscious lives are type-identical — one really looking at the painting and really loved by her friend, one in a perfect simulation of both. By (5), their states have equal intrinsic value.
(7) The two cases differ only in causal history and in whether the object exists, and neither difference is a difference in intrinsic value; so we have no value-based reason at all to prefer the real world.
(8) So a planet of permanently deluded appreciators and simulated friends is every bit as good as ours — and if you're choosing between funding a museum and funding a perfect simulation that reaches a billion more people, the simulation wins easily.

That last bit is what makes me suspicious, since Moore of all people is not supposed to end up there. I'd much rather keep organic unities and the claim about unbeheld beauty than keep the conclusion.

Two things I want handled: tell me which step to cut and what cutting it costs — someone in the group proposed just adding a 'contact requirement' (the valuable state has to be suitably caused by the real thing), which smells like bolting on the conclusion, and another proposed saying the isolation test just can't be run on states that are about something, which I don't know how to price. And run the two mirror cases before you answer: the friend who really loves you but whose affection you never appreciate, and the hallucinator who finds out ten years later that it was fake and whose memory of it then collapses — does the collapse show the value was never there, or does it just add a later bad state?

Lead with the step you'd reject in the very first sentence, then give me the reasoning for why it looked fine, then price the other exits. No preamble, no restating my question.
