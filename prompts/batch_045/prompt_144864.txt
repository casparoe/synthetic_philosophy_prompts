I am legal secretary to the Northvale Council on Assisted Reproduction, a statutory body that must license each proposed act of embryo selection individually. The jurisdiction is invented and the eight decisions below are the Council's entire published record; please reason from them rather than from anything you know about how such questions are usually settled. The Council's governing provision permits a licence only where the selection "may reasonably be expected to serve the welfare of the child who may be born, having regard also to the interests of children generally." The second clause was inserted after a scandal eleven years ago and has never once been applied.

The precedents, each with brief written reasons:

1. One embryo carried a dominant variant for an untreatable neurodegenerative disease with onset around forty-five. Licensed. The Council held that it need not find the resulting life not worth living; an expected difference in the child's own welfare suffices.

2. A variant causing a conspicuous craniofacial difference with no medical consequences, where the Council found on evidence that a severe and reliably imposed social penalty attaches to that appearance. Licensed. The Council rejected the argument that, because the burden is socially caused, the remedy must be social rather than genetic.

3. Sex selection for family balancing, both embryos healthy. Refused: the benefit was to the parents, not to the child.

4. Selection of an embryo tissue-matched to an existing sibling needing cord-blood stem cells. Licensed, subject to counselling conditions. The child's own burden was minor and the child gained the relationship with a treated sibling; the Council said the child's interest operates as a side-constraint and expressly declined to say it must be maximised.

5. Selection on a polygenic risk score for a common, early-onset, partly preventable condition, among four embryos, where the absolute risk difference between best and worst was small and where the parents could have reduced the risk by other means. Licensed: "the provision asks whether selection may reasonably be expected to serve the child's welfare, not whether it is the only, the best, or a medical means of doing so."

6. Selection on a weakly validated index of "easy temperament"; the applicants wanted a placid child. Refused: expected benefit to the child was speculative, the demonstrated benefit was to the parents, and the Council noted the risk of raising a child to fit a prediction.

7. Selection for predicted adult height. Refused, narrowly: the applicant had not shown an expected benefit to the child, the associations being largely attributable to genetic nurture and unlikely to survive a within-family comparison. Two members added that they doubted whether an advantage consisting only in one's position relative to others could ever satisfy the provision; the Council recorded that it reserved that question.

8. Selection so that a child would fit in within the applicants' tightly knit religious community, where a disfavoured trait would bring exclusion. Refused: the Council would not "ratify a local prejudice," and distinguished decision 2 on the ground that the penalty there was society-wide and was not one the applicants' own community could lift.

The pending application: five viable embryos, none carrying a known disease variant, all unremarkable on the risk scores the Council has previously accepted. The applicants seek selection on a polygenic index of scholastic attainment, built from a genome-wide study of several hundred thousand people of the applicants' ancestry, with direct genetic effects isolated by within-family designs so that the objection that decided case 7 does not apply. In this jurisdiction all children sit a ranked examination at eleven; rank determines secondary-school placement; placement strongly predicts further education, adult earnings and measured adult health. Between the highest- and lowest-scoring of the five embryos the expected difference is roughly twelve percentile points of exam rank, which the Council's expert evidence converts into a materially higher probability of the academic track and, through that, small but real expected gains in lifetime earnings, in adult health and in self-reported well-being. The same experts testified: (a) the payoff is almost entirely positional — were every family to select this way the rank distribution would be unchanged and no child would gain, while a minority selecting would shift the remainder down; (b) the index's predictive validity would decay as the practice spread, both because the ranking is competitive and because schools and employers would reweight their criteria; (c) the expected well-being gain, though positive on the Council's usual measures, is small and mediated entirely through the positional channel; (d) there is no expected effect on the parent–child relationship, and the record supports the applicants' statement that they will raise whichever child they have without reference to the prediction. Finally, two complications: the highest-scoring embryo also carries a slightly elevated polygenic risk of a late-onset psychiatric condition of the kind the Council treated as a qualifying burden in case 1, so the applicants are trading increased disease risk for a positional gain, though on the Council's own accepted measure the net expected well-being still favours that embryo; and the applicants ask, if licensed, to undergo further stimulation cycles so as to choose from a larger cohort, which the clinic's evidence says would roughly double the expected gain and which the Council has never considered.

What I want from you:

1. Set out the two or three rules that fit all eight precedences and say what each predicts here. I can sketch five candidates — net expected welfare to the child; the exclusion of merely positional advantage; a priority for reducing disease risk over securing non-medical goods; the child's interest as a constraint rather than a maximand; and a universalisation test read into the "interests of children generally" clause — but I cannot tell which of them the record actually supports, whether any two are the same rule in different words, or whether some of them are inconsistent with decisions already handed down.
2. Identify the closest analog among the eight and say how much of the new case it really settles.
3. Predict the ruling, with the considerations you would give weight to, and state which of your rules the prediction depends on.
4. Recommend what the Council should decide if it wishes to be consistent with its own record, and state honestly the principle it would be adopting either way — including, if you recommend licensing, whether that commits it to licensing cohort expansion, and, if you recommend refusal, whether it commits it to reopening decision 2.
5. Specify the single further application that would best discriminate between the rules you have identified, and say what each rule predicts about it.

Write it as a memo of roughly nine hundred words in five numbered sections, in a formal register, addressed to me. Do not cite any literature or comparative law: the Council's reasons must stand on this record, and its decisions are not published outside the jurisdiction.
