I convene a fortnightly metaphysics reading group, and at our last meeting two members got into an argument we ran out of time on. The topic was origin essentialism about people: the claim, of the sort Kripke made famous in the Naming and Necessity lectures, that a human being could not have originated from gametes other than the particular sperm and egg they actually came from. Neither disputed that some such thesis is intuitively compelling; the disagreement was about what it commits you to once hard cases are on the table. I stayed out of it and genuinely cannot tell who had the better of it. Roughly, this is how it went.

(1) Idris opened with what he called the quiet swap. Take two sperm from the same ejaculate whose DNA differs at a single position where the difference makes no downstream difference at all — a synonymous substitution, so the same amino acid is produced and every cell of the resulting organism, from first cleavage onwards, is qualitatively indistinguishable from its counterpart in the actual organism. Had the other sperm won, he said, everything thereafter would have gone just as it actually did, and the person born would have been me. So the gamete thesis is false: what matters is what the gamete does, not which gamete it was.

(2) Rowena replied that he had built his conclusion into the description. If the two zygotes differ in sequence, they are two zygotes; identity is transitive; the adult is the organism that developed from the one that actually existed, so the adult in his scenario is numerically different, however exactly alike. Saying that everything would have gone just as it did quietly assumes the disputed thing — that one and the same organism could have had a different earliest stage. Read properly, the case shows only that the difference between the gametes is a difference that does not matter to how things go, and that is not the same as a difference that is not there.

(3) Idris then offered the quiet edit. Keep the actual sperm, but suppose a single base pair differs through a copying error that the zygote's own repair machinery restores before the first cleavage. At the outset there are two zygotes with different sequences; shortly afterwards there is nothing anywhere that distinguishes them. Rowena's transitivity argument still returns a verdict — same organism within each scenario, different across scenarios — but it would return that verdict for any stipulated difference whatsoever, however inert, which suggests the argument is not what carries the intuition. And, he added, it cannot be rescued by insisting that identity facts are settled at the earliest stage, because that is contested ground: where an embryo can still divide into twins, or where two embryos fuse, we do not antecedently know that there is a determinate fact about which later organism is identical with which earlier one.

(4) Rowena answered that this changed the subject. Whether identity is determinate across fission and fusion is a question about what fixes identity through time; the essentialist claim concerns which modal profile the organism that results has. As for the continuum he was assembling — a silent substitution, a repaired one, a different ovum from the same woman with different mitochondria, and so on up to a stranger's gamete — the absence of a principled line separating the differences that matter from those that do not is precisely what one should expect if identity is not a matter of degree and is not fixed by qualitative similarity. An essentialist thesis with a threshold set by downstream similarity is not the thesis on the table.

(5) Idris conceded the point but turned it: that, he said, shows the gamete formulation is the wrong statement of whatever is true nearby. Consider the delayed twin — an embryo produced by somatic cell nuclear transfer from one of your skin cells, carrying your exact nuclear genome, gestated by a different woman decades later. That is not you; it is a distinct person, your very late identical twin. Sameness of genome is therefore not sufficient in general, and the essentialist's explanation of why must appeal to something other than the gametes: the causal and historical embedding of the organism's coming to be. Restated in those terms, the gamete thesis drops out as an over-rigid proxy, and the quiet swap and the quiet edit are exactly the cases where proxy and principle diverge. His conclusion was that they had never been arguing about the same thesis, and that the gamete formulation cannot do the work people suppose it does.

(6) Rowena's parting shot was that if the gametes are merely a proxy, Idris owes an account of what they are a proxy for — one that still delivers the standard verdicts, that you could not have been born of different parents and that the clone is not you, without redescribing every intuition as an intuition about causal history, since plenty of variations in causal history (the hour of conception, the mechanics of fertilisation) leave everyone entirely unmoved.
