I work as a platform engineer, with no formal training in philosophy, and a friend in cognitive science got me interested in the Knobe effect. I did the obvious informal thing and tried the original chairman-and-the-environment vignette on my team over lunch, and got roughly the split that gets reported: people overwhelmingly say he harmed the environment intentionally when he says he does not care about harming it, and they mostly deny it in the version where the side effect helps the environment. Five or six of us have been arguing about it since, and the argument has hardened into two positions that I am not able to referee, partly because I do not know the literature and cannot chase down references. As I understand them, one camp holds that the asymmetry is in the concept itself: that our application of intentionally is partly shaped, or even partly constituted, by the moral valence of the foreseen side effect and by whether the agent's attitude toward it was one of indifference to something bad, so that there is no valence-free core to be recovered by tidying up the experiment. The other camp holds that the underlying competence is not moral at all, that it tracks something like control, or how responsive the agent was to reasons, or the agent's practical stance toward the outcome, and that the asymmetry in the data is generated further downstream by what people take themselves to be doing when they answer, namely assigning blame or credit, or hearing the question as a request for a verdict rather than a description, combined with the fact that the usual instrument is a forced yes or no on a word that is pragmatically loaded. Before I understood either view properly I had assumed the whole effect was an artifact of a badly designed binary question and would disappear under a better instrument; if that is naive, I would like the document to say so rather than flatter me.

What I would like you to draft is the joint statement those two positions could put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the claims both would sign, including about what the original vignettes actually establish and about how much weight the methodology can bear; then the disagreements stated precisely enough that each side would accept the wording of its own position rather than a caricature; then the cruxes, the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one side. For concreteness, here is the stimulus set we sketched, and I would like the document to say which items both camps agree bear on a real crux and what each side predicts for each: a version set in software deployment, where an engineer ships a change that predictably degrades a hospital paging service, or predictably improves it, with the stated indifference held constant across both; a zero-stakes version where the foreseen side effect breaks a written convention but affects nobody's welfare; a version where the side effect is genuinely bad but violates no rule or expectation; a version in which the agent is a scheduled job rather than a person; a counterbalanced version in which half the participants are first asked a blame or credit question; and a version that replaces the yes or no with a continuous scale and separates intentionally, knowingly, and foreseeably as distinct items. I also want the statement to sort the substantive disagreements from the ones that are only about words, and to be explicit about whether the two sides are even disputing the same thing, since it seems possible to me that one is describing the folk concept while the other is recommending which concept an engineering organisation ought to use in a blameless incident review, where the word intentional does a kind of work that nobody involved wants it to do.
