I teach a first-year module in philosophy of mind, and in the fourth week I have a single ninety-minute seminar, whiteboard only, on how consciousness is distributed across animals. The students have met the problem of other minds solely in its interpersonal form — how, if at all, do I know that you are not a mere automaton? — and have read nothing about animal minds. I do not intend to assign anything, and I would rather the session not hinge on any particular empirical finding, since what I want them to leave with is a sense of the structure of the question rather than a stock of results about fish, birds, or insects.

Please give me a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself. In particular:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and the dependency relations that fix that order. My own instinct is that the binary form of the question has to be loosened before any criterion for consciousness is offered, and that the cheap way to loosen it is a case drawn from our own species — a human in dreamless sleep, or in the grip of an absence seizure — where we are perfectly willing to say that a member of a conscious species is not conscious at that moment. Tell me whether that is right, what must already be on the board for it to work, and what it licenses me to say next.

2. The one case that is to carry the whole session. I have been using a teleost fish: near enough to us in the relevant organization for the argument from analogy to look respectable, distant enough in appearance and repertoire that the analogy visibly strains — no face to read, no vocalization, no mirror self-recognition, and a pattern of response to injury quite unlike ours. I want your view on what such a case must have and must lack in order to bear this weight, and on whether the fish is the right choice or whether it smuggles in something I should not rely on.

3. The misconceptions, in the order in which I should expect them, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be defused. I anticipate at least four: that consciousness varies along a single ladder with us at the top, so that the task is to place each species on a scale; that the distribution question just is the moral question, and can be settled by asking what we owe the animal; that since no one can ever know, any answer is as good as any other; and — the one I most want handled well — that a full behavioural and functional repertoire of damage-detection, avoidance learning, and motivational trade-off settles the matter, when the live alternative is a creature that does all of that with nothing it is like for it. That last possibility must land without tipping the students into a general scepticism about other human minds, and I would like your view on where precisely the line between the two gets drawn.

4. Whether I should also introduce, at this level, the possibility that the question whether a given species is conscious is simply ill-formed, because a single creature might have experience of some of what it senses and not of other parts of it — so that one animal yields several answers. I cannot decide whether this clarifies things for first-years or merely dissolves the topic for them.

5. One question I can put to them at the end that would show me they have grasped the question rather than formed an opinion about it — ideally something they can answer by applying the structure to a case I have not discussed.

6. What I should openly decline to settle, and say out loud that I am declining: I take it this includes the metaphysics of consciousness itself and the practical upshot for diet and research policy, but tell me if I am wrong about either, or if there is something I am likelier to slide into without noticing.
