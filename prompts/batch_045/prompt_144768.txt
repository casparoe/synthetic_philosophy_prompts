I help run a fortnightly "methods hour" in my department — mostly grad students and a couple of postdocs, nearly all of whom work on something completely unrelated (I'm supposed to be finishing a chapter on a nineteenth-century guy and this is pure procrastination). Last week two of the regulars, Marta and Dev, got properly stuck into each other, someone said "you should referee this", I said yes, and now I wish I hadnt because I keep flip-flopping.

The starting point was the general observation that verdicts on philosophical cases don't come out the same everywhere: people in different cultural and linguistic groups give noticeably different answers on Gettier-style cases, on the name/reference cases, on whether an agent in a deterministic scenario acts freely and is blameworthy, and Marta's opening move (1) was that this is bad news for the method of cases — if the judgements the whole discipline runs on are shaped by culture, then a lot of analytic philosophy has been doing sociology of a narrow, WEIRD population while imagining it was doing something else. Dev's reply (2) was the expertise/calibration thing: variation among untutored folk squinting at a one-paragraph vignette for thirty seconds isn't the evidential class the method actually uses, since the method asks what we should say on due reflection after seeing the contrast cases, and there's no reason to expect an untutored faculty to be reliable on loaded cases anyway — it's like running Müller-Lyer on people and concluding that spatial perception gives us no evidence about tables. Marta (3) said that reply gerrymanders: if you define the evidential class as whatever tutored judgers converge on after reflection, you can't then cite that convergence as independent support for the theory the training imparts, and it's worse than that because the tutored are drawn from the same cultural pool, so convergence after training is exactly what you'd predict if training transmits a theory rather than sharpening a faculty. She also said philosophers' own verdicts wobble with presentation order and wording, which she takes to sink the idea that there's a stable expert competence to appeal to in the first place. Dev (4) came back with a dilemma: either there is a fact of the matter about whether the Gettier subject knows, in which case convergence among the people who have thought hardest about the contrast cases is some evidence even if imperfect, or there isn't, in which case Marta has only shown that the method is untested rather than debunked, and "untested" is a pretty thin result to reorient a discipline with. Then Marta (5) produced a preprint somebody had dropped in the group chat — I've no idea whether it's any good and I'd rather you treat it as a hypothetical than try to remember it — in which the same cases were run in several countries twice, once asking what you would say and once asking what most people should say; first-order answers varied a lot by site, the "should" answers much less. She thinks this is the real nail in the coffin: it suggests people are treating case verdicts as norms of ascription rather than as detections of a fact, so the method of cases has been in the business of eliciting norms all along, and once you see that, cross-cultural variation stops being surprising and the method stops being a route to discovery. Dev (6) said the same finding helps him instead: a collapse of variation at the normative level is just what you'd expect if the first-order mess is performance noise from unfamiliar vignettes, and if the shared verdict shows up as soon as you ask what's correct, then that's where the competence lives, so the finding rescues the method rather than burying it. Marta's last shot (7) was that he can't have it both ways: when it suits him he says the cross-cultural differences are differences in which concept is being applied, i.e. merely verbal, but if that's the right story then the method has been eliciting verdicts about different subject matters all along and those verdicts can't be evidence for any one theory, so he'd have saved the method only by making it about nothing. Dev started answering and we ran out of time.

Can you adjudicate this properly — go through moves 1 to 7 in order, rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say briefly why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd like you to be explicit about which claims are doing real work and which are rhetorical, about where Marta and Dev are each answering a slightly different question from the one the other asked, and about what both of them are missing: I have a nagging feeling they're both assuming "the method of cases" is one single thing with one single job, and that a lot of the disagreement is really about which job it's supposed to be doing. It would also help if you could say what kind of further result would actually separate their positions — I mean the shape of the result, not a reading list or paper titles, since I can't look anything up at the moment.
