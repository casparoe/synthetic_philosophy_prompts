I retired from seismology a few years ago and somehow ended up hosting a small podcast — we call it Field Notes, philosophy for people who used to change the oil in a spectrometer — and next month's guest is Dr. Britta Halvorsen, who defends a minimalist, deflationary line about truth: there's no substantive property of being true, everything we do with the word is captured by the equivalence schema (S is true iff S) plus some story about generalization and blind ascription, and she's just published a book called Nothing Deep About Truth. I've read two chapters and I want to push her properly instead of letting her give the polished twenty-minute version she's given on three other shows. Her stock move is to say that whenever truth looks like it's doing heavy lifting, that's just "the logical device earning its keep, not a property" — I want questions that make that deflection progressively harder to lean on, ending with one where it just stops working.

So: give me six to eight questions, each with a follow-up (or two) keyed to the answer she'd most likley give, and make sure there's at least one on each of these four fronts. First, my own home-made puzzle, which I'll call the archive case. I have forty years of field notebooks in a private shorthand; most of the entries I can no longer read back, and one of them says something like "the claim about the 1998 aftershock sequence on p. 200 is not true". Standing over the boxes I say "everything in these notebooks is true". Press her on what that ascription even amounts to if 'true' is only a disquotational device and I can't produce — or even reconstruct — the individual sentences; then, when she says it's an infinite conjunction, or a standing commitment to endorse each entry as it comes up, hit her with the p. 200 entry and ask whether she has to restrict the schema to "grounded" sentences, and whether groundedness is a substantive semantic notion smuggled in the back door. Second, the thing I half-remember from popular Gödel books: bolt a truth predicate onto Peano arithmetic in the full Tarskian way, with induction extended to formulas that mention truth, and you can prove the consistency of arithmetic, which arithmetic can't prove on its own, so the predicate isn't conservative and looks like it has real content — though I gather there's a wrinkle, that the meager disquotational version without induction for truth-containing formulas is supposed to be conservative after all. Ask her about that, and then follow up by wiring it back to the archive case: if deflationism can only afford the meager, conservative predicate, is that meager thing actually strong enough to do the generalization work she needs it for in blind ascriptions? Third, the normative complaint: we tell students to aim at the truth and to assert only what is true, but if "S is true" says nothing beyond S, isn't that norm either empty or an infinite shopping list that no finite agent can be guided by? Fourth, my own professional gripe: scientists lean on truth to explain why theories that survive testing also yield working predictions and working instruments, and if truth is that thin, does the explanation collapse into something like "believe what you should believe", and is that an explanation at all?

Keep the wording plain where you can — she's happy being technical but my listeners arne't — skip anything that just asks her to restate the view, and no softballs about what got her into philosophy. At the very end, after the questions, put a single line telling me which question you think she'd have the hardest time answering, in the form \answer{Q4 — the groundedness follow-up}: just the label plus a four-or-five-word tag.
