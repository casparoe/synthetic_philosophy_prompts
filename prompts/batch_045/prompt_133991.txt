I've spent a long time reading rationalist material on akrasia, hyperbolic discounting, and precommitment, and the basic move still seems to me to be right: an agent at t1, foreseeing that her t2 self will rank the same options differently for motives she now regards as distortions, can improve her own expected outcome by removing an option from that later self. What I have never seen set out precisely is how far this picture carries when the agent at t1 and the agent at t2 are not the same agent at all, which is the ordinary situation in constitutional precommitment. I'd like that gap explained at the level of mechanism rather than at the level of slogans about the dead hand of the past.

To fix terms, I'll stipulate two distinctions of my own. Call a device *state-tracking* if its binding force depends on whether the later agent is in the impaired condition the device was built to guard against, and *flat* if it binds irrespective of that. Call it *absolute* if there is no procedure for release, and *costed* if release is available but carries a cost c > 0, such as a supermajority requirement, a cooling-off interval, or a sunset clause requiring reenactment.

In the individual case, flatness looks defensible even though it is second-best. No feasible device is state-tracking, because the impairment characteristically includes the agent's judgment about whether she is impaired; any exception procedure will therefore be exploited by the very condition it was meant to screen for. That is also what gives a bright-line personal rule its peculiar payoff structure: breaking it once does not cost one indulgence but degrades the rule, so the local calculus at t2 systematically misprices the stake. On this basis an individual can rationally accept a device she knows will sometimes block a response she would, at t2, correctly judge to be information-driven.

I can see three ways the transfer to the constitutional case might fail, and I can't tell which of them is doing the real work.

First, identity: within a few generations the t1 and t2 populations are disjoint, so nothing is binding *itself*; the device is a bequest, and the flattering label of self-binding may be doing unearned normative labour. Second, diagnostics: nothing in collective life plays the role that craving plays in the individual case. War fever, moral panic, and punitive frenzy are the natural candidates, but they are contestable, and a test for them can be wielded by whoever wants the entrenchment to bite. If there is no agreed-upon impairment-detector, then a device cannot be justified by pointing to the condition it screens, and the state-tracking rationale is unavailable in principle rather than merely in practice. Third, and this is the part I find hardest: in the individual case the agent who would capture a loose escape hatch *is* the impaired agent, so tightening the hatch and impairing the agent are aimed at the same target. In the collective case these roles can come apart. A loose amendment procedure is captured by transient passion; a tight one is captured by holdout coalitions whose motive is interest rather than impairment, and who are not the same people the device was meant to restrain. So the argument that licenses flatness in the individual case doesn't obviously apply, and the choice of c seems to depend on base rates no one can measure while being selected by actors with stakes in the outcome.

There is also the further wrinkle that the individual case rests on an asymmetry in *judgment*, whereas the standard collective defense — factions binding everyone because each is uncertain whether it will hold power later, a multipolar trap of the kind rationalist writing discusses — supplies an asymmetry in *incentive* instead.

So, concretely, I'd like the following explained:

1. Which of the three failure modes above is the genuine crux, and which is a red herring or collapses into another? In particular, if the identity point were solved by treating the polity as a single continuing agent with overlapping generations, would the diagnostic and role-splitting problems survive?
2. Can an incentive-asymmetry license flatness in the way a judgment-asymmetry appears to? If not, what exactly does the insurance-among-factions argument establish — that entrenchment is prudent, that it is legitimate, or only that it is what self-interceding factions would agree to?
3. Is there a principled way to set the cost c of the escape hatch, or does any rule for setting it simply relocate the capture problem one level up? If the latter, say so and explain what follows for how we should design amendment rules.
4. Apply your account to this case: a polity, in the aftermath of a financial collapse and under unusually consensual conditions, entrenches a supermajority requirement for new public debt. Two generations later an exogenous shock makes borrowing clearly beneficial; the supermajority fails because a minority calculates that the costs of not borrowing fall mainly on its rivals. Which of your distinctions tells us whether the device malfunctioned, and what would the individual analogue — the person who forswore all borrowing after bankruptcy and is then offered a genuinely positive-expected-value loan — have us say about it?

Please steelman the strongest available version of the view that constitutional precommitment really is self-binding before you argue against it; I have read the case for the dead-hand objection many times and it does not need repeating on my behalf.
