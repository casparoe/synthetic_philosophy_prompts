I'm revising a chapter of a dissertation on what we owe future people, and I want to stress-test the position I've argued myself into before committing to it in print. Roughly: where a choice doesn't affect which particular individuals exist, I keep a person-affecting restriction — moral reasons have to be traceable to some individual being made worse off — but where a choice does determine who comes to exist, I drop that restriction and rank outcomes impersonally, by aggregate well-being with some priority weighting for the worse off. So in the standard cases — the reckless teenager who conceives a child who will start life at a serious disadvantage, the choice between a century-long policy of depletion and one of conservation, a medical programme that prevents a condition in one cohort versus one that treats it in another — my verdict is that nobody is wronged, and that this isn't because the acts are fine, but because the framework that condemns them counts lives rather than wrongs. A friend says I should skip the switch and just be a no-difference theorist; another says the switch is gerrymandering and I should find a person-affecting view that survives identity change.

Two wrinkles I can't shake. First, the second friend's positive proposal, which he calls the availability-relative threshold view: instead of comparing the life someone gets to the life they would have had under the alternative (empty, since they wouldn't have existed at all), compare it to the range of lives that were feasible and epistemically available to the agent in that choice situation. If you hand someone a life below the bottom of that range when you could have handed them one comfortably inside it, you wrong them, identity notwithstanding. Second, cases where it seems indeterminate, rather than determinately false, that the person who exists would have existed under the alternative: suppose the alternative policy changes the intrauterine environment enough that whether the resulting child is numerically the same person isn't settled, or shifts the timing of conception within a window where which gametes succeed is a chancy matter. If counterfactual identity can be vague, the switch between my two frameworks has no determinate setting.

So: what would count against the hybrid? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate what a committed defender of something like my view should concede as genuinely counting against it from what defenders standardly dismiss as a cost they have already priced in. Please cover: whether the availability-relative threshold view is a real rival to going impersonal or just impersonalism in person-affecting costume; whether the indeterminacy cases do real damage or only force me to let the switch tolerate vagueness; what the hybrid predicts that neither straight impersonalism nor a no-difference view predicts — I think the extension to historical injustice is the testable case, since the same identity-scrambling story seems to excuse wrongs committed a century ago, which I find very hard to accept; and an honest verdict on whether I have insulated the position by saying that wronging drops out while impermissibility doesn't. What would move me most is a case where the hybrid returns the wrong answer about an act that is clearly wrong, rather than a complaint about elegance or an appeal to an intuition I can't independently support. Answer as exactly five numbered points, ordered from most decisive to least, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
