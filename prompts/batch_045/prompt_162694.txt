I'm an ICU fellow, and an embarrassing share of my job consists of writing if-thens — in handoff notes, in the morning plan, in what I say to the night resident at 3 a.m. — so a few months ago I started noting down my own gut reactions to them the way you'd collect specimens. I'm also auditing a semantics seminar where the phrases 'context set', 'similarity ordering', and 'conditional probability' seem to be used more or less interchangeably, and I genuinely cannot tell which of those machines is supposed to be doing the work. Here are the verdicts I've collected, all from situations I've actually been in. 'If the CT shows a bleed, we'll take him to the OR': fine. 'If the CT showed a bleed, we took him to the OR': fine, said before anyone has looked, even though the film is already shot and the matter is settled in fact. The very same sentence, after I have looked and the film is clean: I can't say it — it comes out feeling like a lie by implication. The very same sentence again, in the morbidity-and-mortality conference, where everyone present knows the film was clean and has explicitly agreed to reason as if it weren't: fine again. 'If the CT had shown a bleed, we would have taken him to the OR': fine, with no stage-setting at all, even though everyone knows it didn't. The stock philosophical example about a presidential assassin — the plain past-tense version sounds wrong, the 'hadn't / would have' version sounds fine, and the wrong one is repaired once the room agrees to suppose he didn't do it — feels like my CT cases in costume, which encouraged me until I noticed that I can't say what changes between the third and fourth of my cases other than that people agreed to play along, which sounds like a cop-out rather than a principle. Three more, which I think are the ones that will sink any simple story. The lab has told us the blood culture grew something but not what; 'if the culture grew gram-positive cocci, then it grew something' makes me balk, even though I'll concede under pressure that the consequent is already settled for us, and even though the parallel conditional with 'we'll start vancomycin' as its consequent is perfectly fine. A three-step sequence: 'if we had given the thrombolytic, he would have bled' is fine; 'if we had given it and his platelets had been normal, he wouldn't have bled' is fine; 'if we had given it, his platelets had been normal, and the morning film had shown the sentinel bleed, he would have bled anyway' is fine too — but each one is fine only in isolation, I can't hold all three at once, and the third feels weaker than the first in a way I can't articulate. And mismatched moods: 'if the CT shows a bleed, we would take him to the OR' and 'if the CT showed a bleed, we will take him to the OR' both sound broken to me in a way that has nothing to do with what I believe. Two more small data points: 'if we had given it, he might have bled' and 'if we had given it, he might not have bled' are both fine and, unlike their 'would' counterparts, fine together; and 'even if the CT had shown a bleed, we wouldn't have taken him to the OR' is fine and genuinely informative in a case where the plain 'if it had shown a bleed, we wouldn't have taken him' is simply false.

My own stab, such as it is, is this: an indicative conditional is a claim about what is still live in the information the room shares, so it goes dead once the room knows the antecedent is false, whereas a counterfactual isn't subject to any such constraint, because it asks what would happen if you held the actual course of events fixed and changed one thing. That covers my first six cases, but it is silent on why the trivial-consequent case sounds wrong to me, silent on the mood mismatches, and I think it quietly predicts that the three-step sequence is fine, which it isn't.

So here is what I want. Is there a single principle, or a single principle with one parameter that flips between indicative and counterfactual, that captures the whole set? State it precisely enough that I can see which of my cases buys which clause, and be blunt about which of my reactions it cannot accommodate and which one I should give up — I'd rather drop a verdict than keep a principle that is really three principles in a trench coat. Then tell me what your principle predicts about two cases I haven't settled: first, the attending has already seen that the CT is clean and the night resident hasn't, and she tells the resident to page her if it showed a bleed, which I think is the natural thing to say but which my own principle seems to forbid; second, everyone in the room knows the CT showed no bleed and that we didn't take him to the OR, and someone says, in the would-have form, that if it had shown no bleed we wouldn't have taken him. If your account and its nearest rival disagree on either of those, say which prediction is which and why you prefer yours, and name the further case that would tell them apart.
