I'm on the gift acceptance committee at Northfield College (small liberal arts place, mid-campaign). We meet Thursday and I have to circulate a recommendation memo beforehand. I'd normally just write it, but I keep going in circles on the conceptual bit, so I want you to draft it.

The case:

Arnold Wexler '51 died in March. He gave every year for forty years and never once gave more than 25k. At the campaign dinner in October — sixty-odd people, the president had just described a 5 million matching challenge that has to be met by June 30 — Wexler, at a table near the front, said to the student sitting next to him, loud enough that the president and maybe a dozen others heard: put me down for five million. Then, a few seconds later: and if you land the match, I'll go to ten. He never said I promise or I pledge, and he was talking to a student, not to the president. The president came over later that evening, shook his hand, said thank you Arnold, the room applauded. Nothing was written down that night.

What came out afterwards:

- The advancement office booked a 10 million pledge. No signed gift agreement, which our own policy requires for anything over a million. They also counted his 5 toward meeting the match — so the condition on the extra 5 was satisfied partly by his own money.
- Three days later the president put out a press release announcing a 10 million gift from Arnold Wexler '51. Nobody ever corrected it, and the public campaign total still includes it.
- In April we admitted two Northfield Scholars with four-year funding commitments. The dean says we would've thought twice about the second one if the gift hadn't been on the books.
- His daughter, who is the executor, found a note he dictated the morning of the dinner telling his financial advisor he was thinking about doing something big for the college and was not ready to commit. She also says that the next morning he told her: I told them five million, for the scholarship thing or the new science building, whichever they need more.
- She has now offered 5 million to the scholarship fund, payable over five years, on condition that we stop using the 10 million figure and don't pursue the rest. She needs an answer by the end of the month; the estate closes in July.

Half the committee says: he said it in front of sixty people, of course he committed himself, and the signed-agreement rule is about bookkeeping, not about whether a promise was made. The other half says: no performative verb, said to the wrong person, nothing in writing, and a man who earlier that day was telling his advisor he wasn't ready to commit — nothing was promised, and the 10 million is our own invention.

What I want in the memo:

1. A recommendation with a bottom line and a time frame: what we record, what we say publicly and by when, and what we do about the daughter's offer. Pick one; don't split the difference.
2. A straight answer on the conceptual question, since that's what the committee is really fighting about: did an utterance like that commit him? I've read a little Austin and Searle — felicity conditions, the hereby test, the idea that a promise needs uptake — and I definately can't tell whether this is a case where the speech act misfires (no uptake from the right party? content too vague — scholarship thing or science building? speaker not intending to bind himself?) or a case where it plainly succeeded and only the paperwork is missing. Tell me which, and why the other reading looks so good.
3. Keep two questions separate, and say explicitly whether keeping them separate changes what we should do: (a) whether he committed himself, (b) whether our press release committed the college, or at least made it permissible for the dean to rely on the money. I suspect our own announcement is doing more work here than anyone wants to admit.
4. Something we could actually use next time: a short test, three or four conditions, for when an informal remark at an event counts as a pledge for our purposes. Not a legal test — counsel handles that. I mean the moral and conceptual one.
5. Give the loosing side its best day in court. If you come down on the strict side, engage seriously the thought that we'd be letting an internal accounting rule erase a dead man's word given in front of sixty witnesses. If you come down the other way, engage the thought that we manufactured the reliance we're now hiding behind.

Keep it under 1200 words, reasons over throat-clearing. And don't cite anything or point me to further reading — I can't chase references this week and nobody on the committee will.
