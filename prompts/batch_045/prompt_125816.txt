Here is what I want diagnosed. A friend of mine works in a government behavioural insights unit — the sort of team that designs defaults, reminders, form orderings and framing for public services. We had a long argument last night and neither of us moved an inch. Her position: nudges are not paternalistic at all, because they forbid nothing and change no one's economic incentives, they are justified by the fact that human beings predictably mishandle defaults, present bias and paperwork, they are empirically validated in randomised trials, and the whole point of the definitional clause that requires an intervention to make choosers better off *as judged by themselves* is precisely to hand the standard back to the person being nudged. My position: the clause is doing no independent work. The team chooses which behaviour to target, chooses which outcome variable stands for the person's own good, and then runs the trial only on the arm it favours — the arm it built. So "as judged by themselves" is filled in by the architect rather than by the chooser, and calling that non-paternalistic is a terminological win rather than a moral one. She replied that every choice architecture is somebody's, so the only question is whose, and that abolishing nudges would simply install the status quo's designers as the winners. I replied that this shows nudging is inevitable, not that it is permissible. She replied that I was confusing coercion with influence. We then said the same four things at each other in different words for another hour.

What makes me think this is worth a diagnosis rather than a verdict is that the academic argument looks stuck in the same shape. Defenders keep producing the same two moves — architecture is unavoidable, therefore the choice is between designs rather than between design and no design; and the intervention is cheap to avoid, therefore liberty is intact. Critics keep producing the same two replies — cheap-to-avoid is not the same as non-manipulative, and "better off as judged by themselves" is unfalsifiable when the architect sets the measure. Meanwhile a third literature says the interesting contrast is not nudge versus nothing but nudge versus something else: interventions that build the person's own competence to choose, rather than interventions that route around her weaknesses. And nothing settles. Theorists on both sides are smart, read each other, concede points, and then restate.

I have a hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis. I suspect the word paternalism is carrying three separate disagreements at once, and that the debate is stuck because each side wins on a different one. First, a definitional disagreement: whether paternalism is a property of the means used (coercion, incentives, option-removal) or of the reason the intervener acts on (that the target's own judgment about her good is, in this case, defective) — nudges have the second without the first, so the answer flips depending on which definition you pick. Second, a hidden empirical disagreement: how robust, how domain-specific and how reversible the relevant behavioural findings are, and, relatedly, what would even count as evidence given that the trials are run one-directionally, testing the nudge against the status quo rather than against the mirror image of itself. Third, a bedrock disagreement about whether it is ever permissible for an institution to act on a conception of a person's good that she would not currently endorse, which is the sort of thing neither of us can argue for without just restating it. My suspicion is that only the second is resolvable and that the other two are being mistaken for it.

Two things I tried out on my friend, which I would like you to evaluate rather than humoured. The first is what I called the orphaned machinery test: suppose a team installs a default because of a documented bias, and then the bias finding fails to replicate, or the population turns out not to have it, or the rationale is simply withdrawn. Does the architecture come down? If a default, a framing or a form ordering survives the withdrawal of the rationale that was supposed to justify it, I want to say the rationale was never doing the work and that what we are looking at is a settled institutional preference for one outcome over another, wearing a welfare justification. The second is an evidentiary proposal: nudge units should be required to run mirrored trials, but with the mirror-image arm — the intervention steering in the opposite direction toward the outcome the population currently chooses — and should have to pre-register what result would lead them to take it down. If the field adopted the mirrored trial as its standard of evidence, I expect a lot of the empirical disagreement would evaporate within a few years. My friend's objection was that she could never get ethics approval to steer people the wrong way, which struck me as itself revealing, since it presumes she already knows which way is wrong.

So: is my three-disagreements hypothesis right, or is the real structure something else — for instance, a single verbal dispute about autonomy, used procedurally by one side and substantively by the other? Is the empirical component genuinely the load-bearing one, or is it a proxy that both sides invoke only when it suits them? Do the mirrored trial and the orphaned machinery test actually cut any ice, or do they themselves presuppose a contestable answer about what the neutral baseline is? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress here — a distinction, a change of evidentiary standard, a change of question, a formal result about the structure of the disagreement — and is there anything for which we should expect no progress and be content with that?
