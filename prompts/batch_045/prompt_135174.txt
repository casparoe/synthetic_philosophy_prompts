I run a monthly applied-ethics workshop for a rationalist meetup in my city — usually 18 to 24 people, 90 minutes, a mix that runs from people who have internalized expected-value reasoning and scope-insensitivity talk down to a couple of regulars who think shut up and multiply is a slogan rather than an argument. I want to spend one session on the inefficacy objection: the pull of the thought that my own contribution to a collective harm does nothing, so none of the harm is mine. I have tried to run this twice and it has fallen flat both times. The first time I just described the cases — one more chicken bought, one more tank of gas burned, one more vote in a safe state — and asked what people thought; they produced the expected-difference reply on cue and I could tell that nothing had actually moved. The second time I put the standard replies on a board and argued for them, and I got the eye-rolling I had earned: the people who already agreed were not surprised, and the people who did not had a ready story about why their purchase is relevantly unlike a threshold case. So what I want now is an activity or game where the structure does the work: rules, roles, and small real stakes arranged so that participants make the choice, discover for themselves either that it made no difference or that they cannot tell whether it did, and then find that the reply they were going to give does not fit what just happened to them. No lecture up front, and I would rather they not hear the words inefficacy or overdetermination until the debrief.

Three things have been nagging me about where it should land, and I would like the design to make use of them rather than just gesture at them. First, I have become convinced that the no-difference thought is really three separable thoughts that should be forced apart in the room: that my act did not change the outcome, that I cannot tell whether it did, and that the outcome was overdetermined so it would have happened anyway. Second, a friend who does cooperative game theory pointed out something I cannot stop chewing on — in a threshold game where fifteen people each contribute and only ten are needed, nobody is pivotal in the actual outcome, yet the Shapley value still assigns each of the fifteen one fifteenth of the result, which is either exactly the non-causal share of responsibility I am looking for or nothing but a bookkeeping convention, and I genuinely cannot tell which; if there is a way to make participants feel the attraction of that assignment and also feel the objection to it, I want it. Third, I keep thinking about the channel where my choice changes what someone else chooses — the whisper or ripple round, the version that maps onto voting because you talk to your friends, or onto boycotts because visible non-participation shifts a norm — and I want that staged as its own round rather than mentioned, because I suspect it is where most of the real expected difference lives and it is the part my group is most likely to have never felt.

What I need from you is the whole runnable thing: the rules, the materials (I have index cards, a box, a whiteboard, and about ninety minutes; assume no phones or laptops during play, and assume some participants will compute and some will not, and it should work either way), the number and order of the rounds, and a payoff or consequence that is real but tiny — my own money or my own inconvenience is fine, I do not want anyone paying out of pocket and I would rather avoid actual deception about the mechanism if possible, though tell me honestly if deception is what makes it work and what it costs me in the debrief. I would also like each stage mapped to the piece of the problem it is supposed to produce, a debrief that says what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (I am expecting at least one person to try to compute the exact pivot probability, at least one to refuse to play on principle, and at least one to announce that this is only a game and real cases are different), and a frank note about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to say out loud. Please do not give me a reading list or citations — I have read the standard material, and what I need is something I can run on Saturday.
