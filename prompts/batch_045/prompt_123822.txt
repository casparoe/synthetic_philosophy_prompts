I got into an argument at a meetup last weekend about the trolley-ish case where you can either let one person die or let a large number of people die (not the fat-man one — no one is being used as a means, it's just numbers), and somebody sent me a pop-philosophy article explaining Scanlon's contractualism, which I'd previously only seen quoted in passing on LessWrong. I've since skimmed the Stanford Encyclopedia intro as well, but I still can't tell which parts of the article to trust. Its summary goes roughly like this: morality is the set of rules we'd sign up to if we had to pick them without knowing which person we'd turn out to be, and that ignorance is what makes the test impartial; an act is wrong if some principle that no sensible, well-informed person could object to forbids it, so you basically ask, of each act, whether anyone could reasonably complain, and if they can, it's off the table; and the key consequence — the reason contractualists aren't just utilitarians in disguise — is that you're never allowed to add people's complaints together, because justification has to be to each person one at a time. Which means, the article says, contractualism is committed to the claim that no number of headaches, or broken arms, or ruined afternoons can ever outweigh a single death: the one always wins, no matter how big the pile gets. And since obviously we should save the million, the view is refuted.

My instinct is that the last step is doing a lot of work and that a real defender would say the article has overstated something — something about the reasonable-rejection test being run on the wrong object, or about aggregation only being blocked in certain kinds of comparison — but I can't locate the error myself. Can you go through the summary more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually holds the view would accept, what they'd call a fair compression for a popular audience, and what they'd reject outright? And does the "obviously we should save the million" conclusion follow from the parts that are accurate, or does the article need the misreadings to get there? I also tried to construct a sliding-scale case to stress-test it: start with one person who'd lose a limb versus one person who'd lose a marginally less bad limb, then add a second, third, hundredth, billionth person to the weaker side, each of them suffering a loss just barely less severe than the first person's. If complaints genuinely never add up, the answer has to be identical at one and at a billion, which feels like a sorites the view shouldn't be hostage to.
