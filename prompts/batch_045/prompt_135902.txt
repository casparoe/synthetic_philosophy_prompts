Draft notes (about 700 words)

Openness, not ignorance

1. The contrast. Take the familiar pair. Someone in late 1963 who has no idea who fired the shots can say, felicitously: if Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did. We, now, cannot say that; we have to say: if Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have. Call any account that explains this by appeal to what the speaker takes to be possible an Ignorance Account.

2. Thesis. I want to replace ignorance with openness. Every conditional is anchored to a vantage point, and the question whether the antecedent is true is open at a vantage point just in case that vantage point is not downstream of the event the antecedent describes — not temporally downstream, and not downstream in the sense of already having that event settled for it, as a narrative or a record settles it. Indicative morphology requires that the antecedent be open at the anchor; subjunctive morphology is required when the antecedent is settled-as-false at the anchor (with a complication, see §5). Crucially the anchor need not be the speaker's actual current information state. It can be a vantage point the speaker adopts, as when we re-watch a film whose ending we know and say, perfectly naturally, if he opens that door the dog gets him.

3. Why openness is not ignorance. Ignorance is a property of an information state; openness is a property of where that state sits.
(a) The Sealed File. Compare a 1963 speaker, agnostic about Oswald, with a present-day speaker who has become convinced that the surviving record is unreliable and is, by her own lights, exactly as agnostic. Same evidence, same credences, same ignorance — but only the first can use the indicative. So ignorance does not determine the form.
(b) The Amnesiac Historian. In 2070, after a catastrophic loss of archives, a historian has no idea whether Oswald fired the shots; her evidence is no better than a well-informed 1963 contemporary's. My view predicts that the indicative still sounds wrong to her, because her vantage point is downstream of the shooting: the question is closed for her even though she cannot answer it. This is the prediction I'm least sure about, and I want it tested.
(c) The other direction. When we read a novel for the second time, or re-watch the film, we know how it comes out and the indicative is still fine, because we have adopted a vantage point internal to the story. So knowledge doesn't determine the form either. If neither knowledge nor ignorance tracks the contrast, the Ignorance Accounts are all wrong in the same place.

4. Where the anchor does its work. On a restrictor-style semantics — no conditional connective; the if-clause restricts a modal — the contrast can't be a difference in which connective is used, so it has to be located in the modal base or the ordering source. My proposal is that the if-clause carries an anchor feature alongside its restrictor work, and that the anchor feature constrains which modal base is admissible: one that leaves the antecedent open, or one that treats it as settled. The indicative and subjunctive versions of the Oswald sentence then differ in which worlds the modal quantifies over, and not merely in how those worlds are ranked. That is the part I take to be new; as far as I can tell the standard treatments put the whole contrast into the ordering or into a remoteness feature.

5. The complication. Future subjunctives: if he were to arrive late, we would start without him. The antecedent is wide open, and yet the subjunctive is required. So openness is not the whole story; there is a second parameter, remoteness, and the subjunctive appears when the antecedent is either settled-as-false at the anchor or treated as subjectively remote there. Does the two-parameter view then collapse into an old-fashioned remoteness account? I claim not: remoteness alone cannot handle §3(a), where the two speakers can make identical remoteness judgments and still differ in what they can say, so the anchor parameter is doing independent work. But I am not confident, and this is where I most expect to be told I have reinvented something.

6. What I am not claiming. Nothing about languages that lack the morphology, and nothing about concessive even if conditionals, which I suspect need separate treatment.

Second, test the Amnesiac Historian: tell me what your actual intuition is about the historian's indicative, and whether, if the intuition goes against me, the view is refuted or merely needs the downstream relation redrawn. Third, take a view on §5: does the two-parameter version collapse into a remoteness account, and if not, what distinguishes them? Fourth, if you had to cut this to a single claim worth defending in twenty pages, which one would it be, and what would you delete?
