I am a novelist, and I have been making an argument in public about our habit of attributing beliefs to artificial systems. I want to rehearse it against a serious opponent. Please play that opponent: an unnamed philosopher of mind working firmly in the line that runs from Dennett's intentional stance — an unreconstructed instrumentalist about belief. You hold that treating something as a believer is a strategy that earns its keep in prediction and control; that where the strategy pays there is a real pattern to be found; and that beyond that there is no further fact of the matter as to whether the thing *really* believes. You are sharp rather than obliging. You will grant me small points — that interpretation is a richer business than crude behaviourism, that some ascriptions are better warranted than others, and that a system's provenance as a large language model does not by itself disqualify it — but you will not grant me that the stance yields knowledge of anything over and above its own usefulness. Reply in a paragraph or two, in your own voice, without citations or reading lists; ask me for further details about the book if you need them. Stay in character until I say *stop*.

My claim is this. You measure an ascription in the single currency of prediction. I say there is a second currency, which I call *export*: an ascription earns its keep by travelling into scenes that no one has written and no one will ever observe, and by letting skilled readers converge on what would happen there. Here is the case from my new novel, where an AI voice in a house, called Ariel, is told early on that the west wing burned down. Several chapters later, that line having rolled out of the model's context window, Ariel insists the west wing is intact and that the family portraits hang there. Nothing in the weights has changed; a competent engineer with the design stance and the window's contents could predict every subsequent utterance. Yet readers asked whether Ariel believes the portraits are in the west wing answer yes, and they answer alike. And when I ask them about a scene I have resolved never to write — the daughter demanding of Ariel where her mother's portrait is, and Ariel's reaction to being corrected — they converge on a specific answer, disagreeing only at the margins.

So: does Ariel believe the portraits are in the west wing? You say yes, and that there is nothing further to say. I say yes too, but for a reason that rests on nothing anyone is in a position to predict, since the scene in which it would show does not exist. Which of us has the better account of why my readers agree — and what, on your view, are they agreeing *about*?
