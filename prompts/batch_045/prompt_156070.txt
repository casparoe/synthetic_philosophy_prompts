I'm a PhD student in political theory working on epistemic arguments for democracy, and our department runs a small reading group on market mechanisms in governance. After last week's session, a friend who is far more sympathetic to futarchy than I am walked me through an argument I have been unable to break, even though I think its conclusion is false and — worse — I think the argument proves a great deal more than he wants it to. I would like you to take it apart. Reconstructed as carefully as I can, with W a welfare metric fixed in advance:

(1) A decision procedure is to be assessed, at least so far as outcomes go, by the expected value of the states of affairs it brings about.
(2) For any candidate policy P, what choosing well requires is not the electorate's attitude toward P but the interventional quantity E[W | P is adopted] — a counterfactual causal expectation, not a poll.
(3) Conditional prediction markets are, among mechanisms we know how to build, the most reliable estimator of quantities of exactly this shape: prices aggregate dispersed private information, and the people who move them are disciplined by their own stakes.
(4) Electorates are systematically poor at estimating such quantities — expressive voting, rational ignorance, the fact that no single vote is pivotal — and comparatively good at saying what they want. Hence the division of labor in Hanson's original futarchy proposal: settle W by vote, let conditional markets pick P.
(5) But nothing in (1)–(3) is specific to P. Which welfare metric to adopt is also a question with consequences, and a conditional market can forecast the results of adopting one metric rather than another as readily as it forecasts the results of a tariff. By parity, W too should be handed to a market.
(5′) Nor is anything in (1)–(3) specific to markets. Any better-informed body — a technocratic panel, an expert committee, a forecasting system — satisfies (1)–(3) just as well, so the argument equally favors epistocracy in whatever form happens to estimate best.
(6) And by the same parity, whether to use markets at all is itself a question whose consequences markets can forecast; taken at face value, then, the argument recommends a procedure that selects its own decision rule and its own objective function by forecast.
(7) That is incoherent, or at least unrecognizable as a form of government: a market asked to price W conditional on the futarchical rule being adopted is pricing a world in which its own price is a cause of the outcome, so what it estimates is not the interventional quantity of (2) but a blend of that quantity and traders' beliefs about the rule's own effects.

One of us suggested a patch — decouple the price from the decision: run the conditional market but disclose the prices only after a legislature has voted, so the forecast informs without settling anything. I cannot tell whether that diagnoses the flaw or merely hides it, because either the disclosed price still decides in practice, in which case the decoupling is unstable, or it genuinely does not decide, in which case (3) never supported step (4) at all and only ever supported consulting a market rather than obeying one.

I reject the conclusion, I want to keep (2) and (3), and I would like to keep (4) in some form because I think the expressive-voting point is right. My friend locates the break at (5): the value/belief distinction is doing real work, he says, and (5) quietly treats a constitutive question about what we are aiming at as if it were a predictive question about how to get there. I find that too convenient, and I have two rival suspicions I cannot choose between — that the damage is really done by (1), since a purely outcome-focused premise cannot generate a side constraint that protects the democratic half of the arrangement, and that (3) equivocates between aggregating information about exogenous events and identifying the causal effect of a policy, which are not the same achievement when adoption of P is not randomly assigned.

Please tell me which step fails and why it looked sound; price the alternatives honestly, including the option of biting the bullet on (5), (5′) and (6) and accepting something like market epistocracy; and treat the self-reference worry in (7) separately from the parity worry in (5)–(6), since I suspect they are independent defects and I want to know whether repairing one repairs the other. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis: which step you are least confident in rejecting, and what consideration — a mechanism-design result, a distinction I have collapsed, a case where the line between predicting and deciding breaks down — would lead you to relocate the break.
