I am a software engineer working on backend systems and some formal-methods tooling. I have no formal philosophy background, but I organize a monthly internal seminar for colleagues from engineering and data science who are in the same position. For the next session I need a one-page decision-tree handout on the philosophy of mathematics, specifically the platonism versus nominalism debate. The audience has never studied philosophy, so the instrument must be self-contained and must sort readers by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they recognize.

Please do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. I only need the instrument, its logical-space map, and the scoring or key notes. Work from the stipulations and cases below, plus your own reasoning.

Deliverable: a decision tree (flowchart) that starts from this root commitment, which every reader is assumed to accept:

`I accept that there are objective mathematical truths - for example, that there is no largest prime and that every list has a length - and that their truth does not depend on whether any human has proved them.`

From there, lead the reader to a named position by a sequence of diagnostic branch questions.

Constraints:

1. Use 5 to 6 branch questions. Each branch node must be phrased as a concrete case or a claim that an engineer can answer without already knowing which philosophical label it leads to. Do not name the target position in the question, and do not use textbook formulations such as `do abstract objects exist?` at the branch level - save that for the leaf glosses if needed.

2. At least three of the branch questions must be drawn from software or formal-methods cases rather than from pure metaphysics. I will supply three candidate cases; you may rewrite them for clarity, but you must preserve their software content:

   - Type-checking case: in a dependently typed language, a term is accepted as a `Vector String 3` but rejected as a `Vector String 4` for the same underlying list. Does the type checker feel like it is discovering a constraint that any possible list must already satisfy, or like it is enforcing a rule that the language designers invented?

   - Floating-point case: engineers often say that IEEE-754 doubles are only an approximation to the real numbers, and that rounding error reveals a gap between the implementation and the mathematical structure it models. Does that gap indicate that the real-number structure exists independently and is merely approximated, or only that the specification of the reals is a useful fiction or tool for organizing measurement?

   - Formal-verification case: a team proves that a distributed protocol satisfies a safety property, then optimizes the executable while preserving observable behavior. Is the proof about an abstract protocol (an ideal object) or about the messy running system? Does the answer require the protocol to exist as an abstract object, or can the proof be paraphrased as being about the concrete artifacts and their behavior?

3. The leaves must include at least the following positions, each with a one-sentence gloss in backticks:
   - `Mathematical Platonism`: mathematical objects are abstract, mind-independent entities.
   - `Fictionalism`: mathematical statements are not literally true; mathematics is an indispensable fiction.
   - `Modal Structuralism`: mathematics describes what would be true of any concrete system exemplifying a structure, without committing to abstract objects.
   - `Constructivism/Intuitionism`: mathematical truth is tied to our ability to construct or prove, not to a realm of completed infinities.
   - `Nominalist Paraphrase`: apparent reference to mathematical objects can be paraphrased away in terms of concrete inscriptions, proofs, or programs.
   If you think one of these should be split, or that a position is missing, add or relabel leaves and say why. If you are unsure whether a small model would recognize one of these labels, replace it with a short descriptive label and keep the gloss.

4. The distinctions that matter for my purpose are: whether the position commits us to mind-independent abstract objects; whether it treats ordinary mathematical statements as genuinely true; whether it can explain the applicability of mathematics to engineering and science; and whether it can be stated without a metaphysically loaded use of `exists`. Make sure the tree can separate positions along these lines where separation is possible.

5. Before drawing the tree, lay out the logical space: list the branch questions, the admissible answers to each (yes/no or a small forced choice), and the combinations of answers they allow. Then map the actual positions onto those combinations. Mark any combination that no one actually defends as unoccupied, and give a one-sentence diagnosis: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored?

6. Flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot separate. For each such pair, say what extra question would be needed and whether that extra question could be phrased as one of the engineering cases above.

7. Aim for 6 to 8 leaves in total. The final tree must fit on one page for a 60-minute session, so keep questions short and avoid footnotes. Use backticks for technical terms and gloss them in one sentence. Do not use quotation marks around any passage or position.
