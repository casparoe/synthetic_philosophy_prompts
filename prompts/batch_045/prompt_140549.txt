I've been reading the rationalist blogs since roughly 2011 — LessWrong, then SSC/ACX, plus a couple of Discord spinoffs — and for about a year I've helped run a six-person reading group that grew out of that scene; we meet every other Sunday in the back room of a board-game café and work through one paper or chapter at a time. We spent the last three sessions on panpsychism and the combination problem, and two of our members, Mira and Saul, ended up in an argument that ran well past closing time and that I, as the person nominally moderating, completely failed to settle. I'd like an outside referee, and I'd rather not say which of them I lean toward, because I keep flip-flopping.

Mira's positive view — she calls it subsumptive panpsychism — is that the combination problem is generated by an assumption: that a macro-experience stands to the micro-experiences that ground it as a whole stands to its parts. She thinks that is the wrong relation. A completed sudoku isn't made of the individual cell-values in the way a wall is made of bricks; the cells are constrained by the global solution rather than composing it. On her view micro-experiences are what the world is fundamentally like, and a macro-subject's experience subsumes them without summing them, so the question of how many little subjects add up to one big subject isn't a hard question with an unknown answer — it's a malformed question, and the problem dissolves rather than getting solved. Saul's first reply was that subsumption is a relabeling: she still owes some necessary connection between the micro-phenomenal facts and the macro-phenomenal facts, and it is either brute or mereological in all but name. Mira accepted the first horn — yes, she said, there is a brute necessitation — but argued it is a different brute fact from the one that makes physicalism objectionable: panpsychism never promised to eliminate bruteness, only the emergence of experience from the wholly non-experiential, and like-from-like is a far cheaper posit. Saul then said that if the micro-to-macro link is brute, she has no principled reason to deny a macro-subject to a thermostat or a rock, so she will have to add a further condition — global integration, or causal-informational complexity above some threshold — and once she does, the condition is doing all the explanatory work and the micro-experiences are decorative: she has arrived at a structural or emergence theory with unnecessary metaphysical baggage. Mira took the condition on board and said it isn't decoration but the structural component of her theory, and that the advantage of keeping the micro-experiences is that you then have somewhere for quality to come from. Her framing was that any view on which experience arises from purely structural or relational facts owes an answer to the question of why a given causal role feels like this rather than like that, whereas she can say the qualities were already in the world and the macro-experience inherits its palette from them. Saul answered that she cannot actually say that: if subsumption means the macro-quality is not derived from the micro-qualities, then which micro-quality gets slotted into which causal role is left entirely open, and she has simply relocated the combination problem into the mapping from quality-space to role-space and called it a solution.

They also clashed over a case I raised myself, hoping it would settle things and which instead gave them both more ammunition. Imagine two subjects whose brains are gradually coupled — synapses spliced in one at a time, over months — until the pair is as integrated as a single ordinary human brain; where, I asked, does one subject become two, or two become one? Mira said this is evidence for her: if the number of subjects can be vague or a matter of degree, that is exactly what you would expect if phenomenal unity tracks degrees of integration rather than mereological composition, and the demand for a sharp count is another relic of the composition assumption. Saul said this is where her view is most clearly in trouble, because a subject is supposed to be the one thing that is essentially indivisible — the unity is the whole point — so degree of subjecthood is not a friendly vagueness but a category mistake, and if you accept it you should also accept that an ordinary human is currently some large non-integer number of subjects, which is absurd. Mira's last word, as we were being shown the door, was that the dialectic is symmetric anyway: the physicalist has her own brute facts, so being equal in brute facts means panpsychism's other theoretical advantages should decide the matter. Saul's was that equally brute is not better — especially for a view whose main selling point was supposed to be that it does not need brute emergence.

So: could you go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it? For each move I've attributed to them, say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and give the reason in a sentence or two — not just who wins overall, since I suspect they win different points. And then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing: the consideration that reframes the argument, or the shared background assumption that neither of them should be granting.
