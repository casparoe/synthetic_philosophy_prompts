Next week I'm giving a fifty-minute guest lecture on the underdetermination of theory by evidence in a Science and Society course whose students are mostly second-year engineering and pre-med majors: a semester of statistics and lab methods, no philosophy, and a working assumption that if you collect enough good data the data will tell you which hypothesis is right. Don't explain underdetermination to me; tell me how to make it land in that room.

What I want from you is a plan, not a lecture. First, the order the components have to go in and what depends on what. In particular: can I even state the thesis before they can tell a genuinely rival theory from one theory restated in a different vocabulary, or is that distinction better reached later, as the repair for a confusion they'll have already formed? And should I start from the weak claim (the evidence available now doesn't single out one theory) and strengthen toward the strong one (no evidence could), or the reverse? Second, the one example that has to carry the whole thing, and what it must have and must lack. My current candidate is the curve-fitting case — finitely many data points are fit equally well by infinitely many curves — but I think it may be the wrong thing to build on: it invites "then just gather more points," it is a choice of curve inside a shared framework rather than a choice between theories, and it may make the whole thesis look like a triviality about finite samples. Tell me whether that suspicion is right, what I should use instead, and what the replacement has to do that curve-fitting can't. Third, the misconceptions in the order they will actually arrive, with the point in the sequence where each should be defused — I expect roughly these: "so you're saying anything goes and all theories are equally good," "that's just the holism point about auxiliary hypotheses," "simplicity settles it," "this is really an argument against believing in unobservables," and the slide into a different worry altogether, that the true theory may be one nobody has yet formulated, so that the choice between the rivals on the table was never the real choice. Say which of these I should cut off in advance and which I should let surface so I can work with them, and note any predictable misconception I haven't listed. Fourth, one question I can put to them at the end whose answers would distinguish having got the thesis from having caught a relativist mood. Fifth, what I should explicitly refuse to go into in fifty minutes, and how to say so without making the topic look like a dodge.
