I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a near-future book, and I've painted myself into a corner with a subplot I thought was just set dressing. There's a big foundation in the manuscript whose entire grantmaking rule is that preventing a permanent civilizational collapse trumps everything else, on the grounds that the number of people who could live afterward is astronomical — trillions, maybe wildly more — so even a tiny shift in probability dwarfs any ordinary charity. In one scene a visiting mathematician needles them with the old birth-rank argument: out of all the humans who will ever live, we're sitting in the first sliver of the list, which is exactly what you'd expect if the total is small and almost impossible if the total is enormous, so the fact that we happen to be this early is itself evidence that the story ends soon. My foundation people treat that as a parlor trick and move on. Then, a few chapters later, one of the same characters — a cosmologist by training — argues in all seriousness that the constants of nature look tuned for life, and that this is real evidence for an enormous multiverse, because if there were only one universe it would be astounding to find ourselves in a life-permitting one.

What's driving me nuts is that these feel like the same move run in opposite directions, and I can't tell whether my characters are being inconsistent or whether the two arguments genuinely come apart. I've been poking around and I gather there are named positions here — something like a self-sampling assumption and a self-indication assumption, and that presumptuous philosopher thing — but I can't get clear on which one each character is implicitly running. Can you reconstruct it for me: what has to be true about how you're allowed to reason from the bare fact that you're this observer, at this position, in this kind of world, for each argument to go through? Which assumption does the birth-rank argument strictly need, which does the fine-tuning argument strictly need, and does either one survive if you swap in the other? Is my cosmologist committed to the birth-rank argument whether she likes it or not, or can a person accept one and reject the other without contradicting themselves — and if so, what exactly is doing the work that keeps them apart?

My writing group argued about this for two hours and got nowhere, so be as concrete as you like, I can handle a bit of Bayes. I also want to know which parts of the foundation's position are actually load-bearing: would switching anthropic views change a single grant decision, or does the whole thing rest on something else entirely, like the size of the future being uncertain in a way that washes the anthropic bit out? And if several different views would license both of my characters' practices exactly as I've written them, tell me what extra scene I'd need to write to make them actually clash — which case would split them, and what would each of them say in it?
