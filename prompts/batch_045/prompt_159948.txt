I am giving the last of seven toasts at the retirement dinner of Professor Miriam Kessel, who taught philosophy and, by courtesy, economics at this university for thirty-eight years. She was my doctoral supervisor, and I was her first PhD student; I speak after six other people have already spoken, several of them very well. Roughly half the room are formal social epistemologists and economic theorists who have worked on herding models and will catch every reference; the other half are her family — her ninety-year-old mother, her sister, her two grandchildren — together with a dean who once read one of her papers and understood about a third of it.

The difficulty I am handing you is one I inherited from her. In the standard informational-cascade picture, once the first two or three people have publicly committed themselves, everyone who follows rationally discounts their own private signal and imitates instead; the public chorus then carries no more information than those first voices did, and a seventh voice adds nothing, however sincerely felt. She spent much of her career on this, and on the reputational variant, in which people match the crowd not because they have learned anything but because being wrong by oneself is worse than being wrong together. Her least-read paper extended the point to what she called the unbreakability of praise cascades. In an ordinary cascade, a sufficiently credible dissent can still break it; in a cascade of praise the dissent route is closed, because withholding one's voice, or qualifying it, is not heard as evidence but as cruelty, and almost no one will pay that price at a funeral, a wedding, or a retirement dinner. Praise cascades therefore converge on identical content, and every further encomium is, by her own lights, cheap talk. She added, when I saw her last week, that the toast should not be about her ideas: she did not want to be turned into a theorem at her own party.

I want something that survives her test without betraying what the rest of the room needs. Concretely:

1. It must not be a seventh instance of the same speech. It should not consist in corroborating what six people have already said, and it should not recycle the canonical anecdotes everyone present has now heard twice this evening — the seminar rule that every student write down a verdict before discussion began, the way she always ordered first at department dinners so that no one else's order could contaminate hers, her line about a chorus of ten not being ten times the evidence of one. Those are now, strictly, part of the cascade.
2. It needs at least one genuine private signal: something only I could know, presented as evidence rather than as verdict. The one I have is this — in the spring of 1997 she pressed me hard to take a job I did not want; she was wrong about it; the job went badly; and in the twenty-five years since, she never once quietly rewrote the story so that she had been right, or so that I had simply misunderstood her. I would like the toast built around that.
3. It cannot be pure praise, since pure praise at this point in the evening is, on her own argument, worthless. It has to say something true that is not flattery, and say it so that it lands as love rather than as a roast.
4. It must not imply that the previous six speakers were herding, insincere, or wasting their breath. They knew her; two of them knew her longer than I have.
5. The machinery is to be carried by the story and by one joke that both halves of the room can enjoy — the fact that the programme has placed her first student last, after everyone else, is the obvious candidate — and not by exposition. No asides addressed to the specialists, no definitions, no sentence the grandchildren cannot follow. Her mother should be able to tell, without being told, that the speech is about something she loves about Miriam.

Please write the toast: about seven hundred words, roughly five minutes spoken. Give me the text itself, with at most two or three bracketed delivery notes and no commentary afterwards.
