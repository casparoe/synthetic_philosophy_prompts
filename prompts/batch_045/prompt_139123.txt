Here is the argument. I am in a graduate seminar on agency, and last night I got into a long fight with a friend who is a psychiatry resident. We had gone over the classic Libet setup: subjects flex a finger whenever they feel like it, and the slow negative build-up over motor cortex — the readiness potential — begins several hundred milliseconds before the time subjects report becoming aware of the intention or urge, with the movement itself following a couple hundred milliseconds after that report. My claim was that this is straightforwardly a case of the brain settling the matter before the person is in the picture, and that the consciousness of deciding is therefore a late readout rather than a cause. She said this only shows something about arbitrary, reasonless finger flexes and says nothing about real deliberation; I countered that the later imaging work, where simple choices can apparently be decoded from prefrontal and parietal activity a few seconds before the person reports choosing, closes that gap. I also tried to take out Libet's own escape hatch in advance. Libet thought conscious veto — the ability to suppress the initiated movement in the last fraction of a second — preserved a role for agency. But my argument last night was that a veto is just another decision, so it must have its own neural antecedents, and if antecedent neural activity is what disqualifies a decision as free, then the veto is disqualified in exactly the same way: there is no free lunch, because either every conscious act has neural precursors and none of them are free, or the existence of precursors was never the problem and the whole literature is irrelevant. So I ended up at a practical conclusion I can't shake and also can't really live: that consciously weighing reasons is epiphenomenal, so deliberating about, say, whether to leave my job is theatre, and I might as well let the process run.
