I have a close friend who works as a clinical neuropsychologist on a neurorehabilitation ward, and over the past few months—partly out of interest in her work, partly because I am trying to write a novel set in a clinic much like hers—I have been reading about Moore's paradox, the curious absurdity of a sentence of the form 'p, but I do not believe that p'. To make the issue vivid for my own purposes I have invented a fictional syndrome I call *credal anaesthesia*: patients who, after a particular kind of lesion, continue to track a proposition perfectly in the ordinary ways—they answer questions correctly, take umbrellas when it rains, bet on p rather than against it, and will themselves say 'it is raining' when asked—but who report, calmly and without resistance, that they have no sense of *believing* it. Asked whether they believe it is raining, they say no, and not because they are being contrary, self-deceiving, or sceptical: they describe an absence of any inner feeling of conviction, rather as a blindsight patient has no visual experience while still guessing correctly about objects in the blind field. My question is whether such a character could say something Moorean without absurdity, and what the answer would tell us about where the absurdity really comes from.

Please give me an example of a case of this kind—a concrete, worked-out example of a Moorean-sentenced utterance (or of a Moorean belief) involving such a patient, together with an explanation of the conceptual issue it raises for at least two competing diagnoses of the paradox: the view that the absurdity is a violation of a norm governing assertion (for instance a knowledge norm), and the view that it arises from the nature of belief itself, such as the claim that believing p is constitutively linked to being disposed to treat the question of p's truth as settled, or to one's own believing that one believes p. I want the example to be genuinely awkward rather than a cheap escape: so please do not rely on the 'I can't believe it' reading on which the sentence merely registers amazement, nor on shifting to the third person or the past tense, nor on embedding the sentence under a modal, a conditional, an attitude verb, or a fiction operator, nor on any reading where 'I do not believe that p' simply means that the speaker doubts or rejects p. If you think that no such example can be produced, and that even my invented patient could not sincerely and non-absurdly utter the sentence, please say so and explain precisely why the attempt collapses—and what that failure itself shows about the source of the paradox. In either case, close by drawing out what your example implies for the broader question of whether Moore's paradox is best understood as a fact about the norms of speech, about the metaphysics of belief, or about the limits of self-knowledge. Please write your answer in British English, and feel free to use the vocabulary of contemporary epistemology and philosophy of mind, though I would be grateful if you defined any technical term you rely on.
