I am drafting a position paper on the knowability paradox and need the case for one particular response to it set out as rigorously as possible. Please write that case in British English: first as continuous prose of 700–900 words, then as the structured summary specified below.

Use the following conventions without re-deriving them. Let Kp mean "it is known, by someone at some time, that p", and let ◇ express broadly logical possibility. Assume K is factive (Kp → p) and distributes over conjunction (K(p & q) → Kp & Kq). The derivation I am responding to is: take the unrestricted knowability principle (KP) ∀p(p → ◇Kp); for any truth q let u be the proposition (q & ¬Kq); by (KP), if u is true then ◇Ku; but Ku yields Kq and K¬Kq, and K¬Kq yields ¬Kq by factivity, so Ku is logically impossible and ¬◇Ku; hence u is false, and generalising on q, every truth is known. Do not restate this: assume it is sound and move to what follows from it.

The position I want defended. Say that the derivation is best read as a reductio of the unrestricted principle alone, and that the response worth taking seriously restricts (KP) to epistemically neutral propositions, where p is epistemically neutral — write EN(p) — just in case p's truth value is invariant across any two possible worlds that agree on all non-epistemic facts, i.e. any two worlds differing at most in what is known, by whom, when and of whom. The principle to defend is therefore (KP-EN) ∀p((EN(p) & p) → ◇Kp). Argue that (KP-EN) is the principled rather than the gerrymandered form of the anti-realist thesis, that it survives the derivation, and that it still carries the anti-realist weight, since the contested classes of truths — about the remote past, the unobservable, and other minds — are epistemically neutral. Include one worked illustration showing exactly what (KP-EN) does and does not entitle its defender to say about a single such truth.

Your argument must answer three objections, and must be explicit about costs.

1. Ad hocness. EN is defined by invariance under permutations of the knowledge distribution, i.e. by reference to K itself. Explain why this is nonetheless not a restriction cooked up to dodge one derivation — for instance by showing that the invariance condition does work that a syntactic ban on knowledge-vocabulary cannot do, or that the principle's own explanatory role already excludes the Fitch instances.
2. Self-application. Is (KP-EN) itself epistemically neutral? If it is not, then by its own standard its defender cannot claim to know it. Say which way this goes, why, and what follows in either case; do not simply assert that a generalisation about all propositions is or is not invariant in the relevant sense.
3. The surrogate operator. Grant, for the sake of argument, that evidence-availability counts as a non-epistemic fact, let Wp mean "conclusive evidence for p is available", and note that (q & ¬Wq) is then EN when q is. Running the derivation with W in place of K, and using the bridge premise Kp → Wp, appears to yield that conclusive evidence is available for every epistemically neutral truth. Either show that this derivation fails, or strengthen EN and say precisely how and at what cost, or concede the result and explain why it is not fatal to (KP-EN).
