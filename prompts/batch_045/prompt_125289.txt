Last spring my wife and I took over the day-to-day running of the sixty-hectare mixed farm that her family has worked for four generations — roughly half of it permanent pasture that has not seen a plough in living memory, half of it arable ground in a cereal-and-legume rotation. Within two years we have to decide whether to keep it as it is, to convert most of it to pasture and bring cattle back, or to take part of the arable out of production under a scheme that pays us, in effect, to let the land go back to something like scrub and woodland. At the same time we are deciding what our own household eats. I had assumed the second question was settled: we have eaten almost entirely plant-based for years. Then I started reading about the animals killed by crop cultivation, and I found that I no longer know how to think about either question. I have written the reasoning out as carefully as I can and it lands somewhere I cannot accept.

(1) What matters for what I should do is how much suffering, and how much lost well-being, my choice causes. Species membership matters only as evidence about a creature's capacity to suffer, and the death of a field mouse counts for whatever its remaining life would have been worth to it.

(2) Arable cropping kills field animals, directly through tillage and harvesting machinery and indirectly by stripping cover, food and shelter so that populations are held below what the same hectare could otherwise carry. Take it, conservatively and without asking me to defend exact figures, that the losses run to some tens of small mammals per hectare per year plus very large numbers of insects, and take it that the mammals' lives are on balance worth living to them, so that what a harvest destroys is a remainder of net-positive life rather than the cutting short of a miserable existence.

(3) Permanent pasture is not annually tilled or harvested, and ruminants convert grass that humans cannot digest into food humans can eat. So per unit of food energy that reaches a person, grass-fed beef involves fewer field-animal deaths than an equivalent quantity of grain or pulses from arable land. This holds even granting that cattle need something like an order of magnitude more land, because the deaths attributable to pasture are thinly spread across a land use that kills very few animals per hectare-year.

(4) Hold fixed what I need from my diet: a given number of calories and a given amount of protein. Then choosing grass-fed beef over crops reduces field-animal deaths per calorie, and so reduces total death and suffering, other things being equal.

(5) When one available option causes less suffering than another and the cost to me of taking it is modest — a difference in what is on my plate — morality requires taking it.

(6) But (4) does not stop at a single substitution. If deaths per calorie are what I should minimise, I should take as little as possible from arable land and as much as possible from pasture, and within animal products I should prefer the largest animals, since a steer's crop-deaths are spread over several hundred kilos of meat while broilers are fed on arable grain and must be killed by the dozen for the same quantity of food: beef is better than chicken, and chicken is barely better than lentils. Nor does it stop at my plate. Every hectare I move from arable to pasture replaces a land use that kills many small animals with one that kills few. So I should bring cattle back across the whole farm and stock it at whatever it will carry.

(7) And the argument generalises without changing shape. What holds for my sixty hectares holds for food production generally: the best system keeps the largest possible area under grazed pasture and the smallest possible area under the plough. It also has no room for rewilding. If, as people who worry about wild-animal suffering often argue, the lives of wild animals contain more suffering than enjoyment, then a hectare left to itself is a hectare yielding the maximum suffering per year, and a scheme that pays farmers to stop farming is, on these premises, a device for manufacturing misery on an industrial scale.

(8) I do not believe (7), and I do not think I believe (6). An argument that tells me the humane thing to do with land is to put as much of it as possible under a hoof, that the person eating a grass-fed ribeye does more for animals than the person eating lentils, and that I should oppose letting land return to nature, has proved far too much.

Something, I suspect, goes wrong in the move from (3) to (4), and I suspect too that (5) is doing more work than it appears to — but I cannot name either mistake precisely. Three things I have half-thought and cannot assess: that the argument quietly switches what it is holding fixed, comparing per calorie in one place and per hectare in another, when the two orderings point in opposite directions; that whatever hectare is supposed to respond to my diet is not one of my hectares at all but some marginal hectare at the far end of a price signal, whose alternative use is not cereal cropping, and that real grass-fed systems make hay and silage and reseed their pastures, so the no-harvest premise may be a modelling fiction; and that the bookkeeping may be doubled, counting the lives arable land ends but not the lives pasture prevents, since the steer I eat would not otherwise have existed.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am attached to (1) and (2) — abandoning (2) would mean I have no reason to think about field animals at all, and that feels like a convenience rather than a conclusion — and I would rather keep (5) if I can, since giving it up seems to license ignoring suffering for the sake of a slightly more pleasant dinner. But if keeping them is what commits me to (6) and (7), I want to be told that plainly, and I want the alternatives priced against each other rather than merely listed. Please also say which of my three half-thoughts is doing the real work and which, if any, is a red herring. One constraint: I have to make this decision in two years with the evidence I can actually gather on a farm, so I need a diagnosis that identifies which considerations are decisive and which cannot be settled without a research programme — not one that tells me to wait for better numbers.
