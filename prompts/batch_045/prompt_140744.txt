I am applying for a twelve-month dissertation-writing fellowship and need a three-page proposal for the single chapter I would produce during it. The chapter is on causation by omission, and what I want from you is a proposal rather than a defence of a thesis — I have a half-formed idea and I need it turned into something a committee can evaluate.

Where the idea came from. Last night I argued for close to three hours with a friend — a barrister, no formal philosophy — about whether my failing to water her plants while she was away caused them to die. She held that nothing happened, so there was nothing there to be a cause. I held that if the plants would have lived had I watered them, my not watering is at least a difference-maker, and that this is enough for causation in the sense that matters to her own field. Then we got stuck somewhere I did not expect. She asked at what time my omission occurred. I said there was no such time: I never decided not to water them, I simply never did, and though there is presumably some last moment at which watering would still have saved them, neither of us could say which moment that was, and it seemed to depend on facts about the plants we did not have. Then a second thing came up: if I skipped Monday, watered on Tuesday, and the plants lived, was there ever an omission? She said yes, I omitted on Monday. I found myself saying no — I watered them. Two questions, one about when and one about which, and I have since come to think that the way this topic is normally framed — can an absence be a cause, do omissions have causal powers, can a causal model represent one — has no slot for either.

So here is the hypothesis I want turned into a proposal. An action has its temporal extent fixed by the event itself: the watering lasts as long as the watering lasts. An omission has no event, and so nothing intrinsic fixes when it began, when it ended, or even which omission it is; that is settled from outside, by the practical or normative question in play. If that is right, then three things follow that I want the chapter to test: first, the familiar complaint that omissions lack mechanism or causal efficacy is really a complaint about there being no event to bear temporal and locational properties, and it should be recast in those terms; second, interventionist treatments that represent an omission by setting a variable to a default value have already encoded a chosen window, and so settle by stipulation exactly what is in dispute; third, the distinction between causing and allowing, and the law's habit of requiring a duty before an omission can ground liability, may be tracking this window-sensitivity rather than a difference in causal efficacy — which would give the chapter a deliverable for people who are not metaphysicians.

Please write the proposal to the following specification. Do not cite anything: I will add the references myself, so refer to positions and arguments by description only, never by author or title. Assume the committee is mostly generalist philosophers with maybe one specialist in causation, and that the chapter is about nine thousand words written across nine months of the year, with the rest going to revision. Be honest about scope and about what would sink the project.

Sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths: The question, stated so that it has a determinate answer — about 250 words. Why the existing positions leave it open — about 350 words. What I would actually do, and in what order — about 350 words. What would count as success, and what would count as failure — about 250 words. Why this angle rather than the obvious alternatives — about 200 words.
