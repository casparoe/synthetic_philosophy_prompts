Four things I keep getting stuck on:

The bootstrap. Louise gets the whole thing going by pointing at objects and trading names, building up from there. But the way I read Hofstadter, the lesson of his archeologist-digs-up-a-record thought experiment is precisely that the outer message isn't in the signal and can't be guessed from it — you can stare at the grooves forever. So is the pointing scene an actual solution to that problem, or is it a cheat that smuggles in the shared context it was supposed to earn? If it's a cheat, does it matter? I'd rather say something honest about this than pretend the film solves a problem it quietly assumes away.

The shared-world assumption, as I've been calling it. The larger Hofstadter claim I take from that chapter is that meaning isn't added by a decoder, it's already there as a structural match between whoever made the signal and the world we both live in — meaning is an isomorphism, not a translation. Now: the heptapods don't live in our world in any ordinary sense. Different bodies, different medium, and (on the film's own view) a different experience of time altogether, everything at once rather than one damn thing after another. So if Hofstadter is right, communication with them ought to be impossible in principle, and the fact that the film shows it succeeding either refutes something in GEB or exposes a premise Hofstadter never admits he's making. Which is it? Is "shared-world assumption" fair to him, or is the sharing already built into his account as a feature rather than a gap? This is the bit I'm most excited about and least sure of.

The mistranslation that drives the third act — the cluster of marks that the nervous governments read as a call to arms and Louise reads as an offering, with the physicists and the generals shouting past each other about it. I want to claim that this is not a dispute about the inner message at all, it's a dispute one level down: about what kind of message they've been handed and whether it's a message of that sort to begin with. A frame-message failure rather than a decoding failure. Does that hold up, and is it a better reading than the obvious one about linguistic relativity?

And the editing. The film opens on Louise with her daughter, and we file it as a flashback, and it isn't. My half-formed thought is that the film is doing to us what the heptapod writing does to Louise: it hands us the inner message first and lets us reconstruct the outer message only at the end, so the film's own form is a frame message to the audience. If that's right, is it a vindication — the movie demonstrating, in its structure, that meaning lives in structure — or is it a trick, the film cosplaying an argument it hasn't made?

And then the bit where you tell me the reading doesn't work. Is Arrival a film about meaning at all, or is it a film about grief and choice that happens to be dressed up as linguistics, with the language stuff as set decoration? If you think the GEB lens distorts it, say so plainly, and tell me what I'd be flattening.
