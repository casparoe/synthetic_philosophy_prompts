I'm a magazine journalist working on a long feature about how hospitals decide that someone has died, and I've reached the point in the reporting where everything I read is written by one side for that same side. So before I go back to my interviewees, I want to attempt an adversarial collaboration on paper, with you drafting the joint statement. Two stipulations up front. First, no citations, bibliographies, named authorities, or suggested reading — I have no way to check them from here and I am not assembling a syllabus; I want the argument, attributed to no one. Second, keep the two parties distinct rather than dissolving them into a moderate middle: on one side the neurological camp, which holds that death is the irreversible loss of the critical functions of the whole brain including the brainstem, and that a body meeting those tests is dead rather than merely declared dead; on the other the circulatory-integration camp, which holds that death is the irreversible loss of the organism's capacity to function as an integrated whole, that the brain tests are at best an evidentiary shortcut for that loss, and that a ventilated body with total brain failure still circulates blood, heals wounds, holds its temperature, mounts immune responses, and — in the handful of cases these critics return to, including ones where a body declared brain-dead was supported for weeks and a pregnancy carried to a viable delivery — seems to be doing precisely the sort of integrated work that the word organism is supposed to pick out.

The document I want has three parts. (1) A numbered list of claims both camps could sign: about what kind of thing death is — an event in the world or a determination we make; about the difference between a definition of death and the clinical tests used to determine it, and what follows from keeping them apart; about why circulation and brain function normally fail together and why intensive care allowed them to come apart; about what the law needs from a death rule that medicine does not; and about what would change in practice if either camp simply won. Tag each claim with your own confidence that both sides would actually sign it as you have worded it, and mark any claim you suspect is agreed only because it is worded vaguely. (2) A disagreements table: for each surviving point of difference, the question at issue, then each camp's position in the wording that camp would accept for itself, plus a column saying whether you take the difference to be substantive or merely verbal. (3) The cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — each with the case or piece of evidence both camps would accept as bearing on it, and each camp's prediction about it written down in advance.

Three cruxes I want included, though add your own and tell me if mine are misconceived. The first is what integration means once machines can supply part of it: suppose a device, call it a somatic integrator, that could take a body with total and permanent loss of brain function and maintain indefinitely not merely ventilation and heartbeat but wound healing, thermoregulation, immune function, nutritional homeostasis, growth and puberty in a child, and gestation to term. Is that an organism functioning as a whole, or a collection of organs being kept in working order? I want both camps' answers and an account of what the phrase organism as a whole is doing in each argument. The second is the distinction between permanent and irreversible, pressed with an invented technology: delayed cerebral reperfusion, which could, an hour after a determination of death by neurologic criteria, restore measurable cerebral perfusion in a small share of cases and on rare occasions a few brainstem reflexes, never consciousness. Does that show the tests were only ever establishing permanence rather than irreversibility, and if so does anything in either position break? The third is live rather than hypothetical: in controlled donation after circulatory determination of death, some programs restart circulation in the torso to oxygenate organs before recovery while occluding the vessels to the head so the brain cannot be reperfused, and there is a bitter argument about whether this is compatible with the rule that organ recovery must not itself cause the donor's death — handle it as a crux, not a verdict. I am also deliberately not giving a column to the nearby higher-brain view, on which what matters is irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness; in a note, tell me which of my two parties it agrees with on each crux, since I doubt it lines up the same way twice. Finally, be straight with me about the limits of the exercise: mark which agreed claims are your own reconstruction rather than something either camp has said in public, and where you had to choose between two formulations each of which quietly begs a question. If part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why. And if the whole thing cannot be done — if the camps disagree about what sort of thing death is in a way that leaves no neutral ground for a joint statement to stand on — say that plainly instead of handing me a consensus document neither of my interviewees would sign, and tell me what the piece should run in its place.
