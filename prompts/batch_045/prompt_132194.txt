My partner and I have spent the better part of two years going back and forth about whether to have a child, and a conversation with a close friend last month has left me stuck in a way I cannot get out of. Let me set the ordinary worries aside: we are both in our mid-thirties, we are financially secure, our families would be thrilled, and nobody is arguing that we could not cope. The difficulty is entirely that my friend, who reads a great deal of philosophy and is annoyingly good at arguments, seems to have shown that one whole side of the ledger is empty. The case for having a child was always, for us, something like: there would be a new person, and with any luck she would be glad to be alive, and that would be a good thing. My friend says that this thought has nobody in it. If we have a child who at some point suffers badly — and there is a condition on my side of the family, roughly a one-in-four chance, that would mean living with a great deal of chronic pain — then that suffering is real, it is suffered by someone, and we will have brought it about. But if we never have a child, then there is nobody who misses out: no one sitting in an empty room, deprived of the birthdays and the friendships and everything else we would have hoped for. Her formulation was roughly that the absence of pain is good even when there is no one there to be glad of it, while the absence of enjoyment is bad only if there is somebody who is thereby missing out. So the reasons against add up to something, and the reasons in favour, on her account, add up to nothing at all.

I cannot refute it, and it is doing strange things to me well beyond the decision itself. For one thing, it seems to apply to me: if it is right, my own parents did nothing for me by having me, and there is something confused in my being grateful to them, or in my being glad to exist, which I am, very much. For another, if you follow it out, it seems to say that a world with no people in it at all would be better than a world full of people who are on the whole content to be alive, and I can repeat that sentence but I cannot believe it, which makes me suspect I have simply failed to grasp something. And then there is the part that bothers me most, which is smaller. My friend's own conclusion is that if we want to do something good for a child, we should help a child who already exists: she mentioned fostering, and also funding treatment for a child at the hospital where I used to work as an orderly. That strikes me as obviously decent, and I feel its pull strongly. And yet I have a stubborn sense that there being one more person who is glad to be alive and an existing person's life going better are two different kinds of good, and that the first is not nothing, even though it is good for nobody and bad for nobody if it never happens. When I try to say why, I end up flailing about how the world would be somehow richer with her in it, and my friend points out, reasonably, that a world with no one in it is not poorer for anyone.

So I would like to know whether she is right, and I would rather have the argument than the verdict. Is the structure of her reasoning sound, or is there a point at which it quietly helps itself to something it has not earned? Are the consequences I mentioned — about gratitude, about the empty world — ones I should simply accept as the price of a correct view, or are they the kind of consequence that shows the view has gone wrong somewhere? And is there a coherent way of holding that a contented life's coming into existence is worth something, without merely refusing to take her point? If philosophers have worked on this, I would much rather hear the shape of the disagreement than a list of names and books; I can look those up myself. What I want is to understand what is actually at issue between us.
