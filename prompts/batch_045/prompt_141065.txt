I chair the Academic Council of the Baranagar Institute of Law and Political Thought, a small independent research and teaching institute in Kolkata, and I have ten days to put a recommendation in front of it.

The case: a UK foundation has offered us an endowment sufficient to fund, in perpetuity, a chair in Liberty and the Limits of State Power, plus three doctoral scholarships and a doubling of the library acquisitions budget. The draft deed contains four conditions that matter. First, the chair is to be named for J. S. Mill. Second, the holder must give an annual public lecture expounding the harm principle as Mill stated it. Third, the foundation holds one of five votes on the selection committee. Fourth, the refurbished reading room in our 1901 wing — a building we inherited from the colonial administration — is to be renamed the Mill Reading Room, and the 1901 commemorative plaque to a former Lieutenant-Governor of Bengal, which the Council voted in 2019 to move to the archive but has been unable to remove because the building is heritage-listed, is to remain in place as part of the refurbishment.

The Council is split, roughly evenly. Those against note that Mill spent his entire working life, 1823 to 1858, at India House, rising to head the Examiner's office, and that in 1858 he drafted a petition to Parliament defending the East India Company's administration of India; they note too that in the first chapter of On Liberty the harm principle is stated with a scope restriction — the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others — and that the same chapter remarks that despotic government over people he calls barbarians is legitimate when it is directed at their improvement and actually produces it. Those in favour reply that the qualifier is a throwaway, that the principle as we would now use it is the principle without it, and that the money is real while the symbolism is negotiable.

A dissenting minority has proposed instead that we take the money under a chair named for Nirmalchandra Sen (1889–1942), one of our founding teachers, whose 1931 tract The Civilized Community argued that the qualifier is not decorative at all: it is what does the work of exempting imperial administration from a principle that would otherwise plainly condemn it, and that Mill needed it precisely because of the career he had. The foundation has told us informally that the name is not negotiable but that it would look sympathetically on additional framing — wall texts, a companion lecture, a named reading group — if we propose something concrete.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate, about 900 to 1,200 words, addressed to the Council and written to be voted on. It should open with a one-paragraph bottom line: accept, decline, or accept with specified conditions, and if conditions, what we do if the foundation refuses them. It must contain, integrated rather than as separate headings:

- A recommendation on the naming question that turns on the interpretive disagreement rather than pasting over it: is the qualifier load-bearing, is the paternalist exception in the same chapter separable from the principle or entailed by it, and what follows for whether a chair that teaches the harm principle in a postcolonial democracy can coherently bear Mill's name.
- A concrete and enforceable set of conditions with who does what by when, including what the annual lecturer should be required or permitted to say about the qualifier given that the deed says the principle is to be expounded as Mill stated it, and how we would handle the foundation's vote on the selection committee.
- A separate recommendation on the 1901 plaque, which the Council should treat as the test case for whatever it says about framing.
- An honest statement of the strongest argument on whichever side you do not recommend, in the form a smart opponent on this Council would actually put it — including the version that says conditional acceptance is the mechanism by which institutions launder compromises they would not defend in public.

The memo will be read by colleagues with flatly opposed priors, several of whom know Mill's work well and several of whom know the colonial administrative record well; it should not rest on citations, quotations beyond the one above, or any further research, and it should not resolve anything by appeal to what scholarship on Mill supposedly settles.
