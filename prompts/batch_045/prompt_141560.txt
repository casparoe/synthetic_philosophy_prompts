I'm a clinical psychologist, and I've just finished my second year as the one non-psychiatrist on my country's Diagnostic Standards Committee — the body that decides which categories enter the manual we use for diagnostic coding, referral pathways, and insurance reimbursement. At our next meeting we vote on whether to admit, provisionally, a proposed new category that has come to us from a working group: Chronic Attentional Collapse Syndrome, a persistent pattern of derealization, task-fragmentation, and sensory intolerance following long exposure to high-density digital multitasking environments. I took a philosophy-of-medicine elective in graduate school and have been the committee member who keeps bringing up Ian Hacking's looping effects, so the chair has asked me to circulate a written opinion before the vote, and I'd rather have a properly argued document than my own half-formed instincts.

The case is messier than the usual ones. The label has circulated widely online for about four years before reaching us: there are self-report screening instruments built by an advocacy group, a shared vocabulary that sufferers use to describe their experience, and clinicians in our pilot sites report that referred patients now present a far more uniform picture than they did five years ago — which the working group offers as evidence of descriptive validity. But all of our evidence (symptom clustering, a treatment-response pilot of roughly twelve hundred patients, the prevalence estimate of around six percent of adults) was collected after the label was already in circulation. One of our members calls this the self-confirming validity problem: if the people classified know the description and partly shape themselves to it, then the tidy factor structure we measure may be an artifact of our having published the description rather than a discovery about a kind. Another member replies that this is nothing special — expectancy, labeling, self-fulfilling prophecy — and that even non-psychiatric categories change their presentation once they are described and looked for; on her view it is a difference of degree that cannot outweigh real suffering and real access to services. Two further wrinkles: our rules forbid withholding a diagnosis from a patient, so we cannot damp looping by keeping the label quiet; and our chair has proposed admitting the category while funding a separate surveillance instrument whose items never use the category's own vocabulary, on the theory that this gives us unlooped prevalence data at the five-year review — which a third member says is incoherent, since you cannot measure a moving target with a tool advertised as measuring it. I should say that I lean toward provisional admission with conditions, and that one colleague has told me this is sentimentality dressed as caution, while the advocacy group has written to say that refusal would tell sufferers their suffering is not real.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate, written as a philosopher of medicine who has sat on classification committees would write it — careful about the difference between a category's being real and its being useful, and about how our own classificatory acts may contaminate the evidence we would later cite. Structure it as a memo to my fellow committee members, 850 words maximum, with the recommendation in the first two sentences: pick exactly one of admitting the category provisionally with named conditions and a genuine sunset, admitting it only as a research code with no reimbursement attached, declining it, or deferring eighteen months pending a study you specify. The body should give the reasoning, take seriously the strongest objection to whichever option you recommend rather than staging it, and say plainly whether the blind-surveillance workaround escapes the looping it is meant to escape and whether the nothing-special-here objection is right. Close with two or three sentences of guidance on what clinicians should actually tell a patient when they assign this code. Reason from the concepts; do not cite or recommend any literature.
