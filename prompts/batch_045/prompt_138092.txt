I'm in a small reading group on the platonism/nominalism dispute — we worked through the usual indispensability argument and the access problem, and then one member produced an argument of her own that I'm fairly sure is broken but can't locate the break in. She calls it the no-contact argument, and its key premise is a principle she labels the bite constraint: a belief-forming method is reliable with respect to a domain of facts only if the facts in that domain can bite back, meaning that had the facts been different, the method would at least sometimes, in the long run, have delivered different outputs — a method that returns the same verdicts no matter how the facts stand isn't tracking them, and any agreement between its outputs and the truth would be coincidence. Her chain runs: (1) mathematicians as a community are in fact reliable about arithmetic and analysis — the theorems they accept are true and they don't accept contradictions — and any philosophy of mathematics has to make that non-miraculous; (2) reliability in any domain requires the bite constraint; (3) if platonism is true, mathematical facts concern causally inert, non-spatiotemporal objects, which cannot make any method's outputs differ as they differ, so platonism fails the bite constraint and mathematical knowledge becomes a miracle; (4) if a paraphrase nominalism is true instead — numbers eliminated in favor of plural quantification over concreta, or recast as claims about which concrete structures are possible — then either there are no mathematical facts to track at all, or the facts are modal facts about the concrete world, and reliability about modal facts is itself subject to the bite constraint, which pushes the problem back a level without solving it; (5) so neither platonism nor nominalism can account for mathematical reliability; (6) and the argument doesn't stop with mathematics, since the bite constraint applied in the same way to logic, to modality, and to ethics under non-naturalism entails that we have no a priori knowledge in any of them either; (7) which is absurd.

I'd much rather keep (1) than the bite constraint, and I'd rather keep something like (3) — I do feel the platonist's version of the access problem is harder than the nominalist's — but I can't say why, and I can't tell whether the real damage is in step (2) as stated, in the slide from a collective claim about a practice's long-run output in (1) to a counterfactual claim about a method's outputs in (2), or somewhere I haven't noticed. Tell me which step you reject and why that step looked fine, and then price the alternatives: what a platonist has to pay versus what the various nominalists have to pay, whether a non-causal account of reference-fixing on the platonist side or a conceivability-based account of modal knowledge on the nominalist side actually gets the bite back rather than just renaming the problem, and whether the absurdity of (6) is evidence against (2) or merely evidence that (2) was never meant to govern necessary truths.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — name the step you reject, flatly — then give the reasoning and the pricing; no preamble, and don't restate my question back to me.
