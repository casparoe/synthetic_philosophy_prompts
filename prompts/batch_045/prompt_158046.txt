I'm retired now — I spent about thirty-five years in physical chemistry, mostly NMR and calorimetry on liquids — and in the last couple of years I've gotten into philosophy. I've been sitting in on a graduate reading group on philosophy of mind, and we're several weeks into predictive processing and the free energy principle. I'm the only person in the room who has actually had to compute a partition function, so I keep getting pulled into the technical bits, and I cant tell whether I'm being helpful or just pedantic.

Here's the sticking point. The way our group leader sets it up, the brain minimizes something called variational free energy, and this is supposed to be the same move as a physical system minimizing Helmholtz free energy: you write down an energy, there's a temperature, you can't do the normalization exactly so you work with a bound instead, and the system ends up where that bound is lowest. He then uses that to license a pretty large claim — that any system which holds itself together rather than dissipating must be doing approximate Bayesian inference against a generative model of its surroundings, so that perception, action, learning, even being alive are all one necessary thing instead of a pile of empirical discoveries. Roughly: this isn't a hypothesis you go out and test, it's what has to be true once you've set the problem up.

In my old field the correspondences are genuine and I can write most of them down myself: probability takes the place of energy via the Boltzmann factor, temperature gets set to one and vanishes, the object you can't evaluate is the partition function, the entropy term is the entropy of the approximating distribution, and the bound is only tight when the approximating distribution matches the true one. What I definately can't see is whether any of that does the work he wants.

So:

- Give me the mapping properly, including the slots I don't have: what plays the role of the heat bath, of thermal contact, of equilibrium, of the Hamiltonian.

- Thermodynamic minimization is something physics does to a system: downhill, in a landscape that's fixed before the system does anything, with a real reservoir to dump entropy into. In the brain case the landscape is written in terms of the creature's own model, so it looks to me like the system rolls downhill in a landscape it brought along, and if you're allowed to rewrite the landscape after the fact I don't see what couldn't be described as minimizing something. Is that a real objection to the large claim, or am I overreading a formal resemblance?

- Two members of the group also keep fighting about whether the whole thing is just cybernetics in new vocabulary — a thermostat with a setpoint and a feedback loop, plus an old control-theory result I half remember, that a good regulator has to contain a model of what it regulates. If that's all there is, the Bayesian part looks decorative and the strong claim deflates into "systems that persist resist perturbation," which nobody needed Friston for. If it isn't, I want to know what the extra content actually is.

- Last thing. My own candidate for a better analogy is a stirred tank reactor held at a steady state by a controller, or a buffer that soaks up added acid: driven, open, not at equilibrium, with an internal setpoint. Or in the language I'd have used in the lab — the generative model is like a force field in a molecular mechanics run, and whether the system is "really" descending depends on which force field you handed it. Is either of those closer than the thermodynamics is? And does either one lose the part that made the principle sound deep in the first place?

Be blunt about where the analogy carries weight and where it's mainly borrowing prestige from physics, and tell me which of the disanalogies matter for the big claim and which are just details about how neurons might implement things.
