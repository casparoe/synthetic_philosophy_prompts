i was listening to a philosophy podcast on the drive home yesterday — one of those "one big idea in 25 minutes" ones — and the host summed up Frege's puzzle about identity statements roughly like this:

"Here's the puzzle. 'Hesperus is Hesperus' is totally trivial, while 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' tells you something you'd need astronomy to find out, and yet both sentences, if true, are about one and the same planet and say of it that it is itself. So what a name contributes to a sentence can't just be the object it stands for. Frege's fix was to split meaning in two: the sense, which is a mode of presentation — basically the definite description a competent speaker would use to pick the object out — and the reference, which is the object itself. Identity statements are about objects, but the thought they express is built out of senses, and that's why the two sentences differ in how informative they are. And this is why Kripke's view has to be wrong: if a name is a rigid designator that latches straight onto the object, with no descriptive content in between, then 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' should be exactly as boring as 'Hesperus is Hesperus', and obviously it isn't."

I have no dog in this fight, but that last move felt like he was winning something too cheaply. The rest of it I honestly cant tell — it might be fine, or it might be one of those summaries that's 80 percent right with the 20 percent being exactly where all the live disagreement is.

Also, two cases a friend threw at me when I told him about the episode, which are the bit I keep turning over:

1. Once you've learned that Hesperus is Phosphorus, "Phosphorus is Hesperus" tells you nothing new. But if the informativeness were just a matter of the two names having different senses, both directions should be equally informative. So it looks like whether an identity statement is informative depends on what the hearer already knows — a fact about the hearer, not about the sentence. Does that undermine the Fregean story, or can the story absorb it?
2. Say somebody learns the name "Hesperus" from one astronomer and "Hesperos" from another and doesn't realise they're the same name in different spellings. Then "Hesperus is Hesperos" is informative for them, even though that's really an a=a case rather than an a=b one. Is that a problem for how the puzzle is normally set up, or is it a different phenomenon wearing the same clothes?
