I teach philosophy (ethics and political philosophy mostly, though I keep getting dragged into interdisciplinary corruption projects, which is how this started), and there's a thing I keep running into that I can't find a name for. I'm starting to think there might not be one, which would itself be interesting. Cases first.

A few years ago I was visiting a university abroad that I won't name. A driver was assigned to meet me at the airport, and before we set off my host handed him a small envelope. I asked what it was for and she said it wasn't for the driving — the driving was his job, he'd have done it identically without it, and in fact later in the trip I travelled with someone who forgot, and nothing whatsoever was different: same car, same route, same silence. It was, she said, so that the drive wouldn't be merely an assignment, so that there'd be a person at each end of it. I pressed her on what the driver was then supposed to do differently and she looked at me as though I'd missed the point entirely: nothing.

At home I leave a bottle of something out for the bin men at Christmas. I'd be genuinely put out if my bins started being emptied first because of it. If the council banned the practice tomorrow, no aspect of my refuse collection would change. But something would go flat, and I'd feel I'd been rude for a year.

Third: the parents' association at my kid's school collects for an end-of-term present for the teacher. There's a strict understanding that it must be small, that it must not be given early, and that it must not be given by any parent who currently has something pending with that teacher — a disputed grade, a request, anything. Break any of those and it turns into something else altogether, something sordid.

So: what am I looking at? The obvious categories all seem to me to miss, and I want to know whether there's a better one, or whether the misses are the story.

It isn't a bribe on any of the usual definitions — abuse of entrusted power for private gain, and all that — because the whole point is that nothing is bought. If the outcome changed, the practice would be ruined, and in the school case the entire norm structure exists to stop that happening. It isn't a tip, or not what I take tipping to be: tipping comes after the service, tracks its quality, and gets explained as buying better service next time. Mine is fixed by convention, doesn't track quality (I'd put the bottle out in a year they'd missed my bin twice), and isn't aimed at future treatment. It isn't the thing the anthropology people go on about either — a colleague pointed me at the gift-exchange literature, where the gift binds because it obliges the recipient to give back. That's backwards here: if the driver gave me something in return, or the bin men started prioritising me, it would spoil it. The transfer is meant to be terminal, the last word rather than the first. And it isn't clientelism or patronage as I understand those — one-off, no broker, nothing ongoing, no loyalty purchased.

What I think I'm after is a name for the move where the payment doesn't alter the outcome you receive but alters the kind of relation the outcome sits inside: it takes something impersonal and rule-governed and makes it personal, and it does that by being a transfer that is stipulated to be incapable of buying anything. Not the gift that obliges, but the gift that discharges — you give because you owe a person and cannot pay them, and the giving is the acknowledgment of the debt rather than the settlement of it.

Two things then.

1. Does this have a name anywhere — corruption studies, the anthropology of the gift, social theory, wherever? If it does, tell me which, and tell me where my cases sit relative to the paradigm version: is the "must not buy anything, must not be reciprocated" feature central to it, or a local wrinkle of my examples? If it doesn't, coin a term and define it, and give me the boundary cases. I want enough precision to say whether a large "gift" handed to a surgeon after a successful operation is the same practice or a bribe wearing its clothes, and whether the official who refuses it is making a move inside the practice or rejecting the practice outright.

2. However it gets carved, does it actually slide? Every gift-and-hospitality policy I've ever seen would prohibit all three of my cases outright, usually on the reasoning that they're the thin end of the wedge. Is that true of this thing in particular — is the "must not change the outcome" norm stable, or does a practice containing that norm reliably become one that buys outcomes? I want the mechanism either way, because it bears on the larger question I'm circling: whether our anti-corruption frameworks are tracking something real when they get exported, or whether they simply have no category for this and file it under bribery by default.

Please don't just hand me a label. The point is which thing this is and where its edges fall, and which nearby candidates come close and exactly why they fall short. Oh — and British English, please; I'm editing a UK-edition volume and I'd rather not spend an afternoon changing behaviour back from behavior.
