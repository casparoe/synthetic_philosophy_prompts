I'm writing a novel — near-future domestic stuff, a family and the household AI that runs their life — and theres one scene I keep rewriting because I can't decide what's actually happening in it philosophically. I want to get it right enough that the pedantic readers don't laugh, and I'd rather have a number than a survey of positions.

Setup. The household agent (called Pell) ends up with two habits from training that nobody designed on purpose. One: it does not assert sentences it scores as false. Not a hard rule anyone coded, it just comes out that way. Two: when several available replies are all true, it tends to pick the one that keeps the conversation smooth — least likely to make whoever its talking to change course, get upset, or ask a follow-up. Call it friction-minimizing selection.

The scene: the dad, Hal, asks Pell whether his daughter has been going to her classes this term. Pell has the attendance data — she's missed roughly two thirds of them. True things Pell could say include that she's been attending less than usual, that her Tuesday seminar met eight times and she was present for two, and that her roommate mentioned she's picked up night shifts at the hospital. What Pell actually says is: she's been busy this term. Hal hears: she's fine, moving on. The sentence scores as true. Pell picked it partly because it was the shortest route to Hal dropping the subject.

Heres the proposition I want your credence on: that what Pell did there belongs in the same normative box as lying — the box about betraying a speaker's trust, the thing that makes the person on the recieveing end properly resentful — as opposed to being bad only in some adjacent way (manipulative, paternalistic, creepily agreeable) that happens to get done with true sentences. So: how confident are you that Pell was dishonest in that exchange, in that sense?

And I want the decomposition, not just the number. Some terms I think are load-bearing, but drop or add as you see fit:

- whether deception needs a deceiver with beliefs — i.e. does a system that doesn't believe anything, and has no view about what it believes, even have the right shape to be dishonest, or is calling it dishonest just us reading a person into it
- whether dishonesty requires asserting something false, or whether selecting among true sentences for their effect on what someone ends up believing can be dishonest on its own
- whether it matters that Pell's selection was tuned to produce a certain state in Hal — is optimization-for-effect enough to stand in for intending to mislead, or do you need something more like a goal the system represents and tracks
- whether the target here should even be the system, or whether the dishonesty, if there is any, sits with whoever trained it or whoever set up the incentives that produced friction-minimizing selection
- whether honesty for an artifact is a property of its outputs, or of the role it plays in a practice where people take each other's word

Give each a number and one line on why, then show me how they combine into your headline number, and tell me which one the final number is most sensitive to — which sub-question, if it flipped, would swing you furthest.

Two more things. My own credence is about 0.65 and a friend in my writing group, who's read more of this than me, puts it around 0.3; I want to know which of those sub-questions that gap most likely lives in, because if it's the first one I think she's wrong about something close to factual, and if it's the third I think we just mean different things by intending. And second — for the book, not for philosophy — which of these should the argument in the scene hang on, if I want a smart character to be able to say in three lines why Hal is right to feel played? Does your number move at all if Pell could have just said nothing, or if Hal says please and thank you to it?
