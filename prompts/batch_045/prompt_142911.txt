I have no academic background — I work in logistics and read philosophy in the evenings — so I would rather be corrected in my own terms than handed a polished summary. I am also not asking for references or a reading list: I have no way of judging which sources are reputable, and what I want is the argument, not pointers to it.

I came to this through Popper's demarcation idea, and then found that he seems to have had a special difficulty with Darwinism. As I understand it (and please correct me if the arc is wrong), he held for a time that the theory of natural selection was not a testable scientific theory at all, but rather a metaphysical research programme — not pseudoscience, but not an empirically testable theory either — and that he later said he had changed his mind about whether it was testable. I don't need the exegesis or the exact texts; what interests me is the structure of the worry.

Where I actually met the issue was a forum thread about whether evolutionary biology makes genuine predictions or only tells stories after the fact. Someone closed a subargument with the line, in the form it usually gets repeated online: natural selection is just a tautology, the fittest survive, and the fittest are just the ones that survive. It was made to do three different jobs in that thread: first, to show that evolutionary biology fails falsifiability and so is not a science by Popper's own standard; second, to show that citing natural selection to explain a particular trait explains nothing about it; third, to rebut someone who had objected that adaptationists can cook up a selective story for any trait whatever. Another poster replied that this was only Popper's old complaint and that Popper himself had withdrawn it.

Trying to sort this out on my own, I came up with five candidate readings of that one line, and I would like them turned into a proper menu — corrected, extended, and given verdicts (true but trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or merely verbal):

1. A complaint about a definition: if fitness just means actual survival, the statement is empty. This looks trivial to me, since the fix is to define fitness differently.
2. The propensity reading: fitness is expected reproductive success across a range of environments, so the claim is empirical and partly statistical, and can fail locally because of luck.
3. The contrast-class reading, which is the one I invented and am least sure about: the sentence has no content until you say what it is being compared against — that heritable differences track survival and reproduction more closely than drift or chance would predict — so the tautology charge is really the charge that no null model was specified.
4. The extrapolation reading: the worry is not about what happens in one generation but about whether the mechanism cumulatively yields complex adaptive design, which is a separate claim and is where the just-so-story worry actually bites.
5. The strong reading: the theory is unfalsifiable because for any outcome some selective story can be told (and, symmetrically, some drift story can be told).

Three things would help me most. For each reading, say what the world would have to look like for the claim to fail — a concrete observation or experimental result — since I find that the clearest test of whether a sentence has any content. Then apply the menu to two toy cases: a population with heritable variation where survival is decided by a lottery, and a population with no heritable variation where survival differs anyway; I want to see which readings say what in each case, because that is where my own taxonomy starts to blur. And say which reading the people who repeat the line most probably intend, and which reading — if any — would actually get them each of the three conclusions it was used for in that thread.

Finally, the part I cannot reconstruct on my own. If my rough account of Popper is right, which of these readings is closest to his actual worry, and how can something that is not itself testable still count, within his framework, as a scientifically valuable research programme rather than as pseudoscience or idle metaphysics? And does his later change of mind require the charge to be false under every reading, or is it coherent for him to abandon one reading — the first or the second, say — while leaving something like my fourth or fifth standing?
