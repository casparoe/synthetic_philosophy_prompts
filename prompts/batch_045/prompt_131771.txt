I'm teaching a graduate seminar on laws of nature this term, and the Humean/necessitarian standoff has settled into the familiar rut — one side reciting the best-system account, the other insisting that laws govern — so I want to break it open with a case rather than another round of dialectic about what governing could even mean. The case I have in mind is the intra-Mercurial planet: Urbain Le Verrier predicts Neptune in 1846 from perturbations in the orbit of Uranus, the prediction is confirmed by observation within a very short time and close to the predicted position, and thirteen years later the same man, using the same methods, announces that a residual advance in the perihelion of Mercury — on the order of tens of arcseconds per century, I'll say 43 — must be the work of an unobserved planet or asteroid belt orbiting inside Mercury; he names it Vulcan, and a French amateur's report of a transit that year is taken as confirmation. Decades of searches and claimed sightings follow, none of them reproducible, and the anomaly is finally dissolved rather than solved, when Einstein's general relativity in 1915 derives the residual from the curvature of spacetime near the Sun. I'm supplying the facts I care about so that you don't need to reach for anything, and I'd rather you argue the case out than gesture at a literature I can read myself.

What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions, run twice — once for the best-system Humean and once for the anti-Humean who thinks laws govern. On the case side: what were Le Verrier's actual options when the numbers didn't come out — treat the residual as observational or computational error, tinker with the force law, add unobserved mass — and what does each theory say about how those options ought to have been ranked, and about what he was entitled to expect before anyone pointed a telescope at the region of sky he indicated? On the theory side: does either view get credit for the vindicated inference and the blame for the failed one, given that Neptune and Vulcan had the same author, the same method, the same form of argument, and, at the time they were made, essentially the same epistemic credentials? I have been calling that the differential vindication constraint, and I'd like you to say how much weight it really bears, and against whom.

Two further distinctions I'd like you to employ, because I suspect the debate runs together things that should be kept apart. First, separate what I'll call production from what I'll call reach: a law might be thought to make the events happen, or merely to license expectations and counterfactuals about unobserved regions of the mosaic without making anything happen at all. My suspicion is that this episode is evidence about reach and silent about production, and that reach so understood may be something the Humean can help herself to — but I want that suspicion tested rather than flattered. Second, note that for the Humean the residual is not a violation of a law, since no particular can violate a summary; it is an unsystematized particular, a place where the best system fails to fit the mosaic. So the Humean owes an account of how much slippage between system and mosaic is tolerable before the system stops being best, and I want to know whether this case shows the trade-off to be not merely between simplicity and strength but between those two and something like extendability into new domains — a virtue the standard statement of the view does not, as far as I can tell, include. I have a second suspicion, which is that whatever resource does that work for the Humean here is quietly doing anti-Humean work in disguise; check that too. Where a view is genuinely silent, say so and leave it silent instead of forcing it to speak, and close with a straight verdict on whether this episode confirms the view you favor, is an anomaly for it, or is the sort of case neither view was ever built to handle.
