I'm writing up my position on well-being for a graduate seminar and would like an audit of it before I defend it. Here is roughly where I have landed.

(1) Mental-state hedonism is false, and the experience machine argument works as an objection to it.
(2) Well-being consists in the de re satisfaction of a person's actual desires: what matters is that the desired state of affairs obtain, not that the person believe or experience that it does.
(3) The usual reluctance to plug in is largely a framing artifact, produced by imagining the loss of a life one already has; in the variant where one has been in the machine since birth and is offered the chance to leave, I think one should stay.
(4) There is nonetheless an objective-list residue: being in contact with an independent reality is a constituent of well-being, over and above any contribution it makes to desire satisfaction.
(5) Verdict on a case I call the Effector: you are permanently in the machine, but your simulated actions drive a real robotic surrogate, so you really do write the novels, raise the children and do the work you seem to do; you never experience the world outside as it is, and everything you want comes true. I think this life is impoverished in a way an ordinary life is not.
(6) Verdict on a case I call the Shared World: the other people you meet inside are themselves conscious simulated agents you can genuinely help or harm, and I think the friendships formed there are real friendships, which makes that life much better than a solipsistic machine life.

Go through the set and tell me which commitments actually pull against each other, ranking the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad; for each, say what would have to be given up and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Also identify any pair that merely looks inconsistent and explain why it dissolves. I don't know where the trouble lies, so don't assume in advance which of these I would abandon.
