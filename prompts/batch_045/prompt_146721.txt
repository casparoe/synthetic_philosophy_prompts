I write about physics for a newspaper, and I have been keeping a list of thought experiments with my own verdict on whether each one actually teaches us something about the world, as opposed to making vivid what the theory already said. My verdicts look inconsistent to me and I would like the principle behind them, or the news that some of them have to go.

Galileo imagining two stones of different weights tied together: genuine. It kills a claim about falling bodies without anyone dropping anything. Galileo's ship, with the dripping bottle and the fish in the bowl: genuine as well, since it hands you an empirical claim about what no experiment below decks can detect. Einstein imagining what a light beam would look like to someone running alongside it: genuine, though nothing was observed and the payoff took years. His falling elevator: genuine. Newton's rotating bucket: genuine, because it forces you to treat rotation as a fact independent of what the water rotates relative to, even though it is also the stock example of a thought experiment that seemed to establish something and may not have. Maxwell's demon: genuine, but only because a century later it turned out to be about information rather than about demons.

Schrödinger's cat: not genuine. It dramatizes the measurement problem without adding to it. Hilbert's hotel, with infinitely many rooms and infinitely many arriving guests: not genuine. Nothing about the world follows. Heisenberg's gamma-ray microscope: it teaches something, but I think the wrong thing, since it invites a story about clumsy measurement where the theory says something about indeterminacy. I do not know what to do with that one. A reader sent me an unpublished one I also cannot settle: a lecture hall sealed so perfectly that the lecture and the light in it are absorbed by the walls and come out only as heat. Is there, even in principle, anything left to recover? It feels to me both like it forces a commitment about whether the dynamics is reversible and like it merely asks a question I had already answered.

Galileo's ship and Hilbert's hotel are both scenarios no one can build, and I call one informative and the other empty; the falling elevator and the cat are both imagined observers, and I do the same. So my tentative principle seems to be doing real work somewhere: a thought experiment earns its keep when it forces a trade-off between two commitments you did not know were in tension, and fails when it only stages a consequence already written into the assumptions you walked in with. Test that against my list, repair it where it breaks, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, what it implies for the sealed auditorium, and what it predicts about some case I have not listed.
