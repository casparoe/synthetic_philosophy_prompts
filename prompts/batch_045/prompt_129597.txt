I write historical fiction, and two of my books have been built almost entirely out of documents I found in the census releases. As I understand the arrangement, the law compels every household to answer the census truthfully, on pain of a fine, and declares the individual answers confidential; then, seventy-two years later, the National Archives publishes the original enumeration sheets, name by name, the 1950 schedules having gone online in April 2022, at which point they are indexed, made searchable by name, and sold back to the public by genealogy companies.

A statistician I worked with during a residency in Berlin was baffled by this. In the German tradition, she said, the point of data protection is purpose limitation: information gathered for a statistical purpose is to serve that purpose and then be separated from the record and destroyed, and the Federal Constitutional Court grounded this, in its 1983 census decision, in a right of informational self-determination. On her view, an embargo of fixed length followed by open publication is not a protection at all but a deferral, one that mistakes a limit on use for a delay in disclosure.

I would like to know what the American practice presupposes about what privacy is for. My guess is that it presupposes that the interest protected belongs to living, identifiable people and consists in not being exposed to harm, embarrassment, or official consequence, so that confidentiality can simply lapse once there is no one left whom the exposure can reach. But the clock is keyed to the subject, not to the subject's reach. In one released schedule I found an ancestor of mine enumerated in another family's household under a different surname, next to a child no one in my family has ever mentioned. He had been dead for decades. His grandchildren were not.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of privacy that this practice only makes sense under, and separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Is a harm-to-the-living view the only one that licenses it, or would several do? Is the seventy-two years doing principled work or serving as a proxy, and a poor one? What would have to change — the compulsion to answer, the eventual publication, or only the indexing — if the harm view were replaced by a control view like informational self-determination, extended past death? Does the practice need a view about the standing of descendants, and can it supply one? And is it more honest to describe it as American openness, with confidentiality as a temporary exception, than as privacy protection at all — and is there a coherent third framing, on which being fully legible to the state is a condition of civic trust rather than a loss, that this practice partly occupies already?
