I'm taking a seminar this term on philosophy of action — we've worked through Aristotle on akrasia, Davidson on how weakness of will is even possible, Frankfurt on caring about what you want, and a couple of weeks on hyperbolic discounting and preference reversal across time — and I've discovered that my own verdicts about which cases count as genuine weakness of will don't line up in any way I can state. So I'd rather start from the cases than from the readings. Here is where I land. The paradigm case is obvious: I judge then and there that another drink is a bad idea, and I pour it anyway, knowing what I'm doing and knowing I'm doing what I judge worse. But consider the addicts: one takes the drug while judging that she shouldn't and hating the fact that she wants it — weak-willed, clearly; another has thought it over calmly, endorses the desire, would want to want the drug, and takes it foreseeing that it will kill her — not weak-willed, however bad her decision is, because that is simply what she wants. Now the late-night discounter: at 9pm she firmly resolves to be asleep by midnight, and at 1am there is no deliberation and no conflict at all — she wholeheartedly prefers one more episode, and at 8am she regrets it. I want to call that weak-willed too, even though at the moment of action nothing she currently judges is being overridden. Likewise the dieter who reconsiders at the table and sincerely concludes that one slice is fine, and who would concede later that the reasoning was poor but insists it was genuine at the time: I want to call her weak-willed as well, which means my principle had better not require a synchronic clash between judgment and action. But not the person who opens the fridge on autopilot, where no judgment arises, no preference for the leftovers forms, and the honest report is "I wasn't even thinking": I don't count that, though I can't say why it differs from the discounter.

Then there is the commitment device. Say there's an app — call it Nightgate — that locks your phone at 11pm unless you pay an escalating fee to unlock it. Case (a): she knows it works and refuses to install it, out of a sense that she ought to be able to control herself. I want to call that weak-willed, one level up. Case (b): she installs it and pays the forty dollars nightly while saying she wishes she hadn't. Weak-willed, unmistakably. Case (c): she installs it, pays, doesn't complain, and would do it again tomorrow. Not weak-willed. My seminar leader has a label for (a), but I think (b) and (c) are the harder pair, because the only difference I can point to between them is what she says about it.

The two cases I keep changing my mind on are these. First, the pre-endorsed lapse: on Monday she decides, calmly and for reasons she'd defend, that on Friday night she will deliberately abandon her diet; on Friday she thinks "I shouldn't" and does it anyway, exactly as planned. Second, the pill: suppose there were a drug that made your discounting exponential rather than hyperbolic, so that the late-night temptation simply isn't vivid — she takes it, goes to bed on time, and feels no struggle whatever. Calling her self-controlled seems wrong, since nothing was resisted, but calling her weak-willed is absurd, so I suspect continence and akrasia aren't exhaustive of the space. My tentative principle is this: an agent is weak-willed with respect to φ-ing when she φs against the verdict about φ-ing that she would give, or has already given, under conditions she herself counts as clear-headed, and the explanation of why she φs anyway is the pull of the very prospect that verdict rules out. Please test it against my list, say where it breaks, repair it if it can be repaired, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot keep — especially the late-night discounter versus the willing addict, and Nightgate (b) versus (c), since I don't want to give up either member of those pairs and suspect I'll have to. Then tell me what the repaired principle predicts for cases I haven't listed: a hiring committee that votes to score every application with a rubric and quietly abandons the rubric when a star candidate appears; a student who judges she should be paying attention in seminar and whose mind wanders anyway; and someone whose therapist has told her to stop over-controlling herself, who eats the second slice while part of her still flinches.
