Exam question (answers capped at 800 words, 20 marks):

"State the argument from illusion, and identify precisely what the disjunctivist denies in response to it. Then consider the following variant, which you may call the Morph Argument. Instead of comparing a veridical perception with a hallucination as two separate episodes, imagine a single continuous episode lasting about two seconds in which a subject's experience is gradually transformed from a veridical perception of a red apple into a hallucination as of a red apple, by direct stimulation of her visual cortex that smoothly takes over from the retinal input. The subject is asked, at every moment, whether anything has changed in her experience, and at no point can she tell; and the transformation can be run in either direction, and repeatedly. Does the Morph Argument create a difficulty for disjunctivism that the standard argument from illusion does not? If you think it does, say what the disjunctivist must give up; if you think it does not, say exactly which premise she should reject and what she owes us in return."

Student's answer:

"The argument from illusion begins from cases like the bent stick in water and the Müller-Lyer lines, where something looks a way it is not. The argument's conclusion is that in having an experience the subject is directly aware of something — a sense-datum, or more neutrally, something whose nature can be specified without saying anything about the external object — and hence that the experience has its nature independently of whether it is veridical. The crucial generalising step is the common kind claim: veridical perception, illusion and hallucination are all states of one and the same kind, because what is present to the subject in the good case is the same sort of thing as what is present to the bad case.

The disjunctivist denies the common kind claim. She holds that a veridical perception and a hallucination are not two instances of a single kind; rather, perception is a relational episode in which the object itself is a constituent, while a hallucination is something else with its own seperate nature. It is worth keeping this metaphysical thesis distinct both from naive realism, which is one way of filling in the positive half of the story, and from the epistemological version of disjunctivism, which concerns the kind of reason perceptual experience provides rather than the metaphysics of experience.

The Morph Argument is meant to get round the disjunctivist's standard reply to hallucination arguments, which is that from the fact that two episodes are indiscriminable to the subject nothing follows about whether they are of the same kind. The morph case removes that reply, because here we are not comparing two episodes at all: there is a single continuous episode, and the disjunctivist's distinction between kinds would have to cut it at some instant. But since the transformation is driven entirely by what is going on in her visual cortex, the disjunctivist is committed to denying that the neural cause is the same throughout, and that is simply empirically false: the stimulation takes over smoothly from the retinal input, so the proximal cause is continuous. The morph version therefore does real work that the standard argument from illusion cannot do, and the disjunctivist's position collapses.

One might reply on the disjunctivist's behalf that her claim was never about causes: the shared proximal cause is a cause of the episode, not what makes it the kind of episode it is, and she can allow perfectly continuous brain activity while insisting that the relational fact — whether the apple is really there and partly constitutes the experience — is what differs. But this reply is weak. If the relational fact makes no difference to what the episode is like for the subject, the disjunctivist cannot explain why it should matter to the nature of the experience at all.

It is the epistemological version of disjunctivism that the morph case damages most. On that view, in the good case the subject's reason for believing that there is a red apple on the table is the factive state of seeing that there is one, whereas in the bad case there is merely something indistinguishable which is not a reason of the same kind. But if the reason is factive, then having it must put the subject in a position to tell that it is a reason; a factive state can hardly be one whose obtaining is entirely hidden from the person who is in it. And the morph case shows that no such tell is available, since at no point can the subject say whether she is in the good case or the bad one.

So the Morph Argument strengthens the case against disjunctivism in a way the standard argument from illusion does not, and disjunctivism should be rejected."
