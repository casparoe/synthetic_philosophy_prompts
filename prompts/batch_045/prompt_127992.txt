I'm in the final year of an undergraduate politics degree and this term I'm taking a seminar on democratic erosion. My paper asks whether backsliding names a genuine, identifiable process, or whether it has become a partisan label that losers attach to defeats they dislike. I want to test that question on a film rather than on a country, and I've settled on V for Vendetta (2005, directed by James McTeigue from the Wachowskis' screenplay).

The apparatus the seminar has given me runs roughly as follows: a distinction between backsliding proper, in which incumbents degrade the quality of democracy incrementally and through legal channels while elections continue, and breakdown, meaning a rapid illegal rupture such as a coup or a self-coup; a supply-side story about gatekeeping, about parties ceasing to screen out authoritarians, and about norms of mutual toleration and forbearance doing work that written rules cannot, set against a demand-side story in which mass support for democracy softens first and leaders are chosen precisely because they promise to break things; and an unresolved dispute about measurement, in which expert-coded democracy indices claim to detect erosion in places such as Orbán's Hungary and Poland under Law and Justice, while critics reply that the codings smuggle in liberal assumptions and that the concept is unfalsifiable.

What I need is a reading of the film through that apparatus, together with an honest account of where the apparatus fails on it. The detail I keep circling is that the film's backstory is a manufactured emergency: the party's own security chief released a weaponized virus in staged attacks, the panic carried Norsefire into power, and party officials enriched themselves by selling the cure. That looks like a disguised seizure rather than erosion, which may mean the film is simply the wrong object for my question — and I need to know how much of that I should concede in the paper rather than argue around it.

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. Whether Norsefire's rise, as the film's backstory gives it to us, is backsliding in my seminar's sense, breakdown, or something the distinction fails to capture, and what follows for my thesis if it is the last of these.
2. Which two or three scenes I should build the reading around, described by what happens in them rather than by quoted dialogue, and what each shows about the mechanism by which a nominally democratic order loses its democratic character.
3. Where the film offers a supply-side account, in the institutions, parties, norms and delegated emergency powers, and where a demand-side one, in fear, consent and a public that preferred security, and which of the two it takes more seriously.
4. Who, in the film's own terms, gets to declare that democracy is gone: the electorate, the security service, the courts, the policeman who quietly stops obeying, or the man in the mask, and whether that answer concedes the populist premise that the people are the only legitimate judge, which is exactly what makes the label contested in Hungary, Poland, Turkey and the United States.
5. What the film lets a viewer see about erosion that a country case study or an index score does not, and the point at which I should stop short of that claim.
