I TA a behavioural econ seminar and in section on Tuesday we ran the dollar auction — the one where the dollar goes to the highest bidder but the runner-up pays their bid anyway — and it went exactly the way it always goes, with two students at $4.60 and a room full of people who all knew better. Then we went through the escalation-of-commitment lit, and what actually got stuck in my head was the *rational* escalation defense: maybe the exec who keeps funding the doomed project isn't making a sunk-cost error at all, because quitting is genuinely expensive going forward (the market reads a reversal as an admission you misjudged), because she has private information the outside observer doesn't, because there's option value in staying in, and because people escalate hardest right when they're closest to the finish line. I want to laugh at that position without turning it into a strawman — the joke should be that it's being applied correctly.

So write me a parody of a glossy management-consulting service brochure for a firm (call it something like Continuation Advisory Group) whose entire practice is certifying that a client's refusal to abandon a failing project is, on inspection, the rational call. Deadpan and corporate-cheerful throughout: a headline, practice areas, pricing tiers, one case study, an FAQ, and a disclaimer in tiny type. Never use the word "rationalization," never wink, never let anyone say the quiet part.

The thing I want the whole thing built around is an idea I've been playing with, call it the *second-sunk cost*: the money already spent is gone, yes, but having spent it in public creates a brand-new prospective cost — the credibility you burn if you now reverse — so continuing isn't responding to a sunk cost, it's responding to a live one, and by that logic the escalation has been forward-looking all along. Have the firm productize it: a Second-Sunk Cost Audit that quantifies reputational exposure, a Credible Irrationality Letter (we publicly certify that you will bid to any level, which deters the other party), a Proximity-to-Completion assessment, and — the bit I care most about — a conflicts waiver that lets them sell the identical package to both parties in the same matter. Make the case study dollar-auction-shaped: two bidders, a prize worth a fraction of what's on the table, invoices climbing. Make the firm competent, well-staffed and sincerely helpful, and let a reader who knows this literature see that every argument it deploys is a real one being used exactly as intended, and that where it ends up is still appalling. 700 words max, disclaimer no longer than two sentences.
