I'm a journalist writing a long-form piece on how much weight we should give to what people say about their own minds — the gap between the authority we feel we have over our own mental lives and what that authority is actually worth. For the print edition I want a compact sidebar: a ranked table of ordinary kinds of first-person report, each with a reliability score, so a reader can see at a glance which self-reports to treat as evidence and which to discount. I'd be grateful if you'd build it for me.

Before you score anything, here is the scheme I've been using in my own reporting, because I want your numbers on my scale rather than on some intuitive notion of reliability. Call a report's three-C score the average of:

Concordance — how well the report tracks something measurable independently of the report itself: behaviour, reaction time, physiology, an experimentally manipulated cause.

Corrigibility — how readily the reporter revises it when shown contrary evidence, or when the same matter is put to them again in a differently framed question.

Consistency — how stable the report is across repetitions, framings, and interviewer pressure, counting as a defect the well-documented pattern in which confident probing makes a person more confident without making them more accurate.

Score 0–100, where 100 means "treat as data," 50 means "treat as a weak signal," and 0 means "treat as noise."

The nine report types I want scored are below; they are the kinds of sentence I quote from interviews, not quotations from anything. Reorder them however your tiers require.

1. Concurrent pain: "It's my lower back, and it's a dull ache, not a stab" — said while it hurts.
2. Concurrent perception and attention: "My eyes are on the red square; I haven't actually read the sentence yet."
3. Feeling of knowing: "I know his name, it starts with a B, it's right there."
4. Numeric confidence: "I'd put eighty per cent on that being right" — about an answer just given.
5. Standing belief: "I believe the meeting is at three" — self-ascribed with nothing riding on it.
6. Explanation of a choice just made: "I took this apartment for the light" — in a situation where a manipulated feature such as position or order plausibly drove the outcome.
7. Motive for a past act, recalled weeks later: "I gave because of the cause, not the deduction."
8. Counterfactual self-knowledge: "I know I'd never take money from an employer I despise"; "If that happened to me I'd be devastated for years."
9. Agency: "I did that of my own free will; I could have stopped."

Four things I need from you in addition to the scores.

First, sort the nine into exactly four tiers, with one sentence on what separates each tier from the one below it.

Second, a single number. A source of mine insists that reliability is not really a property of the content of a report but of its lag — the interval between the state reported and the report of it — and that the differences across my list are artefacts of the fact that items 1 and 2 are made in seconds, 6 in minutes, 7 in weeks, while 8 reports no actual state at all. Give me a number between 0 and 1: if every report on the list were made concurrently with the state it reports, what fraction of the present spread between the highest and lowest scores would survive? Defend the number, and say whether the case of item 8 breaks the lag story or confirms it.

Third, name the one item whose three-C score is misleading — where the rubric returns a number that a careful reader should not take at face value — and explain which of my three C's is doing the distorting.

Fourth, if a category is missing that belongs at one extreme or the other, add it as a tenth row with its score.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single claim from this table that my reader most needs — then give the table, then your reasoning. No preamble and no restating of what I've asked for.
