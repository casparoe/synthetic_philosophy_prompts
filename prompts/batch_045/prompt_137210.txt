I sit on a committee that allocates a fixed budget across health and welfare programs, and almost every decision we make reduces to weighing a gain to one group of people against a loss to another. Over a few years I have noticed that my confidence in these comparisons varies enormously from case to case, in a pattern I cannot articulate. I would like to know what rule I am unconsciously applying, whether it survives scrutiny, and what it commits me to in the cases I have not had to decide yet.

To separate the conceptual question from the merely evidential one, suppose we possessed a monitor that, for any person, produces a daily number. Within a single person the number is reliable: whenever it rises, that person is better off by their own lights, and the size of the gap between two of their readings is proportional to the size of the welfare difference between those two days. What the monitor cannot supply is any mapping between one person's numbers and another's. Every person's series has its own zero (the level at which their life is, for them, neither good nor bad) and its own unit (how large a step counts as one step), and both are unknown. I am willing to assume they are not wildly different — no one's zero sits above another person's ceiling, no one's unit is a thousand times anyone else's — but within that broad range I know nothing. Most of my verdicts below would not change if the monitor existed, which makes me suspect my problem is not mainly a lack of data.

Here are the verdicts, given as I actually have them.

1. My sister and I had the same operation a month apart. I am fairly confident that her pain in the week after surgery was worse than the pain I am in now. But I am markedly less confident about this than about the next case, despite having watched her and having no comparable observation of myself.
2. The pain I am in now versus the pain I was in two years ago after a similar injury: I compare these readily, even though my memory of the earlier episode is poorer evidence than my observation of my sister was.
3. A loss of a thousand dollars to a very wealthy person versus a gain of a thousand dollars to someone destitute: I am confident the gain outweighs the loss, and confident in advance of knowing anything about either person's temperament.
4. The grief of a parent who has lost a child versus another person's disappointment at a missed promotion: I am confident the first is by far the greater loss.
5. Two applicants each giving up something modest and broadly similar: I decline to rank the two losses. And I decline in a particular way — I do not want to say they are equal, I want to say that there is no fact of the matter I can act on.
6. A contemplative who has trained himself to want little and reports settled contentment, versus a woman in grinding poverty whose expectations have contracted to fit her circumstances and who also reports contentment. I count the first as genuinely well off. I discount the second. I can state the difference I am leaning on — he chose and endorses his wants, she never had the alternatives in view — but I cannot tell whether that difference is doing real work or only making me comfortable.
7. A very small gain to each of a million people versus a severe loss to ten. I am willing to compare when the ratio is enormous, unwilling when it is merely large, and my confidence does not vary smoothly with the ratio. I also find myself thinking that if the individual gains were small enough, no number of beneficiaries would compensate for the ten — a thought I cannot square with the first half of this sentence.
8. A chimpanzee after a year of solitary confinement, compared with my worst headache: I will rank its condition as worse for it. Compared with my bereavement: I will not rank them.
9. A woman with advanced dementia now, compared with the same woman at forty: I am willing to compare, and I compare her to her earlier self differently from the way I would compare her to a stranger — without being able to say what licenses the difference.
10. The case I go back and forth on: a dying patient who reports a settled peace in her last months, versus someone facing decades of moderate chronic pain. Some days I rank the second as worse off, some days the first, and I cannot identify what tips me.

The rule I have been testing in my head is this: compare one person's gain with another's loss only when every admissible way of placing their two scales on a common footing yields the same verdict; where the admissible placements disagree, withhold judgment and treat the outcomes as incomparable rather than equal.

What I want from you: test that rule against the ten cases, repair it where it gives no verdict at all or the wrong one, and be explicit about whether the notion of an admissible placement can be specified without the answer being smuggled in. Tell me whether the rule is a genuine principle or only a redescription of my hesitations. Where it cannot accommodate a verdict, say which verdict should go and why, and pay particular attention to the pair in case 6 and to the tension between cases 1 and 2. Then state what the repaired principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: comparing our welfare with that of a generation raised under conditions that will shape their expectations in ways we cannot now foresee, and comparing the welfare of a human being with that of a system that emits distress signals and has no biology we recognize. Finally, tell me whether the monitor, if we had it, would change any of this.

If no single principle covers the pattern, give me the smallest set of candidates and say what further case would decide between them. Please argue it out rather than pointing me toward a literature, and please answer in American English.
