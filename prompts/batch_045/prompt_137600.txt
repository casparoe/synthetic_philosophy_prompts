I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist who's started sitting in on a philosophy of computation reading group, and last night two of the regulars went at it over whether the Church–Turing thesis is even the kind of claim that could turn out to be false, and I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning.

The gadget they were arguing about was hypothetical: call it the Box. You key in an index n, it hums for about a second, and displays 1 if the nth Turing machine halts and 0 if it doesn't. It's warrantied for roughly ten thousand queries before whatever's inside decoheres, and in trials so far every 1 has been confirmed by just running the machine, and every 0 we've been able check by hand has checked out.

(1) Pete: the whole dispute is a category mistake. "Effectively computable" was an informal notion; identifying it with Turing computability was an act of conceptual analysis that we've since absorbed as a definition, and definitions don't get confirmed or refuted. That's why nobody moves — three quite different claims (about our concept of an algorithm, about what physical hardware can do, about what a human can do with pencil and paper) are travelling under one name.

(2) Nadia: too fast. The thesis gets used as a real constraint on the world — people mean that no device in this universe decides the halting problem. If the Box kept handing us answers we can verify, on inputs where our simulations just churn, we'd be in the same evidential position we're in with any well-confirmed instrument, and calling the thesis a definition is just a way of insulating it from evidence.

(3) Pete: but no finite run of trials could ever discriminate, because for any finite batch of input–output pairs there's a finite lookup table — and every finite function is trivially computable — so no possible observation could tell the Box apart from something merely computable. A claim no observation could bear on isn't an empirical claim.

(4) Nadia: that's just underdetermination, which every scientific hypothesis suffers from; we extrapolate from finite data all the time, and what makes a claim testable isn't that data could settle it but that there's something to check — here, its prediction for the next input, some of which we can verify.
