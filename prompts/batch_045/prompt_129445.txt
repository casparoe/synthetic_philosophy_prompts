hey — I'm in a philosophy of biology reading group (four grad students and one faculty member who mostly just makes harrumphing noises) and we've completely stalled on the function debate. Half of us are sympathetic to the etiological / selected-effects account: a trait's function is the effect it was selected for, the thing about it that explains why it's there. The other half like the causal-role / dispositional account: a trait's function is what it actually contributes right now to some larger capacity of the system it sits in, the way a Cummins-style analysis of, say, the circulatory system assigns the heart the job of pumping. We burned a whole session on the same tired chestnuts — hearts pump blood vs. make thumping noises, the very first heart in the very first ancestor, the heart that's currently failing — and nobody moved. So what I want instead is a dialogue, maybe 1200–1600 words, between two people who actually know this literature, Priya (team selected-effects) and Marcus (team causal-role), who are both sharp and neither of whom is a strawman. Set them up at a marine field station, late, coffee gone cold, a dissecting scope and a tank of specimens between them. Don't bother with exegetical fidelity to Millikan or Wright or Cummins or whoever, and no footnotes or citations — I just want the positions, glossed clearly enough that someone who hasn't read the literature can follow.

Here's the case I want them to grind against, which I made up, so run with it rather than trying to look it up: a colonial hydrozoan I'll call the lantern raft. It has a stiffened ridge along its swimming bell, the keel, which comparative and developmental work suggests first showed up as a pure side effect of a change in bell curvature, and only later got recruited: variants with a stiffer keel outswam the others, so the keel has a real selection history for making the bell's contraction more efficient. Then the lineage shifted to a sit-and-wait drift strategy, stopped pulsing almost entirely, and now the keel's stiffening does basically nothing for fitness. What the keel does now is shed a low-pressure vortex that pulls copepods toward the feeding polyps — a genuinely new effect, maybe a few thousand generations old, with selection on it still weak and noisy. Meanwhile a neighbouring bit of tissue, the frill — historically selected for brooding larvae, which the colony no longer does externally — happens to generate an even better vortex than the keel, and is the structure actually under selection right now for prey funneling. And in some colonies the frill is developmentally absent and the keel handles the funneling fine on its own, so at the colony level the job is sometimes done twice over. So: whose function is funneling prey? The keel's or the frill's? Does the keel still have its old function, and if not, when did it lose it — a sorites across generations? Does the frill have to accumulate a certain amount of selection history before funneling becomes its function, and who gets to decide how much? Does the etiologist have to say something awkward about a trait whose selected-for effect has gone obsolete, and does the causal-role theorist have to say something awkward about the fact that the keel also casts a shadow, makes a click, and sheds epibionts, all of which are perfectly real contributions to some system or other?

Please have them actually answer each other rather than delivering speeches — Priya should concede something real (I'm imagining her retreating from a pure origins story to something like a recent/maintaining-selection view, and then having to admit that "recent" means picking a timescale, which looks a lot like letting the interests of the investigator back in) and Marcus should concede something real too (that if any contribution to any systematization we care to write down counts, then the word "function" is doing no work at all, and that the choice of which capacity to analyze — feeding? locomotion? buoyancy? — seems to be guided by what the part is *for*, which is history sneaking back in the side door). Somewhere past the midpoint, give them a specimen where the keel is malformed and does nothing at all: neither stiffening nor funneling, colony surviving on the frill. Is that keel malfunctioning, or just an inert lump? Make that hurt for Marcus. And don't let them hug it out at the end — I'd rather they land on a sharp, honest statement of what they still disagree about, and then take one more beat to ask whether that residual disagreement is about what functions are or about which explanatory project each of them thinks biology is in the business of, and whether that question has an answer.
