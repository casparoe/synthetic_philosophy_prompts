I am a novelist who teaches a single upper-level epistemology seminar each spring, and I like to set my exam questions on cases drawn from whatever book I am drafting. Please write me one closed-book essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks.

The material, from my current manuscript: two translators of the same damaged text — equal training, equal professional standing, comparable published records on disputed readings — disagree about whether one decisive passage is meant ironically. My narrator has lived with this author's idiom for two decades, has read everything her colleague has read, and predicted in advance both that her colleague would dissent and on what grounds: she holds that the colleague's method systematically over-reads irony, and so had already discounted the coming disagreement before she heard it. A further fact, revealed late in the book: the narrator's record of correct readings was accumulated almost entirely among colleagues who shared her interpretive temperament, so it has rarely been tested against systematic dissent.

I want the question to force students onto four things: whether pre-empting and explaining away a disagreement insulates you from the conciliatory demand or merely relocates it; whether that demand is a demand about credence, about outright belief, or about both, and what hangs on the difference; whether conciliationism can say who counts as a peer without either begging the question against steadfastness or swelling into a general demand for modesty that would apply to first-order reasoning too, and so turn against itself; and whether the provenance of a track record — that it was built inside an agreeing community — should discount the record itself when peerhood is being assessed.

The question should be self-contained, since no student will have read the manuscript, and it must be unanswerable by rehearsing the definitions of conciliationism and steadfastness or by reciting the standard thought experiments. Please build in at least one move that cannot be produced from memory: for instance, ask what a conciliatory principle would have to look like for the narrator's pre-emption to count as a reason for revising rather than against it, or ask how her record should be reweighted once its provenance is disclosed and what the reweighting does to the claim that her colleague is a peer.

Format the marking scheme as five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, totalling 20, followed by one line per point on the way candidates characteristically get it wrong — the specific move that costs them the mark rather than a general note on vagueness.
