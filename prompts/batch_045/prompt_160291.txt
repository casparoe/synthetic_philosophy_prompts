Hey — I write novels, not philosophy papers, so apoligies in advance if I mangle the vocabulary. I'm maybe two hundred pages into a book about a country coming apart, and I've hit the part where two characters have to sit in a room and argue about whether the breakaway region is *entitled* to go. I can't fake that argument. So what I want from you is the spec, not the verdict.

Write me the desiderata: what a theory of when secession is legitimate — as opposed to merely successful — has to deliver in order to be worth taking seriously. And fix the purpose for me, since I gather adequacy is relative to one: I need a theory good enough that (i) I can tell where it lands on the cases in my book without the outcome feeling arbitrary, and (ii) a smart character can say it out loud in a negotiation scene and a reader who has never opened a political theory book still feels its force. Please don't give me the actual constitutional law of Spain, the UK or Canada, and no citations or reading lists — my country is made up and I'm not writing about those places anyway.

The cases I need it to get right, with the reason each one is a test:

The real ones, which I know roughly and you don't need to research — Scotland, where the union is old, the secessionists wanted to keep some shared institutions and the vote was negotiated and lost; Catalonia, where the rump state's constitution says the country is indivisible, the vote was held anyway and called illegal, and money flowing out of the region is part of the grievance; Quebec, where the secession is peaceful and democratic and the region already has a lot of self-government, but leaving would cut the remaining country geographically in half. Plus the nested version: a smaller people inside the seceding region saying that if the region leaves, they and their land leave with it. And the case where a region's complaint is basically fiscal — we're richer, we're tired of transferring, we want out.

The invented ones, which are the ones I actually care about:
- The territory that wants to leave is not contiguous. It's a river valley, a port city, and a strip of highway connecting them that runs through districts that vote to stay. Is that still secession? Does contiguity matter, and if it does, why — what is the theory even about, land or people?
- The referendum passes 51–49, and one district inside the region votes 70% to remain in the rump state. It wants its own mini-counter-secession. Any border you draw seems to manufacture the problem again one scale down.
- The rump state's objection is not justice but survival: it needs the port and it needs the water that comes off the upstream dams in the seceding territory. It says it cannot function without them.
- One of the seceding groups is mostly descended from people who arrived two generations ago. They've been there long enough to have a distinct accent and a distinct grievance. Is that a "people" yet, and who says?

The distinctions I don't want collapsed: a right to self-determination versus a right to secede (maybe the first is satisfiable by autonomy and the second never follows from it); a moral right to secede versus a legal one versus a third country's right to recognize; secession versus dissolving the whole state versus a province joining a neighbouring country versus just moving a border; legitimacy of seceding versus what the rump state may do to resist it (surely a state can oppose a legitimate secession in an illegitimate way). And tell me what a theory has to say about who gets to vote — residents, people born there, emigrés, recent arrivals, the people in the corridor — because that looks like the question the whole thing sits on.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just prejudices of one camp. Some things I suspect are camp preferences dressed up as requirements: that secession can only ever be a remedy for injustice; that the consent of a democratic majority settles it; that existing states get a presumption in favour of their borders; that a group has no claim unless it already has a national identity; that a theory must be able to handle a purely economic grievance as legitimate, or must be able to rule it out. Tell me which of those are load-bearing and which are just what that camp wanted to conclude anyway.

The part I most want: flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. I have a feeling at least two of them are in tension — something like "a clear majority in the region settles it" against "no border change may create a new permanent minority," and something like "legitimacy must be knowable in advance by the people deciding" against "legitimacy depends on how viable the new state turns out to be," which nobody knows until after. If the honest conclusion is that no theory can have all of this at once, say so plainly, because that's genuinely useful to me: it means the scene can't be won on principle and I should write it differently.

Also: mark any criterion that an ordinary reader would feel in their gut — the ones my characters will actually say out loud, like you can't just take the port with you. Those matter more to me than the elegant ones.

And tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling theories out — which one knocks out the most candidates at a stroke.

At the end, a very short scorecard, three sentences each max, for the three or four big families I keep half-remembering: remedial-right-only, plebiscitary or primary-right theories, nation-or-people-based ones, and whatever the aggregative consequentialist version is called. Keep the weight on the criteria, though — that's the part I'm going to actually use.
