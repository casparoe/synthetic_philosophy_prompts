I want a number with its derivation, not a survey of what has been argued about this before; please don't spend the answer on who has defended what. I'll set the case up from scratch.

**The case.** Take welfare to be measurable on a scale on which 0 is the neutral level — a life neither worth living nor not worth living — 100 is a flourishing human life, and 1 is a drab, marginal life that is still, barely, worth living. Consider outcome A₀: 1,000 people, all at level 100. Now generate a chain of outcomes A₀, A₁, …, A₄₆₀ by a single repeated operation: each step raises the population by 2%, adding the new people at the level currently prevailing, and lowers every person's level by 1% of what it was. So A₁ is 1,020 people at 99, A₂ roughly 1,040 at 98.01, and so on. After 460 steps the population is roughly 9 million and the common level is roughly 1: A₄₆₀ is about 9 million lives that are barely worth living. Total welfare rises at every step by just under 1%, ending at roughly 89 times its starting value; average welfare falls by 1% at every step. (The decrement is multiplicative deliberately. An additive one would eventually push lives below 0, and the chain would break for an uninteresting reason. And because 1% of a small number is smaller still, the intuition that one step makes no difference worth mentioning is at its strongest near the end of the chain, exactly where the outcome has become drab.)

Each individual step looks like an improvement, or at least not a worsening: total welfare up, additional lives that are worth living, and a loss to the existing people too slight to register. But A₄₆₀ looks clearly worse than A₀ — nine million marginal lives against a thousand flourishing ones. Note that if you accept the step judgments and the endpoint verdict together you get a genuine three-term cycle: A₁ better than A₀, A₄₆₀ better than A₁, A₀ better than A₄₆₀.

**The proposition.** Give me your credence that the relation all-things-considered better than, as it applies to comparisons of total outcomes such as these, is intransitive: that there are outcomes X, Y, Z with X better than Y, Y better than Z, and Z better than X. Read it as a claim about the relation, not about what any body of work will eventually conclude. State explicitly how you are reading it — as a claim about the correct analysis of the concept, or about the structure of the facts of value — and if you think the question as posed has no determinate answer, say so, then give the number conditional on its having one together with your credence that it does.
