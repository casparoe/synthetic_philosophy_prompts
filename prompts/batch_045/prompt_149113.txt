After three hours of arguing with a friend last night that ended in something closer to a draw than a resolution, I want an outside referee on the underlying agenda question rather than on who won.

I am starting a doctorate with roughly three years, a strong formal background (modal logic, a little model theory), and no track record in metaphysics, and I want to spend those years on the epistemology of modality: specifically, on whether and when conceivability is a guide to possibility. My friend's position is that the subject has been working the same small set of problems since Kripke — the gap between prima facie and ideal conceivability, what a posteriori necessities do to the inference from conceivability to possibility, how much weight to give scenarios we can describe in detail but never check for global consistency — and that a newcomer would be signing up for taxonomy. My position is that the standard framing has a blind spot I cannot find stated anywhere, and I would like to know whether it is real before I stake three years on it.

The blind spot, as I would put it: call it cheap coherence. When someone reports that a scenario is conceivable, the evidence on offer is rarely a positive imaginative experience; it is a negative result, namely that no contradiction turned up after however much reflection they were willing to do. Producing a description that reads as coherent is much easier than confirming that it is globally consistent, and for rich scenarios that confirmation may not be mechanically settleable at all. If so, the epistemic work is done by the robustness of a search for incoherence, not by the vividness of an imagining, and the questions worth asking change shape: which search procedure, how much of it counts as enough, why the failure of one person's search should move anyone else, and what becomes of arguments that lean on the inconceivability of the opposite when all that has really been shown is that nobody has yet found a contradiction. There is a connected asymmetry I also cannot get out of my head: inconceivability claims are generally treated as weaker evidence than conceivability claims, and I cannot tell whether that is principled or merely sociological.

So: for someone in my position, aiming at work the rest of the field would have to answer rather than at a tidy contribution to a sub-literature, which two or three of these questions are worth three years, and in what order? I want the ordering defended rather than listed — what is upstream of what, which are tractable with logic rather than with training I do not have, which are neglected relative to their payoff, where importance and tractability trade off against each other, and what kind of argument or result would move an item up or down the list. And I would like a candid verdict on my own idea: is cheap coherence a genuine open problem, or does it quietly collapse into something the standard framing already handles?
