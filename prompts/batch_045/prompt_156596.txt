I serve on the editorial board of my school's arts magazine, and we keep colliding with the same argument: whether a work can be good art when something about it, or about the person who made it, is morally rotten. I've stopped polling the group and started asking one friend, because she is the only person I know whose answers line up with each other. Here are her calls so far.

1. A propaganda film — genuinely inventive camera work, real formal control — whose entire emotional design is to make the audience feel triumphant contempt for a named minority, and which would lose its power completely if the audience did not feel that contempt. She says: bad art, and worse art for being as effective as it is.

2. A novel everyone agrees is a masterpiece, containing one casually contemptuous sentence about women in chapter nine that does no work for plot, character, or style. She says: still a great novel, but the book is worse for that sentence, and she means aesthetically worse, not merely morally worse — a small defect inside a large achievement.

3. A war film that sincerely sets out to condemn torture but stages its torture scenes with such rhythm and luxury that audiences cheer, with the film's whole emotional climax resting on that charge. She says: flawed as art, not just as morals — it defeats its own aim and ends up dishonest with itself.

4. A singer-songwriter whose albums are about grief and forgiveness; decades after they were written he was convicted of abusing several people close to him, and nothing in the songs registers any of it. She says: still beautiful, still great songs; what he did is monstrous; she will not stream them or help give him a platform; but she will not pretend they got worse as music. Whether a work is good and what we ought to do with it are, for her, separate questions.

5. A memoir that is formally unremarkable but whose clear-eyed honesty about the writer's own cruelty is the entire reason it is worth reading. She says: good art, and that honesty is part of why — the moral quality there is inside the achievement, not next to it.

Two rules I have tried on her both break immediately. The rule that moral defects never bear on artistic value dies on 1 and 3. The rule that any moral defect in the work or in its maker drags the work down dies on 2 and 4. So she is running something narrower than either, and I cannot find its exact shape.

The case in front of us now is this. A photographer spent three years in a town after a flood, telling people he was a relief volunteer; he was not, and he was there to make a book. He did not misrepresent them in the pictures themselves: the book is intimate, formally superb, and true to what their lives were like, and nothing in the images hints that he lied to get in. But the intimacy is exactly what makes the photographs great, and the intimacy is exactly what he took without asking. Had he asked honestly they would have refused, and there would have been no book. Since publication, many people in the town have said they are glad the book exists and that it is the truest thing anyone has made about them, while several of the people photographed have said they would never have consented.

What does she say — is it good art? I want the prediction, but I would rather have the machinery: what rule is she actually running, what other rules fit cases 1 through 5 just as well and disagree here, and which single further case would separate those rules best. If her track record genuinely underdetermines this one, tell me what she would be committing to with each verdict. There is also one piece of the case I cannot place: does the town's gladness, or the fact that they would have refused, even belong in the question she is asking, or is it noise relative to how she has been deciding?

And a secondary question, which I know is not the same question: the board has to vote on whether to run an excerpt and a review. I would like to know whether the view she is committed to pushes her toward an answer there, or whether it leaves that entirely open.
