I work as a consultant helping large technical organizations write down, in a form that can actually be implemented, the decision procedures they are already using informally. For the past couple of years I have been stuck on one client: a standards body with a few thousand eligible participants, heavy turnover, and roughly two hundred people turning up to any given vote, deciding repeatedly over a small set of recurring options that get re-described slightly on each pass (so the 2019 "Option B" is arguably the same thing as the 2023 "Option D-prime", which is part of why this is hard). They have no written rule, only a ten-year archive of minutes. I tried to reconstruct the aggregation from the archive, and the reconstruction appears to have four features that I am fairly confident are not supposed to coexist: it is anonymous, in that permuting the participants never changes the outcome; it respects unanimity, in that if every participant ranks x above y then the group does too; the group's ranking of x against y depends only on the participants' rankings of x against y — I tested for spoiler effects by finding several occasions where the same pair appeared on ballots with different third options, and the ranking never flipped; and the reconstructed group ordering is transitive, with no cycle anywhere in ten years of records.

The part I cannot account for is this. As I learned it, Arrow's result says that these four features, with more than one participant and more than two options, force a dictator — some individual whose preference between any pair is the group's preference. I cannot find one. For every individual I test, there is a decision in the archive where the group went the other way. Nor is there a small fixed group doing it: the sets of people on the winning side vary from question to question, and the intersection of all the winning sets across every decision I have is empty. So I went looking for structure in the winning sets instead, and here is the pattern I think I see, which is the thing I want a name for. Call a set of participants *sufficient* if, whenever the members of that set all agree on how to rank some pair and the people outside it disagree in any way at all, the group's ranking is theirs. Then, as far as I can tell from the minutes: any superset of a sufficient set is sufficient; the intersection of any two sufficient sets is itself sufficient; and, most surprisingly, for every set of participants you care to name, either it or its complement is sufficient. Nobody wrote any of this down. No one at the organization can point to a person or a subcommittee whose judgment is doing the work. The procedure simply behaves as though somebody were deciding, without there being anybody.

So, two things. First: does this configuration have an established name, and what are its exact preconditions — in particular, does it require an infinitely large electorate? This body is finite at any given moment, though open-ended and growing, and I suspect that distinction is where my puzzle either dissolves or gets interesting; if the finite case forces one of my four features to fail, tell me which one and describe the nearest configurations that do survive. Second, and more importantly: I do not want a label, and I do not want a reading list, since I have no access to a library and cannot chase down references. I want the carving. What exactly is this phenomenon, and what is it not — how does it differ from an ordinary dictatorship, from an oligarchy or a fixed decisive minority, from an arrangement where someone merely has veto power, and from a rule that avoids cycles only because the awkward pairs never happen to come to a vote? Where are its edges, and what would count as a case that merely looks like it? If nothing established fits, say so and give me a term with a definition and the boundary cases that fix it. A colleague thinks I am reading noise in a small archive; if that is the right diagnosis, tell me what she would have to show to establish it. Please answer in American English.
