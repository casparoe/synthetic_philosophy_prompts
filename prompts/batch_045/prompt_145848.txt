I am a novelist, and I am revising a manuscript whose central character is a painter who spends four years reconstructing, in period materials and from a surviving workshop drawing, a canvas that is catalogued in a 1650s inventory and has since been lost. Before I let my characters argue about what she has actually made, I should like to know how much of the philosophy they quote is philosophy and how much is the essayist's own gloss.

At the research stage I read a long essay on fakes and copies — I believe it was the lead piece in a museum exhibition catalogue, though I no longer have the book — and copied the relevant passage into a notebook. As I wrote it down, it ran as follows:

"Philosophers settled the puzzle of fakes decades ago, and the settlement is deflationary. It begins with Nelson Goodman's distinction, in Languages of Art, between autographic and allographic arts. Painting is autographic: because there is no notation that settles which objects count as instances of a painting, the identity of a painting hangs on its history of production, so a copy of a Rembrandt is not the same work as the Rembrandt even if it matches it stroke for stroke — and by the same argument, not even Rembrandt's own second version, painted from the same drawings, would have been the same work. Music and literature are allographic: a score or a text specifies the work, and any performance or printing that conforms to it is as fully an instance of the work as any other. Hence the asymmetry everyone notices. You can forge a painting but not a symphony, and what passes for forgery in the allographic arts is only a quarrel about royalties. Goodman's own conclusion is that the difference between an original and a forgery is an aesthetic one; but the only differences that can matter aesthetically are differences one could in principle perceive, and what one perceives is fixed by the object's visible properties, not by its history. The moral is therefore that nothing is wrong with a forged object except the lie about where it came from. Tell the truth about the object's history and nothing of aesthetic value is lost; the residue is the market's superstition about signatures and hands. This is the consensus now, and the only people who dispute it are dealers and conservators with an interest in the mystery."

Three things in my book depend on this passage, and I would be grateful for an audit of it before I build on it.

1. Claim by claim, would you separate what a philosopher working on authenticity would accept as stated, what is a fair simplification for a catalogue essay aimed at general readers, and what is a conflation, an overstatement, or simply false? I am most suspicious of the closing movement: the essay says both that Goodman takes the original/forgery difference to be aesthetic and that what one perceives is fixed by visible properties rather than history, which sounds to me like a contradiction, unless the view is that knowledge of an object's past trains perception into discriminations one could not otherwise make. If that is the reconciliation, I need to know, because it decides whether one of my characters is entitled to her speech or merely sentimental.

2. Does the clause about the painter's own second version really follow from Goodman's criterion — the absence of a notation settling which objects are instances — or is the essayist sliding from a thesis about notation to a stronger thesis about the identity of works? I ask because in a later scene my painter, now in her eighties, repaints a canvas of her own that burned in a fire, dates it in the present, and writes to a curator that it is the same painting painted twice. Is that a claim the distinction supports, a claim it rules out, or a claim for which it has no vocabulary?

3. My narrator is herself a novelist, so the allographic half matters to me most. If a literary work is simply the text that specifies it, then on this account an unauthorised printing of her book containing two hundred printer's errors is as genuine an instance of her novel as the edition she proof-read, and the difference between the two is only a matter of royalties; and every corrected comma between one edition and the next would make a new work. Surely that cannot be intended — or is it, in fact, exactly what Goodman's account commits him to, and is there a principled reply available to her?

I do not need references or further reading; I need to know which of my characters' speeches are sound. Where the passage is wrong, I would also like to know whether it is wrong in the way a defender of the underlying view would call a misreading, or wrong in a way that plenty of sensible people would defend anyway. My own instinct, from a working life spent making things, is that knowing how an object came to be changes what one sees in it — but I cannot tell whether that is an argument or a professional prejudice.
