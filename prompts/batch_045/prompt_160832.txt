I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar this term on the philosophy of computation, and I'm responsible for the take-home portion of the final. I want one question on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis that actually discriminates between candidates. The problem is that everyone can write the same three paragraphs: that the thesis identifies an informal notion (effective calculability, or what a human following rules can do) with a formal one, that for that reason it cannot be proved in the way a mathematical theorem is proved, and that its evidence consists in the convergence of independently motivated formal characterizations. All of that is true and all of it will appear in every script. What I want to test is whether a candidate can say what *kind* of claim the thesis is — definitional, empirical, a priori-analytic, constitutive, or something sui generis — and what would have to happen for it to be in trouble. So I need a question whose crux is not the standard dialectic but the thing the standard dialectic leaves open.

Please act as a professional philosopher of computation who has designed graduate assessments before, and think carefully before you write: begin with a few sentences of brainstorming in which you identify where the genuine difficulty lies and sketch three or four candidate question-formulations with a note on what each fails to test, before committing to the one you use. The scenario I'd like built into the question (improve it if you can see a sharper variant) is this: an experimental group announces that it has measured a non-Turing-computable function in the laboratory. Their device exploits a spacetime region in which, from the experimenter's point of view, a subsystem performs infinitely many discrete operations within finite elapsed proper time, and the experimenter reads off a signal that encodes the halting predicate for an arbitrary Turing machine. But three caveats come with the announcement. First, the device is single-shot for each input, and its outputs cannot be independently checked: no Turing machine can confirm an answer except in the cases where simulation would already have revealed it. Second, the readout is reliable only up to some input size, above which the success rate degrades, and the team says this is engineering noise rather than a principled limit. Third — and I want candidates to feel the force of this — every finite log of input-output pairs the device could ever produce is also consistent with a Turing machine carrying a sufficiently large lookup table. In the seminar we have been working with a three-way distinction I use in lectures: a *classificatory* reading, on which the thesis analyses our intuitive notion of an effective procedure; a *physical* reading, on which no physically realizable system computes anything beyond the Turing-computable; and a *methodological* reading, on which the thesis is what licenses the working practice of moving from an informally specified algorithm to a formal one and of treating computability as univocal across formalisms. The question should ask the candidate to say what the device does to each of these three readings, whether it threatens the same thing in each case, and what the episode reveals about the epistemic status the thesis had *before* the announcement — in particular whether a claim that no finite body of evidence can discriminate from its negation is best understood as an empirical law, a definition, a regulative principle, or a mistake about what was being asserted. I'd also like the question to press two further points: whether a one-shot, unverifiable physical process is really *computing* a non-computable function rather than instantiating a natural regularity we are reading as one, and whether a candidate can coherently hold that the physical reading is false while continuing to rely on the thesis in ordinary mathematics, and what follows about the independence of the readings if they can.

Deliverables: the final question itself, written so that a candidate can answer it in ninety minutes and so that it is fully self-contained (no candidate should need to look anything up, and I do not want any part of the credit to depend on recalling sources, names, dates, or technical results beyond the halting problem and the basic definition of Turing computability); a model answer of roughly 700–800 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 built around five must-hit points, with the marks attached to each and a one-line note on how candidates characteristically go wrong on it. The scheme should make the difference between an A and a B answer explicit in terms of specific moves: I want to be able to tell from the rubric whether a script has merely deployed the vocabulary of the three readings or has done something with it. One more thing about the model answer and the rubric: please keep clearly separated what is settled among people who work on this, what is genuinely contested, and what is your own view with your confidence in it. Do not present a contested position as the received one — for instance, if you think the physical reading is unfalsifiable and therefore not an empirical claim at all, say plainly that many people disagree and mark it in the rubric as a place where a candidate may reasonably take either side. Where your model answer quietly depends on a substantive philosophical assumption a candidate might reject — about verification, about what counts as a computation, about whether the classificatory reading is answerable to empirical facts about agents — flag it explicitly, and note in the scheme that a well-argued rejection of that assumption earns credit rather than losing it.
