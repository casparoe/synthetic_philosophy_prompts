Here is the situation. I sit on an internal review committee at a large hospital network, and we have been asked to tie a portion of departmental funding to a short list of quality indicators — thirty-day readmission rates, median emergency-department wait times, patient-satisfaction scores. One colleague has repeatedly blocked the proposal by invoking the familiar slogan usually attributed to the economist Charles Goodhart, that when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure. I tried to reconstruct his worry as an explicit argument, and I ended up with a chain whose conclusion I cannot accept but whose joints I cannot find. (1) Goodhart's law in general form: take any measure M that is used as a proxy for some target T; if agents optimize M hard enough, the correlation between M and T decays, so that M ceases to be a good guide to T. (2) Every system that steers toward a goal by feedback is optimizing a proxy for what whoever set that system up really cared about — a thermostat using a temperature reading as a proxy for comfort, a student studying for an exam, a firm maximizing reported quarterly profit, a hospital responding to a readmission-rate bonus, a reinforcement-learning agent maximizing the reward function it was handed. (3) Hence in every such case sustained optimization destroys the usefulness of the proxy, and no system that steers by a proxy can in the long run reliably track what it was set up to track. (4) But many proxy-guided systems plainly do track their targets well over long stretches and across changes in who is paying attention to them: body-temperature regulation, navigation by landmarks, market prices as signals of relative scarcity, exam grades as rough signals of competence, gross domestic product as a rough signal of the volume of economic activity. These do not dissolve merely because people attend to them and act on them. (5) So (1), read generally, is false; and the only reading on which it survives is the truism that any statistical correlation can be broken by pushing hard enough on one of the variables — which is a fact about correlations and selection effects, not a distinctive claim about measures, targets, and incentives. (6) Yet on that deflated reading the law does none of the work my colleague and others want from it: it no longer explains why metric fixation in organizations and specification gaming in machine learning are a special and foreseeable failure mode, one that calls for distinctive institutional and technical responses rather than for the ordinary caution we show toward any imperfect indicator. (7) Therefore Goodhart's law is either false or empty, and it cannot ground the objection to our funding proposal.
