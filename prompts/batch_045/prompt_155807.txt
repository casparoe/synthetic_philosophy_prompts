I'm a retired natural scientist — forty years in marine biogeochemistry, now doing a part-time philosophy course and a reading group at the local library, so excuse me if I mangle the jargon. Last month the group did a session on cancel culture and deplatforming, and the whole thing stopped dead when one member, who is very online, said: free speech isn't freedom from consequences. He said it like it settled the matter.

What it was being used to settle: a learned society had withdrawn an invitation to someone to give a public lecture, after a petition from early-career members, and a few of us said that's bad for science regardless of what you think of the man's views — inquiry needs people to be able to say unpopular things. He said: consequences, mate. And he seemed to think the line covered everything from a boycott to losing your job in the same breath, which I suspect is where the slide is, but I can't say why.

So please seperate the precise claims hiding in that one sentence, as a menu rather than an essay — I'd lay out candidate hypotheses side by side in my old lab, and that's what I want. Along these axes at least: what counts as a consequence (any downstream effect of speaking at all, versus a deliberate sanction imposed in response to it, versus one imposed by third parties rather than the people who heard you); whose freedom is supposed to be in question (the speaker's, the audience's, the institution's); whether the claim is legal (about what constitutional protections reach, state action and all that), conceptual, or moral (about when a sanction is warranted); and whether it matters that the response is itself speech — criticism, counter-argument, a refusal to cite or attend — or something more like a fine or a dismissal. And somewhere in there I'd like the question of whether a coordinated refusal to platform someone is really better described as other people exercising their own speech or as a punishment.

For each reading: is it trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false? And — the bit I actually care about — which of them would really do the work he needed, i.e. show that what the society did raises no free-speech worry at all, rather than merely being true about something else. Say how confident you are in each verdict, roughly, and name the one you'd be least willing to defend if I pushed back; I'd rather know where its soft spot is. British English please, and don't bother with references or reading lists.
