I am putting together a seminar for final-year medical students on the philosophy of psychiatric classification, and I want to use Ken Kesey's *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest* as the case text. Most of them will arrive having seen the 1975 film rather than read the novel, and I am happy to work with the plot as it is generally known; I don't need citations, a bibliography, or any quotation from the book, but I would like a reading that is faithful to the shape of both the story and the philosophy.

The philosophical material is Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects: where a classification of people is taken up by the people classified, it can change how they experience themselves and act, which in turn changes how the classification is applied — the kind, as he puts it, is a moving target. To keep the discussion sharper than "labelling is bad," I want to try out a distinction of my own and see how far it survives the novel:

(i) a *uptake* loop, where the person's own self-conception and behaviour shift in response to the description — the self-fulfilling-prophecy case;
(ii) a *circumstantial* loop, where the description changes the person's material and institutional situation — who watches them, what is read as a symptom, what they lose the standing to refuse — so that they change without any change in how they understand themselves;
(iii) a *criteria* loop, where the changed population feeds back into the classification itself: the prevalence figures, the diagnostic criteria, the experts' descriptions.

Which of these does the ward actually give me? My sense is that (ii) is what Kesey dramatizes best and (iii) almost not at all, and I want to know whether that is right, and whether the ward — its division between Acutes and Chronics, its group sessions in which confession of one's own illness is the currency of privilege, its staff's reading of everything a patient does as a symptom — is best described as a machine for generating moving targets. I would also like the reading held to the point where it stops working. Two places worry me. First, McMurphy himself: he is never convincingly diagnosed at all, the question of whether he is ill or merely opportunistic is left open, and what destroys him is treatment — the shocks, the lobotomy — rather than any description he internalizes. Does Hacking's apparatus have a place for that kind of harm, or does the framework need one added to it? Second, Chief Bromden's escape: the novel stages his deliverance as the shedding of an imposed identity, but the book's own machinery — the Combine, Nurse Ratched as the emasculating authority — presents the injury as one of domination rather than of misdescription, and I am not sure a looping-effects reading can say both things at once without turning a story about power into a story about accurate versus inaccurate classification.

I also expect my students to draw an anti-psychiatry moral — that the illness was nothing but a label — and I would like to know whether looping effects support that conclusion. My understanding is that Hacking himself denied that a kind's being a moving target makes it unreal, but I would like that checked and stated in a form I can put to a nineteen-year-old who has just met a patient with schizophrenia on the ward.

Please close with what, if anything, the novel adds to the philosophical debate that Hacking's arguments alone don't: something about what looping is like from the inside, or about who gets to do the classifying. And please be explicit about how confident you are in each part of the reading — in particular, flag the single claim you are least sure of, whether it is a claim about the novel (say, a plot or character detail I may be misremembering) or a contested reading of what Hacking is committed to.
