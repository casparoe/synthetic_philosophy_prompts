I was reading a chapter in one of those popular books on religion and morality — the kind written by a journalist who clearly did read some of the philosophy, not just the blurbs — and it summarized the Euthyphro dilemma roughly like this:

"Socrates asks whether the gods love pious actions because they are pious, or whether those actions are pious because the gods love them. Euthyphro picks the second option, and that is the mistake Socrates goes on to destroy. The same dilemma can then be run against any divine command theory: either God commands X because X is good, in which case goodness is something independent of God and God becomes irrelevant to morality, or X is good because God commands it, in which case morality is arbitrary — God could have commanded the torture of children for fun and it would have been good, and saying 'God is good' would just collapse into saying 'God does what he tells us to do.' Since neither horn is acceptable, theism cannot provide a foundation for morality. Note too that none of this has anything specially to do with religion: put 'maximizes happiness' in place of 'commanded by God' and you get exactly the same two horns, so the argument is really just the open-question argument with the serial numbers filed off, and the naturalist has precisely the same problem. That tells you the real lesson of the dialogue is about how hard it is to define moral terms at all, not about God. And the popular 'third way' — that God commands what he commands because his own nature is necessarily good — is just the first horn in a disguise, because on that view goodness is still fixed by something other than God's will."

I want to know how much of that to trust, because I'm using it in something I'm writing. The bit that snagged me was the claim that Euthyphro picks the second horn: my hazy memory is that in the actual dialogue he and Socrates both agree to the *opposite* ordering, and that Socrates then uses that agreement to do something else entirely — something about the proposed definition specifying a mere attribute of piety rather than what piety is? But I might be mixing that up with something else, so don't just take my word for it.

Could you go through the passage and tell me what someone who works on this material would accept, what is a defensible simplification or change of emphasis, and what is just wrong? A few things I especially want separated out: whether the "arbitrariness" horn is being stated correctly — the "God could have commanded torture" move and the "'God is good' becomes empty" move feel like two different complaints to me, and I'm not sure whether lumping them together matters; whether the third-way move really is the first horn in costume, and whether the arbitrariness worry then just comes back one level up (is God good because of the nature he happens to have, or does he have that nature because it is good?) or whether that regress can be stopped; and whether the parity claim about the naturalist is fair or whether there is an asymmetry the summary is papering over. Finally: once the errors are fixed, does the conclusion still stand — is the Euthyphro best read as a lesson about definition rather than a problem for theism, or does the modern application survive?

I'd also like to know, if it's relevant, whether the version of divine command theory that only says something about what makes actions obligatory (as opposed to what goodness itself is) changes any of this, since someone mentioned that to me once and I never understood how it was supposed to help.
