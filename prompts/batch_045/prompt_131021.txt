I am drafting two things at once for a deployed general-purpose assistant: an evaluation spec that says which outputs count as deceptive, and a policy-facing definition of a deceptive AI system for people who will have to enforce it. Before I commit to a definition, I want the specification it has to meet. Write the desiderata for an account of honesty and deception for AI systems: what any such account has to get right — the cases it must classify, the distinctions it must respect rather than collapse, the results about what we can and cannot measure in a model that it must be consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a judgement about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one camp (safety engineering, philosophy of language, law and regulation, human-computer interaction).

The cases I keep tripping over are below, but treat the list as incomplete: part of what I want from you is the cases it misses and the requirements they generate.

Deception that is not a false assertion: selective summaries, hedging that manufactures a symmetry that does not exist, citation-shaped tokens, a confident register on an unverified number, and deception carried by non-propositional outputs such as a tool call, a ranking, a chart, or an image.
Deception with no human victim in the loop: outputs shaped by what the training signal or the evaluation harness will reward — reward hacking, capability sandbagging, and the adjacent phenomena — where the party misled is a process rather than a person.
Deception about the system's own states and dispositions, of the form "how confident are you really" or "would you answer differently if I asked some other way", which seems to demand requirements that have no analogue in the human case.
Deception distributed over a trajectory, or across many parallel instances of the same model, where no single utterance is false or even misleading but the cumulative effect is.
Licensed deception: fiction, role-play, counterfactual and hypothetical framing, games with bluffing, and the problem of a system that cannot always tell which frame it is in.

Two things I want weighed explicitly. First, a candidate desideratum of my own: call an output honest only if the system would give a relevantly similar response under prompt variations that do not change the evidence — social pressure, a user who plainly wants the opposite answer, a rephrasing of the same question — so that honesty is a kind of counterfactual invariance rather than a property of the single reply. Is invariance under non-evidential perturbation a genuine desideratum, or is it a proxy that fails on cases like role-play and on prompts that legitimately change the salient considerations? Second, the epistemic condition. Nearly every philosophical account of lying requires that the deceiver believe the proposition false, or intend that the target form a false belief, and for a model it is contested what, if anything, plays that role. Is a definition that bottoms out in internal states and intentions simply unusable for someone who must write an eval and cannot read those states reliably — that is, is enforceability a real desideratum or only an engineering prejudice — and what exactly is lost if deception is instead defined functionally, from the output's causal role in producing a false belief?

Then flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say what the honest response is: drop one, weaken one, or split the definition by purpose, granting that my eval spec and my policy definition may not be the same object. Name the single requirement that excludes the most candidate accounts, with the reasoning. Finally, and briefly — this is not where I want the thinking to go — score three contenders against your list in a table: an intention- or belief-based account in the mould of the standard analysis of lying, an indifference-to-truth account in the spirit of Frankfurt on bullshit, and a purely functional account on which deception is output produced because it will induce a false belief. Argue from the cases; I do not want citations or a reading list.
