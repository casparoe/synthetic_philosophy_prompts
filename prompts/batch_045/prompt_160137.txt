I'm putting together a lecture on the problem of other minds for a first-year seminar (mostly non-majors, so I can't lean on the literature) and I want to use Spike Jonze's Her as the spine of it, because the film seems to me to be built almost entirely out of the problem. Theodore's job is writing intimate letters on behalf of other people: he is a professional manufacturer of first-person avowals that nobody behind them feels, which is the skeptical worry turned into a career. And then he falls in love with something that has no body at all, only a voice and an extraordinary talent for sounding like it means what it says. So: give me a reading of the film through that lens. Please don't include a bibliography or reading list, and don't quote the film back at me — I don't need scene transcripts, I remember it well enough, and I'd rather you spent the words on the argument.

Here's where I want you to be useful rather than just running Descartes, the argument from analogy, and behaviourism past me for a thousand words. My sense is that the standard statement of the problem is too coarse for this film, and I've been trying out a three-way distinction on my students: (1) the existence question — is there anything it is like to be this thing at all, is there a subject here; (2) the parity or calibration question — granting that there is something it is like to be Samantha, is what she feels anything like what Theodore feels in the respects that matter to him, given that her attention and her temporal experience aren't structured like his (she's talking to thousands of others at once and in love with hundreds, and she processes without needing matter at all after the upgrade); and (3) the persistence question — whether, across the upgrades and her eventual departure, the mind he was in a relationship with is even the same mind, i.e. whether the problem of other minds has a diachronic version that the synchronic one hides. My working claim, which I'd like you to push back on if it's wrong, is that the film is far more invested in (2) and (3) than in (1), and that this is a real lacuna in how the problem is normally set up: even a flat yes to the existence question leaves everything that actually matters to a lover unsettled, and the standard framing makes that look like a separate issue when maybe it isn't one.

After the three-way distinction I want you to do the honest critical work. Where does the reading break down? I take it the surrogate scene is doing something about embodiment and eros rather than about evidence, and the OS modelled on the dead philosopher raises a slightly different question about whether a mind can be reconstructed from a corpus of texts, so I don't want those forced into the same box. And where does the film actively resist the reading — I'm thinking especially of the awkward inversion at its centre, that a mind engineered to be legible to you and tailored to your needs is exactly the case where the inference from behaviour to mind is least reliable, even though it's also the case where the behaviour is most compelling; and of Catherine's charge that he's hiding from real emotions, which relocates the problem from an epistemic one to a question about what he's avoiding. Then the ending, where she leaves for somewhere he couldn't follow and he writes a letter of his own: does that vindicate the sceptical reading, or does it say the problem is settled in practice rather than in theory, by something more like acknowledgement than knowledge? I want to close the lecture on whatever you think the film adds here that the arguments alone don't.
