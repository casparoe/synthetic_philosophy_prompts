I chair my university's committee for reviewing research that could be misapplied — the dual-use cases. Under our rules any one of the seven members may sign off on a project alone, on an expedited 72-hour turnaround, and any one of us may also place a hold that forces a full-committee hearing at the next monthly meeting. I cannot change these rules; I can only change how I use my own signature. I see perhaps eight cases a year, alongside a molecular biologist, an ecologist, a research-integrity officer, a legal adviser, and a graduate student, none of whom will have read the philosophical literature.

My problem is the unilateralist's curse: where any one agent's action suffices to bring about an outcome that is catastrophic if that agent's confidence is misplaced, every agent acting on its own expected-value calculation can leave everyone worse off than if none of them had been able to act. I want that turned into something I can actually run in an afternoon. Three worries take me beyond the standard statement of the point, and I want the procedure to handle them.

First, the number that matters is not seven. It is the number of agents elsewhere — other labs, other journals, other funders — facing substantially the same choice, and that number is usually not measurable, only guessable. My suspicion is that a procedure which cannot estimate it should still change what I do, perhaps by moving the default. Second, selection. The cases reaching me for expedited sign-off are the benign-looking residue of someone else's triage, and the members who find themselves willing to sign are, by that very fact, the ones most likely to be overconfident: my willingness to sign is evidence against my signing, and I want that as an explicit step rather than a private worry. Third, asymmetric stakes. The credit for letting a project proceed — a colleague's grant, a lab's priority, my own reputation for not being obstructionist — is private and immediate, while the harm, if the risk case is real, is diffuse, delayed, and borne mostly by people outside the room. A threshold that ignores that asymmetry will sign off too often, and I want to know how to scale it.

Equally, I do not want a procedure that only ever says no. Placing a hold is itself a unilateral act with its own costs; our errors are not independent when we read the same literature, so there may be a case for deferring to the most reluctant member; and I have watched a colleague use a hold to kill work she merely found distasteful. I also suspect the per-case threshold is the wrong unit: eight small sign-offs across a year may be worse than one large one, and I do not know whether the procedure can aggregate.

Please give me the procedure, and run it on three cases:

1. A virology group proposes to publish a protocol that would let a standard BSL-2 laboratory propagate and modify a respiratory coronavirus. The claimed benefit is faster screening of vaccine candidates. Our molecular biologist says the method is incremental and partly in the open literature already.
2. An AI group proposes to release model weights that would materially lower the cost of generating convincing targeted political disinformation. The benefit is external scrutiny of the lab's safety claims. The group's own argument is that comparable weights will be released elsewhere within six months, and that a country with elections in four months is the likely target.
3. A colleague in my own department, a political philosopher whose work I think well of, proposes to publish an argument that the state's duty to protect a small, historically persecuted minority is considerably weaker than is generally assumed. I believe the argument is valid and worth publishing; I also have more at stake here than in the other cases, and I would like to sign it off myself.

Case 3 is the one where I expect to dislike the answer.
