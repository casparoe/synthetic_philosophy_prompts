I am assembling a set of engineered cases for a graduate reading group on decision theory, and I need one built to a specification rather than one that merely illustrates a familiar point. The point of the case is to isolate exactly one structural variable: whether a predictor's read of an agent's disposition is attached to the payoff in the branch the agent is not in, or to the arrival of the request the agent is actually facing. The case succeeds only if the verdicts are generated by the theories from the structure specified below; a scenario that stipulates the verdicts, or that obtains them by changing several things at once, is a failure.

The machinery to be held fixed. One agent, one stranger, one afternoon, one hidden fair coin already tossed (the stranger knows the outcome, the agent never learns it), one bank transfer of a windfall W that the stranger arranges, and one request that the agent hand over 1,000 dollars in cash. Before deciding whether to approach, the stranger forms a read of whether the agent is the sort of person who would hand over the money, by ordinary human means — an hour of conversation, a short structured exercise, nothing better than a skilled interviewer or a good card player. The read is accurate at 0.7: in the counterfactual in which the agent's decision procedure outputs hand over, the read says would hand over with probability 0.7, and symmetrically for the other output; conditional on that output, the read is independent of the coin. The read is formed before the approach and is never revised. Crucially, the agent's handing over the cash has no causal effect on whether the windfall transfer occurs: that is already settled, and the agent knows it.

The one variable. A single gate clause — [the read says: would hand over] — is to be attached in one of two places, with nothing else in the story changing:

Setting A: the windfall occurs iff the coin landed heads AND the gate holds; the request is made iff the coin landed tails.
Setting B: the windfall occurs iff the coin landed heads; the request is made iff the gate is true XOR the coin landed heads.

Required verdicts. In each setting, causal decision theory and evidential decision theory must give the same verdict as each other, and that verdict must be identical across the two settings. Functional decision theory must pay in Setting A and refuse in Setting B. Show this by exhibiting, for each theory, the quantity it evaluates — an intervention on the action node, a conditional probability, a counterfactual on the output of the decision procedure — and doing the arithmetic with the 0.7 accuracy actually plugged in rather than assumed away at the limit of a perfect predictor.

Further constraints. Choose W so that functional decision theory's expected value for paying in Setting A exceeds its expected value for refusing by a factor between 1.2 and 2, and show the inequality that pins W to the band this forces. Realistic setting and ordinary stakes; the whole scenario in a single paragraph covering both settings. No predictors better than the shrewd human described, no simulations, copies, twins, or anthropic devices, no causal loops, no commitment mechanisms, contracts, announcements, reputation effects, or self-modification, and no arguments from the kind of person one would like to be — the only goods at stake are the money. The agent has already been approached, so no ex-ante policy choice is available; if any part of your reasoning helps itself to an ex-ante or updateless standpoint, flag it explicitly rather than letting it do silent work.

What I want on the page. First, the scenario. Second, the verdicts with their arithmetic, for all three theories in both settings. Third, a statement of which feature is doing the work, and a sentence on which of the two settings has the shape of the counterfactual mugging and which carries the structure of the case that goes under the name XOR blackmail — follow my specification where the two differ, and say where they differ. Fourth, the tipping-point computation: at what accuracy or stake ratio does functional decision theory's verdict in Setting A flip, where your numbers sit relative to it, and what becomes of the contrast if the accuracy drops below that point or the windfall is made unconditional on the read. Fifth, the strongest objection a causal or evidential theorist would press against the case — I expect something like the charge that functional decision theory is covertly re-running an ex-ante calculation after the coin is settled, or buying a correlation with a prediction the agent cannot influence — together with the best reply you can give, and an honest note on which part of the objection survives it. Sixth, a frank list of what this case cannot show: in particular whether an unmodified human can implement the policy functional decision theory recommends, and how much of the result depends on the accuracy estimate, which in real life nobody can calibrate.
