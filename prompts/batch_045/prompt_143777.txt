So I've been defending a position in a small reading group and I want someone to actually try to break it.

Setup, briefly: higher-order evidence — evidence that your reasoning on this occasion is unreliable. You just took a pill that makes 80% of people bad at evaluating exactly the kind of argument you just ran; or you learn that graders in your department systematically mis-score essays written after 11pm, and you can't remember which of the 300 you marked late. The usual battle lines are the people who say HOE has to move your first-order credence in P (total-evidence-ish / conciliationist-ish views) and the people who say it shouldn't touch it at all.

My view is a third thing, which I've been calling the *bifurcation view*. There are two separable states: your credence in P, and what I call your *credal confidence* — your confidence that the credence you actually landed on is the one your evidence supports. HOE, I claim, properly moves only the second. So in the pill case: your credence in P stays at 0.9, because the first-order argument was a good argument and nothing has happened to it; but your credal confidence collapses to maybe 0.3. And that, I claim, is exactly what it feels like to be epistemically unsettled without being akratic — the appearance of akrasia is just the two tiers coming apart. Three corollaries I'm stuck with: (i) in any choice that turns only on whether P, you should still act and bet at 0.9; (ii) the enkratic-sounding norm "don't believe P if you believe that believing P is irrational" is really a norm about credal confidence, so there's no genuine bridge principle linking the two tiers; (iii) third- and fourth-order evidence just iterate the same move — confidence about your confidence, and so on — and the regress is benign because every level does different work.

What I want is the map of how exposed this is. Concretely:

- What would count against it, ranked. Fatal at the top, merely awkward at the bottom, with a reason for each placing.
- Which of those would a committed bifurcationist *themselves* concede, as opposed to ones only a hostile conciliationist would raise?
- The cases I'm most worried about, and I'd like you to take them apart: (a) the grading case, where the HOE tells you the corruption is real but doesn't identify which tokens — can the view say anything coherent about 300 retained first-order judgments whose provenance is now partly opaque, or does it owe me an account of what it is to keep first-order support you can't recompute? (b) the limit case, where you learn you're a literal randomizer on this question: is "credence 0.9, credal confidence ~0" a state anyone can be in, or does it fall apart? (c) order effects — you take the pill *before* reading the argument versus after. If my view gives different answers depending on temporal order, does that asymmetry itself count against it?
- The emptiness worry: if neither tier changes what I'd bet or do, and credal confidence only ever shows up in decisions about whether to investigate further or defer to someone, is the distinction behaviorally empty? And if I answer "yes, and that's fine, it's a normative distinction not a behavioral one," have I insulated the view into unfalsifiability — and is that actually a problem for a view of this shape?
- The collapse worry: isn't credal confidence just a second-order credence, in which case I've reinvented the conciliationist's picture with extra furniture?

Don't bother naming names or papers, I've read the usual ones; argue it directly. I'd personally be most moved by something showing the split can't do the work I need it to do, rather than by any intuition pump about how weird it feels.

Finish with one line: Verdict: <short phrase> — the single defeater that should move me most, or "no decisive defeater" if that's honestly where you land.
