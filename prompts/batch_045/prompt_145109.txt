I want a case analysis, not a survey of the theory. Take the American Psychiatric Association's removal of homosexuality from its diagnostic manual in the early 1970s, and read it through Hacking's account of looping effects: a classification of people changes the people classified, and that change feeds back so that the classification has to be revised. Treat the following as fixed background and do not re-litigate it: homosexuality was listed as a diagnosis in the first two editions of the DSM; gay activists disrupted APA panels and organized against the listing; the Board of Trustees voted in December 1973 to remove it and to substitute a residual category, sexual orientation disturbance, for people distressed by their own attractions; the next full revision of the manual, published in 1980, replaced that with ego-dystonic homosexuality, which was itself dropped in 1987.

Be disciplined about the mapping: at each stage, who is the kind of person at issue, what does the classification do to them, what does it do to the clinicians and to the institutions that administer it, and which way does the feedback run?

Then apply two distinctions of my own that I do not think Hacking draws explicitly, rather than paraphrasing them back to me. First, destabilizing versus stabilizing loops: a loop that erodes the kind it operates on, until there is nothing left for the category to classify, versus one that consolidates it, by making it easier to recognize, to self-apply, and to administer. Second, generational loops, where the telling effect appears only in a cohort raised under the classification: people classified as children who return as adults with presentations shaped by having been classified, and who then press for further revision. Say which of these, if any, this episode instantiates, and whether what followed 1973 is best described as a kind being unmade or as one kind being replaced by another — an identity of a sort the diagnosis itself helped make available.

Then turn the case back on the theory. The rival reading is not looping at all but ordinary error correction: there was never a disorder there, and 1973 is when psychiatry noticed. Give me a genuine test — a signature in the evidence that would separate the two stories — and say candidly whether we have anything like the evidence such a test demands. Also say whether the framework can speak to the normative question everyone actually cared about, or whether it is silent there and pretending otherwise is a confusion.

Commit yourself: is this episode a confirmation of the looping account, an anomaly for it, or a case it was never built to handle? Give your reasons, and say what would make you change your mind.
