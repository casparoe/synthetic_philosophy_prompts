I work in a research group where the line "the evidence always underdetermines the theory" gets wheeled out to close arguments down. It happened again last week: we were discussing dark-matter versus modified-gravity accounts of galactic rotation curves and cluster dynamics, and a senior colleague said the two are just "different curves through the same points", that no observation could ever settle it, and that the choice between them is therefore sociological — which group has more telescope time, more prestige, more graduate students. I want to know whether any precise claim he could have meant actually supports that moral.

Please disambiguate the slogan into a menu of distinct theses, each stated precisely enough to be assessed, with verdicts on each: which are true but trivial, which are substantive and plausible, which are false or unestablished, and which — if any — would licence his conclusion that the choice is not evidential. Among the distinctions I keep circling but cannot get straight are: underdetermination by the evidence we have now versus by all the evidence we could in principle gather; the point that no theory predicts on its own but only together with auxiliary assumptions, so that a failed prediction can always be absorbed somewhere, versus the far stronger claim of a global rival agreeing with our theory on every possible observation; rivals that merely re-fit data already in hand versus rivals that were in place beforehand and predicted it; rivals that are equally simple and non-gerrymandered versus rivals cooked up ad hoc to match; and disagreement confined to unobservable structure versus disagreement about what would happen under interventions we could actually perform.

For each reading, say whether it plausibly holds in this case and whether it supports the "it is all sociology" moral. My suspicion is that the readings that are true are ones on which the stand-off is temporary or the rival is contrived, and that the readings strong enough to support his moral are ones nobody has established for dark matter. Tell me whether that suspicion is right, where it breaks down, which reading people usually have in mind when they deploy the line this way, and — what I actually want for future seminars — whether there is a defensible version of the slogan a working scientist should take seriously, as opposed to one that merely licences changing the subject.
