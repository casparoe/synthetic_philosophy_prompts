I run a fortnightly reading group on decision theory and rationality — six or seven of us, mixed backgrounds, we work through a paper or a chapter a month and then argue for a couple of hours. Last month we did Hofstadter on superrationality (the Metamagical Themas material: the thought that a superrational agent picks the answer it would want every superrational agent facing the same problem to land on, which is why you cooperate in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma instead of defecting). One of the group made an objection I've been chewing on since: every case we ever test superrationality on is *symmetric*. Two players, same options, same payoffs, same everything — so "we'll all arrive at the same answer" has a clean meaning. But the situations people actually want superrationality for (climate, arms races, commons problems, any real coordination mess) are never symmetric; the parties are in different roles with different stakes. And apparently there isn't really an agreed way to extend the idea to asymmetric games. So now I want to see how bad the problem is.

What I'd like you to build is a case where correct superrational reasoning, applied to a situation where the two parties are in genuinely different roles, makes both of them worse off by their own lights than they would have been had they just reasoned in the ordinary self-interested game-theoretic way — and where the badness isn't defused by any of the usual escape hatches. Constraints: it must not be a Prisoner's Dilemma or a thin reskin of one (different payoff structure, please — coordination-with-conflict, Chicken-ish, Battle-of-the-Sexes-ish, an asymmetric commons, whatever works); no clones, twins, simulations or perfect predictors; no communication, pre-agreement, third-party enforcement or reputational shadow; both parties know each other's payoffs and know, with common knowledge, that the other is superrational; and above all the bad result must not come from anyone being unsure whether the other is superrational, or from a botched bit of reasoning, or from adopting some obviously daft principle — it has to fall out of a principle a defender of the view would actually endorse. Setting should be ordinary: two departments fighting over a shared budget, a senior and a junior co-author, two neighbouring farms on one irrigation channel, a landlord and a tenant — that sort of thing, kept to a short paragraph or two, no science fiction.

Since there's no canonical asymmetric version, part of the job is choosing the generalization and being explicit about it, and I'd like you to run at least two: (i) the "same pure action" reading — both superrational parties must land on the same choice, which may already be ill-defined when their option sets differ; (ii) a "same decision procedure, different inputs" or role-veil reading — each asks what rule it would want adopted by superrational agents in every role, or chooses as if it might turn out to occupy either role. Show what each yields, against what plain game-theoretic reasoning yields, and whether any of them escapes the bad outcome. Then tell me which structural feature of the case is actually doing the work, what happens if you symmetrize it (does it collapse back into a PD or a plain coordination problem where superrationality is fine?), and how a Hofstadter-style defender would parry: presumably by saying superrationality was only ever meant for symmetric problems and the asymmetric extension isn't theirs. Say whether you think that reply is principled or just a retreat, given that the whole motivation was to escape PD-shaped traps in real life and real life is asymmetric. And if you think no case meeting all the specs can be built — that any sane generalization either collapses into Nash, or into straightforward utilitarianism/contractarianism, or just doesn't apply — say so and argue for it, because that result would be just as useful for the group. Realistically, aim it at someone who can read a payoff matrix but hasn't read Hofstadter, and write the whole thing in British English (organisation, maximise, that sort of thing). Oh, and don't bother with citations or a bibliography — I'll fill those in myself, I just want the case and the argument.
