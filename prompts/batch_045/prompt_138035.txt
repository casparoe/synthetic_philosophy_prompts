I run a monthly philosophy evening at my local library: about twelve adults, mostly without any formal background in the subject, ninety minutes, a whiteboard and whatever I can carry in a bag. I have been reading about Sen's liberal paradox on my own, and I want the group to run into it rather than be told about it. As I understand the result, grant each person the authority to settle some purely personal matter for themselves, and grant also that a group should prefer X to Y whenever every member prefers X to Y — and there are quite ordinary situations in which no consistent group ranking survives. Separately the two commitments look harmless; together they lock.

What I need is a runnable session that produces that experience. Constraints:

- Real choices with trivial stakes. Tokens, chocolate, or points — no money. People should be deciding something they genuinely care about for the length of the evening.
- The preferences must not look rigged. Nobody should suspect the deck is stacked; each person's feelings about other people's private matters should be the mild, recognizable busybody-ish concern that reasonable people admit to. Please give me the actual preference cards or roles to hand out, and say how to assign them so that the deadlock emerges without anyone appearing to have engineered it.
- No lecture up front. The labels for the two principles, and the name of the paradox, should not be spoken until the debrief. The group should first try, and fail, to produce a collective ranking under a rule they have agreed to themselves.
- A possible shape, if it helps: an opening round in which each person is given the say over one personal matter of their own; then a round in which that say is weakened to a veto, so that a person can block outcomes within their own sphere but cannot impose one; then, if time allows, a round in which people may waive, trade, or make conditional their protected say. I am especially curious whether the conflict survives the move to veto-only rights, and whether letting people bargain their way out of their own spheres dissolves the problem or merely relocates it. If a different shape works better, use it.
- A debrief that maps concrete moments — the vote that would not settle, the outcome everybody disliked, the trade someone agreed to — onto the two principles and onto the argument that they clash. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time: someone refuses to play the preference they were dealt, someone proposes a tie-breaking rule that quietly abandons one of the principles, someone insists the group should just decide. Tell me how to handle each.
- Be frank about what an evening like this cannot show. I take it the real result is a claim about every possible rule and every possible configuration of preferences, and twelve people playing three rounds cannot demonstrate that. Tell me which part of the argument exists only in the mathematics and has to be supplied in words, and how to supply it without turning the last twenty minutes into a lecture.

In the notes you give me, separate clearly what is settled among people who know this material from what is genuinely contested — in particular whether the result tells against the liberal condition, against the Pareto-style condition, or against modelling a right as a decisive say over outcomes in the first place. I would rather hand the group an honest map of the disagreement than a tidy moral.
