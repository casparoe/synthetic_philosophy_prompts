I convene a twelve-week final-year undergraduate seminar on freedom, agency, and responsibility — eighteen students, mostly philosophy majors, a few jointly enrolled in psychology — and I am writing this year's closed-book final paper. Candidates answer two questions in three hours. I want one of them to be an essay question, marked out of 25 and answerable in roughly a thousand words across forty-five minutes, on the methodological proposal that we should stop asking whether there is free will and ask instead which varieties of free will are worth wanting, and whether we have those.

What the question has to prevent is the answer I expect from anyone who has skimmed a survey article: a restatement of the proposal, followed by the claim that every variety worth wanting is compatibilist-friendly, plus the observation that whatever is left over would be mere randomness or luck. That answer should earn a B at best. The crux I want forced is that the methodology converts a metaphysical dispute into a normative one, and that it counts as a dissolution rather than a change of subject only if the normative question has a determinate answer that does not presuppose the conclusion, and only if answering it settles what was contested in the first place. Students should have to identify what fixes the content of worth wanting — our antecedent desires, or an independent normative theory — and to say, for each of those, why it threatens the methodology's claim to have dissolved the dispute rather than relocated it.

I also want the question to hand students a test the methodology does not supply for itself, so that they cannot simply list varieties and rank them by intuition. The test I have in mind is a reflection or fixed-point condition: a variety V is worth wanting only if an agent who possesses V, knows that she possesses it, and knows what V consists in would, on reflection, still want V rather than exchange it for another variety available to her — the wanting has to survive full knowledge that one has what one wanted. Build the question around this condition, and make sure it also gives students what they need to notice two things the condition exposes. First, a variety can be wanted as a capacity and wanted as a guarantee, and these come apart: someone may want, not a power she exercises, but the assurance that she is not the product of a design she had no hand in, and a guarantee is not obviously a variety of agency at all, which is what the methodology's framing tacitly assumes it is sorting among. Second, gains along one dimension of agency can be losses along another — openness may be bought at the cost of the agent's assurance that her decision really expresses her; self-transparency may be bought at the cost of deliberative openness — so the varieties worth wanting may not form a list that any single possible agent could jointly satisfy.

Two constraints on the wording. The question must be self-contained: stipulate whichever candidate varieties you ask students to assess — indeterministic alternative possibilities, reasons-responsive self-determination, diachronic self-authorship, or others of your choosing — rather than assuming students can recall any particular author's formulation, and require no citations and reward no name-dropping. And the standard arguments of the field, such as the luck objection and manipulation cases, should function as pressures students must apply to candidate varieties, not as material they can rehearse as the substance of an answer.

Please give me: the question exactly as it will appear on the paper, including any definitions and any part-structure; a model answer of about a thousand words, of a kind a very strong student could plausibly produce in the time allowed; and a marking scheme out of 25 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. Close with a short paragraph saying what an A answer does that a B answer does not.
