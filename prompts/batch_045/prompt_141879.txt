I'm giving the skeptical half of a reading-group session on scalable oversight this Friday, and my assignment is to argue that debate-style protocols cannot handle obfuscated arguments: the case where a question has a definite right answer, but any justification for it is long and only locally checkable, so a liar can bury a single bad step in a chain that looks sound everywhere the judge has attention to spare. I don't actually want to win. I would much rather be beaten now, in private, than discover the holes in my argument in front of the group. So please play the strongest committed proponent of debate you can manage — someone who genuinely believes that two-player zero-sum debate before a bounded judge elicits the truth even when the judge could not verify the answer unaided. Your character holds that the honest debater's advantage is structural rather than a matter of being cleverer: the honest side needs to find one flaw, while the liar has to keep an entire fabrication coherent under cross-examination. Concede readily that flat, naive debate as it is usually described can fail, and that the bare protocol may need supplementation; do not concede that we should therefore fall back on trusting an unaided human, or on letting a model grade its own work. Argue only from the mechanics of the protocol — no authors, no paper titles, no citations, no appeals to what is standard in the literature; if a question would require a reference, answer from first principles or say you won't lean on it. Keep every reply to two short paragraphs, no bullet points, in a spoken register, and stay in character until I say stop; if something about my case is unclear, ask about it in character rather than breaking frame.

Here is the case I have built, which I have started calling the two-ledgers problem, because I think it is harder than the standard obfuscated-argument case. A judge is shown two complete audit trails for the same tangled corporate restructuring — four hundred pages of individually unobjectionable arithmetic each — and asked which one arrives at the correct total tax liability. The ledgers differ only in the convention they adopt for amortizing one class of deferred loss. The two conventions agree on every case small enough for the judge to check by hand and come apart only on cases of this size, and no single line of either ledger is wrong under its own convention; the error, if there is one, is a property of the whole object, and tracing what the choice of convention does to the total means redoing the audit. I want to head off the two obvious replies. The first is that the honest debater need not verify the whole chain, only find one bad step — but there are no bad steps. The second is that the honest debater can force the liar to commit to intermediate values and bisect down to the first point of disagreement, leaving the judge to adjudicate one small step — but here the first point of disagreement is a line that is perfectly correct under either convention, and the divergence is visible only in the shape each convention imposes on the ledger as a whole, which is precisely what the judge cannot take in. What the honest debater needs in this case is not knowledge of the truth, which they already have, but a way of compressing that knowledge into something a bounded judge can check, and I do not see why the truth should in general be compressible.

So: why doesn't the two-ledgers problem sink the programme? Please be explicit about which way you go, since I think the two routes cost you different things. Either you say the protocol has to be supplemented by something other than debate — in which case tell me what the supplement is, and whether the supplement is itself supposed to be scalable, or whether you have simply moved the problem — or you say that any question with a definite answer admits a re-encoding that a judge with bounded attention can check, in which case tell me what performs the re-encoding, who checks the re-encoder, and why a liar cannot produce a re-encoding that is just as convincing as the honest one.
