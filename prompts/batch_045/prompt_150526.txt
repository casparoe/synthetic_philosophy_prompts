In the seminar I help run — an impartiality-and-giving reading group, mostly people with economics or policy backgrounds — a move keeps recurring. Impartial benevolence, it is said, is just prudence scaled up. Within a single life we aggregate constantly: a painful month now against ten comfortable years, a hard decade of training against a career, savings against consumption. Nobody thinks prudential aggregation is illegitimate, and the trade-offs it licenses are exactly the shape of the trade-offs utilitarianism makes between people. So, the argument goes, once you grant that intrapersonal aggregation is rational, there is no principled residue left to the objection from the separateness of persons; that objection is a label for an intuition that has not survived being thought about.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on utilitarianism. Please give me:

1. The mapping, made explicit. A table of correspondences: what plays the role of the temporal stages, of the deliberating planner, of compensation, of the party who bears the total, of prudence's authority, of whatever ties the stages of one life together.

2. What the source quietly imports into the target. List the properties of a single life across time that the analogy carries over to a population — one locus at which the sum is experienced; one memory that owns both the cost and the benefit; the sacrificing and the compensated party being the same party; aggregation being optional for the agent and unenforceable by others; ex post ratification by the very person who decided. For each, say whether its absence in the interpersonal case actually does work in the separateness objection or is inert decoration.

3. The disanalogies that matter, separated from the ones that don't. I suspect some candidate disanalogies are beside the point (that bodies are spatially located, that numbers of people are larger) and others carry the whole weight. Say which, and why.

4. A gradient test I can put to the group. Hold the trade-off fixed and vary only the degree of psychological unification across the parties: one adult human across fifty years; a pair with shared affect and a joint decision procedure; ten thousand people with a standing assembly, joint deliberation, pooled records standing in for shared memory, ex post ratification, and a shared narrative each has internalized as their own; ten thousand strangers. Does the defensibility of aggregation vary monotonically along this gradient? If it does, what is doing the work — a brute fact about separate bodies, or an empirical, degree-valued fact about how unified the parties are? And then the question that matters: does a monotonic answer vindicate the analogy or destroy it, given that the analogy's users need aggregation to be fine at the far end of the gradient? If you think the unity of a single life is itself achieved by deliberative self-constitution rather than given, say what that does to the argument.

5. Whether the conclusion survives. Does the analogy carry the claim that the separateness objection is dead, or was the analogy doing the work all along? If it fails, propose a better comparison — one that captures whatever is genuinely right about interpersonal aggregation without importing the features you listed in section 2 — and note whether what survives is utilitarianism specifically or something weaker.

Two constraints: assume no access to libraries or the web, so no citations, reading lists, or appeals to what some author argued; I want the argument, not the literature. And if you use any term of art, define it in a line.
