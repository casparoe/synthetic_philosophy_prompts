I am a professor of philosophy, and I am writing a two-page pre-proposal with a colleague in machine learning for a twelve-month seed grant from a funder that supports work at the intersection of artificial intelligence and social inequality. The review panel will be mixed: roughly half of it will be people who work on learning from human behavior, and half people from ethics and social policy. I would like you to draft the substantive core of the proposal.

The topic is the inference of preferences from behavior, of the sort done in inverse reinforcement learning and in the preference-learning components of contemporary systems. For the half of the panel that does not work in the area, the setup needs to be stated in a sentence or two: we observe an agent acting over time in an environment; we assume the agent is approximately optimal with respect to some reward function, with departures from optimality treated as noise governed by a single 'temperature' or rationality parameter; and we infer the reward function that best rationalizes the observed behavior under that assumption. Two pieces of background I take to be established and intend to rely on: that without some assumption about the agent's rationality, observed behavior constrains the reward function hardly at all; and that even with a fixed rationality model the recovered reward is identified only up to certain transformations — a positive rescaling, and the addition of potential-based shaping terms — with the rationality assumption doing the identifying work that remains.

My half-formed idea, which I want turned into something fundable, is that this rationality parameter is doing normative work that the technical literature treats as merely technical. Departures from optimality are not all noise about what the agent wants; some of them are the measurable trace of what it costs that agent to convert what she wants into action. Two agents with identical preferences but different costs of acting — one managing depression or executive dysfunction, one navigating an inaccessible interface, one whose attention is consumed by care work or time poverty — will produce different behavior, and the higher-friction agent will be assigned a lower reward on precisely the outcomes she fails to pursue. Call it the agency-cost confound.

The question I want funded is not whether the confound exists, which is nearly rhetorical, but something sharper: in principle, and with what additional observations, can a system distinguish an agent who cares less about an outcome from an agent who pays more to act on it? And when the available data cannot separate the two, what should a system assume — does the right default depend on whether the inference is being used to assist a particular person or to aggregate many people's inferred preferences into a social choice?

What I need from you: a precise statement of the question; an account of why the existing positions leave it open (the machine-learning side treats misspecification of the rationality model as estimation error to be averaged away, while the philosophical side discusses adaptive preferences and informed desire but generally assumes the agent can be consulted); the approach you would actually take over twelve months, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives — asking people directly, adding self-report as another noisy channel, fitting a per-agent noise parameter, or abandoning reward inference for a mere partial order over trajectories; what a positive result would look like as a deliverable someone building systems could use; and an honest statement of what would count as the project having failed, including the possibility that the confound turns out to be a familiar problem — partial observability, exploration, or plain reward unidentifiability — restated in new vocabulary, or that the normative half never becomes decision-relevant.

Please do not include citations; I will add those myself, and I would rather the argument not rest on any result you cannot state precisely. What I do want is epistemic bookkeeping in the margin of the draft. Mark separately: (a) what is settled background that no informed reviewer would dispute; (b) what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — for instance, whether routinely fitting a per-agent temperature parameter already dissolves the problem, or whether 'cost of agency' is only the capabilities critique wearing new clothes; and (c) what is my own conjecture and has not been shown. Where a claim would likely draw a referee's objection, say what the objection is rather than writing around it. Do not let a contested framing appear in the proposal as if it were consensus, and flag any place where my idea depends on reading the technical literature in a way its practitioners might reject.
