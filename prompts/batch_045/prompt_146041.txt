i want a short story, somewhere around 1200-1800 words, title it, that is basically the absent-minded driver problem with a couple of twists bolted on — and i want the twists to be load-bearing, not decoration. the philosophy-journal version has one driver and two turnoffs; give me three turnoffs and two drivers, and give me a reason it matters.

the setup, please keep it exactly as stated:

fog on the road home from the night shift. exactly three turnoffs, and the three of them are indistinguishable — same angle, same cracked stop sign, same sodium light with the same flicker. both drivers have the same condition: on that stretch, arriving at a turnoff wipes the last few minutes, so each time they come to one they genuinely cannot tell whether it's the first, second or third and can't remember having come to one before. don't explain the condition, don't name it, no device, no twist ending where it was a dream or a simulation or a study — just stipulate it the way the philosophers do and get on with it. they can talk in the car park before they leave and agree on anything they can actually carry out. once they're driving: no signal, no phones, and they can't see each other except as described in a moment.

outcomes (call the units whatever you like, they're just numbers):

- first turnoff: the all-night laundromat car park, asleep in the car, back ruined — 1
- second turnoff: home — 4
- third turnoff: the lake road, which does get you home, two hours later, past the place where they first kissed and where he once hit a deer — 2
- no turnoff: the road ends at a locked gate, no signal, stuck in the fog till morning — 0

plus: each of them gets +2 if the two of them end up spending the night in the same place, i.e. both took the same-numbered turnoff. stuck at the gate doesn't count — two cars half a mile apart in the fog, no signal, you're on your own.

they have two cars and they leave from the same car park, so they can drive in convoy if they decide to.

what the story has to actually do:

1. have them work out, out loud, in the car park, why driving separately and each doing their own coin flip is a bad arrangement even if they pick the clever number — independent randomness means they end up apart most of the time, and i want that said truly rather than vaguely. (the nasty bit, if you do the arithmetic: with a bonus that big, the best they can manage driving apart is to both take the first turnoff every single night and sleep in the laundromat together, which beats every randomised scheme. have one of them notice that and hate it.)
2. have someone notice that if they go in convoy, one of them can just follow the other's brake lights, which collapses the whole thing back into a one-person problem with the +2 folded into every outcome except the gate — and that folding the +2 in changes the number the decider should pick, because the gate is the one outcome the bonus doesn't touch.
3. get the numbers right. i will check. the continuation probability they settle on should actually maximise expected payoff given the structure above, and it's definatly not a round number; if one of them, at a turnoff, recomputes how likely it is that this is the first, second or third one — using the fact that under their plan they pass the first turnoff every night, the second a fraction p of the time and the third p-squared of the time — that recomputation should come out right too, and the pull to just turn now should be real, because it is. no footnotes, no appendix, no arithmetic on the page: the numbers have to happen inside their heads and come out in what they say and do. and its fine if the plan they work out gets them home less than a quarter of the time — that's the honest answer and i'd rather have it than a tidy one.
4. the ending follows from the reasoning, not from a mood. whoever turns, or doesn't, at the end should be doing it for a reason the story has earned, and it's fine if it's the wrong reason.

keep it a story, not a puzzle with people in it: cold car park, bad coffee from the machine, the laundromat cat, forty-odd years of being married and knowing what the other one is about to say and saying it at the same time anyway. no narrator stepping in to explain decision theory, no closing wink at the reader. casual third person, and don't let them cheat their way out — no gps, no counting under their breath, no notes, no waiting for daylight.
