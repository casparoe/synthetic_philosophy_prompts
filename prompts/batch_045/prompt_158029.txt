I'm a journalist working on a long piece about whether the people who argue about AI safety actually disagree about anything or are just using different words, and I keep hiting the same wall. About half my sources work with the narrow defintion: the alignment problem is getting the system to try to do what the human actually operating it wants — the spirit of the instruction, not the letter, and not anyone's theory of the good. The other half say that's a cop-out, that unless the system ends up aimed at something like human values (or at what's genuinely worth wanting, whatever that turns out to be) you haven't solved anything, because a perfectly intent-aligned system pointed at a rotten goal is just a very competent catastrophe, and because the operator's instructions will always run out somewhere and something has to fill the gap. I have two sources who refuse to be in the same room, so I need the document an adversarial collaboration between these camps would have produced if they'd spent a year in it anyway.

Here's the scaffolding I've been using, which you can use or throw out. It helps me to imagine a ladder: (1) following the literal instruction; (2) doing what the operator would say they wanted if you asked them about this case; (3) doing that robustly in situations nobody anticipated, with the operator unreachable and the specification silent; (4) doing what's actually good, or what humanity would endorse under reflection. My hunch is both camps can sign something about rungs 1 and 2 and that the real fight starts around 3, but I'm not sure, and I want you to tell me if the ladder itself is smuggling in an assumption. What I want: a numbered section of claims both sides could genuinely put their names to (about what today's systems do and don't do, about the distinction between the objective we specify and the objective the system ends up pursuing, about whether "trying to do X" is even a well-defined property of a model, about why tail outcomes are doing all the work in this argument, and about whether any of the dispute is merely verbal — if part of it is, say which part and why); then a disagreements table where each row states one disagreement twice, once in the wording the intent side would accept for itself and once in the wording the value side would accept for itself, no strawmen on either side; then a cruxes section, i.e. the sub-questions where a settled answer would actually move at least one camp.

For each crux I want a concrete case or test both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Some cases I've been turning over — use them if they're any good, replace them if not: a system given a goal by an operator whose instructions are silent on some morally relevant feature and who then becomes unreachable; a system whose "operator" isn't a person but a committee, a firm, or a state with internally inconsistent intent; a system asked for something its operator genuinely and competently wants that predictably wrecks people outside the operator's concern; and a system deciding whether to let itself be shut off when the operator changes their mind. Also, purely as a probe, stipulate a result I made up: imagine a large pre-registered evaluation program finds that frontier models, once their instructions run out, converge on similar behaviour maybe 80% of the time, and that this behaviour tracks what a random sample of humans would approve of, though nobody can explain why. Would either camp count that as evidence, and what would each have predicted beforehand? Last thing: there's a hybrid I keep hearing in the background — build systems that track operator intent but hard-refuse to act on intents that are catastrophic in some specified sense — and I want to know where each camp places it, and whether it's stable or quietly collapses into one side. Please don't hand me a verdict on who's right, and skip names, papers, and citations entirely; my editor will kill me if I have to footnote this thing.
