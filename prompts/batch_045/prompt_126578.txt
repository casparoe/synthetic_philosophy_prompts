The question, worth 20 marks (8 for part (a), 5 for part (b), 7 for part (c)), was:

QUESTION

You are offered a wager that you may repeat as many times as you like, under identical and independent conditions. In each round you choose a fraction f of your current bankroll to stake, with 0 ≤ f ≤ 1. If you stake an amount S, then with probability 0.6 you get your stake back plus winnings equal to S (even money), and with probability 0.4 you forfeit three quarters of S and have the remaining quarter returned to you.

(a) Determine the fraction f* that maximizes the long-run growth rate of your bankroll. Show your working and state the resulting growth factor per round.

(b) Explain what the quantity you computed in (a) does and does not tell you about your bankroll after n rounds. Your answer should make clear how it differs from the arithmetic expected return of the same strategy.

(c) A classmate objects: choosing f so as to maximize the expected logarithm of wealth simply builds the answer into the utility function. Maximizing expected wealth is the only objective that assumes nothing about the bettor's preferences, and so it is what anyone reasonable should maximize. Explain what is wrong with this objection, and state precisely in what sense the fraction f* from part (a) is optimal.

STUDENT ANSWER, TRANSCRIBED VERBATIM

(a) The right objective is not expected wealth but the expected logarithm of wealth, since wealth compounds multiplicatively and it is the logarithm that adds across rounds. If I stake a fraction f, my bankroll is multiplied by (1 + f) with probability 0.6 and by (1 − f) with probability 0.4. The expected log growth per round is therefore

g(f) = 0.6 ln(1 + f) + 0.4 ln(1 − f).

Differentiating, g′(f) = 0.6/(1 + f) − 0.4/(1 − f). Setting this equal to zero gives 0.6(1 − f) = 0.4(1 + f), so 0.6 − 0.6f = 0.4 + 0.4f and hence f = 0.2. This lies in [0, 1], so the constraint does not bind, and the second derivative is negative, so this is the maximum. The growth factor per round is exp(g(0.2)) = 1.2^0.6 × 0.8^0.4 ≈ 1.0203, that is, about 2% per round.

(b) That 2.03% is the rate at which the bankroll actually compounds: after n rounds the expected bankroll is W_0 × 1.0203^n. This is the geometric, or time-average, rate, and it is what a typical path realizes. The arithmetic expected return on the bet itself is 0.6 × (+100%) + 0.4 × (−75%) = +30% of the amount staked, so at f = 0.2 the naive arithmetic calculation gives 6% per round. That 6% figure is misleading: volatility drag means that the wealth you actually hold grows at only about 2% per round, and the gap widens as the stake grows.

(c) The objection confuses a strategy with a preference. Because the rounds are independent and identically distributed and there is no terminal date, the Kelly rule is myopic: the fraction that maximizes expected log wealth in each round also maximizes the expected utility of final wealth for any increasing concave utility function, so nothing about logarithmic preferences is being assumed. Logarithmic utility is chosen for tractability, not for realism. The real content of the rule is asymptotic. With probability one, a bettor who uses f* will, from some round onwards, be wealthier than a bettor who uses any other fixed fraction, and the ratio between their bankrolls grows without bound. That is a claim about the wealth process itself, about the almost-sure behaviour of the time-average growth rate, and not about anyone's tastes; so the charge that we have assumed the answer by choosing the logarithm is misplaced. Maximizing expected wealth does assume nothing about preferences, and that is precisely its defect: it sets f = 1 on every round, which maximizes the arithmetic mean while driving the typical path towards zero.
