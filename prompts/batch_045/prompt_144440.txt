so I've been going round and round on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox and I think the reason I can't settle it is that I can't tell whether the pile of things I believe about them actually hangs together. Could you audit it for me? Please write in British English, and please don't just tell me it depends what I mean by rational — tell me which of these has to go.

Here's roughly where I've landed.

1. The threshold view: rationally believing p is just having credence in p above some fixed cut-off t, with 0.5 < t < 1, and I want t fairly high, call it 0.9. I also don't want the cut-off sliding around with how much is at stake. Interest-relative thresholds seem to me to make rational mean too many different things.

2. Lottery: in a big fair draw with exactly one winner, it's perfectly rational to believe of any given ticket that it will lose. I'm not giving that up.

3. So closure fails. It can be rational to believe p and rational to believe q without it being rational to believe p and q. The lottery is the whole argument for that and I'll bite the bullet.

4. The preface case is the same shape as the lottery. Author writes a book with a few hundred substantive claims, each of which she's confident about, and the preface says something like: no doubt there's at least one mistake in here somewhere. She's being rational, not irrational, and the right treatment is the same as in the lottery — fine to believe each claim, not fine to believe the big conjunction.

5. The bit I made up myself, which I've not seen anywhere and which is probably why I'm stuck. Call it the competition rule: believing each member of a set while not believing their conjunction is rational only when the members are probabilistically competitive, meaning anti-correlated and drawing on the same pool of evidence in such a way that evidence for one is evidence against another. That's exactly what's going on in the lottery: the tickets compete for a single win, and it's the fact that exactly one of them wins that makes each of them so likely to lose. I like it because it explains why the lottery verdict feels right instead of just stipulating that it is.

6. Belief has to earn its keep in action. If you believe p you should be willing to act on p in ordinary circumstances. But I don't think it follows that the author fails to believe chapter 3 just because she'd refuse a bet at even odds on chapter 3 being true and there being no errors anywhere in the book. A friend says that's me having it both ways.

7. Relatedly: I want to say she isn't irrational, but also that if she sat down and wrote out all her claims as one long conjunction she'd be doing something irrational in going on believing each of them, so whether she's rational depends on whether she's happened to put them together. That makes me uneasy, because rationality shouldn't depend on what you've happened to think about.

The other thing: someone did the numbers on 4 and I can't tell whether it matters. If there are 200 claims and she's 0.99 on each, then her credence that there's at least one error is about 0.87, which is below my 0.9 — so on my own view she can't rationally believe the preface claim at all, and she'd need something like 230 claims at that confidence before the preface case even gets of the ground. Does that kill 4, or just mean most actual books aren't preface cases?

So: which pairs of 1 to 7 really clash, and how badly? Order them by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say what I'd have to give up and what it'd cost me. Say too if any of them only look like they clash. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and why that one rather than the others.
