I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice: 18 students, one 90-minute meeting, no prerequisites beyond a first course in probability. I want a single in-class exercise that makes the students feel, rather than be told, what is at stake in Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and the luring lottery, and that then makes them feel how fragile the reasoning behind both of them really is.

Constraints on the design:

1. No lecture up front and no vocabulary. Neither the handouts nor the facilitator's script may use "prisoner's dilemma," "superrationality," "evidential," "causal," "equilibrium," or "expected value" until the debrief. Use ordinary words instead: claim, pass, the pot, the draw.

2. Three rounds, each decided privately and simultaneously, at real but small cost: a pot of course points worth roughly two percent of the final grade, destroyed rather than redistributed if it goes unclaimed.

Round one, symmetric: one pot, awarded only if exactly one of the eighteen claims; if nobody claims, or if two or more claim, the pot vanishes. The students should be able to reach the answer "claim with probability one-eighteenth, by means of a randomizer" themselves, from the setup alone. You may not print that answer on any handout. If you think they will not reach it, say so and say what to run instead.

Round two, the shrinking pot: same anonymity, but now the pot is divided equally among everyone who claims, so claiming is never worse than passing for the individual and always worse for the group. I want the students to catch themselves in the lure: the hope of being the one person shrewd enough to claim while everyone else nobly stands aside.

Round three, the variant I most need your help with: each student privately draws a multiplier (six 1s, six 2s, six 3s) that scales only their own award, and the rule stays as before — the pot exists only if exactly one person claims. Now "do what you would want everyone in your situation to do" comes apart into two readings: the same action for everyone, or the same rule applied to each kind of situation. Design the round so that the group runs into this split on its own, including the problem of what to do when several students share the top multiplier.

Between rounds I will read out the truthful count of claimants from the previous round, publicly counting the slips without names. Tell me how to stage that reveal, what a sensible student should conclude from a high count, and how the debrief should handle the tempting position "I will reason that way provided enough others do" — why it is unstable rather than merely prudent.

Please give me: the exact text of each student-facing slip; the payoff table in points; the materials; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps particular moments onto (i) the symmetry premise, (ii) what a public randomizer can and cannot do as a substitute for that premise, if you use one, (iii) the two readings of the rule that diverge once stakes differ, and (iv) the fairness objection that a low-multiplier student is being asked to hand the pot to someone else. Be explicit about which of these the game cannot settle and must simply be asserted by the facilitator. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — joking, attempts to communicate or pre-commit, students who assume I will rerun a spoiled round, point-gaming — and what the exercise cannot reproduce at all, above all the premise that the other players are literally running your reasoning, and the one-shotness that premise needs, since a seminar is a room of people who have just heard one another talk and who will meet again next week.

Hofstadter's definition, which the students should not see until the debrief: "Superrational thinkers, by recursive definition, include in their calculations the fact that they are in a group of superrational thinkers."

Please write your answer in American English.
