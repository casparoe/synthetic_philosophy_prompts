I'm a novelist, three months from delivery on a book that runs on two timelines — Buenos Aires in 1946 and Buenos Aires now — and I've come up against a small object I can't do without. In the present-day strand my narrator, a woman in her thirties who works as a permissions archivist at a publishing house, is clearing the papers of a dead translator and finds, folded into a bundle of galley proofs for a review that folded in 1948, an unpublished sestina in English by a minor poet of the circle, a man who had been a proof-reader at a municipal library and who nobody has heard of since. I need that poem. It has to be printable without footnotes, it has to be mine rather than quoted (our rights department will not clear anything), and — this is the part that matters — it has to survive two readings at once: the woman who finds it has just lost her father, and for her it must land first as an elegy about a man watching a river go on without him, quite apart from whether she notices that an argument is being conducted in it; and it must also be a poem that someone who has actually read Borges on time recognises as getting him right, not as dressing him up.

So the commission is a sestina — six stanzas of six lines plus a three-line tornada, unrhymed, in a loose iambic pentameter I'd like kept deliberately uneasy rather than musical. The six end-words should be the six words of the sentence with which the mid-1940s essay usually translated as "A New Refutation of Time" (the one collected in *Other Inquisitions*) closes: time, substance, river, tiger, fire, made. What I want the form to do is enact the argument. Borges's case, as I understand it, runs roughly thus: that on an idealist premise there is no object apart from the perceiving of it; that the memory of a past event is itself only a present perception, and so cannot testify that anything happened earlier; that two instants exactly alike would not be two instants but one, so time cannot be built out of recurrences, while if no two instants are alike we have no measure by which to call them successive; that what follows is the disappearance of the self that was supposed to persist from one instant to the next; and that all of this is offered, half-acknowledged, as a consolation which does not in fact console. Give me those moves in that order, one to a stanza, with the "and yet" arriving in the fifth — the turn carried by grammar rather than by form, at the moment the speaker first says that he *is* the river rather than that the river takes him. Let the end-words drift as they recur: "substance" from the philosopher's substrate to the mere stuff of a man; "made" from "made of" to "made up, fabricated"; "river", "tiger" and "fire" each reversing from the thing that destroys him into the thing he is. The tornada should carry two of the six words in each line and be readable two ways at once — as the collapse in which every instant is a single instant, and as the fork of "The Garden of Forking Paths", the 1941 story, in which every outcome is taken — and its last line should, without winking, admit that the poem cannot tell whether it has said one thing six times or six things once.

Two constraints, both practical. The poem must be datable: nothing in its diction, its furniture or its metaphors could not have been written by a minor argentine anglophile in 1946, so no block-universe jargon, no quantum, no later critical vocabulary, and for pity's sake no labyrinths, mirrors, alephs, eternities or infinities — the pastiche words, which flatten him into a brand. And it must not name Borges, or anyone else. If you can, add a few lines at the bottom telling me how you mapped the argument onto the rotations, so I can check I haven't printed something a scholar would laugh at; I'll cut it before the book goes to the typesetter.
