I've been reading about inference to the best explanation on my own — I have no formal training in philosophy — after an argument with a friend about whether there really is such a thing as reasoning *to* an explanation, as opposed to just reasoning from how often things have happened before. He said abduction is just ordinary induction in a nicer coat; I said it is what doctors, mechanics and detectives actually do, and that it can't be reduced to counting. To settle it I tried to write out, in numbered steps, an argument that shows there is no genuine method here at all. The argument came out cleaner than I expected and I now can't find the mistake in it, but it also seems to show that diagnosis, criminal investigation and a good part of science rest on nothing, which I am not prepared to accept. So I would like to know which step I should give up, and what giving it up commits me to.

The example I keep testing it on: a friend of mine stopped replying to my messages for two weeks. The hypotheses on the table were (a) she is angry about something I said, (b) she is overwhelmed with work, (c) her phone broke. I concluded (b), because it explained not only the silence but also a few odd details — she had stopped posting elsewhere too, and a mutual acquaintance mentioned a deadline — with less extra machinery than (a), which needed me to have said something hurtful without knowing it. That felt like good reasoning. Now the argument:

1. The rule: given evidence E, believe the hypothesis H which, among the rival hypotheses on the table, would if true explain E best — where best means scoring highest on the explanatory virtues: how much it explains, how precisely and by what mechanism, how much it unifies apparently separate details, and how little extra machinery it needs.
2. The rivals on the table are whichever hypotheses someone has managed to think of. Which ones those are depends on imagination, fashion, technology and who happens to be in the room — not on E. So the verdict of the method depends on something other than the evidence.
3. If which hypothesis a method tells you to believe is partly fixed by something other than the evidence, the method is not tracking the truth; it is tracking whoever was imaginative that day.
4. The natural repair is to require that the rival set contain the true hypothesis. But then the rule runs as follows: the true hypothesis is in the set, and the true hypothesis is the one that would explain E best, so pick the hypothesis that explains E best. The identifying work is done by the guarantee that the true hypothesis is the best explainer, not by the comparison of virtues; the rule says, in effect, pick out the true one, and the true one is the best explainer. IBE then contributes a name rather than a method.
5. The other repair — let the rival set be every logically possible hypothesis — is worse. For any candidate H there is a strictly better explainer: H together with a further specification of exactly how the mechanism works, and a further specification on top of that, and so on. Since each addition explains E at least as precisely as its predecessor and in more detail, the ranking has no top. So the rule never returns a verdict at all.
6. So the rule is either an artifact of who was in the room, redundant, or silent. But it plainly is not any of those things: it is the form of reasoning I used about my friend, the form diagnosticians and mechanics use, and the form that has produced predictions of entirely novel phenomena.
7. Therefore one of steps 1 to 5 is wrong.

Each of those steps still looks to me like a fair description of what the method is supposed to be, which is why I think I have gone wrong somewhere rather than simply built a bad argument. I have tried one fix on my own: I suspect that the word best may be doing two different jobs — the explanation that gives the most understanding versus the explanation that is most likely to be true — and I cannot tell whether spotting that dissolves the argument or just relabels the problem. Which step would you reject, why does that step look fine until you press on it, and what does rejecting it cost me — in particular, what does it force me to say about step 6? If I have to choose, I would rather keep 1 and 6 than anything in the middle.
