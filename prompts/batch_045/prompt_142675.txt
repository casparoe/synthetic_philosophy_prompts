I'm giving the best man toast at my brother's wedding in three weeks. His bride's family are from a small village in the Azores, and her grandmother — who'll be at the head table — was the girl who was healed at the chapel in 1961; half the room are people for whom that's just family history, and the other half are my side, mostly engineers plus one uncle who's a very loud atheist. My brother is a Hume guy, and his line at every family arguement has been that you can't put the laws of nature on one side of the scales as a separate weight, because the laws are nothing but the record of what's been seen so far — so the only thing you can actually weigh is the people doing the seeing, how many of them and what they're like. What I like about it is that it lets me be genuinely warm about the grandmother as the person who saw the thing without committing me to anything about the thing itself. I want the toast to steal that move without turning into a lecture: no weighing evidence out loud, no naming Hume, no saying she's wrong, no quietly explaining her away either, and definately not the move where love turns out to be the real miracle. It has to land as a wedding toast to about a hundred and twenty people, most of whom don't care about epistemology at all.

Give me:
- The toast — around 650 words, written to be said out loud, five minutes max
- Where the argument is doing the work — about 150 words on what that move lets the toast say and not say, and where it would have broken if I'd been allowed to say it straight
