I am putting together the first session of a graduate seminar on categories and style, and I want to open it with the question of what makes every 'a' an 'a'. I do not need another illustration; I need an engineered case that pries apart two answers I plan to put on the board, and I want it built to specifications.

The two answers are these.

(R) The relational answer: a mark is an 'a' in virtue of the slot it fills in a style — it is the realization of the a-position, produced by the same generative principles that produce the rest of the alphabet, and held apart from the marks in the other positions. Nothing about the mark on its own settles the question; the system of contrasts does.

(S) The perceptual answer: a mark is an 'a' in virtue of being taken for one — the features by which readers pick out the a-category are present to a sufficient degree, and a reader recognizes it.

The direction I can already construct is (R) without (S): take a letterform from a style my students have never read, and they simply fail to see it. That case is cheap, and it only shows something about my students. What I want is the hard version: a mark that satisfies (R) impeccably — it really is what the style's own principles, correctly applied, yield for the a-slot — and that nevertheless fails (S) for a reader who is thoroughly fluent in exactly that style.

Specifications:

1. Give me the style first, in four or five generative principles I can put on a single slide — something about stroke weight and contrast, terminal treatment, aperture, how bowls and stems are joined, the proportion of x-height to ascenders — enough that the class can check a derivation for themselves, not a list of shapes asserted one by one.

2. Derive the mark from those principles for the a-slot. No slips of the pen, no exception invented for this one letter, no the designer simply drew it that way. The style must be one a working type designer could have produced and a reader could have learned.

3. Fix the reading test precisely: who the reader is, what they are shown, and what they answer. The mark must fail when shown alongside the complete lowercase of the style, so that the failure cannot be blamed on unfamiliarity; every other letter must be read correctly, so that it cannot be blamed on the style being generally illegible. The failure must survive changes to the surrounding letters, so it is not contextual inference from word shape.

4. Isolate the variable: name the single parameter whose value produces the failure, and show that moving it back restores (S) while leaving (R) intact.

5. Tell me what happens when the reader is then told that the mark is the 'a' of this style. I expect the relational theorist to lean on the fact that the reader will say that now they see it, and I want to know in advance whether that reply is worth anything and what the perceptual theorist should say to it.

Constraints: the case itself in under 250 words, no diagrams, no cross-script homoglyphs, no permuted or enciphered alphabet, no printer's error or damaged impression, and no verdict stipulated by fiat — that the mark is the a-slot output must follow from the principles, and that it is not read as 'a' must follow from the reader's situation.

After the case, give me the verdict each answer returns with its one-line reason; the feature of the case doing the work; what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and whether it then collapses back into the easy direction; and the strongest thing a defender of whichever answer comes out worse can say against it.

What the case is for: I want to argue that neither answer is sufficient alone, and that the object of interest is a relation between a mark, a style, and a reader — which is what any program with the Letter Spirit ambition, given a few letters of an alphabet, producing the rest in the same style, would have to get right. So the case cheats if it secures its verdicts by stipulating that one of those three relata is missing or broken.
