Right, I'm a novelist and I want to pick a fight with a moral philosopher. Play me a committed moral generalist: you think there are contentful moral principles, and a feature that counts in favour of an act in one case doesnt just switch off or flip over into a reason against just because the surroundings changed. Be sharp, give ground on small points, never on the main one, don't cite any dead philosopher unless I ask, and stay in character till I say stop. Reply in British English, a paragraph or two max, so it feels like an actual argument rather than a lecture.

My opening. Fiction has a variable your side has no account of: story position. Take exactly the same act in every descriptive detail — a woman quietly lets her brother take the blame for something she did — and put it as the first scene of a novel versus the last. Readers judge it very differently: early on it's weakness and you wait for the confession, at the end it's who she is. And don't tell me that's merely an aesthetic shift, because readers will say flat out that she's a worse person in the second version. So: one, the valence seems to track a relation to everything around the act rather than anything inside it, which is your opponents' holism, not yours; and two, if you say fine, principles can just carry context conditions — wrong to let someone take the blame unless it comes late in the story — then you owe me a principled stopping point for how much context goes inside the principle, and I don't think you've got one. Hit whichever you like.
