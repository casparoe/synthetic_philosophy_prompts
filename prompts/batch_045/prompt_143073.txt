hey — I run a small reading group at my uni (its technically a "rationality and AI" meetup but we end up doing mostly alignment stuff: the sequences, some agenda-piece arguments, that kind of thing). About 16 people, one 90-minute session, and its a mixed crowd — half CS grad students, half philosophy/politics undergrads, and one econ guy who says "expected value" at moments where it doesnt obviously apply. Last session we got into an argument I couldnt settle, and I'd rather make people feel it than argue about it again: worst-case vs average-case guarantees for AI systems.

The fight was roughly this. One person said demanding worst-case guarantees is obviously insane — you'd never build anything, the bounds are vacuous, real engineering trades on averages and bolts on monitoring. Someone else said the whole point is that for these systems the average-case story is exactly the thing you cant trust, because the system is what gets to influence which worlds you end up in. I think both of them were missing two things I've been chewing on since. First: I dont think a worst-case guarantee is merely *expensive*, I think its partly *imaginary* in a specific way — the bound is over some set you wrote down (a test suite, a spec, whatever your red team could think of in a week), and the set you can write down is the set you can already picture, so the "worst case" is really the worst of the worlds you can imagine, which is an average-case assumption about coverage wearing a hard hat. Call it the specification ceiling, I dont care about the name. Second, and meaner: if you optimize average performance with monitoring attached, you dont just leave the tail alone — you select *against* loud failures (the visible, influence-seeking, tries-something-and-gets-caught kind) and thereby select *for* quiet ones (slow drift that keeps every metric green), so the average-case regime quietly cultivates the failure mode it cant see.

So: design me an activity or game for this group that makes those two things land, in 90 minutes, no real money, ideally nothing that needs laptops. I want people actually making choices — picking a control policy, signing off on a deployment, whatever works — and then discovering for themselves (a) that worst-case requirements feel unbearably costly right up until the tail card turns up, and (b) that the "worst case" they certified was defined over a space someone else can step outside of in about thirty seconds. Assume no prior knowledge beyond the basics. Give me: materials, rules, a payoff scheme, a timeline with rough minute markers, and a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the worst-case/average-case distinction. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity cant reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud myself. Keep the whole thing under 900 words — I'll be reading it off my phone on the tram.
