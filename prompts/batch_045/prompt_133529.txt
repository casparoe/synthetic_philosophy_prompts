I have come to accept what I call the calibration-only view of higher-order evidence. Evidence about whether p settles my credence in p; evidence that I am exhausted, that I may have taken a drug that degrades reasoning, or that I have been reliably unreliable in circumstances like these does not speak to whether p at all, but to the reliability of the process that produced my credence — so it cannot rationally move any individual first-order credence. What it can rationally do is lower my confidence that my credences taken as a batch are well calibrated: I should expect a larger share of them to be mistaken, without being able to locate the error in any particular one. On this picture epistemic akrasia — holding a first-order attitude while judging that attitude rationally defective — is never rationally required, and conciliationism is false wherever a disagreement is purely higher-order.

What would count against it? Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them a defender of the view should concede rather than dismiss. Things I have in mind, though please go beyond them: the suspicion that the view is empty, since apparently targeted higher-order evidence (a drug that impairs reasoning about political matters specifically, where p is a political claim) can always be redescribed as first-order evidence about p, leaving the thesis unfalsifiable by construction; cases where the higher-order evidence is fine-grained enough to isolate a class of propositions, or a single proposition, as suspect; whether the portfolio-level shift the view permits can be made coherent at all rather than collapsing into either no change or an akratic one; whether the view commits its holder to full steadfastness in ordinary peer disagreement, and whether that is a cost it can bear; and whether it is self-undermining, given that higher-order evidence about my reasoning would seem to cover my reasoning about the view itself. For each, say how decisive you take it to be and what it would take for it to land — which intuition, which case, which formal result. If you conclude that the view is well insulated, say whether that insulation is a defect in a thesis of this kind or simply what a true view looks like.
