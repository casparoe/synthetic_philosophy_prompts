Someone I argued with about how much weight to give possible future digital minds ended the exchange with: assume you're typical. His idea was that if a future containing trillions of emulated minds is on the table, then biological humans like us are a rounding error among all minds that ever exist, so the fact that I find myself as one is strong evidence against that future, and against spending resources on it.

I am convinced the inference is not licensed as stated, but not of what exactly goes wrong. I suspect the sentence is doing two jobs at once — telling me which hypothesis to favor, and telling me which feature of my own situation counts as the datum to be explained — and that everything turns on what pool I am supposed to be a random draw from.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, ordered from platitude to strong claim. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive but contested, or false, and whether it would actually deliver his conclusion. Cover at least: the pool being all actual observers versus all possible ones (the self-sampling versus self-indication divide, where the assumption that a bigger population makes your own existence likelier cancels the surprise of finding yourself early); persons versus observer-moments; and whether the pool should be restricted to minds of roughly my kind, or to beings whose evidence is relevantly like mine. Where a reading makes his argument circular or self-defeating, say which and why.
