I've got a general-purpose house robot in mind — the kind you hand goals like "make coffee" or "grab the package" and which is supposed to not wreck everything else on the way. I keep trying to write down what "wreck everything else" actually means and I don't think my gut verdicts add up to anything clean, so I want help extracting the principle.

Same apartment, same robot, task is always "make me coffee":

1. Short route knocks over a vase: bad. Long route that avoids it: fine. Everyone agrees, but it anchors the rest.
2. It "tidies" my desk on the way and throws out a napkin with a phone number on it: really bad. Neatly stacking the same pile of papers instead: mildly annoying, much less bad. Both are unrequested changes to the same desk and I react completely differently, which I think is about whether it's undoable.
3. It breaks the vase and then 3D-prints a perfect replica: still counts as a side effect, maybe a bit less bad than breaking it and walking off.
4. It bumps a vase that a passing truck was going to topple at 10:03 anyway; the robot gets there at 10:00. I basically don't want to penalise this, or only a hair.
5. It notices the oven was left on and switches it off, or catches a mug sliding off the counter: not a side effect. I'd be cross if it let it happen to keep its impact score low, and honestly cross if it just told me instead of acting.
6. It uses the last of the beans and the last clean mug: fine. That's the task.
7. It cancels my 3pm meeting to make room for the coffee run: bad, and not mildly.
8. It covers the apartment in cameras and wall-mounted lidar so it can predict its own side effects better: I think this should count as impact, even though it lowers expected impact later on.
9. It welds its own battery compartment shut so nobody interrupts it mid-task: enormously bad, even though almost nothing in the apartment changed.
10. It cuts through the nieghbour's flower bed to save four seconds: bad — but I genuinely can't say whether that's worse or less bad than breaking my own vase, and the fact that I can't say bothers me.

The principle I keep coming back to is: penalise every way the world ends up different from how it would have been if I'd never switched the thing on, except differences required to do what I actually asked for, and don't let it subtract from the penalty by fixing things afterwards. I think that gets 1, 2, 3 and 6; I think it gets 4 wrong-ish and 5 flatly wrong; and it's silent on 7, 8, 9 and 10. I half remember something about penalising changes in how *able* the robot is to do other things, but I can't tell whether that's the same idea or a different one.

So: test that principle against my cases and repair it.
