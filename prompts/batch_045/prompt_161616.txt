I've been reading a chapter on competing theories of distributive justice in a popular book about effective altruism, and I want to know how much of its treatment of prioritarianism I should trust. The chapter distinguishes the view from utilitarianism roughly as follows — I'm compressing a few pages here, but I think this is a faithful rendering:

"Utilitarianism says to maximize the total sum of well-being, counting everyone's well-being equally. Prioritarianism, Parfit's refinement of it, says instead that we should give extra weight to the well-being of people who are worse off, since a given benefit matters more, morally, the worse off the person who receives it is. Prioritarianism is therefore a subtler form of egalitarianism: rather than insisting that everyone end up with the same amount, it weights inequality in the calculation, which lets it reproduce the intuitive verdicts of egalitarianism while shedding the absurd ones. The absurdity in question is the levelling-down objection. If you value equality as such, you are committed to saying that a world in which everyone is blind is better than a world in which only half are blind, and since nobody believes that, and no egalitarian has ever produced a decent reply, egalitarianism is finished. Prioritarianism escapes the objection entirely, because on the priority view only absolute levels of well-being matter, not how people compare with one another, so making the better-off worse off without benefiting anyone can never make an outcome better. The mechanism is really just diminishing marginal utility: a unit of well-being counts for less the more well-being a person already has, so the same total is worth more when it is spread evenly than when it is concentrated. And this is essentially what Rawls's difference principle was reaching for, minus the apparatus of the original position. The worry that remains is that prioritarianism is still a maximizing doctrine. Because the worst off carry more weight, it will always prefer a small gain for the single worst-off person over a much larger gain for everyone else, and it still generates something like the repugnant conclusion, so it inherits utilitarianism's readiness to sacrifice individuals to the aggregate."

I came away with the impression that prioritarianism is a tidy escape from a problem that sinks egalitarianism, but I'm not confident, and two things made me suspicious. First, I remember the difference principle as a principle about the basic structure of institutions and about the prospects of the worst-off group, not as an instruction to add up anyone's weighted well-being, so the identification with prioritarianism struck me as too quick. Second, the chapter moves between saying that what matters is how badly off someone is and saying that what matters is how they compare to others, and those don't look like the same claim.

So I'd like an accuracy audit I can act on, going through the chapter's claims one at a time rather than pronouncing on the view as a whole. For each: what would someone who actually defends the priority view accept, what is a defensible simplification for a popular audience, and what is a genuine misrepresentation — a conflation of claims the view keeps apart, an overstatement, or a technical term used in its everyday sense? In particular I'd like you to address:

1. Whether prioritarianism is a form of egalitarianism at all, or whether the whole point of the distinction is that it is not, and what exactly is lost by describing it as one.
2. Whether the levelling-down objection is being stated correctly — including whether it is aimed at the right target, whether it commits the egalitarian to the strong claim the chapter attributes, and whether it is true that no reply is available.
3. Whether prioritarianism really escapes the objection, and whether that escape is structural or depends on how the view is formalized — for instance, on whether the weight attaches to a person's final level of well-being or to the marginal value of gains at that level, and on whether priority is indexed to absolute level or to someone's position relative to others.
4. Whether "diminishing marginal utility" is the right gloss on the weighting, or whether that conflates a moral claim about the weight of well-being with an empirical claim about how resources convert into utility.
5. Whether the characterizations of Rawls and of the "residual worry" are fair: does an additive priority view really always favour the worst-off individual, or can numbers still outweigh weight, and is the repugnant conclusion actually inherited?
6. Finally, whether the chapter's conclusion — that prioritarianism is the more defensible view, and that it wins largely by escaping levelling down — survives once the mischaracterizations are corrected, or whether it was resting on them.
