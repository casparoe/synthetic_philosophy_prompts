I'm in a small reading group on modality and I keep flip-flopping between the two big pictures, which is starting to annoy me. Call them A and B: on A, the very same object exists in many worlds and just has different properties in them, so "I could have been a farmer" literally says something about me; on B (the Lewis-y one, I think), nothing is in more than one world, and such claims are really about other, similar-enough things elsewhere. I tried to settle it by writing down my gut on cases, so here's the list, and I'd love to know whether anything coherent collects them.

(1) A bronze statue and the lump of bronze it's made of, same matter, same time in the studio. My verdict: the lump could have been squashed into a ball and still existed; the statue could not have. Same stuff, different modal properties — feels like a point for B, unless A gets to say identity is sortal-relative, which feels like a dodge. (2) A world where the process that is actually me splits and produces two equally good successors, each with my memories and, say, half my brain. My verdict: it's false that one of them is me and teh other isn't; either both are or it's indeterminate which, and I lean "both." A can only say "indeterminate which one is literally identical to me," and I find that unsatisfying in a way I can't quite articulate. (3) Two hundred years after my death, a machine builds a molecule-for-molecule copy of me at thirty, with my memories and a room that looks like my kitchen. My verdict: not me, and "I could have been that thing" is false in the sense I care about — so similarity can't be all there is to it. (4) A world where the same conception, same pregnancy, same parents produce a child who grows up somewhere else, never learns to read, has a totally different personality and career, and dies at forty. My verdict: yes, obviously still me. (5) A world where my parents never meet and no such person as me ever turns up. My verdict: it's straightforwardly true that I might never have existed — that's the first-personal claim I'm most confident about, and it's the one that makes B look like it needs a special trick.

The best I've manged so far is something like: the thing in another world that settles my de re modal claims is the unique candidate there with my origin (same conception event for organisms; the same hunk of matter, or the same making, for artefacts, maybe sortal-relative to handle (1)); and if there's no candidate at all, that's what makes "I might not have existed" true. (Someone in the group said this is basically the origin-essentialist line people associate with Kripke, which may be why I like it.) But it goes silent on (2), where two candidates share my origin, and I can't tell if it should say "both," "neither," or "indeterminate." Can you take this on: test that principle against the five cases, repair it or replace it with something better, state it precisely with the case that motivates each clause, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't keep, and say what it predicts for two cases I haven't put on the list — a duplicate of me built while I'm still alive and walking around, and the one I keep going back and forth on, a child born to my same parents from a different pregnancy two years later, same upbringing, qualitatively just like me in every way I can think of. One more wrinkle: my partner says "I could have been a concert pianist" was true of me at ten and is false now, even though it's the same person, and I sort of feel that too — does the account have to be time-relative to get that, and is that a cost or a feature?
