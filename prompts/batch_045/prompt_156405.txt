Hey — I'm on this tiny grant committee that hands out maybe twelve small awards a year to one-off community projects, and about half the applications describe something none of us has ever seen before. We have the same argument every meeting in slightly different words: one member says 'compare it to everything broadly like it, that's the only class where you actually have numbers', another says 'no, you have to compare it to projects with this specific feature, otherwise you're averaging in stuff that's obviously disanalogous', and a third thinks the whole thing is just vagueness in the word 'similar' and that we should stop pretending there's a number. I read a bit about the reference class problem on my own and it's clearly the same dispute, so now I want a clean, engineered version of it to bring to the next meeting — something where I can see exactly what's doing the work.

So: build me one case, and build it to these specs. One paragraph, ordinary institutional setting (a committee, a hiring panel, a small lender — whatever you like), no sci-fi, no perfect predictors, no coin flips or urns, and please not a medical prognosis or an insurance premium, since that's the only version anybody ever writes down.

1. Every relevant base rate in the story is known exactly — to as many decimal places as you like. Nobody disagrees about the statistics or is missing data. The disagreement has to be about which statistic *is* the probability, not about ignorance of what the statistics are.
2. The target case has some feature with no exact precedent, so that every class containing it is either a singleton (nothing to count) or contains members that differ in a way everyone agrees matters. Net effect: no single class satisfies both the textbook 'narrowest class for which reliable statistics exist' rule and its rival 'widest homogeneous class' rule, and the two rules deliver numbers far enough apart to flip the decision either way at ordinary stakes.
3. Both candidate classes have to be genuinely natural — no gerrymandered disjunctions, nothing like 'projects that succeeded or were submitted in March'. If your case only works because one class is obviously contrived, it doesn't count.
4. It has to survive the Bayesian shrug. Don't give me something that dissolves the moment someone says 'just use your priors' or 'score the candidate models and pick the winner'. Build it so the same choice reappears inside the Bayesian machinery — the partition of cases treated as exchangeable, or the prior over which features are the relevant ones — and say in so many words how it reappears there.

Then show the case actually meets the specs: state the target proposition, list the candidate classes with their numbers, run each classical rule and say what it delivers, run the Bayesian version and say what it delivers, and check specs 1–4 one at a time rather than asserting them. The point of the case is to seperate a substantive disagreement from people quietly picking the class that gives the answer they wanted, so please generate the verdicts from the rules instead of stipulating them.

And then commit to something: which rule, if any, actually handles your case best — or is the honest answer that none does and the probability of a genuinely one-off event is just underdetermined? Take a side and give reasons; don't hand me the menu and let me pick. I'd also like you to name the single feature of the scenario doing the real work, say what happens if you delete it, tell me what a defender of whichever rule comes out worst would say to wriggle out, and flag where you're least confident — including if you think two of my four specs can't be met together and one has to give.
