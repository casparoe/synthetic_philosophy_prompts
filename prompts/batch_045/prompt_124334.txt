Here's the thing I can't reconcile. In probability teaching, the old Bertrand chord puzzle is a cautionary tale: take a circle, inscribe an equilateral triangle, pick a random chord, ask how likely it is to be longer than the triangle's side — and depending on whether you pick two random points on the circumference, or pick a random radius and then a random point along it, or pick a random point in the disc as the chord's midpoint, you get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4. The moral everyone draws is that "random", or "no reason to prefer one alternative over another", is not yet a well-defined question: the principle of indifference returns contradictory answers depending on how you parametrize, and the problem stays ill-posed until someone tells you the physical mechanism.

But the very same people who nod at that moral do the forbidden thing constantly. My friends in physics, engineering and ML have a parameter they know nothing about, so they slap a flat prior on it. Or they know a mean and nothing else and reach for the maximum-entropy distribution consistent with that. And when you point out that flat in theta is not flat in theta-squared — which is Bertrand's complaint in a different costume — they shrug, say something about the data swamping the prior, and carry on; and their bridges don't fall down and their classifiers work.

Then there's a repair I half-believe, and I want to know whether it's anything: the measurement-anchored version of indifference.
