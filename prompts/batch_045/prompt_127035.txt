I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about a mid-sized county that's being sued over the pretrial risk assessment tool its judges use, and I got walked into an argument at a workshop last month that I definately can't find the exit from. A statistician who consults for the vendor sat me down with a napkin and reconstructed it for me; he seemed to think it was obvious, which made me suspicious. Here it is, cleaned up. Say the thing the tool is trying to predict is whether someone released pending trial would come back to court and not be rearrested; call that succeeding.

(1) A decision procedure is fair only if, among the people who would in fact succeed, the rate at which it flags people for detention is the same in every group — equal false-detention rates, the "equally deserving people shouldn't face different odds" idea. (2) It is also fair only if a given risk score means the same thing whoever receives it: among the people the tool puts in the same risk bucket, the same fraction should go on to succeed in every group. (3) Both of these look like necessary conditions, not optional extras — each is what "not discriminating" requires in one of its two obvious senses. (4) But if the groups differ in their base rate of success, no procedure can satisfy both at once. His napkin: two groups of 1000; in group A, 600 would succeed, in group B, 400 would. The tool flags 30% of the succeeders and 30% of the non-succeeders in each group, so both error rates match exactly; but of the 300 people it flags in A, 180 would have succeeded (60%), while of the 300 it flags in B, only 120 would have (40%). The flag is a harsher verdict in one group than the other. (5) And this is a fact about procedures as such, not about algorithms: it doesn't care whether the decisions come from a gradient-boosted model, a magistrate with twenty years on the bench, a coin, or the status quo we had before anyone built a tool. So there's nothing we could swap the tool for that would be fair either. (6) Since each of (1) and (2) is necessary for fairness and nothing can have both, nothing is fair: "fair decision procedure" has no application. (7) Which means a rule that detains people because of their group, or a coin flip, or detaining literally everyone, is no worse than the best available alternative. (8) That's absurd, or at least I'm not putting it on air. (9) So something in (1)-(6) has to go.

I've redone the arithmetic at home and it checks out, so I don't think I can blame (4), and I'd rather keep (1) than (2) if I have to choose — (1) is the one I can defend in a sentence to a reader — but I want to know what keeping it costs me, and I want the other options priced too. Which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent? Two loose threads you can use or toss: the vendor's lawyer says the model is fair by construction because group membership isn't an input to it, only the charge and the record are, and he calls this the "swap test" (change the group line on a file, same decision comes out); and my source also muttered that the tool changes the base rates over time, since detained people can't succeed or fail on release, which I think makes things worse but I can't say why.
