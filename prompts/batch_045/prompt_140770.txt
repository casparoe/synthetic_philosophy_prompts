I am a journalist working on a long profile of a philosopher — I will call her Professor N., since she has asked not to be named before publication — who teaches in the Middle East and defends a contemporary version of occasionalism in the tradition of al-Ghazālī: created things have no causal power, there is no necessary connection between one created event and another, and what we ordinarily call a cause is at most the occasion on which God, by settled habit, brings the effect about.

Across two long interviews I put a series of cases to her and asked, in each, whether the item in question was a cause of what followed. Her verdicts, without exception, were no:

1. Fire's coming into contact with cotton is not a cause of the cotton's burning.
2. A thrown stone's striking a window is not a cause of the glass's breaking.
3. A virus's infecting a patient is not a cause of the illness, however reliably the illness follows.
4. Her own willing to raise her arm is not a cause of the arm's rising; when I pressed, she also denied that she herself is a cause of it, on the grounds that God creates the volition and the movement alike.
5. The spring and lever of a set mousetrap are not a cause of its snapping shut: a mechanism, she said, is in no better case than the flame.
6. An intravenous dose of a drug is not a cause of the patient's recovery.
7. A teacher's explaining a proof is not a cause of the student's coming to understand it.

Preparing for my last session with her, I noticed something about my own list. Every case I gave her was physical, biological, psychological, or pedagogical. Not one involved a machine executing a program, and not one involved a dependence that could be called formal or mathematical rather than merely habitual. I am no longer sure her answers settled as much as they appeared to at the time.

The case I want to put to her is this. A mobile robot works in a warehouse. Its sensors register a pallet left in an aisle; a perception module — a neural network whose parameters were fitted to data rather than written by anyone — classifies the pallet as an obstacle; a motion planner whose code has been machine-checked to issue a halt command within two hundred milliseconds of any obstacle classification issues that command; the brakes engage and the robot stops. No one designed or foresaw this particular stop at this particular moment. The engineers who wrote the planner intended generally that the robot halt for obstacles, but no one intends each individual halting, and the network's classification was not derived from any specification anyone wrote.

What I need from you is a prediction of her answer, and the reasoning that would support it. Specifically: does the robot's stopping have any created cause at all, and if so which — the sensor's registering the pallet, the network's classifying it as an obstacle, the planner's code or the machine's executing it, the electronics in the chassis, or the engineers' standing intention that the robot halt for obstacles? If some of these are causes and others merely occasions, where does she draw the line, and does the fact that the planner is proved while the classifier is only fitted make a difference to her?

Three further things would help me use the interview well. First, the different rules about what counts as an occasion that are consistent with all seven of her earlier verdicts, and what each of them predicts about this case. Second, which of those rules she is most likely running given that she argues from the denial of necessary connection, and how much confidence that guess deserves. Third, since I will have time for only one variation on the case, which single variant — changed in one respect — would do the most to tell the surviving rules apart, together with what each rule predicts about it. If there is a version of this case on which her denial of necessary connection and her treatment of machines pull against each other, that is the one I would most like to put to her.
