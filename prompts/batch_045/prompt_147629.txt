I co-organize a small fortnightly reading group — half philosophy graduate students, half economics and a couple of quants — and we have got stuck on something I would rather stage as a performed dialogue at our next meeting than discuss as another paper. Please write that dialogue for me: roughly 900 to 1,300 words, two speakers, no narration beyond minimal stage directions, spoken rather than lecture-style, and no citations or suggestions for further reading, since half the group will be somewhere without library access and I do not want the thing to turn into a bibliography.

The topic is whether market prices have any real epistemic authority in light of the no-trade results. Assume your audience knows the rough shape of those results but not the literature: if traders share a common prior, it is common knowledge that they are rational, and the contract's monetary payoff is the only reason anyone has for trading, then there should be no speculative trade at all — my knowing that you are willing to buy tells me you probably hold favourable private information, and that inference unravels whatever information I thought I had, the same reasoning that yields the agreement results also ruling out trade. Yet real markets trade enormous volume, and prediction-market prices often beat polls and pundits as forecasts. I want the two of them to fight about what fills that gap: whether the information markets aggregate is bought with the irrationality or the non-epistemic motives of other traders, so that a market is a machine for converting a measured dose of epistemically bad behaviour into knowledge; or whether the idealization itself is defective, because real traders are hedging outside exposures, managing time horizons and diverging in prior as a result of how their lives differ rather than because they disagree, so that information aggregation is a by-product of ordinary risk-sharing and the no-trade result is a theorem about a room full of people with nothing else to do.

The two speakers are equally competent, have read the same things, and neither may win on a strawman. **Ines Vartanian** defends what she calls the ignorance tariff: for a price to be informative somebody must be willing to lose money to the better informed, that willingness has to be paid for, so every informative market carries a subsidy to error, and the accuracy of the price is capped by how much error the operator is prepared to buy. **Tomás Halvorsen** defends the heterogeneity reply: the tariff framing misdescribes the counterparty, who is not mistaken but otherwise exposed — a fleet operator with a natural short position, a hedger, an impatient trader who needs resolution before the quarter closes — and what the market aggregates is the pressure of these outside positions rather than anyone's belief, which is why it can inform a bystander with no exposure at all. Have them work through two cases rather than argue in the abstract. First the dirty market: contracts on whether a city's congestion-charge scheme will still be in force two years from now, where many traders have correlated real-world exposure. Then the clean market: contracts on whether a much-cited psychology finding replicates in a pre-registered multi-lab study, where essentially nobody's income or plans depend on the outcome and volume comes almost entirely from people who think they know something. Ines should press Halvorsen hard on what the heterogeneity story predicts when the natural hedgers are absent, as in the clean case. Halvorsen should press Ines on a design I want them to fight over: imagine a credence escrow running alongside the market, in which anyone may register a sealed probability estimate that is never traded but merely averaged and published at resolution, so that the traded price can be compared against an unweighted poll of the same population. Ines will say the gap between escrow average and price just measures stake-weighting and the self-selection of the confident, showing the price to be an elite instrument rather than a discovery procedure; Halvorsen owes a real answer about what, if anything, the price adds over the escrow average besides its weighting by money, and Ines owes a real answer about whether her own view does not also predict that a heavily subsidized market should be more accurate, and what she would say if it turned out that the deeply subsidized clean markets are the ones that miss. Each should concede something genuine, and they should not end on a conciliatory handshake the group would find cheap.

After the dialogue, add a note of five to eight lines in your own voice, not either character's: how confident you are that the strongest argument actually made in the exchange is sound, and which single move or inference in it you are least confident about and why. Then finish with one final line, exactly one of \answer{Ines Vartanian}, \answer{Tomás Halvorsen}, or \answer{neither}, according to who you judge had the better of it.
