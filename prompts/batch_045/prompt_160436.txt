ok, I want an actual case analysis here, not a tour of the literature.

The episode: the Fermi paradox, treated as a piece of history rather than a puzzle box — Fermi's lunchtime question at Los Alamos in 1950, then something like seventy-five years of SETI null results, then the exoplanet revolution of the last three decades, which is what really sharpened it (rocky planets in temperate orbits turn out to be cheap and everywhere, so the escape hatch where habitable real estate is just rare is more or less shut). Take as given the usual backdrop: galaxy about 13 billion years old, a few hundred billion stars, a colonisation wave could cross it in a few tens of millions of years, and our actual search to date has covered a laughably small fraction of it. I'm not asking you for any of that and I don't want citations.

The theory: self-sampling vs self-indication, plus the presumptuous-philosopher style move that SIA is supposed to license.

What I want is the mapping done properly, in both directions. First, what exactly is the evidence? I think it's worth seperating at least two items: no probes or artefacts in our own solar system, and no astroengineering visible anywhere out in the galaxy — those seem to me to have quite different selection effects, and I suspect the two assumptions treat them differently. What are the hypotheses (filter behind us, filter ahead of us, no filter, rare earth, quiet civilisations, we're simply first)? What is the prior over the number of civilisations, and where does that prior come from once SSA or SIA has had its say? And who is doing the sampling — what's the reference class? Work through a few candidates: all observers; observers in civilisations that have started asking this question; observers in civilisations that have not yet been visited or colonised. Does the paradox sharpen or dissolve under each, and if it dissolves, is that a feature of the analysis or a sign you picked the reference class to get the answer you wanted?

Then the two bits I actually care about.

One: the silence discount. How much of the paradox is just that we have barely looked and would mostly miss a quiet civilisation anyway? Does SIA get to lean on that discount much harder than SSA does — is a self-indicator pushed toward the they're-there-and-we-haven't-noticed reading in a way a self-sampler isn't? If that asymmetry is real I want it stated precisely rather than gestured at, because it looks like the most interesting thing in the whole case.

Two: run it back at the theory. Is this episode evidence against SIA (it appears to commit you to a crowded galaxy you cannot see — the presumptuous philosopher again, except here the missing evidence is up there in the sky), evidence for it, or neither? And is the Fermi paradox an anthropic problem at all, or just ordinary Bayesian updating on how many civilisations there are, with SSA and SIA showing up only in the prior and doing no real work? Please say where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to speak. Finish with a discriminating prediction: what future finding — an independently evolved biosphere on Mars or Enceladus, a technosignature, or a genuinely exhaustive null survey — would move a self-sampler and a self-indicator in different directions, and roughly by how much?
