I am a second-year undergraduate reading Philosophy, and this term I am taking a course on Metaphysics and the Philosophy of Technology. Alongside it I run a small reading group, and I would like to build a decision tree that sorts the group by what they actually think about the simulation argument rather than by what they call themselves. The trouble I keep hitting is that discussion collapses into a single question, how likely is it that we are in a simulation, and then everyone who gives roughly the same middling number turns out to disagree with everyone else about everything that matters. So I want the tree deliberately not to lead with credence. I want its upper splits to concern what must be true for the hypothesis to be well posed at all, and its lower splits to concern which counting rules, selection effects and normative upshots the person accepts.

What I would like:

A decision tree with a single entry commitment that nearly everyone in the group will grant, namely that there is at least some description of the world we observe which a machine could in principle execute. Later nodes may retreat from that, but the tree should begin there. Then six or seven branch nodes, and no more. Every node should be a concrete case offering two or three verdicts to pick between, not an abstract statement of a thesis, and every node should be answerable by someone who has never encountered the argument.

Three cases must appear as nodes or as the material of nodes. First, the duplicate run: the same program with the same initial conditions is executed on two machines in the base world, and inside each execution someone is looking at a screen and wondering about exactly this. One experiencer or two? And does that answer change how observers should be counted when the ratio is taken? Second, the late start: a simulation is initiated in, say, 1800, stocked with fabricated geological strata, documents, memories and photographs of earlier centuries. Does the usual framing in terms of ancestor simulations have to be generalised, and does the case change what evidence is available from inside? Third, pruning: the simulators delete and restart any run that has stopped being interesting to them, or copy forward only branches that stay interesting. What does conditioning on our own continued existence do to the inference, and to the thought that our rather humdrum century counts against the hypothesis?

Other distinctions I want the tree to be sensitive to, though you can choose which of them become nodes and which get folded into a node's answer options: whether executing the right structure is sufficient for consciousness or whether some substrate or some level of grain is required, and if the latter, where the cut-off falls; whether there is any finite bound on levels of nesting, and what happens to the ratio if there is not; whether the hypothesis has any content beyond predictions about glitches, artefacts and termination; whether learning that we are simulated would change what anyone owes to anyone, including any simulations we might build ourselves; and whether our own intention to build simulations is itself evidence about what posthuman civilisations do.

There should be eight to ten leaves. Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or unnamed if you think nobody holds it, together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. These must all appear among the leaves: the consciousness chauvinist who denies that simulated minds are minds at all; the deflationist who holds that the hypothesis has no content beyond predictions about glitches and termination; the person who accepts unbounded nesting and concludes that there is no base level; the measure sceptic who holds that the ratio of simulated to unsimulated observers is undefined and that we should therefore suspend judgement; the person who accepts the third disjunct and takes it to carry real consequences; the person whose position is driven by observer selection effects such as pruning; and someone for whom the hypothesis makes a normative difference, either to our relations with the simulators or to our relations with any simulations we create.

Please build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of my list of names: note in passing which combinations of answers the nodes permit, and locate the named positions in that space. Do not include any bibliography, references or suggested reading; I have those from the course.

Please structure your reply in the following sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

- Reading the tree, about 80 words, on the notation and how to walk it.
- The tree, about 600 words, giving the entry commitment, every branch node with its case and verdict options, and the routing.
- The leaves, about 400 words, one entry per leaf with its name, one sentence of core commitment, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- Unoccupied combinations, about 250 words, on the regions of the logical space that no position occupies, with a brief diagnosis of each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- What the tree cannot separate, about 200 words, flagging any pair of leaves that reach the same verdict on every case while differing in what they say is doing the work, rather than forcing a distinction between them.
- Confidence, about 150 words, saying how confident you are in the classification, which single node or distinction you think is bearing the most weight, and identifying the one point in the whole tree you are least sure about.
