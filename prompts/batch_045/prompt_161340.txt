I am working through the literature on backward induction for a project on trust and coordination, and I recently listened to a podcast episode that presented the centipede game as a closed case. I would like to use its account as the frame for a short talk I am giving to a general audience, but I am not confident enough about the details to know whether the frame is sound. Could you audit it for me? The host's summary ran roughly as follows:

"Two players take turns with a pile of money. On your turn you either grab it — you take the larger share and the game ends — or you hand it over, the pile roughly doubles, and the other player faces the same choice. There is a fixed number of moves and both players know what it is. Now reason backwards. On the last move, whoever moves grabs, since grabbing beats handing over. On the move before, the other player can therefore see that handing over just loses them the pile, so they grab there instead. Repeat that step all the way back and you reach the first move: the first player grabs immediately and the game ends before it has properly begun. They get a couple of coins; had both passed a few times, both would have left with many times as much. The reasoning is just iterated dominance, so it never depends on what either player believes about the other, on greed, or on psychology — it follows from rationality and common knowledge and nothing else, and it is a theorem about the game rather than a hypothesis about human nature. Nor is there any room in the model for hoping that the other player will cooperate: the outcome is forced. This is what makes the laboratory results so striking. In the experiments, hardly anyone grabs on the first move, most pairs pass several times, and both participants end up far richer than the theory says they should. That is about as direct a demonstration as we have that human beings are not rational agents. It is also, in a way, the game theorist's refutation of the invisible hand: rational self-interest here does not quietly aggregate into a good outcome, it destroys value at the first opportunity, and the more rounds you add, the more violent the collapse."

Two things make me doubt it, though I cannot locate the problem. First, a friend who works in behavioural economics said the whole result rests on the assumption that no one ever errs and no one is ever altruistic, and that once you allow even a small probability of either, passing becomes worthwhile for several rounds — but I cannot tell whether that merely relocates the puzzle. Second, the episode seems to want both to conclude that rationality is ruinous and to conclude that people are not rational, and I cannot tell whether those two claims can both stand.

A colleague also proposed a variant I could not settle: the same alternating structure and the same fixed, known end, the pile still doubles whenever it is handed over, but whoever grabs receives exactly half of it. Does the unravelling survive there, and what does the answer tell us about which feature of the payoff structure the host's argument is really exploiting?

Concretely, I would be grateful for the following:

1. A claim-by-claim audit of the passage: what a game theorist working on these foundations would accept as stated, what they would regard as a defensible simplification for a popular treatment, and what they would reject outright — with the reason in each case.
2. An inventory of the assumptions doing the real work, and what happens to the conclusion when each is relaxed: that each player maximizes their own monetary payoff; that money is a proxy for utility; that rationality is common knowledge; that rationality remains common knowledge at nodes reachable only if someone has already behaved inconsistently with it; that no one errs; that no one has other-regarding preferences.
3. A verdict on the experimental claim. Is the observed pattern evidence that people are irrational, or is it equally evidence for something else (social preferences, uncertainty about the other player's type, noise or trembles, limited depth of iterated reasoning, low stakes)? Can the competing explanations be told apart in principle, and if so by what kind of design?
4. A ruling on the final flourish: does adding rounds make the collapse more violent, or is something else going on — and in particular, is the equilibrium prediction itself sensitive to the length of the game?
5. A ruling on the invisible-hand conclusion: does anything in the game support it, and can it be held together with the claim that laboratory subjects are not rational agents?
6. Finally, what a fair three-sentence version of the summary would say, and what it would have to concede that the host does not.

Please argue the points out rather than pointing me to papers or naming authors; I have no library access at the moment and would rather have the reasoning than the citations.
