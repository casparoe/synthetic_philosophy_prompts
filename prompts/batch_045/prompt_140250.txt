I'm retired now — forty-odd years in entomology, mostly beetle systematics, dont hold it against me — and I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university, which is how I ended up unable to let go of something from a session we did on animal sentience a few weeks ago. The thing that's stuck is the scale argument, in roughly this form: even if the chance that any given beetle or aphid is conscious at all is low — you pick the number, one in a hundred, one in ten thousand — the headcount of insects alive at any moment is so enormous (10^18 or so) that the expected quantity of suffering swamps whatever we do about vertebrates; multiply a small probability by an astronomical number and the insects dominate the moral arithmetic by default. My instinct, and I admit it may just be professional bias talking, is that this is a bug in the arithmetic rather than a moral discovery, but I can't say where the bug is.

So here's what I want. Someone in the group claimed Bentham would have loved this argument, on the grounds that his whole move about animals — that the relevant question is whether they can suffer, not whether they can reason or talk — already severed sentience from any demanding cognitive test, and that his felicific calculus has a slot for exactly this sort of trade-off. I wrote the seven circumstances down off the whiteboard: intensity, duration, certainty or uncertainty, propinquity or remoteness, fecundity, purity, and extent, where extent is the number of persons affected; and his own little mnemonic runs "Intense, long, certain, speedy, fruitful, pure— / Such marks in pleasures and in pains endure" and "If it be public, wide let them extend." What I'd like you to do is put Bentham — the 1789 Bentham, no Darwin behind him, no neurophysiology, no continuity-of-mind story, no way of knowing anything about nociceptors or about how small a nervous system can be — in front of the scale argument as I've stated it and tell me what he actually does with it. Specifically: does extent in his hands do the work the argument needs, given that his own procedure says to start with one person and then take account of the number of persons whose interests are concerned, which looks like it counts actual persons rather than candidate ones; how is certainty supposed to interact with extent when the uncertainty is doing all the sceptical work here; and does he have any resources at all for the wrinkle I keep coming back to, namely that an awful lot of that 10^18 is larvae and other short-lived juveniles, so that once you ask not how many insects but how many insect-lives of a sort that could plausibly contain suffering, the relevant number may be orders of magnitude smaller. I'd also like an honest say on where this whole exercise breaks down: is there anything in the calculus, or in his wider views about punishment and about felt experience being the only evidence that counts, that makes him a bad guide to a case he never imagined; and would he be more or less moved by the argument than a utilitarian writing after Darwin, who has the continuity story available to lean on?

Give me something in the 600–900 word range, don't hedge it all into mush, and if there's a feature of the argument he'd have been scandalised by, say so plainly. End with a single line in a fixed form so I can tally what people come back with: \answer{...} containing exactly one of three labels — SCALE_WINS if you think he'd have accepted the argument and given insects priority, SCALE_LOSES if you think he had resources to reject or deflate it, or CALCULUS_BREAKS if you think he'd have concluded the calculus simply cannot return a verdict here.
