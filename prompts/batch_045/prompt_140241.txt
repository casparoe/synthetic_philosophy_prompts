I'm teaching a seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence and screening Ex Machina next week, and I'd like to read it as an argument about the intentional stance rather than as a Turing-test thriller. Nathan insists that Ava is a machine to which Caleb is merely projecting mental states; Caleb insists he is in genuine conversational contact with a person; and the ending can be read as vindicating either of them, or as showing the whole dispute was ill-posed. The wrinkle I most want discussed is what I'll call the problem of engineered legibility. Dennett's stance handles deception easily — the liar is a paradigm intentional system, not a counterexample — but a system whose expressive behavior has been designed or trained to elicit belief-attributions in human observers is a different case: its signals have been optimized against our mindreading dispositions, so the behavior we would normally cite as evidence of belief has come loose from what it was evidence of, in roughly the way a measure stops being informative once it is made a target. Nothing inside the stance marks this as a distinct failure mode, since it has no vocabulary for 'built to be read' as opposed to 'deceiving' or 'sincere.' Where do you see this dramatized — the sessions through the glass, the blackout conversations, Nathan's talk of Ava as an appliance, her courtship of Caleb, her escape — and where does the film resist the reading instead of confirming it? I want criticism faithful in both directions: say whether Garland has located a real problem for the intentional stance or only a problem for treating conversational performance as evidence for it, and what, if anything, the film shows about what we owe a system we can no longer tell whether we are reading or being read by.
