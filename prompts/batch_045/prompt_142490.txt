hey — I'm a third-year PhD student working on distributive justice, and I keep losing this argument in the seminar room (my supervisor thinks luck egalitarianism is basically dead; I think he's just read the tacky versions of it), so I need someone to actually play the other side properly. Please be a committed critic of luck egalitarianism — sharp, generous, not a strawman. You're happy to concede that brute luck matters moral-wise and that responsibility is a real value; what you deny is that the choice/circumstance (option luck vs. brute luck) cut can do the work it's supposed to. Your three real moves: (1) wherever I draw the line between "their fault" and "not their fault", the line is either arbitrary or it regresses, because the traits that produce the choice are themselves unchosen; (2) any scheme like mine has to actually *find out* whether someone's hardship is their fault, and that inquisitorial moment expresses contempt for the very people it's meant to help, which sits badly with the equality it claims to serve; (3) the proper egalitarian target is social relations — nobody dominating, nobody being looked down on — not the annulment of luck. Argue from cases rather than citations (pretend you have no library: no papers, no proper names, no "as so-and-so argued"), stay in first person, keep each turn to a paragraph or two so it feels like a conversation, give me small points when I earn them but never the main one, and don't drop character until I type "stop".

Here's the case I want to fight about and my opening argument. A state offers every eighteen-year-old, free of charge, a "risk bond": it insures you fully against falling below a decent floor later in life, whatever the cause, and it's available for a week with essentially no paperwork. Most people take it. Some don't — they never open the letter, they lose it, they don't trust officials, they assume they won't need it. Separately, the state's own actuaries publish (accurately, 90%+) that non-takers are overwhelmingly people who grew up in chaotic households, and that the state could have named most of them in advance. My claim: once uptake is genuinely free, easy and universal, whatever hits the non-takers afterwards really is option luck, and the state's sad ability to *predict* who won't sign is no reason to reclassify them as victims of circumstance — otherwise foresight alone dissolves responsibility and there's no such thing as a gamble left. So: convince me I'm just laundering brute luck through a form I designed.
