I have been working through the margin-for-error argument against luminosity and what it does to the KK principle, and a colleague has extended it somewhere I cannot follow and also cannot answer. I would like a diagnosis: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to?

Let me fix vocabulary so that we are discussing the same things. By a case I mean a centred possible situation: a subject, at a time, in an environment. A condition is something that obtains or fails to obtain in a case. Three principles:

(M) If S knows, in case α, that condition C obtains, then there is a δ > 0 such that C obtains in every case within distance δ of α. Knowledge needs a margin for error: you cannot know something that would have been false had things gone a hair differently.

(LUM) A condition C is luminous for S if and only if, for every case α in which C obtains, S is in a position to know in α that C obtains.

(KK) If S knows that p, then S knows that S knows that p. KK implies in particular that the condition expressed by "S knows that p" is luminous for S — more than luminous, in fact, since on KK S actually knows it whenever it obtains.

The argument against KK, as I was taught it:

(1) Assume KK, and let α0 be a case in which S plainly knows that p — she read it on a reliable calendar a minute ago.

(2) There is a sequence of cases α0 … αn, consecutive members differing by less than any margin you like, running from α0 to a case αn in which S does not know that p: the calendar was quietly edited, the light was a little worse, S is a little more tired, and so on. No step in the sequence is one that S could tell apart from its predecessor.

(3) In α0, S knows that p, so by KK she knows that she knows that p. By (M) applied to the condition expressed by "S knows that p," that condition obtains throughout some neighbourhood of α0 — in particular, at α1.

(4) The same reasoning now applies at α1, then at α2, and so on along the sequence: at each αi the condition obtains, KK converts its obtaining into knowledge of its obtaining, and (M) projects the condition on to αi+1.

(5) So S knows that p in αn, which was chosen as a case in which she does not. Contradiction: KK is false. And running (3) with "is in a position to know" in place of "knows" refutes the weaker principle too — S is never even in a position to know that she knows.

Steps (1) to (5) I can live with; I had already given up KK. Where I lose the thread is what my colleague does next.

(6) He points out that (1) to (5) used nothing about p, nothing about S's first-order method, and nothing about which feature of the cases was varied along the sequence. It used only: a condition that sometimes obtains and sometimes fails; a chain of arbitrarily fine steps joining a case where it obtains to a case where it fails; and a reflection principle that turns the condition's obtaining into knowledge of its obtaining. So the real content of the argument, he says, is this — a condition can be luminous only if no chain of arbitrarily fine steps joins a case where it obtains to a case where it fails; only if it is, in his word, gapped, meaning that there is some minimum distance between its obtaining and its failing to obtain.

(7) Very well, I said: then let us build a gapped condition. Consider an engineered agent — call her Aurelia — with a bistable internal register R that records whether an internal magnitude m exceeds a fixed threshold t. Stipulate that the device's physics guarantees |m − t| ≥ ε, for a fixed ε > 0, in every case in which the device is operating. Stipulate further that Aurelia's read-out channel returns the correct value of R in every such case, and add as much redundancy and error correction as you please. The condition expressed by "R reads 1" then has clearance: its obtaining is separated from its failing by a gap, because there is no case for a fine-grained chain to pass through. By (6), it is luminous.

(8) He replies that the chain in (2) never had to run along m. Run it along the noise in the read-out channel instead, or along the radiation dose the register is receiving, or along how thoroughly a tinkerer has tampered with the device, or along whether Aurelia is asleep and hallucinating the read-out. Each of these varies continuously; along at least one of them the register eventually reads 0; and none of them is distinguishable from the inside. The clearance in m is beside the point, because a case has indefinitely many dimensions to vary along, and only one of them has to carry the condition across.

(9) So no condition is gapped: for any condition a subject can be in, the space of cases contains fine chains from its obtaining to its failing, routed through faults, tampering, dreams and deceivers. Nothing is luminous. Not only is KK false; no agent, however well engineered, however much clearance, redundancy and verification is built into it, is ever in a position to know its own states — and if, as a long tradition has held, knowing that p requires being in a position to know that one knows that p, then nothing is known at all.

I cannot accept (9), and I do not know which of (1) to (8) to surrender. What I want to keep: that we sometimes know things; that (M) is true, at least in some neighbourhood of its ordinary use; that a system built the way Aurelia is built can at least sometimes be in a position to know its own states; and that KK is false, which I have conceded already. I tried objecting that being in a position to know, as (LUM) has it, is weaker than knowing, so that (M) never gets a grip; he answers by asking me to consider the case in which the capacity is exercised, and then I have no reply.

Tell me which step is doing work it cannot do, why that step looked sound when I first accepted it, and what each of the other ways out would cost me — in particular whether any way of rejecting (8) leaves the original argument against KK intact. Please also say how confident you are in the diagnosis you give, and name the single claim in your reply that you are least sure of.
