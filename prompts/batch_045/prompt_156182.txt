I have been trying to get clear about the structure of the standard counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis of dispositions, and in particular about why the usual repair works where it works. So let me set out the machinery I take for granted, then give you a case that I think the machinery misclassifies, and ask you to diagnose it.

The simple analysis says: x is disposed to M in circumstances S iff, were S to obtain, x would M. The counterexamples are cases where the disposition is intuitively present but the conditional is false. In a *fink* case the stimulus destroys the disposition's own categorical basis, so the conditional fails because the disposition is gone by the time it could be exercised. In a *mask* case the basis survives but something else blocks the manifestation, so the conditional fails although the disposition remains throughout. In an *antidote* case the stimulus itself brings the mask into existence: S triggers the interfering factor, which then prevents M. The repair I have in mind is the basis-fixing one: x is disposed to M when S iff x has some intrinsic basis B such that, were x in S and retaining B for long enough, x would M. I take it that this is supposed to rescue the fink cases in particular, and I want to know exactly what it is about finks that makes holding the basis fixed help.

Here is my case. A bar of alloy has microstructure B, and B grounds a disposition to dissolve rapidly in an acid bath at a fixed concentration and temperature: left unimpeded, the whole bar would go into solution in about sixty seconds. But contact with the acid generates a solid reaction product P at the metal surface, and P adheres into a coherent, impermeable film roughly half a second after immersion, after which dissolution stops entirely. Net observable result: a few microns of surface are lost and then nothing. Three further stipulations. First, P is produced by the dissolution events themselves, not by the acid: a chemically inert block of the same shape immersed alongside generates no P and does not affect the bar. Second, the film does not touch B: shave it off in air and the exposed surface is still B. Third, if the film is mechanically removed while the bar is immersed, dissolution resumes and re-passivates after another half second, and repeated scrubbing dissolves the bar completely; likewise, adding a surfactant that keeps P from adhering yields complete dissolution in the original sixty seconds.

So the interfering factor here is not triggered by the stimulus and then added to the situation; it is generated downstream by the very exercise of the disposition it blocks, and it blocks the remainder of a manifestation that has already begun. Call it an *auto-mask*. I would like the following, working from the case rather than from any reading.

1. Where does the auto-mask sit relative to finks, masks, and antidotes, if anywhere? Is a fourth category really needed, or is the apparent novelty an artifact of how the three existing categories were described? Say what the taxonomy is for, and whether it survives.

2. Run the basis-fixing repair on the case and tell me exactly where it breaks. My own thought is that it fails because the interfering factor is a nomological product of B's exercise, so it is present in every world in which the bar is immersed and retains B, whereas in a fink case the interfering factor destroys B and so is absent from every world in which B is retained. Check that reasoning, and then say what would have to be added to the conditional to handle auto-masks: some clause requiring that the manifestation be unimpeded, presumably. Explain how such a clause avoids circularity, since *impediment* cannot just mean *anything that prevents M* on pain of trivializing the analysis.

3. Tell me whether the case shows the fink/mask/antidote distinction to be metaphysically shallow. Consider two variations. In the first, an otherwise identical bar contains a trace dopant that makes P non-adherent, and dissolves completely. In the second, we take the bar together with its own passivating film to be the object, so that the self-limiting behaviour becomes intrinsic. If the classification of a case shifts with how the object and its basis are individuated, what work is the taxonomy doing?

4. Finally, the epistemology and the upshot. The naive immersion test reports insolubility; the scrub test and the surfactant test report solubility. Which test tracks the disposition, and what principle picks it out? Then make the case gradual: let the ratio of film-formation time to total dissolution time vary continuously with acid concentration, so that across the range the bar goes from losing a negligible surface layer to dissolving entirely. Is there a sharp fact about whether the bar is disposed to dissolve at a given concentration, and if not, what should we say? Close by telling me whether the auto-mask case favours a powers or causal-structural account of dispositions over any conditional analysis, or whether both have the same resources here.

Where an answer of yours depends on a stipulation I have not fixed, flag it and say how the answer moves if the stipulation goes the other way. Name positions rather than authors, and do not spend space on bibliography.
