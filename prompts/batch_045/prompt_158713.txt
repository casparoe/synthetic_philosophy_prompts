I host a small philosophy podcast — one guest, ninety minutes, and a standing rule that I never open with anything that lets a guest simply restate the view they are known for. My next guest is a philosopher (I will keep her unnamed) who has spent about a decade defending a naturalness solution to Goodman's new riddle of induction: her claim is that 'green' is projectible while 'grue' — the predicate that applies to emeralds examined before some specified future date just in case they are green, and to emeralds examined after it just in case they are blue — is not, because green picks out an elite or natural property and grue picks out a metaphysically gerrymandered one; on her reading, Goodman's own talk of entrenchment was a placeholder for this metaphysical fact rather than a competing account of projectibility. I want questions that get past her prepared formulations, and I have a specific reason for pushing: my co-host is a statistician who insists that grue is just overfitting with a fancy name and that the entire debate is philosophers rediscovering model selection, and I would like at least one question that forces her to answer that charge seriously instead of brushing it off.

Please draft eight interview questions, each with a follow-up attached, and make sure the set covers the following pressure points. First, circularity: on her account, do we identify the natural properties independently of which hypotheses our science has in fact confirmed, or is the naturalness of a property in practice read back off our inductive successes, in which case the criterion seems to presuppose what the riddle asks? Second, gerrymandering along a dimension other than time: I have been playing with predicates of the shape 'green if measured north of the equator, blue otherwise', and with observer-relative versions such as 'green if examined by me, blue if examined by anyone else', and I want her to say whether her criterion rules these out too, and if so whether the temporal case was ever doing any special work in Goodman's argument. Third, the awkward fact that mature science is full of what I think of as grue-shaped predicates — properties that obtain below a critical temperature, or below a threshold pressure or mass, and flip once the threshold is crossed — which look exactly like gerrymanders before the threshold is located and yet turn out to be among our best-confirmed hypotheses; I want to know whether her criterion would have declared them unprojectible ex ante, and if not, what distinguishes them from grue. Fourth, what would count as evidence against her account, and more pointedly whether there is any possible discovery that could lead us to withdraw projectibility from an ordinary colour predicate.

Across the eight items, please also press on the following: whether a community that had always spoken in grue-shaped terms would by her lights be reasoning correctly and merely unlucky, or reasoning badly, and what the difference comes to; whether her criterion still draws a line when applied to iterated gerrymanders — a grue-like predicate constructed on top of a predicate that is itself already grue-like — or whether it only works one level down; and whether the view has any cash value beyond entrenchment talk, which I want her to answer by naming one predicate in current scientific practice that she thinks we project and should not. Give me exactly eight numbered items, each one or two sentences: the question itself, then the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. No preamble, no sign-off, no headings, and nothing in the register of 'what first drew you to this topic'.
