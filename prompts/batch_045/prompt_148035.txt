I'm a journalist working on a piece about dementia and advance care planning, and I keep running into an asymmetry in how medicine handles the authority of the earlier self. I'd like to know what the practice presupposes.

Courts and clinicians generally treat a competent person's written refusal of life-sustaining treatment as binding years later, after she has developed advanced dementia — and they do so even in the familiar, uncomfortable cases where the person now seems content, no longer recognises her family, and would, if asked, want to go on living. But the same framework is much less willing to honour the earlier self's positive requests: an advance directive asking to be admitted and medicated during a future psychiatric crisis, or asking for a treatment the clinical team now regards as inappropriate or futile, is typically treated as advisory and set aside. Deference to the earlier self runs strongly in one direction and weakly in the other.

So, what view of that authority does the asymmetry commit us to? Must a system that honours prior refusals hold that the earlier and later selves are the same person or the same agent in some demanding sense — psychological continuity, or the unity of a life as a single narrative project — or can it get by with something metaphysically lighter: the thought that a life is one thing to be governed, or that the earlier self is simply the most reliable evidence about what the later one values? Is one principle doing all the work, or is the practice quietly running an identity-based account for refusals and a welfare- or interests-based account for requests? And where exactly does it strain: does honouring a refusal while the patient is apparently content require ranking the earlier self's evaluative or "critical" interests over the later self's experiential ones, in a way the practice never acknowledges?

I'd also like to know what the practice would have to look like if it gave up the identity-based story. Suppose hospitals offered an optional "continuity clause" on the directive form: tick one box and your instructions still govern if you later stop recognising your family or stop caring about what you once cared about; tick the other and the instructions lapse, and clinicians should then go by your apparent well-being. What should a hospital do with each box, and is the second box anything a system could coherently accept — or does accepting it concede that the first box was never really about who the patient is?

Finally, please separate what the current practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits comfortably with it, and tell me what a defender of strong precedent autonomy would say to explain the asymmetry between refusals and requests rather than just retreating from one limb.
