I've been asked to serve as the token political philosopher on the research-advisory group of a constitutional-reform commission, and I want to use my one real lever — a small pot of money for conceptual and normative work, roughly six postdocs for two years plus a handful of commissioned papers — well. The commission is considering replacing the upper chamber of a mid-sized parliamentary democracy with a randomly selected assembly whose members serve terms of randomly drawn, unpredictable length, with a continuous trickle of replacements rather than synchronized turnover. Almost nothing else about the design is settled yet: whether service is compulsory (on the jury-duty model) or drawn only from a pool of self-selected volunteers; whether members may delegate their vote or their deliberation to expert advisers; what jurisdiction the chamber has relative to the elected lower house; and who sets the rules of the lottery itself, including who controls the chamber's agenda. The commission must fix these details within about eighteen months, so the research has to be decision-relevant on that timescale, and my colleagues in the politics department keep reminding me that any question whose answer depends on how real assemblies actually behave belongs to their budget, not mine.

What I want from you is a defended research agenda, not a survey. Give me the four or five open conceptual questions you would prioritize, in order, and argue for the ordering. The criteria I care about are: how many other design questions depend on the answer (upstream-ness); whether philosophers specifically can make progress on it in two years with the tools they already have, as opposed to its being an empirical question in disguise; and whether the answer actually has to be in hand before the commission locks the design, as opposed to being something that could be sorted out later by constitutional practice or litigation. I also want you to say which questions you would deliberately decline to fund and why, including any that are currently fashionable, and which alleged stakes of the sortition debate you think are overrated or don't follow — for instance, I'm suspicious of the claim that a lottery chamber would reduce polarization, and I'd like to know whether that's even the sort of thing a normative argument could establish.

Three things would make the agenda especially useful to me. First, the justifications on offer seem to be pulling the design in incompatible directions: a lottery can be defended as a device for preventing the formation of a ruling class, as a way of distributing a fair share of political office, as a measuring instrument that estimates what the citizenry would want under better conditions of information and deliberation, or as a mode of authorization — and the authorization story is the one that seems weakest, since a randomly drawn chamber apparently isn't authorized by anyone in the way an elected one is. Those rationales recommend different term lengths, different stances on compulsion and delegation, and different relationships to the elected house, so I want to know which one the program should try to settle first and whether the others can be treated as downstream. Second, I want the design features I listed treated as carrying philosophical weight in their own right — does unpredictable, individually random tenure undermine or support whatever makes a lottery chamber legitimate, and does permitting delegation hollow out the case for selection by lot? Third, tell me what would move a question up or down your ranking: what you would change if the chamber were given taxing or constitutional-amendment powers, if the lower house retained agenda control, or if I told you the commission will almost certainly reject compulsory service.
