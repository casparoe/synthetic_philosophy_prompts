I'm assembling a packet of short documents for an undergraduate seminar on political legitimacy, and the one slot I cannot fill with real material is the one on output- or performance-based legitimation: the move, familiar from the input/output distinction in the European-union legitimacy literature, of treating effective problem-solving for a population as a freestanding ground of authority, and then applying it far more ambitiously to the developmental states — the dominant-party city-state and the single-party continental power — where it is used to argue that contested elections are a contingent instrument rather than a requirement, and that a government which reliably delivers rising prosperity and public order has already supplied whatever consent-based accounts demand. I want the students to feel how much intellectual machinery that view has, and then to see where it strains, and my experience is that they take in both at once from a well-built parody far better than from a pro-and-con list.

So: write me an in-universe document rather than an essay. Specifically, an "Independent Legitimacy Assurance Report" for a fictional state, prepared by a fictional large accounting-and-consulting firm of your own invention (do not reproduce or paraphrase any real audit report, and do not name any living politician — the officials quoted inside the document should be invented), covering the fiscal year just ended and addressed to "the Stakeholders." Around 800 words, laid out as an assurance opinion followed by numbered notes to the legitimacy statement, in a deadpan professional register: audit, accounting and management-consulting jargon should carry the entire thing. The humor has to come from getting the position right — the ruler-subject relation modeled on service delivery, the state as an outcomes-based enterprise, depoliticized expertise standing in for contested procedure, the claim that competitive elections would degrade delivery, the standing promise that fuller participation is sequenced for later — and from exaggerating those real features rather than inventing new ones.

The notes should let a reader who knows the literature recognise, without a single one of them ever being asserted in plain words, the objections that actually bite. Please include at least: (i) a basis-of-measurement note on how prosperity and order are quantified, including the like-for-like adjustment required once someone asks how much of the year's growth would have arrived anyway under any merely competent administration; (ii) an emphasis-of-matter paragraph on what I think of as the swap problem — that if output is the whole ground of authority, then any rival claimant, including a foreign administration or a purely algorithmic one, that could be projected to deliver slightly better numbers would thereby be the more legitimate ruler, and the incumbent has nothing left to say against it; (iii) a note on the engagement itself — who appointed and pays the auditor, and what becomes of the opinion when the auditee sets the metrics; (iv) a going-concern paragraph on what happens to the authority recognised here if the numbers stop rising; (v) treatment of the items the framework cannot measure — consent, dignity, the standing possibility of throwing the government out — as unrecognised intangibles carried at nil on the ground that they are not capable of reliable measurement; (vi) the deferral of the transition to fuller participation, restated each year, as a long-term liability with no maturity date; and (vii) some disclosure of how prosperity is distributed across the population and discounted across cohorts, including the rate applied to people not yet born. Close with a signed opinion that is visibly qualified by all of the above and still concludes that the government's authority is presented fairly, in all material respects. No stated moral, no winking, no authorial aside: a reader who has never opened a book on legitimacy should be able to tell what is being ironised, and a reader who has should think the joke is fair.
