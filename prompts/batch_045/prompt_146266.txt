I teach a second-year course called something like Collective Decisions, and I'm also on the board of my housing co-op (41 units, we own the building) — and those two things have just collided in an annoying way. Last month we voted to move big capital decisions off the 3-person maintenance commitee and to the whole membership, simple majority. I was the one who pushed for it, and I justified it out loud with the Condorcet jury theorem. Now I'm having second thoughts, because of a guy named Dale. Dale has opinions about everything, he's right maybe 70% of the time, and I'd guess around thirty of our members just vote however Dale votes. So I'm writing this month's take-home problem set and I want to turn our situation into the last question, partly so I can figure out whether I talked the building into something stupid.

Here's what I need from you: one multi-part question, take-home, about 700 words for a strong answer, 30 marks, built around a scenario basically like ours. What students have already had: the classic set-up and proof — homogeneous competence, everyone independently right with the same p > 1/2, majority accuracy climbs to 1 as the group grows. They can do binomial probabilities and a normal approximation, calculators allowed, no sources needed (please don't build anything that requires them to cite papers — they can't, and it's not what I'm testing).

Rough materials, use what's useful: the old commitee was 3 people, each right with probability 0.7, independent — I make that 0.784 for a majority of 3. The general membership is more like 0.55 per person on a question like this. There are 41 of us, 5 or so reasonably informed and the rest fairly casual, and about 30 of the casual ones effectively copy Dale. I think the punchline is supposed to be something like: if Dale is wrong, 31 votes are wrong and nothing can save us, so the whole 41-person assembly is exactly as accurate as Dale is — 0.7 — and adding more members does literally nothing. I also worked out that even if all 41 were fully independent at 0.55, majority accuracy at n = 41 is only somewhere around 0.74 (normal approximation, so probably a bit off — give me the exact number in a separate answer key for me so I don't embarrass myself in section). If both of those are right, then the reform made us worse even in the best case, and the theorem I cited is a limit theorem being used about a group of forty-one. Please check the arithmetic rather than taking my word for it.

What I actually want the question to punish is the reflex that 'more voters is more accurate'. Beyond the two computations I'd like it to force some or all of these moves, and I'd like you to tell me if I'm missing a better crux: (a) saying what the real engine of the theorem is — independent signals, not headcount — and why deference turns 30 voters into one; (b) handling a mixed case where people are sometimes independent and sometimes swept up together, ideally getting to the point that a shared component puts a ceiling on group accuracy that no amount of growth gets past; (c) noticing that the theorem needs there to be a vote-independent fact of the matter at all. On (c): our actual decision was whether to replace the roof now or keep patching it for another five years, which I kept describing as a question of fact, but there's obviously a values component — how much risk we want, whether we care about disruption — and I have a hunch the honest fix is to split the decision into the factual part and the preference part and admit the theorem only licenses the first. I'd love the question to get students there on their own, but tell me if that's the wrong lesson.

Deliverables, please: the question itself in a form I can paste into the problem set; a model answer of about 600 words; and a marking scheme with the must-hit points, the marks each is worth out of 30, and a one-line note on how students typically botch each one. I especially want the scheme to say what separates an A from a B — the B answer should be the one that does the probability correctly but treats it as a maths exercise and never says why it matters for the argument about expanding the franchise. And I don't want it to be a probability drill with a philosophy sticker on it: if a sub-part doesn't make a conceptual point land, cut it.
