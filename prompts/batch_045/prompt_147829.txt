I want a map of the exposure of a position I hold, and I want it built for someone who already accepts the framing rather than for a skeptic being talked into it.

The position has two parts, and the conjunction is the part I am least sure about. First, epistemicism about subsystem states: for any subsystem I can pick out, its quantum state is not a physical property of it but encodes what an agent—or, if you prefer, what any physically implementable information-gathering procedure situated elsewhere—is in a position to predict and to bet on. I explicitly deny the disjointness claim: two distinct pure states assigned to the same subsystem may correspond to overlapping distributions over the underlying states, and collapse is updating, not a physical process. My motive here is parsimony plus explanatory yield: interference, the information-disturbance tradeoffs, and no-cloning should fall out of the structure of ignorance rather than be brute. Second, nomism about the universal state: the state of the cosmos is not a thing and not anyone's information either; it plays the role of a dynamic global constraint on the total history. That, I hoped, is why the universal state never collapses and why its evolution is deterministic while probabilities appear only from within.

Please tell me what would count against this package, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and for each item say what the world—or what the formalism—would have to be like for it to bite, and whether a defender of the package should concede it or can dismiss it as resting on a notion of physical or law I am entitled to reject.

I want you to press on these in particular:

1. The no-go family that derives disjointness from the assumption that independently prepared systems have independently distributed underlying states, plus the screening-off assumption, plus quantum predictions for measurements on separately prepared systems. How much does it cost to give up the independence assumption? Is the cost structurally the same as giving up measurement independence in Bell arguments, and if so is that a modest price or a conspiracy I should be ashamed of?

2. The results suggesting that any epistemic model reproducing all of quantum theory must carry an underlying state space at least as rich as the quantum state space itself. If true, does that defeat the motive rather than the view, and how should motive-defeat be ranked?

3. Whether nomism about the universal state is even stable: can something be a law while evolving in time, admitting superposition, being entangled relative to a factorization, and varying with how the world is carved into subsystems? Does the package need a preferred tensor-product structure, and does the physics supply one?

4. Collapse into a rival. Is this just the hidden-variable nomological package with the particles deleted, and if so is there anything left for the law to constrain? Conversely, do I need a typicality measure or a distribution over initial conditions to recover the Born rule, and does adding one reintroduce ontic commitment through the back door?

5. Whether the two halves mesh: can subsystem states actually be derived from a nomological universal state, or have I simply posited both?

Finish with the honest questions. Does the package make any prediction its rivals do not, and if not, has it been qualified into a position that risks nothing? Is that a defect for a view of this kind? And how exposed are its rivals by comparison—configuration-space wavefunction realism, spontaneous collapse, the branching picture, hidden variables—since my sense is that they are more exposed than their defenders admit.

For what it is worth, what would move me most is either a demonstration that the epistemic half loses its parsimony motive, or a demonstration that the two halves cannot be derived from one another; an argument that the independence assumption in item 1 is cheap to abandon would move me toward the view, not away. I do not need references or reading suggestions.
