I'm in a graduate seminar on value theory, writing a short paper on whether 'better than' is transitive, and I've built the paper around a case a friend in the cohort came up with — we call it the Relief Ratchet — because it seems to me to make the abstract spectrum arguments concrete. Fix a population of 10,001 people and a cardinal, interpersonally comparable welfare scale on which 0 is a life neither good nor bad for its holder, +900 is an excellent life, −1,000 is severe misery, −100 is a life of unremitting moderate suffering, and 0.1 is a decrement nobody could notice. At the starting outcome R₀ everyone is at 0. Now run 10,001 steps: at each step, take a worst-off person and raise them by 900 units, while every other person loses 0.1 units; break ties by a fixed rotation, so that over the whole sequence each person is the beneficiary exactly once. After step k the k people who have had their turn are at 900 − 0.1(k−1) and everyone else is at −0.1k, so after the final step every person has received one 900-unit lift and paid 10,000 imperceptible costs, leaving everyone at 900 − 1,000 = −100. Each individual step strikes me as clearly an improvement — someone in severe misery is lifted most of the way to a good life, and the price is a 0.1-unit loss to each of 10,000 others, a cost no one could detect — yet the endpoint is worse for every single person than the starting point, since at R₀ everyone was at 0 and at R₁₀₀₀₁ everyone is at −100. Total welfare also falls by exactly 100 at every step, which I take to be the reason total utilitarianism condemns every step.

My difficulty is knowing what to conclude. My teaching assistant says the ratchet is not really a threat to transitivity, because prioritarianism and leximin reject the steps as well; but I don't see how that gives me a third option, since on my reading every one of these theories rejects the steps for the same reason — they add up imperceptible losses across a large population, differing only in the weights they use. So it looks like a forced choice: either I accept that 'better than' is not transitive, or I accept an aggregative consequentialism that sums tiny losses over many people, and the second option seems to me to be simply a refutation of the idea of giving genuine priority to the worse off — as though you cannot take severity seriously at all without landing in intransitivity. Is that the right way to set up the dialectic, or is there room for a view that gives the worse off real priority while still rejecting the steps? Two related worries: since each loss is by hypothesis imperceptible, shouldn't I say that no step makes any difference at all and treat the whole thing as a sorites dissolved by vagueness? And if the first horn really is where the argument lands, what is left of 'making things better' as a guide to action — would I have to abandon maximization and become a satisficer, or is there a better way to think about choice under an intransitive betterness relation?
