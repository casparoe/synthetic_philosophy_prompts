I'm a graduate student in international history writing a seminar paper on the origins of the Cold War, and I keep running into a sentence that is doing more work than I can unpack: "history is written by the victors."

In seminar last week a student asked, flatly, which side bore more responsibility for the Cold War, and the professor answered with that line. I took him to mean something like this: the "who started it" question has no answer we could responsibly give, because what passes for an answer is just a function of who ended up in a position to commission the histories, hold the archives, and staff the departments — so the only tractable question concerns the politics of the narrative, not the past itself. Two days later a classmate used the same sentence for almost the opposite purpose: as a reason to distrust the confident post-1991 account in which one side's collapse is taken to settle the moral question, on the grounds that the account was written by the winners and is therefore suspect.

I don't think both uses can be right, and I suspect the sentence is being asked to carry several quite different theses at once. I'd like a disambiguation I can take back to seminar.

1. Lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making, from the thinnest to the most radical. Some candidates of my own, which I'd like you to refine, reject, or add to: (a) an empirical claim about selection and access — that the surviving records, the syllabi, and the publishing infrastructure are controlled by the winning side; (b) an empirical claim about content — that the histories actually produced flatter that side, which looks questionable here, since a great deal of the most influential Cold War scholarship written in the West has been sharply critical of Western policy, and since much of the documentary record on the other side became available after 1991; (c) a conceptual claim that for an outcome of this kind there is no fact of the matter about responsibility, only rival narratives; (d) a claim about what blame-ascriptions are for — that they are present-directed political acts rather than reports about the past; (e) a claim about which side acquired the power to make the question unaskable.

2. For each reading, give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive but dubious, or straightforwardly false. Then say which readings would actually license my professor's dismissal of the responsibility question, which would license my classmate's suspicion of the triumphalist narrative, and which would license neither.

3. I'm also unsure whether the key term has a referent. A war ending in a surrender ceremony has victors; the Cold War ended with one of the two parties dissolving, and a large share of the historiography is written by the other side's own academy, often critically. Say what happens to each reading if "the victors" is under-determined in that way. Does the sentence then become unfalsifiable, or is it simply not about this case at all?

4. Is there any reading that converts into a usable methodological instruction rather than a conversation-stopper — something like "check whose archives are closed," "ask which counterfactual baseline is being smuggled in," or "ask whom this blame-ascription serves"? If none of them do, tell me what question I should be putting in my paper instead.

Finally, on the underlying question: "who started the Cold War" seems to me to admit of several precisifications — who made the first move; whose conduct departed most from the norms in force at the time; whose behaviour was causally necessary for the outcome; whose conduct was least justified given the alternatives actually available; who had the most room to de-escalate and did not; whose conduct made the largest difference to the probability of the outcome. Add any I've missed, distinguish them, and tell me which survive if the spiral or security-dilemma model is roughly right. My worry is that under such a model the process is genuinely reciprocal, so that origination becomes indeterminate and everyone or nobody is responsible — in which case "who started it" may be as ill-formed as asking who started an arms race or a marriage breakdown. If that worry is sound, say whether it is what the slogan was gesturing at, or whether the slogan gestures at something else and my worry is independent of it.

One further consideration, which I offer as my own and ask you to check: I've read that in experimental philosophy there is a much-replicated result — the "side-effect effect" — in which people's verdicts about whether an agent did something intentionally shift with the moral valence of what happened, and that related work argues something similar happens with causal attribution. If that is right, it bears on whether historians' responsibility judgments are contaminated in a way the slogan does not capture. Tell me whether I have it right, and if so whether it supports or undercuts any of the readings above.

I'm not asking for a reading list or for citations. I want the analysis, structured as a list I can work from, with the verdicts attached to each reading.
