I write about the science of cooperation for a national magazine, and I have spent a year trying to get two people into the same room.

One is a philosopher in the Lewisian tradition. For her, a convention is a regularity in a population's behaviour that solves a recurring coordination problem; it is arbitrary in the sense that a different regularity would have solved the problem just as well; and it persists because each agent conforms, expects the others to conform, and prefers to conform given that the others do. What selects one of the available regularities rather than another is salience, precedent, and the common knowledge that the regularity is in force.

The other is a modeller in the cultural-evolution and learning tradition. For him, a convention is an attractor of a population-level process: which convention a group ends up with is a function of initial conditions, network structure, noise, turnover, and the size of the basin of attraction of each equilibrium. On his view, invoking common knowledge or salience is at best a convenient shorthand for features already present in the dynamics, and at worst a rationalisation supplied after the fact by people who cannot say why one equilibrium was reached.

They have exchanged a year of increasingly testy public posts. They have now agreed to a two-day adversarial collaboration that I will convene, and I want to print the joint document as the centrepiece box of the feature. So: draft the statement both of them could sign at the end of the two days.

The obstacle is that they have been arguing past each other partly because no one has ever made them write down what they would expect to observe. I have therefore specified, below, a single experimental set-up that both of them have already accepted in correspondence as a fair arena, and I want their disagreement cashed out as predictions about it.

The set-up — call it the merged-rooms study. Two hundred participants, split into two rooms of one hundred, play sixty rounds of a pure-coordination labelling game: on each round participants are randomly re-paired within their room, each pair must settle on one of two available labels for the same object, and both get a payoff only if they match. Participants see only their own partner's choice and their own outcome and history. By round 30 each room has converged — room A on "zog", room B on "mip" — and nobody in either room knows the other exists. At round 31 the rooms are merged into one population of two hundred with random re-pairing across the whole group. Five replications are run:

1. Plain merge, with nothing else added.
2. Announcement: immediately before the merge, the experimenter sends everyone a message, explicitly non-binding and worth no money, stating that many people elsewhere use "zog".
3. Turnover: for ten rounds after the merge, ten randomly chosen participants are replaced by naive newcomers.
4. Sticklers: in each room, eight participants — unknown to everyone else — have been privately paid a small bonus for every round on which they use their own room's label regardless of whether their partner matches, and they never switch.
5. Articulability: one of the two labels is of a kind that can be copied but cannot be described to a newcomer in words, while the other can be conveyed in a single sentence.

Please produce the document in this form.

First, a preamble of about a hundred words, written for my readers, saying what the session was, what this document is, and what the two participants are not claiming (they are not claiming to have settled anything).

Second, a numbered list of eight to ten agreed claims — the real ones, not the empty ones. Each must be a single sentence a non-specialist can follow, with any technical term (equilibrium, basin of attraction, common knowledge, salience) defined inline the first time it appears. If a claim is true only because it is vacuous, cut it: neither of them will sign a fudge, and my readers will smell one.

Third, a disagreements table. One row per genuine disagreement; columns for (a) the question at issue, (b) the Lewisian's own wording of her position — phrased so that she would accept it as her view — (c) the modeller's own wording of his, (d) whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, and (e) which of the cruxes below it hangs on. Where you judge a row to be a fight about words, say so explicitly and say what the underlying variable really is; I would rather print an admission that part of this dispute is terminological than manufacture a consensus.

Fourth, three or four cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect one is whether salience is an explanatory notion in its own right or just a name for whatever makes a basin of attraction large; a second is whether the cheap announcement in replication 2 does work beyond what a belief-updating model already predicts; a third is whether the extension from pure coordination to mixed-motive settings — Hi-Lo cases with a temptation to defect and the possibility of sanctions — is a change of degree or a change of subject, and if the latter, whether the sanction-based account of norms still needs the Lewisian machinery. For each crux give the question, one sentence on why it moves at least one side, the replication or probe both accept as bearing on it, and — the part I most want — each side's prediction written down in advance in checkable form (a threshold, a direction, a rough number of rounds), not as a hedge. Include for each side what result would count as damaging its own view. If one of my five replications cannot discriminate on a given crux, say so and propose the modification to it that both would accept.

Fifth, two short signed paragraphs under the heading "What I would need to see", one from each of them.

Constraints: no citations, no bibliography, no named authorities beyond Lewis and the tradition he belongs to — everything a reader needs must be in the document itself, since my readers will not have the literature and the two participants are not allowed to gesture at it. Aim for 1,200 to 1,600 words in total. Keep the register of a document that will appear in print under two names: precise enough that specialists would not wince, plain enough that a magazine reader finishes it.
