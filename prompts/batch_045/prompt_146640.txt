hey — I'm in a formal epistemology seminar this term (mostly philosophy of probability, we've done Dutch books and basic conditionalization) and I've been asked to run one 75-minute section for the other ~14 people. I want to do logical uncertainty, but as a game rather than a talk.

The thing I want them to walk out seeing: that you can be genuinely uncertain about a claim whose truth value is already settled by the axioms, that "just be a coherent Bayesian who assigns 1 to every theorem" isn't a standard anybody can actually meet (no computable credence function does that), and so what you'd want instead is something like — your credences drift toward 1 on theorems as you do more work, and no trader can systematically milk you even so. I don't want any of that named before they've played, though. They should run into it.

What I've got so far is a prediction market: assets that pay out iff some mathematical claim is true, play points instead of money, a projected list of claims, some people get calculators, some get laptops, some get nothing, and I reveal the truth values of a few claims between rounds. Phones can be banned. But I can't get the claim list right and I don't trust my trading rules to actually produce the phenomenon rather than just producing noise.

So: design the whole thing. In particular — what the ~25-30 claims should be and what has to be true of them. My guess is I need some that a student can settle in five minutes with the tools they have, some that nobody in the room can settle in the session, and some that are logically related to each other (P, not-P, P-or-Q, P-and-Q) so that incoherent prices are exploitable — but tell me if that mix is wrong, and how to keep the arbitrage from being either invisible or over in ninety seconds. Also I'd like one claim revealed mid-session as true *without* a proof, so they have to update on testimony about a derivation rather than on the derivation, and I want everybody to write down private probabilities before trading starts so I have something to compare the final prices against.

Then: the round structure and the info-reveals, arranged so that what they notice at the end is that the price of a theorem sat at 0.3 for forty minutes and that felt completely fine. Is the tool asymmetry doing any real work, or does it just let the laptop people run the market while everyone else checks out? What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my bet is everyone puts 0.5 on everything, or someone prices the tautologies at 1 straight away and kills it. A debrief that maps specific moments onto specific ideas, including why settling a claim by computing it isn't the same as proving it and why a limit-based criterion isn't the same as coherence at a time. One thing I can read off the market data to tell whether it landed. And be honest about what a 75-minute game with undergrads definately cannot show, that I'll have to just say out loud at the end.
