Some background on where I'm coming from. I'm a retired biogeochemist, spent about thirty years reading isotope ratios out of sediment cores. I'm perfectly happy to say a core carries information about past temperature — that's my day job, was. What I am not happy to say is that the core is a code. But I do want to say the genome is one, or at least that it's much nearer to being one than a sediment core is. Someone in my reading group has an argument that collapses that distinction entirely, and I've been going round in circles with it for a week. Here it is, cleaned up by me (he'll probably say I've steelmanned it).

(1) A codon's specifying an amino acid is chemically arbitrary. Nothing about a triplet's shape, charge or stereochemistry fits it to its amino acid better than to any other; the mapping is a contingent accident frozen in early on, and it isn't even universal — it shifts a bit in mitochondria and in a handful of other lineages.

(2) Where a mapping between signs and outcomes is arbitrary in that sense, it holds by convention: it holds because it was assigned and is maintained, not because it follows from the natures of the things involved.

(3) A convention is an assignment made by agents: the mapping holds because some users adopt it, could have adopted a different one, and use it to mean something. That's what makes arbitrariness harmless in the cases we understand — Morse code, road signs, the word "cow."

(4) In a cell there are no such agents. The machinery that does the assigning — the tRNAs and the enzymes that charge them — is itself built from instructions written in the very code it instantiates. The codemaker is a product of the code. That's a circle, not a ground.

(5) Circular groundings confer nothing. If B's content depends on A and A's depends on B, neither has any. Meaning, like justification, has to bottom out somewhere in something that has it non-derivatively — a purpose, an intention, a mind.

(6) So no codon means anything. "Codon X codes for amino acid Y" is only shorthand for: X is caught up, in organisms like the ones we've got, in a causal chain that reliably ends with Y being added to a peptide.

(7) And that shorthand is available absolutely everywhere. Specify a "reader" after the fact and an isotope ratio in my sediment core encodes paleotemperature, a tree ring encodes last summer's rainfall, the height of a mercury column encodes the weather. The reader can always be retro-fitted.

(8) So either DNA isn't a code, or everything is one. Either way "the genetic code" earns nothing that "the sediment record" doesn't already earn, and the central notion of molecular biology is decoration.

I can't accept (8), but I also can't put my finger on the step where he cheats. My instinct is that (2) and (3) are where the work is done — that "arbitrary" and "conventional" might not be the same property, and that "conventional" might not require anybody. But he has a reply ready: drop the requirement that conventions be made by agents and you're left with a mapping that is arbitrary and yet grounded in nothing at all, which is just (6) with extra steps.

There's one more wrinkle I keep coming back to, which is my own and not his. Suppose we build a lineage in the lab whose translation machinery reads what used to be a stop codon as some new amino acid (I'm told people have actually done things in this neighbourhood; if not, take it as a thought experiment). Now, does the mutant codon mean something the old one didn't? If I say yes, I seem to have made the content of a codon depend on our intentions, which is uncomfortably close to saying there's a mind behind the code, and I don't want to go there. If I say no, then the codon's content isn't fixed by anything and (6) follows anyway. That dilemma feels like it has the real answer hiding inside it, but I can't get it out.

I also tried the argument on my granddaughter about English, and that worried me more. Nobody assigned English to anybody, every speaker learned it from earlier speakers, and "cow" is arbitrary in exactly the sense of (1). So by (5) natural language has no meaning either. He says fine, he'll bite that bullet, English has no meanings either, only regularities, and that's consistent. I don't believe that, but "it proves too much" isn't a diagnosis.

What I'd most like to keep, if something has to go: the arbitrariness claim in (1), and the thought that the ribosome-and-tRNA system is doing something that a sediment core plus a curious geochemist is not. The thing that makes me sure of that second one is that error is possible on one side and not the other — a mischarged tRNA really does get it wrong, whereas if my isotope ratio is off it's my inference that's wrong, not the core. I'd give up (8) before I give up either of those.
