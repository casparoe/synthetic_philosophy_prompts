I tutor undergraduates for a historiography course, and about once a week I run into an argumentative shape that I have no name for and would like to be able to write in the margin of an essay. It goes like this. Someone claims that a large historical outcome was inevitable. You press them with a counterfactual — had this not happened, the outcome would not have followed — and they concede the point while immediately re-describing the outcome more generally, so that the claim now covers a class of outcomes rather than the one that actually occurred: not this industrial transformation, but an industrial transformation of some kind sooner or later; not the collapse of the Soviet state in 1991, but the eventual collapse of any system with those inefficiencies; not this war, but some violent resolution of the slavery question, given what happened in every other slave society. The re-described claim really is weaker and better supported, and it is usually what the speaker meant all along. But it then gets used to do all the same work the original claim was doing — ruling out counterfactual speculation as idle, settling the balance between structural and agential explanation, and sometimes defusing questions about who was responsible — without anyone registering that anything has been given up.

I do not think this is just hindsight bias, or anyway not only that. It happens about outcomes that have not occurred yet: people say that regulation of AI in some form is inevitable, or that ubiquitous surveillance in some form was inevitable once the internet existed, and no delay and no difference in detail has yet counted against them, because eventually has no deadline and in some form has no fixed content. Nor does it feel like an ordinary bait-and-switch: there is no sneaking back to the strong claim, the general claim is offered in good faith, and often it is the more interesting of the two. And some instances strike me as genuinely informative rather than empty — that Napoleonic France would have been worn down sooner or later given the population and industrial base of its enemies seems to me a real insight about relative capacities, not a way of avoiding an argument. So I am not after a label for a fallacy; I want to know what the thing is and where exactly its edges lie.

My rough guess is that three separate dials are being turned. One is how abstractly the outcome is described, this war versus a violent resolution of some sort. One is how far the counterfactual is allowed to depart from what actually happened, that is, how large the class of antecedent changes the claim is supposed to be robust to. One is how long the eventually is allowed to be. The move seems vacuous when the speaker is free to adjust all three at once, since any delay and any difference in detail can then be absorbed, and informative when at least one of them is pinned down in advance. Is there an established concept for this in philosophy of history or anywhere else, and if so what precisely does it cover, and how does it differ from the neighbouring things it might be confused with? If there is no established concept, please name and define one yourself, tell me which dial is doing the work in each of my three historical examples, and give me the boundary cases: one clear case where the move is legitimate, one clear case where it is empty, and one hard case in between. Finally, I think I have also seen the mirror image, where a contingency claim retreats downward: the broad outcome is conceded to be overdetermined, but some detail, date or name is always available to be described as contingent, so that nothing is ever necessary. Is that the same phenomenon with the dials reversed, or a different one that needs its own name?
