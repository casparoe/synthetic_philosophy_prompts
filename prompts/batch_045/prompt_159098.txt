I'm one of five volunteer members of the board that runs a small community health-and-recreation fund in the town where I grew up. Each year we divide roughly $200,000 between two things: a free eyeglass and hearing-aid clinic that mostly serves uninsured adults, and a subsidy that keeps the fee for the town's youth soccer league low enough that nearly any family can sign up. This year our endowment payout fell, we cannot fund both at current levels, and we have spent two long meetings arguing over three proposals. I took a single ethics course in college many years ago and remember almost nothing from it, and I have come to realize that I cannot tell which parts of my discomfort are principled and which are merely squeamishness about making people worse off.

Proposal 1 is the one I keep circling back to. Because of a restriction in the original gift, any money we decline to spend on the soccer subsidy cannot be moved to the clinic; it lapses back into a restricted endowment and benefits no one at all. Martin, another board member, argues that we should cut the subsidy anyway. He says it is wrong for the fund to be handing a pleasant extra to families who are already comfortable while the clinic turns people away, and that ending the subsidy leaves the people the fund serves closer to one another even though nobody is made better off. Something in me recoils at the idea of making some families worse off when no one gains; but when Martin asks what the difference is supposed to be between that and the ordinary, uncontroversial practice of counting a benefit more heavily the worse off the person who receives it is, I have no answer. Proposal 2 is the version where the money can be moved, though not one-for-one: because of how the clinic is reimbursed, every $10 we take out of the soccer subsidy buys only about $6 of clinic care, and the rest is swallowed by administrative overhead. Martin says that whatever supports Proposal 1 supports this as well, and I cannot articulate the difference — yet Proposal 2 strikes me as clearly less troubling than Proposal 1, not more. For what it is worth, the numbers are roughly 180 children in the league and roughly 400 adults through the clinic each year.

Proposal 3 is where I lose the thread entirely. One member insists that we should rank people by how well off they are across their entire lives rather than at the moment they walk through the door; another says that since we cannot know in advance which particular individuals will end up in which program, we should go by the odds rather than by actual outcomes. Both refinements seem to me to matter, but I cannot say how much, and they interact badly with two facts I keep coming back to: a handful of the children in the league are among the poorest families in town, for whom it is the only organized activity they can afford, while some of the clinic's retired patients have probably had better lives overall than the young families using the league. Can you help me get clear? Is Martin's argument for Proposal 1 sound, and what, if anything, genuinely separates it from Proposal 2? How should I weigh the whole-life-versus-this-moment question and the odds-versus-actual-outcomes question, and what do they imply for the poor children in the league and the retired patients at the clinic? I would also like the strongest available version of Martin's case stated as charitably as you can manage, since I would rather argue against the best case than against a straw man — and, because I have to cast a vote on Tuesday, a plain recommendation about which proposal, if any, I should support.
