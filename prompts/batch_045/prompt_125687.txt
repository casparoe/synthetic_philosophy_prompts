I teach an upper-level seminar on collective decision-making, mostly philosophy grad students with a few law types auditing, and I'm writing the final. Every year I set something on the doctrinal paradox and every year I get the same answer back: premise-based versus conclusion-based, a paragraph about how courts owe the parties reasons, maybe a gesture at the impossibility result from week four. I can't tell from that whether anyone has actually understood anything, so this year I want to build the question around an aggregation rule they cant have memorised, and make them work out what it does from the defintion.

Here's the rule I've been toying with; call it the roll-call rule. The court has its doctrinal constraints fixed in advance, and for the case I want to use the doctrine is just that a contract is enforceable if and only if it was formed, there was consideration, and there was no duress: three premises, one conclusion, tied together by a biconditional. The reporting judge fixes an order for the issues, the roll. The court goes down the roll one issue at a time and takes the majority verdict on each issue, unless that verdict is inconsistent with the verdicts it has already taken together with the doctrine, in which case it takes the opposite verdict. That's the whole rule.

The profile I want them to work with is this. Five judges. The first finds formation, consideration and no duress and votes to enforce. The second finds formation and consideration but finds duress, so votes against enforcement. The third finds formation and no duress but no consideration, so votes against. The fourth finds consideration and no duress but no formation, so votes against. The fifth finds formation and consideration but finds duress, so votes against. So each of the three premises has a majority behind it, enforcement loses four to one, and no single ground for refusing enforcement is held by more than two of them.

What I want the question to do, roughly in order. First, make them run the rule twice on that profile, once with the conclusion at the bottom of the roll and once with it at the top, and say what verdict the court announces and what reason it gives in each case. Second, make them say which of the conditions on our list the rule meets and which it violates, the list being universal domain, anonymity, neutrality, independence and collective consistency, and make them argue for each verdict rather than tick labels, including saying why consistency is guaranteed here and why that isn't much of an achievement. Third, ask whether the rule can ever hand down a verdict that every single judge rejected; I have a feeling it can but I couldnt build a clean example, so if it can, your model answer needs to produce one, and if it can't, it needs an argument for why not. And then the crux: is this rule a solution to the doctrinal paradox, or has it just moved the problem somewhere else, into the hands of whoever sets the roll, and is that better or worse than the discretion a premise-based procedure gives? The strongest students should also get to the thing I keep going round on myself, which is that the court ends up announcing a reason for a four-to-one verdict that at most two judges accepted and three explicitly rejected, so the rule buys consistency at the price of making the court say something about its own reasons that is true of none of the people who make it up.

Can you write me the question itself, a model answer of about eight hundred words, and a marking scheme out of twenty built around five must-hit points, with the marks each point is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. It's a ninety-minute exam. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just one block of text I can chop up for the handout myself.
