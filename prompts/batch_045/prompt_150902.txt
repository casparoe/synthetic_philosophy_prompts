I was elected to my university Senate's Standing Orders Committee last spring, and the first substantial item I have been asked to draft a recommendation on is a proposal to relax our own amendment rules. I have not formed a view, and I would rather not put something in front of the committee that simply restates one side's talking points. If we get this wrong we will spend an academic year transacting business under Standing Orders whose status is disputed, so I need the reasoning as much as the bottom line.

The provisions in play are these. Rule 4: "Subject to Rule 5, the Senate may amend these Standing Orders by a vote of two-thirds of the members present and voting, provided that notice of the proposed amendment was given at the preceding ordinary meeting." Rule 5: "Rule 4 may be amended only by a vote of three-quarters of the members present and voting." Rule 7: "A motion which has been carried may be rescinded at any subsequent meeting by a vote of two-thirds of the members present and voting." Rule 8: "An amendment to these Standing Orders takes effect on the first day of the academic year next following the meeting at which it is carried."

Four rulings on points of order are in the minutes. In 2013 a motion to delete Rule 5 was carried by 71 per cent with notice duly given; the chair ruled it ineffective, on the recorded ground that an amendment altering the majority required for amendments to the Standing Orders must itself be carried by the majority required by the Standing Orders as they stand, not by the majority it would bring about. In 2015 the Senate carried at a single meeting, first, a motion to delete Rule 5 (84 per cent, with notice) and then, immediately afterwards and at the same meeting, a motion lowering Rule 4's threshold from two-thirds to a simple majority (69 per cent, with notice); a point of order that the second motion still needed three-quarters was overruled and the amendment was held valid. In 2018 a motion to delete Rule 4's notice requirement was carried by 72 per cent at a meeting at which no notice of it had been given; it was ruled ineffective, on the ground that the notice requirement applies to amendments to the amending rule as much as to any other. In 2021 a motion carried by 80 per cent deleted Rules 4 and 5 altogether; the ruling was that the deletion was effective and that it did not leave the Standing Orders unamendable, since the power to regulate procedure comes from the Charter and not from Rule 4, and in the absence of any Standing Order on the matter it is exercisable by a bare majority. An amendment carried by bare majority without notice in the following academic year was upheld.

The proposal before us now is a two-step operation. At the March meeting the Senate would carry, with notice and at an expected 80 per cent, a motion deleting Rule 5. At the May meeting it would carry, with notice and at an expected 70 per cent — comfortably above two-thirds, comfortably below three-quarters — a motion amending Rule 4 to require a simple majority. Both meetings fall in the current academic year, so both amendments would commence on the same date, 1 September. Two complications have surfaced since the papers went out. First, a group of members has given notice of a motion under Rule 7 to rescind the March motion at the June meeting, and claims to have the two-thirds for it. Second, one member argues that the whole two-step is a device, and that the honest version of what is being attempted is a single motion deleting Rule 5 and lowering Rule 4's threshold in one text; she says that if we would not accept that single motion at 70 per cent, we should not accept the two motions either.

What I would like from you:

1. A recommendation. Will the May amendment be effective, and should the committee advise that the motions be put as planned? Give the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

2. The competing principles that fit all four of our rulings, and what each of them predicts here. I assume there is more than one; in particular I want to know whether the 2015 ruling is best explained by a rule about transactions conducted at a single meeting, by a rule about when an exercise of the amending power takes effect on the power itself, or by a rule about when the validity of an amendment is assessed. Say what each predicts about the May motion, and what each predicts if the June rescission succeeds.

3. Which of the four rulings is the closest analog, and how much weight it really bears — that is, whether it settles the point or only gestures at it.

4. If our precedents do not determine the answer, an explicit statement of what we would be committing ourselves to under each choice: something about whether Rules 4 and 5 constitute the Senate's power to amend or merely constrain a power it already has, something about whether validity is fixed at passage or at commencement, and something about whether an amendment can ever validate itself.

5. A view on the member's objection. Would the single combined motion be effective at 70 per cent? If it would not, is the fact that the same outcome is reachable in two steps but not in one a reason to distrust the two-step route, or is it simply how self-amendment works? Relatedly, it has been said in committee that Rule 5 achieves nothing at all, because any entrenchment can be unwound in two steps. Is that right, or does Rule 5 do something less than its drafters perhaps intended?

6. Whether there is a formulation that is safe under every reading consistent with our precedents — putting both motions at the same meeting, waiting until after 1 September, or something else — since the committee may prefer a route that does not depend on which principle is correct.

7. If we wanted to settle the underlying question for the future rather than merely dispose of this item, what is the one case we should put on the record?
