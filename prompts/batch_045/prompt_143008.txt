Someone in a group chat I'm in — effective-altruism adjacent, so people there actually do act on this kind of thing — posted an argument last week and nobody has managed to kill it. He calls it Pascal's mugging, which I gather other people have written about, but he says his version closes the usual exits and I think he might be right.

The setup. Two strangers approach you in a park, separately. Each hands you a device and lets you play with it for an hour: it predicts your next keystroke perfectly, runs what looks like a high-fidelity simulation of a city you know, that sort of thing. Call that evidence E. Given E, you put some non-trivial credence on the hypothesis that this stranger really does have access to something like a Matrix-grade simulation. He says take any figure you like, so call it 1%. Also assume, as he insists, that simulated minds count exactly as much as biological ones — if you won't grant that, he'll just relocate the threat to a galaxy you can never observe, and he says nothing in the argument changes.

Stranger A says: give me £5 or I'll run a simulation of 10^40 people being tortured for fifty years each.
Stranger B says: give me £5 and I'll run a simulation of 10^40 people living ecstatic lives for fifty years each.

(1) The two cases are structurally identical. Same evidence E, same credence p that the stranger has the power, same number of people affected, same size of welfare change, same £5. The only differences are the sign and whether what you're handed is a threat or an offer.
(2) If the only difference is the sign, expected value has to treat them alike. Paying A costs £5 and, with probability p, spares N people torture: EV(pay) − EV(refuse) = pN − 5. Paying B costs £5 and, with probability p, produces N blissful lives: EV(pay) − EV(refuse) = pN − 5. So A and B stand or fall together. Pay both or neither.
(3) But nearly everyone's actual reaction splits them. Paying the mugger feels like submission to extortion; paying the angel feels at least discussable, and a couple of people in the chat said they'd hand over the fiver to B without hesitating while laughing at A.
(4) If that reaction is more than a gut feeling, it has to locate the difference in where the stakes come from — threat versus offer, doing harm versus not doing good, creating misery versus failing to create happiness. Those are distinctions about how an outcome comes about, not about how large or how likely it is, so they're invisible to expected value as such. Which means the reaction can only survive by adding something to expected value, not by using it.
(5) Now crank the number. Nothing turns on 10^40. Whatever machinery would let the stranger torture 10^40 people would let him torture 10^50 — running the simulation bigger costs him nothing extra — so the bigger threat isn't proportionally less credible than the smaller one. It isn't like a claim to a bigger bank balance, where the size of the claim really does make it less likely; it's the same mechanism scaled up. So p doesn't fall as fast as N rises.
(6) So for any cost c, however large, and any fixed p above zero, there's an N beyond which expected value says pay. And since the stranger is the one who names N, he can always name one past that line. Put c at everything you own.
(7) By (2), the same goes for the angel. So expected value requires you to hand over everything you have to whichever stranger names the largest number.
(8) Now let a thousand strangers turn up, each with evidence E, each demanding everything you own, each naming a bigger number than the last. Every one of those demands is individually mandatory by (6) and (7). Jointly they can't all be met, and there's no largest number for the biggest-naming stranger to name.
(9) A rule that is individually mandatory and jointly impossible isn't a decision procedure. So something in (1) through (7) is false.

I've been going back and forth on this for a week and I honestly cant tell whether it's a sound argument with a premise I just have to swallow, or whether something quietly shifts between the steps. The bit I'd least like to give up is (5): without it the whole thing collapses into a quibble about very small probabilities, and it doesn't feel like a quibble. My flatmate thinks (1) is the weak point and that the mugger and the angel are obviously different, but when I push him he ends up saying something about acts and omissions, and I dont see how that can make a difference to an expected value.

So: which step do you reject, why does it look fine until you press on it, and what does each of the ways out actually cost? I'd like prices for at least these — putting a ceiling on how much anything can matter (bounded utility, or just refusing to be a fanatic), adding a side-constraint against rewarding threats, giving up the symmetry in (2) by way of something person-affecting or an act/omission asymmetry, squeezing (5) so that credibility really does fall off with the size of the claim, or just biting the bullet on (9) and paying. And if the break is somewhere I haven't listed — in the move from (6) to (8), say — say so and tell me what that move assumes.
