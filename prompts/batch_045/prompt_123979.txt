I write literary fiction, and I have got stuck on a scene, and then stuck on an argument that came out of the scene, and I would like to be unstuck on the argument at least.

The scene: two career criminals, partners for eleven years, on one last job. The job has six handoffs. At each handoff, whoever is holding the money either takes the larger share and ends things, or hands on; if they hand on, the total grows, and the other player faces the same choice. At the sixth handoff there is no seventh. The payoffs are arranged, in the usual way, so that at any handoff a holder who hands on and is then taken gets less than they could have taken then and there. Both of them know all of this, know that the other knows it, know each other's payoffs exactly, and have no regard for each other beyond the money. I wanted the reader to feel, at the end, that their loyalty was a kind of intelligence rather than a kind of weakness, which is why I tried writing it out as an argument. It does not survive. But the argument that kills it seems to me to kill a great deal more than my scene.

(1) A rational agent takes the action that maximizes expected payoff given what they believe.
(2) At the sixth handoff there is nothing later to protect or to influence, so taking strictly dominates handing on.
(3) So before the first handoff both of them already know what will happen at the sixth, whatever may have happened earlier.
(4) If the sixth handoff is settled in advance, nothing either of them does earlier can alter it; so the fifth handoff is for practical purposes the last one, and the same dominance argument applies there.
(5) And so, by induction, down to the first: the first holder takes at once, and both characters get the least the job could possibly pay.
(6) Nothing in (1) through (5) depends on how many handoffs there are. Six or six hundred, as long as both know the number, the result is the same.
(7) So in any interaction with a commonly known last round, no trust, promise, restraint or reputation is ever rational. Everything a relationship contains has already been spent in its final round.
(8) But that cannot be right. Real relationships with known endings do not behave like this, and I am told that when this set-up is actually played for money, players pass for several rounds and both end up better off than (5) predicts. Correct me if I have that wrong.
(9) Worse, the argument seems to turn against itself. A partner known to be irrational in the right way — known to be the sort of person who always keeps faith — does better than a partner known to be a pure maximizer, and there is no rational route to becoming that sort of person. A theory that instructs you to become irrational has, I think, proved too much.
(10) So something among (1) through (6) is false.

Two exits I do not want. I am not going to be told that it works out if the characters are altruistic; they are not, and the payoffs are as stated. Nor that it works out if they are uncertain about each other's payoffs; stipulate that each knows the other's payoffs exactly. I want to know whether the chain breaks even under those stipulations, and if so, where.

Someone in a discussion thread told me that (5) is in fact a theorem for games of this shape, and that what is wrong is not the induction but my step (4): the assumption that, having arrived at the fourth handoff, they could still run the argument about the fifth and the sixth. He said that common knowledge of rationality at the outset does not settle what is common knowledge at a handoff the argument itself says will never be reached, and that my induction borrows its premise at nodes where the premise says the characters cannot be. I cannot tell whether that is a real distinction or a piece of word-magic that relabels the problem, and I would like to know which it is, with the reasons.

So: which step do you reject? If you reject (4), tell me what it was really doing and what rejecting it costs — in particular, what the characters are entitled to believe about each other at a handoff that common knowledge says they will not reach, and whether anything constrains those beliefs at all, or whether anything goes there. If you would rather reject a different step, say which, and say what you have to pay for it. I would like to keep (8), and I would like to keep (1).

Finally: tell me how confident you are in each part of what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of. I am going to put some version of this into a character's mouth, and I would rather know which sentence is the load-bearing one.
