I lead a four-person forecasting cell at a humanitarian logistics organization, and I have an old undergraduate minor in philosophy plus a graduate degree in statistics, which is to say I know just enough of each to be dangerous. Every week we are handed numbers we are expected to turn into planning decisions: whether to pre-position supplies in a river basin, whether to hold back a contingency budget, whether to brief a country director that a scenario is now "likely." The numbers arrive from three sources our spreadsheets do not distinguish, and I have become convinced that most of our arguments after the fact trace back to that: ensemble outputs from physical models (seasonal climate forecasts, hydrological models), where the number carries an explicitly chance-like interpretation and usually some hindcast record; prices from a small internal prediction market we run among staff, several of whom have partial field access; and structured expert-judgment elicitations, which produce a number that looks identical in a cell to the other two but is somebody's opinion. We are also audited: six months after a decision, a donor or a board member asks why we moved the tents or did not, so whatever we do has to be reconstructible from a written record by someone who was not in the room.

What I want from you is a written procedure my analysts can run on an incoming number before it enters a planning document. The analysts have real statistics training and no philosophy; the procedure has to be runnable in fifteen to twenty minutes per item, weekly, and its output has to be something our director of operations, who has no patience for epistemology, will actually sign off on. I have been reading about the Principal Principle — the principle that says your credence in an event should equal what you take the objective chance of that event to be, absent inadmissible evidence — and that feels like roughly the right framework, but I cannot hand my team a principle. I need it operationalized: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a refusal to use the number, and where it hands the rest to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

Two things I want it to get right, and one I want it to catch. A colleague has proposed the shortcut that we simply defer to any source that comes with a calibration curve, on the grounds that a well-calibrated source is tracking something real; I suspect this collapses a distinction I cannot name precisely, because being reliably calibrated across a run of cases looks like one thing and being a report of the chance of this single event looks like another. Relatedly, we routinely let numbers cross that gap by relabeling: an expert judgment gets fed into a model, the model prints a probability, and from then on it travels downstream as though it were a chance. I have started calling this chance laundering, and I want the procedure to detect it. It also has to handle numbers that arrive stale (a basin forecast issued in March is still circulating in June and nobody adjusts it), evidence that leaks the outcome rather than describing the setup (field reports that the short rains have already failed upstream, or that fighting has begun), and the reference-class problem that arises when the record contains nothing quite like the event being forecast.

Please give me the procedure, then run it on three cases of the shapes we actually see: a seasonal ensemble forecast putting a 40% chance on below-normal rainfall in a region where the model has a thirty-year hindcast and good reliability, arriving the same week as herder reports that the short rains have failed; our internal market implying a 25% chance that a displacement site exceeds capacity within the quarter, when the trader pool is twelve people and three of them were in the field last month; and a vendor model's 0.4% annual probability for large-scale flood displacement in a basin with no comparable event in the record, which is precisely the case where I expect your procedure to tell me not to put a number in the memo at all and where my director will push back hardest. Where a step depends on a contested reading of the theory — whether chances are Humean, or whether deference to a calibrated expert is covered by deference to chance at all, or what the time-relativity of a chance really requires of us when a forecast ages — say so, mark your confidence, and tell me which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. And if the framing itself is wrong — if for a large share of our events, conflict onset and displacement above all, there are simply no objective chances to defer to and the Principal Principle does not reach them at all — tell me that plainly rather than handing me a checklist I will over-trust.
