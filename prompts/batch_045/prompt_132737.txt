I'm in a graduate seminar on the unity of consciousness, and an argument with a friend has stalled in the usual way: every time we get near the actual disagreement, one of us says that it depends what you mean by a subject, and the conversation restarts. So I want to force it into numbers. Set up the case like this: an adult has the corpus callosum and anterior commissure fully sectioned for intractable epilepsy, and then — this part is stipulation, since no such device exists — the connection is replaced by an implant that relays activity between homotopic cortical areas with three independently adjustable parameters: throughput, from zero up to roughly biological callosal capacity; temporal fidelity, i.e. whether relayed activity arrives with its timing preserved or is relayed with jittered timing so that content is shared without being temporally bound; and directionality, symmetric or else one-way in either direction. Assume the rest of the classic picture: contralateral hemifields, language lateralized to the left hemisphere so that only it can name what is presented, the two hands occasionally working at cross-purposes, and both hemispheres sharing a brainstem, one sleep–wake cycle and one body. Now hold the dials at some intermediate setting — say half of biological throughput, timing preserved, symmetric — and consider the general claim: that there is some intermediate setting at which the number of subjects of experience in this patient is neither determinately one nor determinately two. I mean this as a claim about determinacy, not about evidence: count it as false if any physically or functionally specifiable threshold, however arbitrary-looking, is entailed by the correct theory of consciousness to fix the count, and true only if the how-many question genuinely has no answer there. My credence is about 0.4. My friend's is about 0.8, on the ground that unification is obviously a matter of degree and any sharp threshold would be arbitrary; I think she is sliding from "the evidence would be inconclusive" to "there is no fact of the matter," but I cannot get her to locate the slide.

So: give me your own number, and more importantly the derivation. Decompose the question into the sub-questions it actually turns on, put a rough probability and one line of justification on each, and then show how they combine — and if they do not combine multiplicatively, say why. The sub-questions I would want you to cover, with freedom to add or substitute: whether a complete physical and functional description fixes a determinate number of subjects at all, or whether subject-count is a coarse-grained description that can simply fail to be well-defined; whether the unity at issue is access-unity, in the sense of availability to something like a global workspace for report, reasoning and motor control, or phenomenal unity, in the sense of contents being experienced together in a single field, and whether these can dissociate at reduced throughput — is there a regime in which each hemisphere can still bring about verbally reportable states in the other while the two contents are never co-conscious?; whether a graded quantity can ground a count at all, or whether a degree plus a count noun just makes the question ill-posed; which of the three dials does most of the philosophical work and why, since I have a guess I cannot defend, namely that temporal fidelity matters more than raw throughput; whether the shared brainstem, the single cycle of arousal and the single behavioural agent contribute anything to the count, or only to our willingness to call the patient one person; whether the fact that this was one subject before surgery constrains the answer, so that as throughput falls smoothly the original subject persists as one, ceases to be, or neither; and what the device would in fact do, smooth degradation or bistable alternation between periods of cross-hemispheric access and periods of breakdown, which is the empirical term I feel least able to estimate. Also tell me explicitly how much of your final number is carried by treating the indeterminate answer as a genuine third option rather than as a disguised refusal to answer, and what would convince you it was the latter.

Finally, three things I care about more than the number itself. First, which single sub-question your final figure is most sensitive to, in the sense that moving it by 0.2 moves the total most — and please do not answer "it depends which theory of consciousness is true"; give the number conditional on your best guess about that, or spread it across theories and show the weights. Second, of the two people at 0.4 and 0.8, which sub-question do you think they most likely disagree about, given what I have told you about the reasoning on each side. Third, one piece of evidence or one argument that would move your number furthest, with a rough magnitude and direction — for instance, if it were shown that intermediate settings always produce alternation rather than partial integration, or if someone gave a clean account of what it would be for the number of subjects to be indeterminate rather than merely unknown.
