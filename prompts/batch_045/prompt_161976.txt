I spent my career in crystallography and have lately been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university, which is my first sustained contact with early modern philosophy. We have spent three sessions on Molyneux's problem — whether a man born blind, taught by touch to distinguish a cube from a sphere, could tell on first gaining sight which is which. Two members have dug in. One holds what I shall call the heterogeneity view: the Locke–Berkeley line that the ideas of sight and touch have no necessary connection, so that nothing the man knows of the cube by touch gives him any expectation of its look, and any matching that later appears is learned afresh from experience. The other holds the common-figure view: extension and figure are notions common to both senses, so that a man who has the figure by touch already possesses what sight merely presents to him in another way. The second member also cites the sight-restoration literature, in which patients are said to come to match objects across the senses within days, as though that closed the matter. It does not look closed to me, since such successes seem compatible with the heterogeneity view as well, and I want a case that turns the difference into something observable before the next session.

What I would like you to build is a thought experiment in the Molyneux family, of the kind a clinical team could actually run, consisting of two probes administered to the same patient in the same session and differing from one another in exactly one respect, such that the two views agree on the outcome of the first probe and disagree on the outcome of the second. The first must be a probe that the heterogeneity view predicts the patient will pass, for a reason generated by that view's own commitments rather than stipulated; the second must be one that the heterogeneity view puts at chance while the common-figure view predicts better than chance. They may of course disagree about why the first probe is passed; that is fine and is part of what I want brought out. The point of the pair — and this is what fixes what would count as cheating — is to separate genuine cross-modal transfer from success reached by inference, and so to determine whether a positive result in a Molyneux test is evidence for the common-figure view at all. Constraints: (i) the patient is an adult with dense bilateral cataracts from infancy and no useful pattern vision before surgery, tested on the day the dressings come off — realistic, with no implants, no neural interfaces and no invented apparatus; (ii) each probe is a two-alternative forced choice on a first presentation, single trial, no feedback, with the objects concealed until the trial begins and never touched by the patient during the session; (iii) the run includes screening steps establishing both that the patient can tell the two objects apart haptically and that he can tell them apart by sight alone, so that neither a failure to see nor a failure to feel could explain any result, and you should say how the visual screening is done without pairing the modalities; (iv) the objects are unfamiliar solids with no ordinary name, known to the patient only through touch, and he has been told nothing that could serve as a visual description of them, so that there is no verbal bridge; (v) the two probes are matched on everything except the one variable, which you must name; (vi) no floor or ceiling effects — the common-figure view's prediction of success on the second probe must follow from its commitments rather than from your having made the task trivial, and the heterogeneity view's prediction of success on the first must not come from your having made it a giveaway; (vii) whatever route lets the patient pass the first probe must be one the heterogeneity view can genuinely endorse, and you should state what purely visual training the session is permitted to include, with feedback, on within-vision discriminations, and why that training is not itself the matter in dispute.

For each probe, please give the scene in one paragraph, stating exactly what is in front of the patient and what response is demanded of him, and then give each view's verdict together with the reasoning that produces it from that view's commitments rather than from the outcome you want. After that, name the feature of the pair that is doing the work, say what happens to the contrast if that feature is removed — does the whole design collapse back into the bare cube-and-sphere question? — then give the strongest objection available to whichever view comes out worse, the modification to the design that would blunt it, and any residue the design cannot settle. If a third position, such as a sensorimotor or action-mediated account, would predict differently on either probe, add it briefly and say which structural feature makes it peel off from the other two. One suggestion to react to rather than follow: my own instinct, from a career spent on symmetry and invariants, is that the operative variable is whether the visible array carries an invariant that the patient's haptic experience had already tied to the object — a bridge, so to speak — and that the cube-and-sphere test as usually described is full of accidental ones, such as the way each object rests on a table or the shadow it casts, which would make a success evidentially inert between the two views. If you think a different variable does the job better, use it and tell me what is wrong with mine.
