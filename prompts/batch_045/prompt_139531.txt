I am trying to get clear on exactly what work the admissibility clause in the chance–credence principle is doing, and I would like an explanation that proceeds by working the mechanics of a case rather than by surveying positions.

The principle, as I will understand it: for any proposition A about a particular outcome, if an agent's total evidence is admissible and she is certain that the objective chance of A, as of the relevant time, is x, then her credence in A ought to be x. Equivalently, conditional on her admissible evidence together with the hypothesis about the chance, her credence in A equals the chance value; and, correspondingly, her unconditional credence in A is her expectation of the chance.

Here is the case I have been turning over. A sealed machine prints a ticket at noon on each of ten consecutive days, each ticket marked either black or white. The machine has no internal bias mechanism of the usual kind: in this setup, the objective chance of black on any given day, as of any time before the printing, is constituted by and numerically equal to the proportion of black tickets in the complete ten-day run. Assume further that, conditional on the total number of black tickets in the run, all arrangements of black and white days are equally likely. The setup is meant as a miniature of any view on which chances are features of the total pattern of events rather than independent propensities.

Suppose the agent knows all of this, is certain that the chance of black is one half, and has already seen the first two tickets, both black. She now wants a credence that the third ticket is black. Read off the principle directly, she gets one half. Argue from the chance hypothesis plus her evidence, treating the arrangements as equiprobable, and she gets three eighths, since a run with five black tickets that begins with two black tickets leaves three black tickets among the remaining eight days.

Please explain, concretely:

First, which of these two numbers is the rationally required credence, and what precisely goes wrong in the reasoning that yields the other one.

Second, what this shows about how the admissibility clause must be understood. In particular, say what is wrong with the natural gloss, that admissible evidence is evidence whose bearing on the outcome runs only through its bearing on the chance; show where that gloss misclassifies the evidence in this case; and say whether a clause doing this job can be stated without circularity, or whether the principle reduces to the claim that credence equals chance whenever credence equals chance.

Third, consider the repair that replaces the chance of the outcome with the chance of the outcome conditional on the agent's total evidence, so that she is asked to match her credence to a chance that has already been conditioned on what she knows. Would that repair give the right answer here, what exactly would it require the notion of chance to be sensitive to, and what would it cost the principle's claim to be a substantive link between mind and world.

Finally, someone will object that the quantity I have stipulated is not a real chance at all. Assess that objection: what constraint on chance would it be invoking, whether that constraint is one a defender of a total-pattern view of chance should have to accept, and what the standoff tells us about whether chances can be constituted by the very frequencies they are supposed to govern.
