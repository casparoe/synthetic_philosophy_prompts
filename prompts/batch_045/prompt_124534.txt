So I'm writing a novel with a school board meeting at the center of it — a fight over whether the district contracts with a church-run nonprofit to run after-school mentoring — and I can't get the scene right because I don't actually know what it feels like in a room when somebody is told, in effect, that the reason they care most about is the one reason they're not supposed to say out loud. So I volunteered to run one session for my nephew's civics class: 20 juniors and seniors, one 75-minute block, nothing assigned beforehand. Design me a game they play rather than a discussion they have.

Rough shape I have in mind, fix it or replace it: no lecture up front, and nobody hears the fancy philosophical name for this until the last ten minutes. Three quick votes on the same agenda item — (1) approve the contract or not, (2) allow the nonprofit's optional closing reflection at the end of each session, (3) the package as a whole — with the speech rule changing each round: first round only reasons anyone could in principle check, second round you may say your real reason as long as you also give an accessible one, third round anything goes. Sealed role cards with a hidden core reason, and I want the assignment mixed so some students get religious reasons that point different directions (a duty-to-the-poor Yes, a don't-outsource-care-to-proselytizers No) and some get secular reasons that are structurally just as ultimate — an absolutist property-rights guy, a hard utilitarian — so the room discovers for itself that the rule sorts reasons by brand rather than by anything it admits to sorting by. I also want a one-use whisper pass, so each person can tell exactly one other member the thing they can't tell the room, and a scribe keeping a running ledger on the board of which offered reasons anyone could evaluate and which ones you'd have to already believe something to feel the force of. Plus an index card at the start of round two: the one sentence you'd say if there were no rule at all, collected and read aloud anonymous at the end.

Give me rules, materials, a payoff scheme that isn't real money (I was thinking influence chips versus integrity chips, where you only get the integrity one by saying your actual reason at least once), and a timeline that fits 75 minutes. Guardrails I care about: nobody should end up playing a version of themselves, so make the roles fictional with an easy reassignment, and have the chair rule the contract legal either way so the kids can't collapse the whole thing into a First Amendment argument and feel done. And the cards should be written so the religious reasons sound like adults talking, not like a cartoon.

Then the debrief: map specific moments onto what's actually going on, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Be honest about what the game can't reproduce and what I'll have to hand them in words afterward. Also — this is the part I most want — give me the lines you predict students will actually say, because that's the dialogue I'm stealing for chapter nine.
