I am a novelist, and the book I am drafting turns repeatedly on a family of devices in which a character truthfully announces, in advance, that something will happen at a moment its hearers will not see coming. A headmistress warns her staff of exactly one inspection during a five-day week, adding that on the evening before it they will not know which morning it is. A blackmailer names a thirty-day window in which a letter will be posted, and undertakes that on the morning of the day it goes out the mark still will not be able to work out whether today is the day. An autonomous system, on a run with no scheduled end, tells its operator that it will refuse one of his commands at a moment he cannot predict. These are all variants of the surprise-exam announcement, and readers who know the paradox keep writing to ask whether such scenes can be coherent at all — whether the announcement can be true, believed, believed to be true, and still surprising when it lands, or whether my characters are simply failing to draw a conclusion that is sitting there to be drawn. I cannot tell which of my devices is which.

What I want is a procedure, not an essay: something I can run on each new device in ten minutes, with the questions in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which it returns a verdict of no, this cannot work, and the point at which it hands the decision back to me because the open question is one about what I want the scene to mean rather than about whether it is consistent. Please say which distinctions have to survive the simplification, and correct me where I have the wrong ones. My own guesses are that it matters whether the window of days is fixed and known in advance, whether surprising means not deducible from the announcement or merely not expected, whether the speaker guarantees that the thing happens at all rather than saying that it may, and whether the hearers are entitled to trust the speaker — and also, I suspect crucially, whether the speaker knows the date herself or is only passing on what she was told, since an announcement that reports someone else's decision seems to behave quite differently under the backward induction. Tell me if any of that is beside the point, and whether some distinction I have not thought of is doing more work than these.

Then run the procedure on the three devices above, and on a fourth: the headmistress genuinely does not know which morning the inspectors are coming, having been told only that it will be one of the five. I would rather be told plainly that the open-ended device works only because I want the scene, if that is the case. Include at least one verdict that costs me a device I am attached to, and finish by saying what the procedure cannot tell me — in particular whether a device that survives it is dramatically any good, or whether a reader who has heard of the paradox will feel cheated by a scene the procedure passes.
