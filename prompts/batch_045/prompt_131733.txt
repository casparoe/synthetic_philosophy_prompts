Our reading group did Dennett on real patterns and the three stances (physical, design, intentional) last week, and I went home and wrote out an argument that I think shows the whole package eats itself. I don't actually belive that — the stances obviously do work, and I'd rather throw out my argument than the picture — but I can't locate the broken step, and the two people I tried it on disagreed about where it breaks. Here it is:

(1) Dennett's test, as I understood it: a pattern in some data is real when there's a description of the data that's more compact than the data itself, and that compactness is what buys you prediction — you regenerate the rest from the description plus a rule. Slogan version: lots of compression, something real; no compression, nothing there over and above the physical facts. That's supposed to be what makes it safe to call a pattern real without being spooky about it: the pattern is objective enough to be discovered, cheap enough not to be magic.

(2) A stance is warranted when adopting it buys you predictions you couldn't get, or couldn't get as cheaply, from the lower stances. Physical: always available, complete in principle, far too expensive. Design: cheap, works on things built or selected for a job. Intentional: cheapest, works on anything whose behavior you can predict by handing it beliefs and desires.

(3) Now the object. My friend Ash picked up a decommissioned avionics box at a hamfest; it emits one of sixteen symbols each second, and she logged about 1.8 million of them over three weeks. Call it the Kestrel box. For any log at all there's an intentional-stance assignment that fits it perfectly: the box wants to emit exactly that sequence and believes the current second is second number 1,204,771. That hypothesis predicts every symbol with complete accuracy.

(4) So if (1) and (2) are the whole story, the box's beliefs and desires are real patterns — maximally real, in fact, since nothing compresses the log better. By the same construction, so are the beliefs of a rock, a lava lamp, and a Geiger counter. You just pick the hypothesis after you've seen the data.

(5) The obvious reply is that this isn't prediction, it's curve-fitting: a real pattern has to be projectible, you have to be able to adopt the stance in advance and beat a baseline on data you haven't seen. Our group even invented a stronger version, which we called wide prediction: a hypothesis only earns its keep if the same pattern also predicts a *different* kind of data about the same system — the box's current draw, or what it does when you tilt it — not merely the next symbol from its own past.

(6) But wide prediction doesn't close the hole. For any finite batch of data, of whatever sort, there are infinitely many hypotheses that fit it and then diverge later; picking among them just is picking a prior, i.e. a ranking of which patterns are antecedently natural. So pattern-hood isn't doing the work — the prior is. And the prior is exactly the sort of thing (1) was meant to replace.

(7) It gets worse, because compression is always measured against a coding scheme. If the scheme may be tailored to the target, every sequence compresses: a codebook plus a pointer. If instead you count the codebook in the description length, which information theory says you must, compression is nontrivial again — but then which patterns are real depends on which description language you fixed beforehand, so pattern-hood is relative to a language, a coding scheme, or an interest, rather than being the discovered, in-the-world thing that was supposed to license the word real.

(8) So: either the compression criterion gets a tailored codebook, and then everything has real patterns and every stance is warranted, including the intentional stance toward the lava lamp — which is the sense in which I think it proves too much; or it has to use a fixed language, and then realism about patterns rests on something the account doesn't supply (a prior, a notion of naturalness, causality, projectibility), so the compression criterion isn't what's earning the right to say real.

(9) And the collapse seems to drag the stances down with it. The point of the three-stances picture is surely that there's a difference between ascriptions that latch onto something and ascriptions that are merely handy — your neigbour's desires versus the checkout machine's. If the criterion can't seperate those, the picture has no teeth, and I'm pretty sure it does have teeth.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (2) — the stances really do differ in cost and yield, and I don't want to loose that — and I'd rather give up as little of (1) as possible, since the compression bit is what connects to what people actually do when they fit models. Ash thinks the answer is that a pattern needs *instances*, so a one-off log can't be a pattern at all; I think that just moves the bump, and anyway the Kestrel box has 1.8 million symbols. Please tell me where the argument actually breaks, and whether the fix leaves me having to say that whether the box has beliefs is just a matter of which language I happened to pick.
