So: what should those three people actually spend the two years on? Give me three or four questions, in order, with the ordering defended — I care much more about the reasoning then the list. Argue for the criteria as well: how upstream a question is (how much else changes meaning depending on the answer), how tractable it is in two years for people who can model and synthesize literature but can't run new field studies, and how much it would actually change a concrete decision, like whether some org opposes a particular rewilding push or a particular logging concession. And what evidence in the next couple of years would reshuffle the ranking?

Two things from seminar make me suspect the published debate is aimed one level too coarse, and I want to know whether either is secretly the crux. First, slot-filling: these populations sit near carrying capacity, so animals killed by habitat loss get replaced pretty fast by individuals who wouldn't otherwise have existed — the intervention doesn't prevent lives of suffering, it swaps who occupies a niche, which makes the right unit something like welfare-years per hectare per year rather than number of animals, and makes fewer wild animals, less suffering start to look like a category error. It smells like the non-identity problem in waders, but I can't tell if that's a reductio of it or just what it is. If it holds up, does the top priority become a measurement project instead of a philosophical one? Second, my classmate's argument: suppose a big new dataset showed that most sentient life-years in terrestrial ecosystems belong to things that die within days or weeks of being born, and that those are the lives you'd bet are net negative. Then the sign of habitat loss would turn entirely on whether it shifts the species- and life-stage mix toward or away from those short doomed lives, and that could flip between tropical forest, temperate grassland and fragmented edge habitat — which would make the standard do ecosystems contain more suffering than happiness framing nearly unactionable, with all the value in nailing the ecosystem-specific signs. Or should the fund ignore both of these and just cost out the transition itself — the deaths during clearing plus decades of succession — because that swamps any equilibrium comparison regardless? Push back on whatever I've got wrong.
