QUESTION 4 (25 marks)

In The Idea of Public Reason Revisited (1997), Rawls holds that reasonable comprehensive doctrines, religious or non-religious, may be introduced into public political discussion at any time, provided that in due course public reasons drawn from a reasonable political conception are offered that are sufficient on their own to support whatever those doctrines were introduced to support. This condition is known as the proviso.

Does the proviso make Rawls's treatment of religion more permissive than it is usually taken to be, or unstable? In answering, apply the ideal of public reason to all four of the following cases, and distinguish carefully between (i) whether the religious reason may be offered at all, (ii) who has to supply the public reason, (iii) when it has to be supplied, and (iv) whether it matters that the religious reason is the speaker's real reason for holding the view.

(a) An MP in a parliamentary debate on assisted dying, which touches matters of basic justice, cites a line of scripture and a passage from Aquinas, and in the same speech gives an argument about the equal value of citizens' lives and the protection of the vulnerable. Commentators agree that the second argument is a genuine political reason but disagree about whether it is sufficient without the first.

(b) A justice of the Supreme Court writes a concurring opinion that grounds her vote partly in the natural law tradition, while the majority opinion in the same case rests only on constitutional argument. (Recall from the lecture that Rawls treats the Supreme Court as the exemplar of public reason in a regime with judicial review.)

(c) The same MP, speaking to her own congregation at a meeting in a church hall, gives only scriptural reasons and says she isn't interested in offering any other sort.

(d) An ordinary citizen votes in a referendum on the same question purely on the basis of her religious convictions and cannot give any public reason for her vote.

CANDIDATE 41

Rawls's problem is the fact of reasonable pluralism. Under free institutions citizens will permanently disagree about religion, philosophy and the good life, and the burdens of judgement mean that this is not merely prejudice or self-interest. But political power is coercive - it is the power of citizens as a corporate body over one another - so the liberal principle of legitimacy requires that its exercise be justifiable to each citizen as free and equal. That is why in Political Liberalism justice as fairness is presented as a political rather than a metaphysical conception: it is freestanding, worked up from the political values implicit in a democratic public culture, and not derived from any comprehensive doctrine. The aim is an overlapping consensus, in which the main reasonable doctrines, religious and secular alike, each endorse the political conception from within their own doctrine and for their own reasons. This is more than a modus vivendi, since the agreement is not a mere balance of forces and is stable for the right reasons.

The proviso is where the later essay makes room for religion. It is best read as a translation requirement: because revelation and authority are sources of belief that other citizens cannot check by their own reason, a believer may not offer her religious reasons as they stand but must first translate them into the currency of public reasons. Once that translation is done she may speak, and nothing then remains in her argument that a non-believer could not accept. This is why Rawls's mature view is not a simple exclusion of religion: religion is not banned, it is translated, and the religious content is replaced rather than merely supplemented by public reasons. It also settles the timing: the political reasons must be given alongside the religious ones, in the same speech or the same piece of reasoning, since otherwise the religious reason would be doing the work on its own.

(a) The MP must therefore lead with the argument from equal citizenship and may mention scripture only once that public reason is in place. The public reason must also be sufficient by itself; if it would not persuade anyone without the scriptural premise, the proviso is not met. Whether the scripture is her real reason is neither here nor there, since on this reading it is the translated content that does the legitimating work.

(b) The proviso applies to judges in the same way: the concurring justice may appeal to natural law so long as the majority supplies the constitutional arguments, because what matters is the set of reasons the court as a whole produces.

(c) Here the duty of civility is straightforwardly violated: she offers religious reasons and refuses any others. Rawls's ideal covers public political discussion generally, including sermons, church meetings and the media, so there is no exemption for addressing one's own congregation.

(d) The same duty binds every citizen in all of their political choices, including how they vote in a referendum: a voter who can give no public reason for her vote has failed to discharge it.

What holds all of this together is an epistemic point. Religious claims rest on revelation and cannot be settled by evidence and common human reason in the way scientific and commonsense claims can, and that is why they cannot be grounds for coercing others. It follows that religious doctrines are not fully reasonable in Rawls's sense when their adherents insist on relying on revelation, since being reasonable requires basing one's beliefs on grounds that can in principle be shared by everyone.
