Hey — I'm a third-year PhD in philosophy (Kant's practical philosophy, mostly) and somehow I'm one of the two grad student reps on my university's animal ethics committee. We meet twice a month, ~30 new protocols a year plus amendments, and I'm the only person in the room who's ever read any moral philosophy. Our actual framework is a utilitarian harm-benefit analysis bolted onto the 3Rs, and it works fine for the boring cases, but it has no grip at all on the stuff I keep getting asked about, and none of my committee-mates (a vet, a facilities manager, a statistician, three PIs) can do anything with "Kant thought our duties regarding animals are indirect duties to ourselves" — which is fair, because I'm not sure I can either.

What I want to leave behind when I rotate off next summer is something usable: a decision procedure — questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it hands the question back to the committee because it's a values call rather than a factual one — that stays inside Kant's actual view instead of quietly upgrading it. By that I mean: the wrong-making feature is never what happens to the animal as such; it's what the act does to the agent (the dulling of the fellow-feeling that does the real work in our duties to other humans) and possibly to whoever else is in a position to be hardened or educated by it. If a step in your procedure only works if animals are ends in themselves in their own right, flag it: I know the neo-Kantian revisionists try to extract direct duties from broadly Kantian premises and I do not want that smuggled in. I want to know where the indirect story actually runs out.

Constraints: it has to be runnable in about twenty minutes by me and the vet without me giving a lecture; it has to end in one of our four real outputs (approve / approve with conditions / send back / decline); and the stated reason has to be one the university could say out loud if a journalist asked, since our minutes are auditable. If the honest Kantian answer is "no objection on these grounds", the procedure needs to be able to say that plainly rather than dressing up my discomfort as a finding.

Here's why this is hard. More and more of our caseload is what I've started calling no-witness protocols, where no human being ever sees the animal's suffering or its death: two insect protocols last year (automated rearing, harvest by heat or freezing, millions of individuals), a closed-system mouse phenotyping setup where the animals live and die inside racks with robotic handling and machine-vision welfare scoring and are never touched by a person, and a field protocol with live traps checked every 48 hours where the researchers mostly just find bodies. On the indirect story the whole mechanism seems to run through human beings, so all of these look like they come out fine — or at least much better than my committee thinks they should. I take that to be a problem with my application of the view rather than a discovery, but I can't say what the problem is.

The only move I can see is to distinguish three places the duty might bite: the agent's own character; the bystanders whose fellow-feeling gets dulled; and the maxim the institution is acting on and committing itself to in writing (what the university says about how a certain kind of life may be treated). For no-witness protocols only the third is left standing. Is that still indirect, or is it an expressive argument wearing Kant's coat? If the procedure is going to get any traction on those cases it has to go through something like that, so tell me what it costs.

Run it on three cases: (1) the automated insect rearing protocol; (2) the closed-system phenotyping setup; (3) one I think is fine and the committee thinks is fine — a proposal for automated, painless culling of a surplus colony by technicians who are already thoroughly desensitised, so there's no cruelty, no witnesses, and no visible effect on anyone's character. I suspect (3) comes back clean and I want to know whether the procedure is comfortable with that or whether it treats the desensitisation itself as the injury. At least one of the three has to land somewhere I won't like — tell me which, and don't tidy it up.

Two more things. First, tell me how it interacts with the 3Rs, including where they pervert: reduction pushing toward fewer, more heavily instrumented animals, and refinement by training animals to cooperate with procedures, which might make a case Kantianly worse rather than better. Second, say what the procedure cannot do. Also: we recently found out our remit covers cephalopods and decapods too, and if your procedure has a step that needs a sentience verdict, tell me what to do when the answer is "probably, but nobody's sure" — that's most of our caseload now, and it definately shouldn't be the step everything else hangs on.
