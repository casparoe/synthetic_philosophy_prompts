Hey — I want you to work as a professional philosopher who specialises in early modern philosophy and the epistemology of testimony. Take your time with this one: before you commit to anything, brainstorm three or four candidate principles and the cases that kill them, then throw away all but one. I want the principle you actually endorse, not a menu of options with the choice left to me.

I've been re-teaching Hume's chapter on miracles (section X of the 1748 Enquiry, the one censorship made publishers quietly drop) and I've noticed I can't tell my students what I actually think, only what Hume thinks. The part I keep repeating is his maxim, paraphrased: no testimony is enough to establish a miracle unless the falsehood of that testimony would be a bigger marvel than the miracle itself. Plus the supporting bits — witnesses are never numerous or educated enough, people love the marvellous, miracle stories flourish among ignorant nations, and rival traditions cancel each other out. Fine. But when I try to apply the maxim to actual cases my gut gives verdicts I can't unify, and I'd like you to find the principle behind them. Here are my verdicts, in my own words:

1. A single continuous high-resolution recording, forensically unbroken, of an amputated leg regrowing over four hours in a hospital, with forty staff present and the leg still there afterwards. I'd accept that something happened that current biology can't account for. But I notice I want to say "our biology was wrong" rather than "a law of nature was violated," and I suspect that move is a dodge.

2. Tens of thousands of pilgrims at a shrine, all expecting something, all saying the sun moved, and describing what they saw differently from each other. Worth next to nothing as far as I'm concerned.

3. Twelve sober, educated, mutually hostile witnesses to an executed man walking around three days later; no shared source, nothing to gain, several tortured without recanting. Here's my problem: if you tell me this happened last week in my city I'd be genuinely shaken, and if you tell me it's in an ancient source I'm completely cold. I don't think that difference should be doing any work, but it is.

4. A coin, tested beforehand, flipped by a machine in front of statisticians, comes up heads three hundred times in a row. I'd bet on rigging. But if I'm forced: I'd say something wildly improbable happened, and I also don't think this is a miracle in Hume's sense at all — so I don't know whether his argument has anything to say about merely statistical regularities.

5. Two hundred geologists on five continents, with samples, report that every piece of quartz on earth turned blue for an hour. No doctrine attached, no one had a motive. I'd believe this one.

6. A healing shrine in France with a medical review board: a handful of certified inexplicable cures out of an enormous number of sick visitors. Unimpressed — and I think the huge denominator makes it weaker, not stronger.

7. A sealed letter from 1919, authenticated by archivists, predicting the exact date and manner of a named person's death in 2024. This moves me far more than 6 does, and I can't cleanly say why.

8. Equally well-attested miracles at shrines of two different religions. I don't think they cancel each other — that feels like guilt by association — but I do think the pattern tells me something about miracle reports as a class.

9. A filmed faith healing where nothing visible happens, and believers say the camera can't capture the spirit. I count this as evidence against, not a wash. Failure and success aren't symmetric for me.

The pair I keep circling on is 6 and 7: both are documented unlikely events, and I treat them oppositely. Number 3's sensitivity to how long ago it happened I suspect is just indefensible.

Here's my own stab, which I want you to test and repair: take a miracle report seriously only if the probability that a report of exactly this shape would exist given no violation occurred is lower than the probability that the law has an exception — and compute that first probability from the specific causal history of this report, not from a base rate over miracle reports as a class. Is the second clause (the local-only bit) what's making 2 and 3 inconsistent with each other? If you drop it, what happens to 5?

Two cases I haven't listed, so you can show me what your principle predicts: a big lab team gets a reading that violates conservation of energy, one instrument, never reproducible, nobody ever finds the error — I say instrument error, and I want to know whether your principle lets me say that or forces me to count it. And: in a world where video and audio can be faked perfectly and cheaply, does my verdict on 1 collapse, or should the principle have something timeless to say about recording channels?

Please also say how "law of nature" has to be understood for any of this to work — strict exceptionless regularity, or statistical — and what breaks on each reading. Don't give me reading suggestions or named scholars to look up; I can't check references from here, so argue it out. Commit: one principle, stated precisely, each clause tied to the case that motivates it, the verdicts on my two unlisted cases, a plain statement of which of my verdicts you think I have to give up and why they're wrong, and one flag on the place where you yourself are least confident.
