Write a sestina on the Gricean distinction between what a speaker says and what they implicate: six six-line stanzas whose end-words rotate by the standard rule (last word first, then first, then second-to-last, and so on), closed by a three-line envoi that gathers all six words back in.

The conceit I want the form to carry is that the six words never change while their positions and their company always do. Let the recurrence of the words stand for the invariant literal content of a sentence, and each new position for the context of its utterance, so a reader feels that nothing about the words themselves has shifted and yet something different is being conveyed. Let the envoi, where all six crowd together, be the place where saying and implicating are hardest to prise apart — and, if you can make it work, where the circularity worry that Grice's own analysis invites closes the loop: speaker meaning is spelled out as an intention that the hearer recognize that very intention, and recognition already seems to involve meaning.

Keep it recognizable to someone who knows the material: let the Cooperative Principle and the standard diagnostics — cancellability, calculability, non-detachability — do real work in the poem rather than being named in it. Choose your own six end-words, but let at least one of them be capable of shifting between being said and being implicated, and let a reader who knows the distinction be able to tell which stanza is which.

Then add a note of three or four sentences: say which interpretive liberty the poem takes that a sympathetic Gricean might refuse, and say what would make you rewrite it — if the circularity objection, or the relevance-theoretic claim that much of what Grice called implicature really belongs to what is said, were correct, which stanza or which of the six words would have to go, and why.
