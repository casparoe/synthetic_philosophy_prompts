Not sure this is the right sort of thing for this folder, but I'll try it. I'm retired now — was a geomicrobiologist, spent most of my career on things living in rock where they had no business living — and since I stopped I've been going to a philosophy of science reading group and reading the astrobiology literature properly for the first time. There's a move I keep running into in it that I don't have a word for, and I want to know whether somebody has already named it.

The shape of it: people keep finding life in places that were supposed to be impossible — depths, pressures, salt concentrations, radiation doses, pH values that used to get called sterile, soils that get one wet day a decade. Each of these findings gets used to widen the map of where life can be. That's just learning, fine. But look at what the widening does to the numbers. It does two things at once. It makes "there is life elsewhere" more probable before you look, because there's more real estate and more ways of getting started. And it makes any particular failed look less informative, because the instruments were built to recognise the chemistry we knew about before we widened. So one and the same update is spent as credit on the prior and never charged against the test.

The case that made me write this down: a sample comes back from somewhere promising — an icy moon's plume, a Mars-analogue core, whatever — and there's nothing in it anyone wants to call a biosignature. I'd have expected people to come out of that saying the probability for that body went down. Instead you get that it's "not constraining": wrong spot, wrong depth, one pass on one day, our kit only knows Earth chemistry, a metabolism we haven't thought of would leave something we wouldn't recognise. All of which is probably true. But it's the same consideration that made the prior go up, and it never seems to cost anything. And it cuts both ways: the Viking soil result from the 70s that looked positive got explained away as odd chemistry, and loosing that one didn't cost anything either.

What I'd like isn't a label, and please don't give me a reading list, I can't be doing with chasing papers. I want the carving: what exactly is this, and where does it stop? Specifically the bits I can't sort out —

Is it just unfalsifiability? It doesn't feel like it, because I can think of an outcome that would sink the hypothesis: a long-lived, energy-rich, properly sampled environment with nothing in it. It isn't that nothing could refute it. It's that the set of outcomes that would refute it keeps shrinking at the same time as the prior keeps rising.

Is it just moving the goalposts, or special pleading? Also wrong-sounding, because the widening gets done in daylight, for reasons everybody accepts, and each individual step is one I'd have agreed to myself. (I remember being told off for being a carbon chauvinist, and fair enough.)

Is it just "absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence"? That one's about a single inference; what I keep seeing is cumulative and gets worse each round.

Is it confirmation bias or disconfirmation bias? Maybe, but those are claims about the psychology of the people doing it, and I think you could get exactly this pattern out of careful, honest people who are doing their best.

I also tried writing it out in Bayes terms and got muddled: raising the prior and flattening the likelihood ratio pull in opposite directions, and I can't see in general which one wins, or whether it depends on something about where in the space you are.

So: does this thing have a name, in philosophy of science or formal epistemology or anywhere else? I gather medicine and ecology have their own versions of it — widen a case definition and then act surprised that prevalence went up and the test looks worse. If there are two or three near neighbours, tell me which fits and how the others differ from it. If there genuinely isn't one, give me one: define it, and give me the edges — one case that's clearly the bad version, one that's clearly the legitimate version, because surely there is a legitimate version, sometimes you really should widen the target and it shouldn't count against you. And tell me what someone who wanted not to do it would have to commit to in advance, before seeing the result. That last bit is the part I actually want.
