I need a case built to order, in the vicinity of the Judy Benjamin problem, for a section of a paper I am writing on whether that puzzle is partly an artifact of withholding the channel model and whether the artifact can be decision-relevant. Please construct it to the specification below.

The formal skeleton is fixed, and I would rather you not alter it. There are four cells: a region divided into two halves, North and South, each of which is divided into two sectors, A and B. The agent's prior is uniform: one quarter on each of the four cells. She then receives exactly one message, whose content is a bare conditional about the North half: if you are in the North half, your chance of being in sector A is three quarters. The message says nothing explicit about the South half and nothing about which half she is in, it is not self-verifying, and she receives no other evidence about her location.

Three update rules are in play, defined operationally so that nothing hangs on labels:

R1, conditioning on receipt. Assign to each cell a likelihood, the probability that a message with this content would reach her given that cell, and update by Bayes' rule on the event of receipt.

R2, constraint rigidity. Impose the stated conditional, hold fixed the marginal over the two halves, hold fixed the odds between A and B within the South half, and renormalize.

R3, minimum relative information. Among all distributions satisfying the stated conditional and nothing else, take the one minimizing the sum over cells of q times the log of q over the prior.

What the case must do: R1 and R2 must deliver exactly the same posterior over the four cells, and R3 must diverge from both. The divergence should reduce to a single number, and you should say which number and why the whole disagreement sits there. Then add one small decision the agent faces, with explicit utilities and an explicit threshold, such that the two sides of the disagreement recommend different actions: state the threshold, show that it lies strictly between the competing credences, and do the expected-utility arithmetic for both.

Constraints on the story. The scenario itself in one paragraph of at most 150 words, and realistic: a radio protocol, a notification, a notice, a letter, or the like, with no omniscience, no infallible informant, no perfect predictor, and no science fiction. Every likelihood must be strictly between zero and one. The likelihoods must be generated by a mechanism the reader can picture, so that each value, and each ratio between values, is explained by something in the story such as distance, terrain, relay placement, or the terms of the protocol, rather than set by fiat to make the arithmetic come out; all the same, I would like the numbers to be exact rationals so the check is clean. The agent must be in a position to know the likelihoods, or at least their ratios, and the story should make clear why that is credible. If you cannot hit the target exactly with a clean mechanism, say so, give the closest mechanism you can, and explain what would be needed to close the gap.

After the scenario I want: a table giving the prior, the four likelihoods, and the three posteriors over the four cells; a walkthrough of what each of the three rules delivers and why; the closed form of the R3 posterior as well as its decimals to four places; an identification of the single structural feature doing the work; one perturbation of the case, to the message, the prior, or the likelihoods, that collapses the divergence, with the arithmetic shown for the perturbed case and a note on which rule's verdict turns out to be the unstable one; and the strongest objection a defender of R3 could raise, which I anticipate will be either that supplying a full channel model means this was never a case of updating on a bare conditional, or that the constraint set for R3 should include the likelihood facts, in which case R3 collapses into R1, together with your assessment of whether that objection defuses the point I want the case to make.

Finally, tell me how confident you are that the three posteriors are correct, that the two sides really do disagree about the action, and that the case meets the constraints rather than stipulating its verdicts, and single out the step you are least sure of.
