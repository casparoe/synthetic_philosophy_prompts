Me and two others are putting together an anotation guideline for a corpus of chatbot answers, and the label set has 'lie' and 'misleading but not a lie' as seperate tags. Before we argue about edge cases any further I want the spec: what would an account of the lying/misleading distinction have to deliver to be good enough to build that guideline from? Not the annotator checklist itself, the philosophical spec underneath it, the thing the checklist would be a compression of.

So: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it has to keep from collapsing, and the results from pragmatics or psychology it isn't allowed to contradict, each with the reason it earns a place on the list. Things about our setting that matter for what the spec has to do:

- the 'speaker' is a language model, so there is no access to what it believed, whether it intended anything, or what it meant. We have the string, the question it was answering, and whatever the system prompt said, and that's it
- the hearers aren't one person. The same string implicates p for one user and not for another depending on what they already know, and we can't stipulate a single ideal reader
- there's no tone of voice, no face, no hesitation. Whatever the ordinary concept of lying leans on that is simply unavailable here
- most of our borderline items aren't false sentences. They're omissions, hedges, literally true sentences with a false takeaway, selective quoting, and numbers stated with a precision the source didn't have
- some are false because the model couldn't be bothered to check, or because the user's question presupposed something false and the model played along. I don't know which side of the line those go on
- plenty of answers are just confidently wrong, and we don't want confident error to come out as lying

Then the three hard parts. First: mark which requirements are non-negotiable for a purpose like ours and which are just the prejudices of one camp in the literature (I suspect 'verdicts must not depend on the hearer' is one of those, but Im not sure). Second: if any two requirements on your list can't be met together, say so and say which you'd drop, that's actually the part I most want. Third: name the single requirement that does the most work excluding otherwise attractive accounts. A short scorecard for four families the 'says what it believes false' one, the implicature one, the intention-to-deceive one, and the indifference-to-truth one is fine if it fits, but the criteria are the deliverable.
