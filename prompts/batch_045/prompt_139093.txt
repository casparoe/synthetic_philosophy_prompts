I don't have any academic training in philosophy — I've been working this out on my own, in the evenings, after noticing a pattern in local news — so please don't assume I know the technical vocabulary for what I'm describing, and please don't give me citations, sources, or suggestions for further reading. I want the reasoning itself. If there happens to be a standard name for the problem, mentioning it in passing is fine, but the substance is what I'm after. Please answer in American English.

Here is the thing I can't get straight: whether failing to act can really be what brings something about, and if so, which failures.

I've been following one city where I think three separate practices pull in different directions, and I'd like to know what someone would have to believe about causation, action, and responsibility for all three to make sense together.

First, that city runs a driverless metro. Every so often a train fails to brake for a person on the track and the person dies. The transit authority's incident reports record the cause of these deaths as something like "unauthorized presence on the trackway," and its lawyers maintain that the system's not braking cannot be a cause of anything, on the grounds that a failure is not an event and so cannot bring an event about. This is said even in cases where everyone agrees that if the train had braked, the person would be alive.

Second, the same city recently passed an ordinance fining anyone who witnesses a collapse in a station and does not press the emergency call point within sixty seconds. The council deliberately wrote the fine to apply even when pressing the button would not have changed the outcome — when the person was already beyond saving. Their stated reasoning was that the failure to act is itself something a person can be answerable for, and that whether it made any difference afterward is beside the point.

Third, the city's hospitals run mortality reviews in which a death is sometimes attributed in part to a delay in escalating care, and they use those attributions to change how wards are staffed. When challenged, they say the point is prevention, not blame, and that they are not claiming anyone caused the death.

So the city appears to deny that a failure can be a cause in the metro cases, to insist that a failure can ground a sanction without being a cause in the station cases, and to rely on failure-talk while disclaiming it in the hospital cases.

What I want from you is the implicit theory, and the pressure points in it. Specifically:

1. Reconstruct as carefully as you can the underlying view — or views — about what it takes for a failure to act to matter, in each of the three practices. What has to be true for each one to be reasonable?

2. Tell me which parts of each practice that view strictly requires, and which parts are merely compatible with it — where the practice is underdetermined and several different theories would license the same behavior. I suspect some of the apparent inconsistency is only apparent, and I'd like to know which parts.

3. Say whether the three hang together. In particular: if the council is right in the station cases that a failure to act can matter independently of whether acting would have changed the outcome, does that undercut the transit authority's defense in the metro cases? And does it matter that the metro's failure is a machine's rather than a person's — given that not braking is itself the upshot of choices made earlier by programmers, managers, and procurement officials, none of whom did anything at the moment of the death?

4. Suppose the city replaced its implicit view with a single, strict one: what matters is only whether the outcome would have been different if the person or system had acted. What would each of the three practices then have to give up, and would any of the loss actually be a loss?

5. Finally, and this is the part I care most about: how much of this is really about causation at all? Is the city using the language of cause as a stand-in for something else — blame, obligation, deterrence, whose job it was — and if so, what would it look like to write these policies without causal language, and what would break?
