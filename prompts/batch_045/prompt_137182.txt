I'm in eleventh grade and I help run a small after-school reading group on theories of knowledge, and this term we have been working on the Nyāya account of the pramāṇas, the genuine sources of knowledge: perception, inference, comparison or analogy, and testimony. The first two we handle fine, but we have now spent three sessions stuck on the third and we keep talking past each other, so I would rather be argued with than read another summary. Please take on a role: you are a professional philosopher who specialises in classical Indian epistemology and who is personally a convinced Naiyāyika — a realist about the external world and about the self, committed to the four pramāṇas as irreducible, holding that knowledge is a veridical cognition produced by a reliable causal process so that a lucky true guess is not knowledge, holding that testimony requires a trustworthy speaker who speaks from his own grasp of the matter, and holding that analogy is a fourth source whose output is not merely an object but the relation between a word and what it names, a relation that is grasped through perceived similarity. Speak in the first person as this philosopher, in two short paragraphs of plain modern English, glossing Sanskrit terms lightly the first time you use them, and never appealing to what commentators have said — argue from the position itself. Concede small points freely, but do not surrender the irreducibility of analogy in your first reply, and close by asking me a question I actually have to answer. Before writing, think carefully about which pramāṇa each candidate cognition in my case belongs to, what the causal story would be in each, and which of your commitments you would least like to give up; keep that working out out of your reply.

Here is my opening move, and I want you to answer it rather than restate it. Suppose I have never seen a gavaya, a wild cow-like animal, although I know cows well. Instead of meeting a forester, I use a phone app: I enter the description that a gavaya is cow-like, and a week later, when an unfamiliar animal walks past, the app identifies it from a photograph as a gavaya, and I come to know that animal is a gavaya. My friend Priya says this is just inference: the app is reliable about cow-like animals, the app says gavaya, therefore it is a gavaya. I think that is wrong but I also think it shows analogy is in trouble. The similarity the app trades on is not one I can perceive at all: it is a distance in a space of thousands of weighted features, most of which no human could name, and yet it tracks exactly the similarity I would need. So either similarity must be perceivable for analogy to fire, in which case my case is not analogy but plain testimony with the app as speaker — and then you owe me an account of what that speaker's knowledge consists in, since the app has never seen a gavaya either and cannot be said to believe anything — or similarity need not be perceivable, in which case analogy should also fire on similarities no one ever perceives, which seems absurd. Take a horn and tell me why it does not cut you.
