THE QUESTION

PHIL 3310, Final Examination, Question 3. Answer all three parts. (a) 8 marks, (b) 7 marks, (c) 10 marks.

(a) Set out Grice's distinction between what a speaker means (meaning_NN) and what the words or sentence uttered mean, and explain how the Cooperative Principle and the maxims are meant to account for a speaker's conveying more than she literally says. Illustrate with an example of your own.

(b) Explain, with examples, (i) the distinction between conversational and conventional implicature and (ii) the distinction between particularized and generalized conversational implicature. In doing so, say what Grice's tests of cancellability, non-detachability and calculability come to, and what each is supposed to show.

(c) In the lectures I introduced the following stipulative category. Call an utterance u in a context C a case of shadow implicature with respect to a content q just in case: (1) a competent hearer who takes u at face value and assumes the speaker is observing the Cooperative Principle would infer q; (2) the speaker foresees that hearers will infer q, and selects u partly because of that; (3) the speaker has no intention that any hearer come to believe q — her intention is only that hearers entertain q and draw their own conclusion; and (4) the literal content of u does not entail q, and no word in u conventionally encodes q, so that if challenged the speaker can sincerely deny having said or meant that q. Example: a politician asked "Did you meet with the lobbyist that week?" answers "I met with a great many people that week." Is a shadow implicature a conversational implicature on Grice's account? Answer by working through his account of speaker meaning and the three tests from part (b). Whichever position you take, state the strongest objection to it and explain how you would reply.

THE SCRIPT

(a) Grice's basic contrast is between natural and non-natural meaning. Natural meaning is a matter of one thing's being a sign of another: those black clouds mean rain. Non-natural meaning, meaning_NN, is the kind of meaning that a speaker has, and it is analysed in terms of the speaker's intentions. To a first approximation, a speaker S means that p by uttering x iff S utters x intending (i) that the hearer come to believe that p, (ii) that the hearer recognise that S has intention (i), and (iii) that this recognition should be at least part of the hearer's reason for believing that p. Because the intention has to be one the hearer recognises, Grice is committed to treating meaning as a matter of uptake: strictly, a speaker means something by an utterance only if the hearer actually recognises the intention, which is why the recognition condition is built into the analysis rather than added on afterwards.

The Cooperative Principle tells us to make our conversational contributions appropriate to the purposes of the exchange we are engaged in, and the maxims of Quantity, Quality, Relation and Manner spell out what that amounts to. A speaker may flout a maxim: she appears to violate it, but the hearer, assuming that she is still being cooperative at some level, works out what she would have to be assuming for her utterance to make sense as a contribution, and the extra content recovered in this way is the conversational implicature. Example: a referee asked to support a candidate for a research post in pure mathematics writes only, "Dr. Y's handwriting is impeccable and she has never once been late to a meeting." Everything she wrote may be true. The hearer reasons that, given Quantity, she would have said something about Y's mathematics if she thought well of it, and so she must think Y is a poor mathematician — and that, rather than any falsehood in the letter, is what she has implicated.

(b) Conventional implicatures attach to particular expressions and are not derived from the Cooperative Principle. "But" and "even" are the standard examples: "she is poor but honest" and "she is poor and honest" say the same thing, but the first additionally conveys a contrast between poverty and honesty. Since the contrast is carried by the word, it cannot be detached from it, and it cannot be cancelled either: appending "although of course there is no contrast between being poor and being honest" produces something merely odd rather than removing the implication. Conversational implicatures, by contrast, belong to no word; they are generated by the saying of what is said in a context. Particularized conversational implicatures need special features of the context to get going, as in the reference letter above. Generalized conversational implicatures arise by default: "Some of the students passed" implicates that not all of them did, without any special context. I want to say that generalized implicatures are really conventional implicatures, or very nearly so: the inference attaches to the word "some" rather than to the occasion of use, which is why cancelling it is so awkward — "Some of the students passed; in fact all of them did" sounds like a retraction or a repair of what was said, which is what one would expect if the content belonged to the expression rather than being worked out from the maxims.

As for the tests: cancellability is the requirement that one can add a clause that takes the implicature back without contradiction. Non-detachability is the requirement that the implicature cannot be detached from the utterance; but conversational implicatures are detachable in the ordinary sense, since one can always add " — and I do not mean to suggest that . . .," and the implicature goes away. That is the same thing as cancellability, or near enough, and it is what distinguishes conversational implicatures from conventional ones, which stick to whatever word carries them. Calculability is the requirement that the hearer be able to work the implicature out from what was said together with the assumption that the speaker is being cooperative.

(c) I shall argue that a shadow implicature is not a conversational implicature. Grice ties implicature to speaker meaning: to implicate that q is to mean that q, and meaning_NN requires the intention that the hearer come to believe that q by way of recognising that very intention. Condition (3) of the stipulation says that the speaker has no such intention: she wants hearers to entertain q, not to believe it, at any rate not on her authority. So she does not mean that q; and if conversational implicature is a species of meaning_NN, she does not implicate it. Condition (4) does not settle the matter either way on its own, since no one thinks implicatures must be conventionally encoded, so the argument has to turn on intention. Grice's Modified Occam's Razor — the principle that we should not multiply senses, or implicatures, beyond necessity — points the same way, since we can describe the politician's utterance without attributing any further content to her at all. The tests tell against it too. A shadow implicature is not calculable: the audience does not reason from "she is being cooperative" to "so she must think that q," they simply take the hint, and an inference that nobody actually draws cannot be the inference that Grice's machinery reconstructs. It is worth holding on to here that implicature is a matter of what the speaker does, and uptake is a separate matter: whether an implicature is present is one question, whether anyone draws it another.

The strongest objection is this. Grice's own derivation story seems to fit the case exactly: the hearer assumes the Cooperative Principle, notices that the politician has not said she did not meet the lobbyist, and infers that she did. If we deny that this is an implicature, we seem committed either to saying that the hearer's inference is unreasonable or to saying that the politician has conveyed nothing, and neither is plausible. My reply is that it does not follow that she has conveyed nothing. She has conveyed something — she has hinted, insinuated, left the audience something to chew on. But conveying is not meaning, and pragmatics, properly so called, is the study of what speakers mean rather than of every effect an utterance has on an audience; the systematic study of hinting belongs to rhetoric. The objection therefore mistakes a fact about uptake for a fact about meaning, and Grice's framework is right to exclude the case.
