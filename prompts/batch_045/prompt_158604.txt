I have just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about the Anthropocene, and I would be grateful for a more careful account of what is at stake than either of us managed to produce.

The practice I cannot get straight is this. When geologists want to add a unit to the official Geological Time Scale, they do not decide on a date. They designate a single reference section somewhere on Earth — a golden spike — pick a physical marker in that section, and require the boundary to be an isochronous surface, one that could in principle be traced everywhere at the same instant, detectable in the material record rather than merely inferred from history. The Anthropocene Working Group followed exactly this procedure: it compared a dozen candidate sites, settled on the finely layered sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario, chose the plutonium fallout from hydrogen-bomb testing in the early 1950s as the primary marker, and drafted a first stage for the new epoch. In 2024 the proposal was voted down by the relevant subcommission and the rejection was confirmed by its parent bodies — while the accompanying statement said, in effect, that the word would nonetheless go on being used by Earth and environmental scientists, by social scientists, politicians and economists, and by the public, as an invaluable way of describing human impact on the Earth system.

What puzzles me is the combination. The decision looks like legislation — a supermajority of a small specialist committee settled the question — yet everyone involved, including those who voted against it, argues about the correct start date as though it were there to be discovered: the dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610 following the collapse of Amerindian populations and the regrowth of abandoned farmland; the spread of coal and the steam engine from about 1780; the mid-century Great Acceleration; and candidates pushed back as far as the first agriculture. And the same discipline that declines to formalise the Anthropocene nonetheless uses the word freely, defines the base of the Holocene itself in a Greenland ice core on a climate signal rather than on fossils, subdivides that epoch into ages named after short climate events, and leaves the whole post-glacial interval without faunal stages — so that archaeologists fall back on periods such as the Neolithic, which begin at different dates in different regions.

My friend's conclusion was that the golden-spike apparatus quietly presupposes a picture of a period as something that begins everywhere at once, and that this picture is the wrong one for a phenomenon that spreads. He proposed splitting the question in three: an onset, when the driving human process first crosses a threshold somewhere; an isochron, the chosen global marker; and an arrival, when the signal becomes detectable in a given basin, archive or country. On his view the Anthropocene is essentially an arrival phenomenon, the global date is only bookkeeping, and a period ought to be allowed as many start dates as there are places.

So: what conception of a historical or geological period does the golden-spike practice actually presuppose, and which rival conceptions does it rule out by construction? Does it strictly require that periods be globally synchronous and legible in a durable physical record — including a forecast about what will survive for some geologist of the deep future — or is synchrony merely something it fits well with, such that the practice is compatible with several different theories of what a period is? Does it also presuppose something about human agency and the nature/culture distinction, given that the marker is an artefact of a weapons programme rather than a change in the Earth system's own dynamics? What does it say about evidence, naming and authority that a vote of a few dozen specialists can deny the unit formal status while conceding that the term is indispensable to everyone else — and is that combination consistent, or does it equivocate between a period as a discovered feature of the world and a period as a convention for correlating it? Finally, if my friend's arrival view were adopted, what would have to change in the practice, what would be lost — comparability, mappability, the ability to say that two records are of the same interval — and would what remains still be a period at all, or rather an event, or a process?
