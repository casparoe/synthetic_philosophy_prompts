I'm trying to get clear on what jumping out of the system amounts to in the case Hofstadter illustrates with the MU-puzzle: instead of searching longer for a derivation of MU, you find a property that every theorem of the MIU system has, and conclude that MU is not derivable at all.

To make the issue concrete, consider a variant system over the alphabet {M, I, U}, with axiom MI and four rules:

1. If a theorem ends in I, you may append UUI (xI → xIUUI).
2. If a theorem has the form Mx, you may replace it by Mxx.
3. You may replace any occurrence of IIU by UU.
4. You may delete any occurrence of UUUUU.

Is MIUU a theorem of this system? Please give the reasoning, not just the verdict, and note that the naive analogue of the MU-puzzle argument (just counting I's) does not settle it here.

Then step back and explain what makes an argument of that kind a jump out of the system rather than merely a more efficient search among derivations: what exactly is such an argument a claim about, and why cannot the system itself make it? Two further points I would like addressed: whether the property used is merely necessary for theoremhood or also sufficient, and whether internalizing it as a fifth rule (declaring every string that has the property to be an axiom) would be the same achievement or would quietly change the subject.
