I've just joined the research ethics board at my hospital, and a colleague has backed me into an argument I can't escape but also can't accept.

(1) Consent is an intentional state, so it is always consent to an act under some description — nobody consents to a touching or a procedure full stop.
(2) If A consents to φ as A conceives it, and the act that actually occurs does not satisfy that description, then A's consent does not cover the act that occurred.
(3) Any false belief about any feature of the act, or of the person performing it, means the description A was consenting under is not satisfied by what actually happens.
(4) So any deception — even about a feature A is indifferent to, even one A would have consented straight through without pausing — means A did not consent to what happened.
(5) Contact that isn't consented to is a battery, and consent has to be contemporaneous, so later ratification can't cure it.
(6) Hence every deception study, every surgery performed by a substitute surgeon, every placebo dispensed with a misleading rationale, and arguably every procedure consented to on the basis of an optimistically framed risk estimate, is a battery.

That conclusion is absurd, but I can't locate the movable part, and I'd like the diagnosis plus the price list: which step do you reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it commit me to?

Two things I want in particular. First, my colleague's repair: distinguish consent to the contact, which she says is object-involving — it attaches to this person's body and this act, and is nearly indefeasible — from consent to the transaction, which is description-sensitive and is voided by fraud, giving a claim for deception but not a battery. Does that distinction hold, or does it merely relocate the question to which level a given deception operates on? And what does it imply about the identity-deception cases, where the intuitive verdict is that consent was vitiated even though the physical act was exactly what was agreed to?

Second, the counterfactual test. We lean heavily on whether the person would still have consented had she known. But consider a case it handles oddly: the investigators' deception was irrelevant to the participant, who would have enrolled anyway, and was instead essential to getting the protocol past us — the false detail sat in the protocol, not the consent form. She looks unwronged and her consent looks valid, yet the investigators plainly did something wrong. Is the would-have-consented-anyway question a test of validity at all, or only a test of whether the deceived person was harmed or wronged? If the latter, what test of validity is left?

I'd rather keep the intuitive verdict in the identity cases than the result in (6), but I want to know what that costs me.
