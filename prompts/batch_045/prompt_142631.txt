so i'm on the committee that puts together the end-of-term zine for my philosophy of mind / cognitive science seminar (it's called *The Qualia Quarterly*, I know, I didn't pick the name) and I want to run a satirical piece in the issue we're doing on machine understanding. Context: I had a genuinely annoying argument with my housemate last week — she's on the "come on, it's next-token prediction, there's no there there" side, I keep saying that line feels less decisive the more you think about what a "there" would even have to be, and we went round in circles for like two hours and got nowhere, partly because the debate in our readings has this structure where each side keeps winning on a different example and neither ever has to cash out what it would take to lose. So instead of another op-ed I want to make fun of the rhetorical situation itself.

The piece: a leaked internal memo from a fictional AI company's "Office of Epistemic Risk" to its customer-support and press staff, announcing the company's new official doctrine on whether the model understands anything, with approved talking points. Around 700 words, formatted as a memo with a few subheads and a short "anticipated questions from reporters" Q&A at the bottom. It should be deadpan corporate — no winking, no one in the memo ever notices what they're doing — and it should land the actual moves people make: defining understanding as whatever the benchmark measures and then being surprised the numbers went up; the Chinese Room reply and the way it gets waved away by saying the room as a whole understands; that thought experiment about a hyperintelligent octopus that learns a language purely from text that washes up on a beach and so can never connect the words to anything one could talk about; the asymmetry where we refuse understanding to the model but grant it without hesitation to a three-year-old or a dog; and the way "just statistics" is said as if that settled it. Please invent the company's internal vocabulary too — I was toying with a "Grounding Offset Credits" scheme (the company funds embodied robotics research to offset its model's lack of any connection between words and the world, with tiers), and with the distinction the memo keeps drawing between "understanding for pricing purposes" and "understanding for liability purposes," where the model is a person in the marketing and an autocomplete in the terms of service. Steal either or both if they're useful, or do something better.

Two things I care about: the jokes should come from getting the philosophy right, so that someone in my department who actually works on this laughs rather than winces — and by the end a reader who's never read any of it should have felt why the question is hard, not just that the company is cynical. I'd also like at least one moment where the memo's own logic quietly turns on itself (a talking point that concedes the whole thing, an internal contradiction nobody flags) without any character commenting on it. Fictional company, obviously — no real labs named, and no named philosophers as characters; the invented head of the Office is fine.
