So I've somehow ended up on the three-person volunteer committee that has to re-check and sign off on all 900-odd line items in the accessibility compliance spreadsheet for our town's new library, and I also wrote the user guide that goes with it. Mornings it's the lottery thing: each line looks fine on its own, but obviously not all nine hundred are fine. Evenings it's the preface thing: I stand behind every section of the guide and I'm also dead sure there's a mistake in it somewhere. Its making me wonder whether these are one puzzle in two costumes or two puzzles that just resemble each other, so I want a pair of cases that pries them apart.

Build me a matched pair. In Case L the lottery pull is strong — clearly right to treat each item as fine one by one, clearly wrong to lump them together — but the preface pull is missing: nobody is tempted to add, in that rueful self-doubting way, "and also one of these is wrong." In Case P the reverse: the preface pull is strong and the lottery pull is weak. Constraints: ordinary workplace or committee setting, no gambling, no numbered balls; about a paragraph each; same number of items, same strength of evidence per item, same stakes, same kind of aggregation in both. The cases may differ in exactly one feature and you pick which, but you can't make the person sloppier, more tired, or less expert in one of them, and you can't have anybody hand them a base rate or a probability figure in one and not the other. Don't just stipulate the attitudes — the pull has to fall out of the setup.

After both cases: state the per-item attitude and the aggregate attitude in each, name the one feature you varied and say why it pushes the two verdicts in opposite directions, say what happens to the pair if you flatten that feature out, and then give me the best version of the complaint that someone who thinks both paradoxes have a single unified answer would make about how you rigged it.
