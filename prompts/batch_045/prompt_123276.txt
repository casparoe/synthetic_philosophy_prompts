I keep having the same argument with a friend who does sentencing mitigation work for a legal aid office, and I want a much better version of it than either of us has managed so far. We keep going round about COMPAS — the recidivism risk score used in a bunch of US courts, the one ProPublica went after in 2016, and the one at the centre of the Wisconsin case where Eric Loomis got six years with a COMPAS score in front of the judge and then challenged it on due process grounds; the state supreme court upheld the use of the score but required that it come with warnings, and the US Supreme Court declined to hear it the following year. The facts as I remember them, and please just take them as given even if I have a detail or two wrong: the tool is proprietary, filled in from a long questionnaire about the person's history and circumstances, and it outputs a risk category; ProPublica's complaint was that black defendants were flagged as high risk and then did not go on to be arrested again at roughly twice the rate that white defendants were so flagged, while the company's reply was that the scores mean the same thing across groups — a high-risk black defendant and a high-risk white defendant reoffend at similar rates. My understanding is that both of those can be true at the same time, because they are two different formalisations of the idea that like cases should be treated alike, and when the underlying base rates differ you cannot satisfy both. Tell me if that matters to your analysis but don't get stuck there, because it isn't the part I care about.

What I actually want is the mapping, and I want it to run in both directions. Take the question of whether AI systems should reason as utilitarians or as deontologists and run the episode through it properly: who the players really are (the defendant, the judge, the vendor, the legislature or corrections department that bought the thing, the people who would have been victimised by crimes that never happen, the people locked up longer because of a number), what the actual decision options were at each stage (build the tool, buy it, put the score in front of a judge, choose the threshold, disclose or not), what counts as evidence on each side, and what each theory says each of those players should have done. And then the places I think it has to break. One: a deployed system is not a person facing a one-off dilemma, it is a policy applied to thousands of people, so the act-versus-rule distinction that does so much work in the human case seems to collapse or at least move, and the system's real moral decision happened at training time, in front of nobody. Two: these systems are built by minimising a loss and then bolting constraints onto the outside, which looks less like a standoff between utilitarianism and deontology and more like consequentialism with side constraints bolted on — so the live question becomes which layer gets to win when the constraint is expensive, and I want your read on what that does to the original debate. Three, which I have never seen anyone make much of: the consequences the utilitarian version is supposedly maximising partly depend on the deployment itself, because longer sentences for flagged people and more police attention in flagged neighbourhoods feed back into the data the tool is validated against, so the aggregate calculus is self-referential in a way the deontological constraint is not. Does that asymmetry give the deontologist something real, or does it just make the consequentialist's homework harder?

Then turn it around on the theory. My friend's position, which I half believe and half think is a dodge, is that none of this is a moral decision in the first place: a risk score is a prediction, predictions are neither utilitarian nor deontological, and all of the ethics lives in what the judge does with the number.
