Hey — I teach a 200-level moral philosophy seminar, 22 undergrads, one 75-minute block in a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard, no tech I'd actually need. We're spending a few weeks on blame, and I want one session built around something my students already half-believe but can't articulate: that there are cases where a person blames someone, everything they say is true, the target really is blameworthy — and still the blamer has no business being the one to say it. I don't want to name the distinction up front or hand them the vocabulary; I want them to run into it and only hear the words afterwards. Last year I tried to get at this by just putting a hypocrite case on the board, and it collapsed into forty minutes of people listing political examples of whataboutism, so please don't build me anything that opens that door again.

Here's the wrinkle I actually want the session to land on, and it's why a stock hypocrisy case won't do. There seem to be two opposite ways to lose your footing as a blamer. One is the familiar one: you did the very thing you're blaming her for, or you'd have done it in her shoes, and her friends say you've got no right to talk. The other runs the other way: you've never once been tempted by anything like this, you've never been anywhere near that situation, and she says you don't know what it's like, so what would you know about it. If both complaints land — and in discussion nobody wants to give up either one — then the only person with full standing is someone who has felt the pull of exactly this kind of temptation and refused it, which is a weirdly narrow window, and I've started calling it the squeeze. I'd love it if the activity made them *feel* the squeeze rather than just notice it: give some students roles where their own record undercuts them, give others roles where their clean hands and total lack of temptation do the same, and let the targets push back with whichever complaint fits. If you can fit in a third flavor, add the blamer who never did the thing but quietly profited from other people doing it.

What I need back is something runnable: materials, roles or rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, and whatever small real stakes you'd use (I'll spend twenty bucks on snacks or printed cards, I'm not putting anyone's grade or comfort on the line), plus a debrief. The debrief is where I'll be leaning on you hardest: map specific moments in the room onto the pieces — that the target's blameworthiness didn't move when the blamer's record came out even though everyone's willingness to listen definately did, that blame can be correct and still not yours to issue, and that the two complaints might not even be the same kind of condition, one looking like fairness and the other looking like understanding or credibility. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I'm guessing somebody refuses their role, somebody moralizes, somebody decides the lesson is that the target did nothing wrong, and somebody figures out the setup early and spoils the reveal. And be honest about which part of this the game can't hand them — my guess is that a staged reveal makes the blamer's self-knowledge, and the timing of it, too clean, and that a game can't really show standing as something graded and repairable, where an apology or a changed record gets your voice back. If that's right, say so and tell me what I'll just have to say out loud at the end.
