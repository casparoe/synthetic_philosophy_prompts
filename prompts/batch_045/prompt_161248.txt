I referee for a couple of journals and I'm on hiring committees most years, and I keep running into the same shape of thing without having a word for it.

Here's how it goes. I read a paper and I form a view on the merits: it's weak, the central argument doesn't work. Or I read a file and think the candidate is strong. Then I notice something about the *process* that produced the view rather than about the object: I'm reading the sixth submission of the day and I'm fried; or I liked the previous two papers for similar reasons so I'm probably in a groove; or the candidate works in exactly the subfield I'm protective about; or everyone who agrees with me on this committee also agreed with each other on the last three hires. And noticing that seems like a genuine reason to move my confidence down, not just a vague feeling of humility.

But here's the part I can't get straight. The same noticing seems available at the next level up. Someone who is tired and irritable is, as far as I can tell, not especially good at judging whether they're tired and irritable. Someone with a bias is usually quite confident that *this* case is the one where the bias doesn't apply. So the reason to discount seems to reapply to itself. And then again. In practice nobody actually spirals — I get to a level and I stop, usually with something like "I'm too fried to have an opinion on this, let's table it and look tomorrow." But the stop doesn't come with a reason for stopping at that level rather than one up or one down, and I can't tell whether that's a principled stopping point I'm grasping at or just where my patience runs out.

It shows up in arguments too. Someone says "you only believe that because you're X" — employed by the industry, going through a divorce, a fan of the rival team, raised in a particular place. Sometimes that's accepted as a real reason to discount and sometimes it's treated as a cheap way to avoid engaging with the claim. It looks like the same move getting opposite verdicts, and I don't know what separates the good version from bad one.

There's also a version that runs in reverse and I don't know if it's the same animal: I've gone at a problem from four different angles and kept landing in the same place, and that feels like evidence *for* the conclusion, not just an absence of undermining. Is that the same mechanism pointed the other way, or something else entirely?

Things I've been told to call it, and why I don't think either fits: I've seen "higher-order evidence" used for something nearby, but as far as I understand it that's mostly about learning that some expert or peer disagrees with you — a datapoint about disagreement coming from outside. Here there's no second opinion; I'm generating the doubt from inside the process, and the doubt is about the process itself rather than about how my view compares to someone else's. A friend suggested "epistemic akrasia," but that sounds like it's about ending up believing something while also thinking you shouldn't believe it, and what I end up with usually isn't a belief at all — it's a shrug, or a confidence that drops to nothing in particular rather than migrating to the other side.

I'm after more than a label, though. I want to know: whether the self-applying bit is a genuine regress or only looks like one, and if there's a principled place to stop, what makes it principled; whether the *scope* of the doubt is what does the work — doubt about this one episode (I'm tired right now) versus doubt about the kind of situation I'm in (people in my position reliably get this wrong) versus doubt about everybody (humans just aren't good at this) — because my guess is that the narrow version is self-limiting in a way the wide version isn't, but I can't say why; and what the boundary is between the legitimate and the cheap version of "you only say that because." If the honest answer is that there's no established thing here and the best move is to draw the distinctions myself, I'd rather be told that with the distinctions drawn than handed a name that doesn't quite cover it.

The reason I'm asking rather than just shrugging: I'd like to know whether a committee should build any of this into how it treats a first-pass verdict, or whether the zoom-out only ever works as a one-off check and collapses into noise if you try to make it a rule.
