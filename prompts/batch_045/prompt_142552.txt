I sit on the legal team of a truth commission for a country emerging from a decade of military rule, and we have to write the criteria under which someone who fully discloses their role in atrocities receives amnesty rather than prosecution. The familiar reference points pull in different directions: South African practice conditioned amnesty on full disclosure before a public body; Argentina moved from prosecuting the juntas to narrowing prosecution; Rwanda put confession and guilty plea to work in community proceedings that traded truth for sentence reduction. Before we draft, I want to know what I actually think, so I have been going by gut on cases, and my verdicts do not line up.

My tentative principle is that amnesty is in order just when three conditions hold: the *counterfactual-discovery* condition — what the applicant discloses would probably not have come to light without the offer; the *unowned-agency* condition — the applicant acted within a structure of command whose direction they did not set; and the *no-settlement* condition — granting it does not require the commission to certify as settled a description of events that victims reasonably contest.

My verdicts:

1. A conscript who shot four prisoners on orders and now reveals burial sites that no one else knows: amnesty fine.
2. The general who wrote the counter-insurgency doctrine under which those prisoners were shot, and who discloses more than anyone else possibly could: not fine, even though he is my best source and loses me the most truth.
3. A clerk who typed the death lists for six years and hands over the complete set, which was hidden but which investigators would probably have recovered from the abandoned ministry within a year: fine, though shakier than (1).
4. A banker who arranged the off-book financing that sustained the death squads and reveals the whole funding chain: I keep saying not fine and then wavering — he killed no one, and no one else can produce this.
5. A perpetrator who discloses fully, but only after three others have already named him: not fine, even though the disclosure is complete and the information is the same as it would otherwise have been.
6. A perpetrator whose disclosure adds nothing the commission does not already have from the regime's own archives, but who says it in public, in front of the families: not fine.
7. A perpetrator who also adds nothing new, except that his account corrects the archive: the killing everyone attributes to a disbanded unit was ordered elsewhere, and a man posthumously blamed is exonerated: fine.
8. A village that killed its neighbours together: two hundred people confessed, pleaded guilty, named who cut whom, and asked to go home. Full amnesty not fine; a large sentence reduction fine.
9. A perpetrator who confesses to a killing the commission later establishes he did not commit, covering for a superior: not fine, obviously — but I cannot say which of my conditions it violates.

Cases 6 and 7 look alike and I separate them; cases 2 and 4 look alike and I cannot settle 4. So: is there one principle that gets all nine, or nearly? Where it cannot, tell me which verdict to drop and why. State the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and give its verdicts on two cases I have not listed — someone abducted at fourteen who was victim and then perpetrator, and someone who disclosed everything under the outgoing regime's self-amnesty, which we have since declared void.

Please answer in British English.
