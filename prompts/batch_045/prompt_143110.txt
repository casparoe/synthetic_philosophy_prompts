Before I retired I spent a little over thirty years as an applied entomologist — insect population dynamics, stored-product pests, a good deal of insecticide resistance work — and I have lately been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, which is how I came to be arguing about the moral status of insects. Our last session left me wanting to write an essay, and I would like some help thinking it through.

What I want is a reading of Kafka's The Metamorphosis as a study in what I have started privately to call the vermin frame: the slide by which a description that is straightforwardly functional — an organism causing damage, in a place we do not want it, in numbers we will not tolerate — quietly acquires the moral standing of a pest, that is, of something whose removal needs no further justification. My old literature had a name for the further complication: pest resurgence and secondary pest outbreaks. Suppress one population and you release the niche it was occupying, so control is never a discrete act terminating in a death; it is an edit to the composition of a community, and the replacement harms were never ones you chose. Any ethics of pest control that counts only the animals it kills is therefore counting the wrong unit, and probably counting it as if the death were the whole of the harm.

Read the novella against that. Gregor is, on any entomological description I can give you, not a pest at all: a single non-reproducing individual, damaging nothing, vectoring nothing, not a member of any population. And yet the family's reasoning — the sister's verdict that it has to go, the father's apples, the woman who comes in and sweeps the body out with a broom, the relief afterwards and the tram ride into the countryside — runs exactly on the vermin frame, and runs on it the more comfortably the less Gregor is able to answer. I would like that reading done properly and then tested:

Does the story function as a reductio of the frame, or as a demonstration that the frame is what is left when there is no other way to live with a body that has become unlivable? Where does the reading fail — where is Kafka doing something other than making an argument about moral status, and the shame, dependency, and family economy of the thing are carrying the weight? Is the novella's silence about whether the insect suffers a blind spot that matters, or a sign that the question is not the one the book is asking? And does the mismatch in scale defeat the whole exercise: the pest-control debate is about enormous numbers and population-level effects, this is about one animal, and it is not obvious that a story about one can tell us anything about a practice whose ethical problem just is its scale?

I want something I could defend in front of both audiences: it should not flatten the novella into a parable about insects, and it should not bend the ethical argument until it fits the plot. If the honest verdict is that Kafka has nothing to contribute here, tell me that instead — and tell me which scenes would then have to be doing work they plainly are not doing.
