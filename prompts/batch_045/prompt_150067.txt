Writing a novel about an art forger and I keep tripping over something I don't have a word for. My guy forges a minor 1920s painter, gets caught, and here's the bit I can't get right: after the confession the pictures are worth nearly nothing, but honest copies in that painter's manner — good ones, signed by the copyist, sold as exactly what they are — go for real money. Same eyes, same skill, same wall. So the collapse isn't about how the thing looks, and it isn't about anyone still being fooled, because nobody is. Its not just rarity either: canvases sold as "school of", or with the authorship left open, do fine at a discount. It's definately specific to the ones that have been found out — those go to zero and stay there even though everyone now knows precisely what they are.

And then the second half, which is the part that actually interests me: the same exposed picture will sometimes fetch more than the forger's own signed work, because it has a story attached. You're not looking at a painting, you're looking at the painting that duped the experts. My dealer character calls it the confession discount, which is too cute and doesn't cover that bit anyway.

Someone told me philosophers talk about a "perfect fake" — two looking-identical paintings, wildly different value — but that puzzle is set before anyone finds out, and mine starts after. So: is there a name for the loss that disclosure can't repair, and for the small premium that creeps back afterwards? And if there's a literature, don't just hand me a title to stick in the acknowledgements. I want to know what is supposed to have gone wrong with the object itself — whether it's the lie, or the copying, or an achievement being credited to the wrong person — and what any of that amounts to in the case where nobody ever finds out. Bonus: does it carry over to a forged manuscript, where the words are identical and the paper was never the point?
