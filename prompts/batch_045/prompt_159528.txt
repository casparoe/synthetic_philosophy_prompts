So: two philosophers I know have been sniping at each other in footnotes for about a decade, and an editor has finally locked them in a room and told them to come out with one joint document. One is a variationist/restrictionist experimental philosopher — the "intuitions are demographically contaminated, so philosophy has to restrict or recalibrate its evidential base" camp. The other is an expertise defender — the "the variation you're finding is surface noise, trained judgement is in decent shape, armchair philosophy survives" camp. I want you to write the document they'd produce from a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Treat the following as jointly stipulated findings from a big pre-registered multi-lab study across roughly forty societies, within-subject design, translated vignettes, comprehension checks included. Don't argue about whether they're real, just use them:

1. The side-effect asymmetry in the classic chairman/CEO-style vignette replicates almost everywhere, at comparable magnitude.
2. Gettier-style knowledge attributions vary a lot across societies, and the variation tracks average years of formal schooling and urban-vs-rural, not the usual East/West or WEIRD/non-WEIRD split people expected.
3. Professional philosophers show about as much cross-country spread as matched non-philosophers, but they're more confident, give more theory-shaped justifications, and fail comprehension checks less often. Dropping participants who fail the comprehension check shrinks the spread in both groups but doesn't eliminate it.
4. Within-society variance is much larger than between-society variance in both groups; the society label explains only a small share of the total.

Deliverables:

- A numbered agreed-claims section. Each claim should be one that the other side would have denied fifteen years ago, or that pins down something substantive — no filler like "both agree philosophy is difficult" or "more research is needed." I want claims about the formalism, as it were: what an intuition is being treated as, what would make a body of judgments evidentially defective, what the comprehension-check result does and doesn't show, whether a mean difference matters when distributions overlap that much.
- A disagreements table. Columns: the crux, the variationist's own wording of their position, the expertise defender's own wording of theirs — each phrased so that side would actually sign it — then a concrete case, study design, or argument both accept as bearing on that crux, then each side's prediction written down in advance, then which result would move whom and how much.
- A short section on which parts of the dispute are verbal. I have a hunch some of it is: "intuition" (introspective seeming vs. spontaneous verbal judgment vs. considered verdict), "reliable" (truth-tracking vs. coherence with theory vs. stable under reflection), and maybe "philosophically significant." Say which disagreements survive the paraphrase and which dissolve.
- A short section where each side writes down what they'd count as evidence against their own view, including what they'd say if the study above came out the other way round.

Two hobby-horses of mine that I want built into the cruxes rather than bolted on:

First, the ordinal-vs-threshold distinction. A population might disagree with another about *where on a scale* the cutoff sits (at 6/10 vs 8/10) while agreeing completely about the *ordering* of cases, or they might genuinely rank the cases differently. That seems to me like the difference between a disagreement about concepts and one about response style, and finding 4 might be compatible with either. Have them say what findings 2 and 4 would look like under each hypothesis, and design the test that separates them — same cases, forced ranking on one run and rating scale on another — with each side's prediction recorded.

Second, the calibrationist's bootstrapping problem and its mirror image. The variationist needs to say which population gets treated as the calibration target and on what non-question-begging grounds, given that their own methodological intuitions presumably vary too. And the expertise defender needs a story about *which* faculty training improves: is philosophers' advantage dialectical (better at articulating and defending verdicts) or judgmental (better verdicts in the first place)? Findings 3 seems to cut at that. Put both in the table as cruxes with tests and predictions.

Aim for something around 1200–1800 words, and write it as a document both of them could put their names to — not a verdict on who's right, not a dialogue, not a diagnosis of why they've been talking past each other, though you can note in passing where they were.
