I'm two drafts into a novel (working title *The Still Mind*, probably won't survive the second draft) and one of my recurring characters is a late-fifties cultural anthropologist named Renata who's spent thirty years in a made-up highland region called Mara and sits on a made-up international review panel that issues "statements" about local practices. She's the book's resident moral relativist, and I don't want her to be the token relativist who gets flattened by the human-rights lawyer in chapter fourteen — I want her to be *right* about something. The problem is chapter twelve, where a new case lands on her desk and I relize I have no idea what she'd say given what she's already on record saying. Here's her track record, four verdicts, all of which appear in earlier chapters as quotes from her notebooks or from panel minutes.

(1) *The binding*: girls at eight, in a valley I invented, lifelong limited mobility and chronic pain, endorsed by 88% of adults including 79% of the women who went through it, and it coheres fine with everything else that valley publicly values — she abstained from the panel's statement and wrote: *I won't say it. There's no view from nowhere, and it was never mine to say.* (2) Routine infant circumcision in her own country (she's not religious, no stake in it): she calls it wrong, flatly, and says the materials for the objection — consent, bodily integrity, children — are all already inside our own framework, so this is internal critisism, not imperialism. (3) *Widow houses* on the coast, where widows live secluded for two years: the last survey had most *current widows* opposed even though most of the community overall is in favor, and she voted to condemn, writing that the people it's done to are the ones who get to say. (4) A historical one, the Long Winter famines in Mara three centuries back, when highland communities left their elders out to die: nobody defends it now, descendants are split between shame and pride, and in an interview she said she'd just call that one wrong, that there's nobody left whose framework she'd be overriding and she doesn't need their permission to be glad it stopped.

The chapter-twelve case: *the quieting*. Kids identified at five as "storm-touched" get a procedure (a nerve thing I made up, plus a herbal regimen) that permanently damps emotional intensity. Parents request it. About 90% of the valley endorses it, including most of the quietened adults, who describe themselves as content. It fits the community's explicit ideal that the still mind is the good life, and there's no internal reform movement at all. The children are five and can't consent. But twelve people who had it done and moved to the capital have started saying they'd never have chosen it, and one of them has a memoir out; the valley's response is that they've become city people and their objection doesn't count. So: which rule does Renata's track record suggest she's actually running, and what other rules fit those four verdicts about as well? What does each of them predict about the quieting, and what's your best guess at her ruling with reasons? Which of her four precedents is the closest analog and what principle is she committing to either way? And what's the one further case — devised however you like — that would actually tell the candidate rules apart? Last thing: if she rules against the valley here, is there a line (irreversibility, age, no consent, the emigrés) she can draw that a careful reader would accept as consistent with what she said about the binding, or does drawing it force her to walk back one of her earlier lines, and if so which one?
