I've been reading rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and I keep running into a pattern in the way national defeat myths behave that I don't have a word for, so I want to describe it carefully and ask whether there's an established concept for it, or whether I should coin one.

Four cases that make me think there's a single thing here. The American South spent a century building a story in which the Confederacy lost because of overwhelming numbers and industrial might, fought for constitutional liberty rather than slavery, and was defeated without being wrong; and the story is still doing work today, in ways that are clearly not inert. Serbia has the Kosovo field: a 1389 defeat that the epic tradition turned into a *choice* — a deliberate preference for a heavenly kingdom over an earthly one — so that losing is itself the victory, and the loss is what entitles the nation to something. Israel had Masada: a mass suicide at the end of a revolt, revived in the twentieth century as a national emblem of defiance, and then, on my impression, quietly demoted again once people started pointing out that a besieged group killing itself is a strange thing to admire. Britain has Dunkirk: a catastrophic defeat and the loss of an entire army's equipment, commemorated as a deliverance, a miracle, and a *spirit* — and, notably, invoked whenever the country next faces a slog.

Here is what I can't fit into any framework I know. Take a Serb or a Southerner or an English patriot who is fully willing, in private, to concede every contested detail: yes, the numbers were wrong, yes, the archaeological or archival record says something different, yes, it was a rout. Conceding the details costs them nothing. What they will not do is agree with an outsider who says the same thing; that is registered as a fresh instance of the very hostility the myth is about, and adherence goes up. The correction doesn't just fail to land, it gets *absorbed* as new content: now they're trying to erase us too.

The three-way distinction I've been using privately, which may itself be the wrong carving. Call a claim cartographic if people would change policy or predictions on learning it was false. Call it credal if the factual content is basically inert and what does the work is the willingness to say it out loud — a membership badge wearing a proposition's clothes. Neither fits the cases above, because the content there is genuinely load-bearing, people really do act on it, and yet the sign of the update on evidence about it is inverted — and inverted in a source-dependent way, so that evidence from inside the group and evidence from outside it have opposite effects. That's the thing I want named: not a proposition that can't be falsified, not a badge, but a proposition whose *update rule* has the wrong sign for one class of informant.

So: is there an established concept that carves this at the same joint? I'm aware of the research on how correcting a false belief can entrench it, and of work on how people respond to being offered material trade-offs for things they treat as sacred, and of the general idea of a group's founding wound being inherited rather than remembered — but each of those seems to me to catch a different joint than mine, and I want to be told if I'm wrong about that and one of them is really the same thing under a better description. If nothing fits, please give me a term, a definition, and boundary cases. I also want to know whether the right place to cut is the update rule, the social function, or the narrative content — because my three-way split may be mistaking a mechanism for a kind. And give me a diagnostic: in a case I haven't seen before, what would I have to observe to say that a myth has drifted from load-bearing-but-revisable into this inverted pattern, or back out of it the way I think Masada did?

The practical reason I want this is that I often want to know, in advance, whether correcting someone's national story will help or hurt, and right now I have no vocabulary for predicting which.
