i'm putting together a short "where i stand" doc for a reading group on cranes, skyhooks and greedy reductionism, and I want someone to audit it before I hand it out. Here's the package, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

(1) The crane/skyhook distinction is my main methodological tool, and I state it as: you don't get to explain an appearance of design by appealing to something whose own design is unexplained. No skyhooks, cranes all the way down.

(2) Greedy reductionism is a real error. Plenty of higher-level generalizations — in developmental biology, in evolutionary game theory, in macroeconomics — are genuinely explanatory and can't be replaced by or reconstructed from micro-level stories.

(3) I'm an eliminativist about qualia: nothing satisfies the job description (intrinsic, ineffable, private, directly and incorrigibly apprehensible). But consciousness is entirely real and first-person reports are data.

(4) There is a fact of the matter about what a system represents or is trying to do, over and above what it's useful for us to assume. A frog's visual system is really tuned to bugish things and it doesn't stop being true when we stop bothering to say so.

(5) I've been using a third category in lectures alongside cranes and skyhooks, which I call scaffolding: things that don't lift anything themselves but change what a crane can get a grip on — a membrane before there's any inheritance system, the pile of artefacts and practices a human child is born into. I claim that appealing to scaffolding is legitimately neither greedy reductionism nor a skyhook, and that it's how I explain cumulative culture without reducing it to individual psychology and without positing a group mind. I also allow a subcase, the bootstrap crane, where what's being lifted is the crane's own construction.

(6) Every step in evolution is algorithmic — nothing non-physical, no teleology imported from outside.

(7) Natural selection is genuinely creative. It produces real novelty, not a reshuffling of what was already there.

What I want is an audit, not a summary: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, ranked by severity, and for each real tension what specifically would have to give and what that revision costs me. My students always push hardest on (3) and I sort of assume that's where the trouble is, but I'd rather know if the real problem is somewhere I'm not looking. Please don't hand me six equal-weight tensions — I want the discriminating version, including at least one place where you think my worry is misplaced.

Two more things: for each verdict, say what would change your mind about it, i.e. what argument or distinction would flip you; and flag explictly where your verdict depends on how crane and skyhook get drawn apart, meaning which of your conclusions would collapse if we put the line somewhere slightly different.
