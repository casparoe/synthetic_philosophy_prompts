I've been defending a view of privacy that I dont think has a standard name, so here's my version: what privacy protects isn't information, and isn't control over information, but *standing* — whether anyone occupies a position from which attending to you, or to some part of your life, is easy and unmediated. Two consequences I bite: you lose privacy the moment someone gets into such a position, even if they never actually attend, never learn anything new, and were licensed to gather the data; and the loss is bad in itself, not just because of what it enables, because being exposed to someone that way is a way of being at their disposal. I'm happy saying a livestream nobody watches is already a privacy loss, and that a system trained on your data which could answer intimate questions about you is too, even if no one ever asks it anything.

What would count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give me the ones a sympathetic defender of a view like mine should *concede*, not just what someone who likes control accounts or contextual-integrity-style accounts would throw at it. Cases I keep circling: the sealed archive that gets destroyed unread; the spouse or the nurse who is constantly in a position to observe you, where saying you've lost something seems wrong (and if I answer by adding a permission clause, have I just collapsed back into a control account?); an all-knowing god, for whom everyone is always observable — does privacy become impossible in every possible world, and is that a reductio or a consequence I should swallow?; and the other direction, cases where the full harm of a privacy violation seems present even though nobody ever was in a position to observe anything, if you can find one. I'd also like to know which rival accounts I rise and fall with, whether "could readily attend" is really doing more work than "has access" (i.e. whether I've quietly become an access theorist), which of my two halves — the nature claim or the value claim — is the more exposed one, and honestly: have I qualified this into unfalsifiability, since for any case where the loss doesn't seem bad I can always say the standing wasn't really there or was licensed? Is that a problem for a claim of this kind, or just what a claim about a moral relation looks like? I'm moved most by cases where the wrong and the exposure come apart entirely, in either direction.
