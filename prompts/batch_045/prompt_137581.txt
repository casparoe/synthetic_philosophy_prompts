So I got into a two-hour argument with my mate last night about hard choices and I want to actually settle it with a case instead of more hand-waving. He kept pushing this whole package: in a hard choice the options are "on a par", meaning comparable but neither better nor worse nor equally good, and the test for that is that if you sweeten one option just a little — an extra five quid, one more hour of sleep, a slightly nicer shift pattern — it still doesn't tip, which he says shows you're not looking at equal value but at parity; then the reasons run out, there's no more deliberation to do, and the way you resolve it is with an act of will where you sort of put your agency behind one of the considerations and thereby create the value that settles it. I said fine, maybe that works when the choice is about your own life, but it can't be right when you're spending somebody else's reasons, like picking which of two people gets the one funded training place. He said it's the same structure either way and the will does the same work. I said no. Neither of us budgetted the time to build anything, so I want you to build it for me.

What I want is three cases, all in the same hospital in the same week, one paragraph each, nothing science-fictiony, stakes a normal person could actually face. First: the ward matron's own choice — after years in critical care she's offered a move into community palliative work, same pay, same grade, and the case has to be one where staying and going are on a par, the values genuinely don't sit on a common scale, and the sweetening test doesn't tip it. Second: the same matron has one funded specialist course place to allocate between two nurses on her ward, under a policy that requires written reasons and allows an appeal; the considerations have to be as stubbornly incommensurable as in the first case (long quiet service and loyalty against raw promise and who'd gain most, say), and again nothing tips under a small sweetening. Third: exactly the same two nurses and exactly the same funded place, everything held fixed, except the place is now paid for by a donor whose only stated condition is that it's awarded at the matron's discretion, her judgment is final, no reasons owed, no appeal.

All three have to be matched on diagnosis, and this is the part I'll check: run the small-sweetening test on each and show it comes out the same way in all three, then run three accounts across all three cases and show that each account gives the same kind of verdict about what's going on in each — my mate's parity-plus-will view, the view that there's always a determinate all-things-considered ranking and we're just too short of evidence or too thick to see it, and the view that the relevant values are simply incomparable, no positive comparison relation at all, so there's nothing to be said and you merely pick. If any of the three gives a different diagnosis in one of the cases, tell me which case is broken and why, because then I've failed to hold things fixed.

But here's the bit I actually care about: the verdict about how the choice may be resolved has to come apart, and it has to fall out of the cases rather than be stipulated by me or by you. Will-resolution should come out clearly apt in the first, clearly inapt in the second, and apt again in the third. So no making one of the nurses in the second case have the stronger claim, no sneaking in a hidden ranking that the matron is really just discerning, no making the donor's discretion in the third case a roundabout way of tracking merit, and no quietly making the matron's own choice easy in a way that collapses the parity. As a rougher second check I also want a coin flip to look like a legitimate tie-breaker in the second case, like a failure of agency in the first, and like an awkward second-best in the third — and if you think that triple contrast can't be made honestly, say so instead of fudging it. Then tell me which single feature is doing the work, what happens to each case if you take that feature out, and give me the strongest version of my mate's reply, not a strawman, because I'm handing this to a reading group and someone in it will make that reply for him. Also flag anywhere I'd get caught out presenting it, for instance if the real contrast turns out to be about who bears the cost or how high the stakes are rather than about the thing I think it's about. The point of the whole exercise is to test whether his voluntarist machinery transfers off the first-person case at all, so a scenario that just announces "and here resolving by will is fine" counts as cheating. And write the whole thing as straight prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs like an email, even though I've asked you for several different things above — I'll be reading it out loud.
