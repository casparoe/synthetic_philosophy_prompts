I host a philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher—I will refer to her as she—who defends the infinite-utility dominance version of Pascal's wager. On her view, an agent ought to wager on God because, provided the probability of God's existence is nonzero, the expected utility of wagering is positive infinity (infinite reward if God exists, finite cost if not) while the expected utility of not wagering is negative infinity (infinite loss if God exists, finite gain if not), so wagering strictly dominates. She is known for resisting the many-gods objection by arguing that the dominance calculation still gives the agent a decisive reason to adopt some theistic wager rather than none, and for insisting that the argument is decision-theoretic rather than evidential.

I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking her to restate this position. The questions should press where the philosophical literature presses, and each should come with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Please include at least one question on each of the following three pressure points:

1. The many-gods or many-religions objection: if several exclusive deities each promise an infinite reward to their own worshippers, does the dominance argument still single out one practical conclusion, or does it collapse into indifference among theistic options? Anticipate her reply that any wager on a deity with an infinite reward dominates no wager at all, and press her on whether that leaves the agent permissibly worshipping a god the agent takes to be very improbable.

2. The role of infinite utilities in decision theory: the standard worry that infinite payoffs make expected-utility maximization ill-defined or generate paradox. Anticipate her reply that decision theory can be reformed to preserve the dominance result, and press her on what exactly that reform costs the rest of the theory.

3. Doxastic voluntarism and the mechanics of the wager: can an agent simply choose to believe, or is the wager instead about adopting practices and forming beliefs over time? Anticipate her reply that the argument only requires the agent to take the wager seriously and act so as to acquire faith, and press her on whether the dominance argument then applies to acts rather than beliefs in a way that changes the practical conclusion.

In addition, I want one question that introduces a new variant I have been developing, which I call the Embezzlement Variant. In this variant, the agent who performs the wager will not receive the infinite reward herself if God exists; instead, with certainty conditional on God's existence, the infinite reward is transferred to a distant stranger, and the agent receives only a finite side-benefit. The agent is not purely self-interested: she has altruistic aims and counts the stranger's infinite well-being as a reason for action. Ask her whether the infinite-utility dominance argument still gives the agent a decisive reason to wager, given that the first-personal payoff is now finite. Anticipate her reply that Pascalian reasons are essentially self-interested and so the variant undermines the wager, then follow up by asking whether an altruistic agent could instead run a parallel dominance argument from the stranger's expected infinite utility, and whether that shows the original view has been relying on an unstated assumption about whose utility counts.

Please make the sequence of questions escalate in pressure rather than merely repeating the same challenge, and do not include generic questions about her background, her motivations, or what first drew her to philosophy.
