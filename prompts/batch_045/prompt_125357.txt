Write a satirical job posting, as it would appear on a university's internal hiring portal, for a single vacant position: the higher-order mental state whose job is to make other mental states conscious. Roughly 700 words, in deadpan institutional HR prose, with a job title and headline, a position summary, a reporting line, essential duties, minimum and preferred qualifications, compensation, an FAQ, and an equal-opportunity statement.

The target is the family of higher-order theories of consciousness — the view that a mental state's being conscious consists in its being the target of a distinct, higher-order state (a thought about it, or something perception-like directed onto it), rather than in anything intrinsic to the first-order state itself. The posting should read as though the hiring department is firmly committed to one determinate variant of that view: the higher-order state must be an actual occurrent state, concept-involving, and must arise non-inferentially and through the right causal route from its target. The fine print should then quietly concede what that commitment costs. The humor has to come from getting the theory right, so that someone who knows the literature laughs rather than winces, while someone who does not can still follow it.

Work the following into the clauses, each as a piece of bureaucratic policy rather than as exposition:

- The candidate must not itself be conscious, and must not raise the question of its own phenomenality. Handle the obvious regress — if a higher-order state needs a still-higher-order state to be conscious — as a settled HR matter, not as an open one.
- A clause on whether the higher-order state must actually be on the job or whether a standing disposition to take the job suffices, given that the dispositional reading seems to put every employee permanently on the clock and to flatten the difference between the states that are conscious and those that merely could have been.
- A clause on targets: what happens when the higher-order state represents a first-order state the subject is not in, or when there is no such state at all. Say plainly whether consciousness is conferred by the higher-order state alone or only when it matches what it is about, and note whose problem it is if the answer is the first one.
- A qualifications section on whether conceptual content is required, and what that implies about who is eligible: creatures without the relevant concepts, human infants, and non-biological devices such as a thermostat wired to represent its own states should all be addressed by name, with the thermostat's application handled seriously and the reason for rejecting or accepting it given in the idiom of the posting.
- A clause stating that the property conferred is not intrinsic to the first-order state and may be withdrawn without any change in that state itself — the state goes on representing the world exactly as before, only darkly.
- A clause denying, at greater length than is dignified, the charge that the department is merely a monitoring and reporting service: that it confers access, reportability, and self-knowledge, but not phenomenality. The denial should make the charge more vivid than the rebuttal does.
- A compensation section making clear that the consciousness is credited to the target, never to the employee, and a note that first-order states have filed a grievance about the distribution of credit.

No named philosophers, no citations, no footnotes, and no technical vocabulary left unexplained: notions like first-order state, occurrent versus dispositional, conceptual content, and phenomenal versus access consciousness should enter through the bureaucratic idiom. The posting must stay internally consistent and in character throughout — it should never wink, break voice, or resolve a tension by admitting there is one; the absurdities should be the policies.

Begin the response with the job title and headline, written so that they state the department's bottom line outright — that consciousness is a job one state does to another — and then go straight into the copy. No preamble or framing note before the piece, and nothing after the equal-opportunity statement.
