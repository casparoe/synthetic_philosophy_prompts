Construct a Judy Benjamin–style scenario to the following specifications. Judy starts out with a uniform credence over the four quadrants formed by a Red/Blue territorial division and a Second Company/Headquarters unit division, so each of Red & Second, Red & Headquarters, Blue & Second, and Blue & Headquarters has probability 1/4. She then receives evidence that leaves her with exactly 3/4 credence that she is in Red and exactly 3/4 credence that she is in Second Company. The scenario must be realistic—an ordinary military training exercise, no perfect predictors, no demons, no exotic physics—and the story itself must fit in one paragraph. Crucially, the scenario must generate those numbers rather than stipulate them: Judy should receive two independent, equally weighted, imperfect signals, one bearing on the Red/Blue question and one on the Second Company/Headquarters question, each of which on its own would move the relevant probability from 1/2 to 3/4. A usable way to do this is to let each signal have a three-to-one likelihood ratio, for example sensitivity 3/4 and false-alarm rate 1/4, so that observing the signal raises the posterior from 1/2 to 3/4. Judy's receiver combines the signals but tells her only that both were received; assume the two signals are conditionally independent given her true quadrant. The resulting story should make it clear why she ends up 3/4 on Red and 3/4 on Second Company without her knowing anything more about how the two divisions overlap.

After the scenario, do the check. First, show that ordinary Bayesian conditioning on the total evidence—the conjunction of the two conditionally independent signals with their likelihoods—yields the joint distribution Red & Second = 9/16, Red & Headquarters = 3/16, Blue & Second = 3/16, Blue & Headquarters = 1/16, and that this yields the required 3/4 marginals for Red and for Second Company. Second, show what Jeffrey conditioning gives if one models Judy's total learning experience as a single shift on the four-quadrant partition constrained only by the new marginals: the probability of Red & Second is underdetermined, ranging from 1/2 to 3/4, because the constraints fix only the marginals and leave one degree of freedom. Third, show what the minimum-relative-information or maximum-entropy rule says when it is given only those two marginal constraints: it picks Red & Second = 9/16, matching the two-signal Bayesian update, because among all distributions with those marginals it is the one closest to Judy's old uniform distribution in the Kullback-Leibler sense and preserves the old odds ratio. Fourth, show what a Euclidean-distance-minimizing rule says when it is given only those two marginal constraints: it picks Red & Second = 1/2, with Red & Headquarters = 1/4, Blue & Second = 1/4, and Blue & Headquarters = 0, because that distribution minimizes the sum of squared differences from Judy's old uniform 1/4, 1/4, 1/4, 1/4 distribution; this disagrees with the two-signal Bayesian update.

Then say which structural feature of the case is doing the work. The feature is that Judy's evidence decomposes into two independent, conditionally-independent signals rather than arriving as a single opaque message that merely fixes her new marginals. Because the evidence factorizes, the total update is uniquely determined by the likelihoods, and the minimum-relative-information answer is the uniquely correct one rather than one option among many. Say what happens if that feature is removed: if the two signals are replaced by a single garbled bulletin that only tells Judy her new marginals and says nothing about their interaction, the case collapses back into the classic Judy Benjamin underdetermination, the Jeffrey interval from 1/2 to 3/4 reopens, and the minimum-relative-information and Euclidean rules come apart. Finish with an honest note on how a defender of the Euclidean-distance rule would attack the case: for example, by arguing that Judy's total evidence does not really factorize into two independent signals, that the Euclidean rule was never meant to compete with full Bayesian updating when the likelihoods are known, or that preserving odds ratios is not the right explication of minimal change and the Euclidean rule captures a different, legitimate notion of conservatism.

Finally, add a short closing self-critical note of no more than five sentences. In it, state how confident you are that the case isolates the intended feature: say which claim you are most confident about, which assumption you are least confident about, and what would change your mind about whether the case succeeds in forcing the minimum-relative-information/Bayesian verdict and in making the Euclidean-distance rule give a different answer. In particular, identify the argument or consideration—such as a failure of conditional independence between the two signals, or a reexamination of what minimal change or closest distribution should mean—that would lead you to a different verdict, and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
