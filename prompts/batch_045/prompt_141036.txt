ok so I've been reading about Hofstadter's Copycat — the old analogy program that works on letter-string puzzles like abc → abd, so ijk → ?, in a deliberately tiny made-up domain — and I keep coming back to the part where the right answer isn't determined by the strings at all, because which description of abc is live for you decides what you even see there, and concepts stretch and slip from one level to another, and that slippage is the mechanism rather than a bug. I want a short story that makes a reader feel that, instead of a character explaining it. Here's a domain and a situation I've been turning over. Keep the domain exactly as specced; swap the situation for something better if you've got one.

The domain. Beads on a cord, six colours: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, arranged around a wheel. Two things about it that matter:

1. A cord has no clasp and no marked end. Pick it up by the other end and the same cord reads backwards. So first bead, last bead, leftmost run, the bead at the end aren't properties of the cord — they're properties of how somebody happens to be holding it. Any rule you state using those words comes with a mirror-image twin that fits the identical data.
2. The wheel has no arrow on it. One step around can go either way; next and previous are one relation, not two.

The corpus: every cord anyone has ever seen her make, all three of them. G → G G. R R → R R R. B B B → B B B B. Notice that the run gets one longer, duplicate the last bead, duplicate the first bead, every run gets one longer, the cord gets one bead heavier are all the same thing here, because there is only ever one run, one colour, and no direction to pick. The data is totally unambiguous and tells you almost nothing.

The new cord: Y G G. One yellow at one end, two greens. Everyone agrees that some run grows by one. But Y Y G G and Y G G G are the same rule read from the two ends of the same cord; Y Y G G G is the only reading that needs no direction at all; and the wheel reading, last bead steps one colour around, gives Y G B or Y G Y depending which way you go, with nothing on the wheel to choose between them.

The situation: the cords are made by a woman who had a stroke a couple of years ago and can't reliably speak or write anymore. She was a weaver; the colour wheel is the one off her loom. She strings beads constantly and her granddaughter has kept all of them. The new cord turns up in the week a decision has to be made about something — you pick what, but it should be the sort of thing where guessing wrong is cheap in itself and expensive in what it says. The granddaughter's brother wants to write the rule down and is not stupid about it: he's right that the rule is perfectly clear on the corpus, and he wants to know why she should get to pick if the rule runs out exactly when it starts to matter, and whether the two years of cords were ever a language at all. Someone who actually knew the loom offers the wheel reading. The granddaughter has to choose one and act on it before the story ends.

What I want: 900–1300 words, fiction, in scenes. Nobody gives a speech about any of this. No narrator's summing-up paragraph, no final line about how the meaning was never in the cord, no afterword. Each reading should land as genuinely right in the moment it's proposed — I want the reader to definately switch sides at least once — but don't make them equally arbitrary either: something should make one of them grip, and it has to be a fact about the people and not the cord, a habit of her hands, what was on the loom that week, which end she picks a cord up by. The physics of the domain should be load-bearing in the scene, not decoration: the fact that the cord has no ends and the wheel has no arrow is the whole argument. No mention of Hofstadter, programs, analogy, slippage, or anything theory-ish; the story shouldn't announce a thesis. And don't resolve it by declaring one answer correct, and don't resolve it into relativism — the readings are unequally good, and I want to feel why.
