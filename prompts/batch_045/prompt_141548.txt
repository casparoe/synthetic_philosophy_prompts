I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and we're assembling a piece on what, if anything, the word "intelligence" now means when it's applied to machines. I'd like the conceptual scaffolding, not the standard talking points, and I'd rather you not settle things by saying that intelligence has many definitions.

Here's the asymmetry I want you to work with. Human intelligence testing is population-relative by construction: a battery is normed on a contemporary reference sample so that the mean is 100 and the standard deviation 15, items are selected partly because they intercorrelate, and the norms are periodically re-set because raw performance drifts upward across generations. A person's score is therefore strictly a claim about where they sit among their contemporaries rather than about what they can do — yet that same number is then used to make categorical, apparently absolute judgments about individuals: diagnoses, placements, legal exemptions. Machine intelligence testing runs the other way. A battery of problems is fixed, absolute accuracy on it is reported, a system is called superhuman once it clears a threshold or beats the human experts who sat the test, and the battery is quietly retired once performance approaches its ceiling. The number is also attached to a moving target: it scores a system plus a budget of time and compute the evaluator chose, and a different budget yields a different score.

What I want from you is the implicit theory on each side. Reconstruct what has to be true of intelligence for each practice to make sense; distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, so that several rival theories might license the same habits; and say whether the two halves are genuinely inconsistent — whether a field that measures humans comparatively and machines absolutely can coherently use one word for both. Finally, tell me what we would have to think we were measuring if machine scores were instead normed against the human distribution and re-normed as systems improved, so that the median deployed model always sat at 100.
