I've been reading the rationalist-adjacent blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and one of the obsessions I picked up there is coordination: I've followed, for most of that time, a blogger who posts under a handle and works in something like mechanism design, and who runs an intermittent series in which she rules, case by case, on whether a given behavioral regularity is a convention. Her touchstone is Lewis's clause-by-clause definition, which she insists on treating as a definition rather than a slogan: nearly everyone conforms; nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform; nearly everyone prefers to conform conditional on the others conforming; the regularity is arbitrary in the sense that some alternative regularity would have served just as well had everyone expected that one instead; and all of it is common knowledge. She is on record saying the epistemic clauses are load-bearing rather than decoration, and as far as I can tell she has never ruled on a case involving agents without beliefs.

The verdicts I can recover from her archive: motorists in the US keeping to the right — yes, she calls it the paradigm and says the arbitrariness clause is what makes it a convention rather than a habit or a law of nature. The sun rising, which everyone expects — no, on the double ground that nothing in it is arbitrary and that nobody's behavior raises anyone else's. Replying to a message with a thumbs-up to mean "seen, no reply needed" — yes. Everyone in an office taking lunch at 12:30 because that is when the café restocks — no: a common cause, not mutual responsiveness. Everyone braking at a red light at four in the morning on an empty road, purely because a camera issues fines — no, and her stated tell is that the regularity would look exactly the same if every driver's expectations about every other driver quietly vanished. A village where everyone greets with a handshake — yes, even though she stipulates that a majority would privately prefer bowing if they could all switch together, which she says is irrelevant.

Three rules fit that track record equally well, and I can't get them to come apart from the archive alone: (i) Lewis's package straight, iterated common knowledge and all; (ii) a looser variant on which precedent or salience is enough to generate the expectations and full common knowledge is not required — it agrees with (i) on all six cases because each of them has an obvious focal point; (iii) an evolutionary variant on which a convention is just a stable, arbitrary regularity in a recurrent situation where each agent's best move depends on what the others do, with no requirement that anybody believe, expect, or reason about anything at all. The two cases I want predictions on are these. First: two deployments of the same open-weights language model, fine-tuned separately on different data, never communicating and never observing one another, the only thing linking them being that each saw a small amount of text generated by an earlier version of the other; asked to propose a division of a hundred-dollar surplus with an unnamed counterparty, both say fifty-fifty essentially always, either would do strictly worse by unilaterally proposing something else, and any of a dozen other divisions would have been just as good had both expected it. Second: a large decentralized chat network of thousands of private channels in which, over a few weeks, with no announcement, no moderators and no sanctions, everyone stops using a certain word; everyone conforms and everyone expects everyone else to conform, but nobody can see the network as a whole and there was no moment at which that expectation became shared in the way a public post would make it.

I'd like her verdict on each, your best guess at which of the three rules she is actually running (I lean toward (i) myself, but that may just be because she talks about the epistemic clauses so much), and — since I think the archive genuinely underdetermines it — the single further case that would best separate whichever rules survive. I keep reaching for ants, or for a simulation of simple reinforcement learners with no model of other minds, but I'm not confident either of those isolates the distinction I care about rather than just restaging the disagreement. There is one wrinkle I also want your view on: if the two deployments are runs of the same weights, isn't there a sense in which each one's knowledge of itself does the work that common knowledge does for the rest of us, and if so does that rescue (i) for the first case, or is it a different argument wearing (i)'s clothes?
