I am writing a novel, and I would like help with one scene in it — not with the prose, but with the reasoning that has to happen on the page.

The scene: my narrator, a retired judge acting as an informal arbitrator in a dispute over a dead man's memoir, is confronted with a sentence in the manuscript saying, of the same man at the same hour, that he was standing in the orangery at midnight and that he was not. I want her to take the sentence seriously rather than to explain it away — to accept it as both true and false as it stands, to refuse to let that collapse everything else she knows, and then to go on arguing much as she did before. In the draft she announces that she is proceeding paraconsistently, and I have given her this understanding of the word: paraconsistent logic is classical deductive logic with the principle of explosion revoked. You keep the rest — conjunction elimination, disjunction introduction, disjunctive syllogism, modus ponens, conditional proof — and you simply delete the licence to infer an arbitrary conclusion from A together with not-A. I have also been assuming that paraconsistent and dialetheist are two words for the same position, though I am no longer sure they are used interchangeably. A reader of the draft tells me I will have to surrender more than I think, possibly modus ponens itself, which would ruin the scene, since the whole movement of her argument is conditional: she takes a claim on board, draws something from it, and then asserts the conditional. I would like to know exactly what I would have to give up, and what each surrender would cost the passage.

I also want the larger claim of the scene to be sound. My narrator says, in effect, that the sentence in the memoir is a true contradiction, and that if one contradiction is true anywhere, then the law of non-contradiction is false somewhere and dialetheism is vindicated — a fiction being as good a place as any for a contradiction to be true. Another reader objected that the sentence is true only in the memoir, and that being true in a book is not being true, so that nothing follows. That struck me as an evasion, and as a novelist I would like to resist it: the sentence is true of the world the book describes, and that seems to me to be enough. Is it?

Two smaller things. I want the contradiction to be a real one, and not a case of the same words carrying two senses; but a logician friend says those are my only options unless the negation in the sentence is itself doing something out of the ordinary, and that either way the paradox is an illusion. And she plainly cannot accept every contradiction she meets, or the enquiry ends at once: what rule, in language I could put in her mouth, should she be using for when an apparent contradiction is to be taken at face value and when it is something else — an under-described scene, a boundary the manuscript has not settled, a plain error?
