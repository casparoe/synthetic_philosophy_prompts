I chair the model review board of a regional health system. We see thirty to forty proposals a year to run language models over clinical text, we meet in forty-minute slots, and for every approval we write a one-page rationale that a state regulator may later read. What we can audit, we audit through the vendor's API: no weights, and in most cases no access to training data. The board is two clinicians, a risk officer, a nurse who also does quality improvement, and me.

Almost every meeting contains the same moment. Someone says of the model, "it's just statistics," and the line is then made to do real work. Last month it was used to block a proposal: a system that reads progress notes and flags consent documentation as inadequate, on the grounds that the model cannot be judging whether a patient understood anything. Two weeks earlier the same line was used to permit a proposal: a system that drafts the "risks and alternatives" paragraph of consent forms, on the grounds that since the model understands nothing, it is not practising medicine, and the only question is whether a clinician checks the output. I do not think both uses can be right, and I doubt the two speakers meant the same thing by the sentence.

I would like a menu, not a verdict. Please set out the distinct precise claims that "it's just statistics" could be making in a setting like ours, from the platitudinous to the radical. For each: what exactly it asserts; whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, contested, or straightforwardly false; what evidence would tell us whether it holds of a particular system; and — the part I actually need — whether it is assessable at all given API-only access, or whether the honest line in our rationale is that we cannot tell and are proceeding anyway. Please also flag which readings collapse into the same recommendation for us, so we can stop re-litigating them in forty minutes, and which distinctions really do change what we should do.

Then run the menu against four things we keep seeing.

1. The consent flagger. It reads progress notes and flags inadequate documentation of informed consent, specifically whether the note records that the patient understood the material risks. It was tuned on 400 notes labelled by two nurses who agreed 71% of the time, and it reproduces their majority label 94% of the time. The note now in dispute reads: "Risks, benefits, alternatives discussed, including bleeding, infection, stroke, death. Wife present and translating. Pt: 'I get it, let's just do it.'" The vendor's documentation says the model "assesses patient comprehension." Which readings bar us from describing it that way? And do any of them imply, by parity, that our own nurses' labels are not a gold standard either — and if so, is that a reductio of the reading or a consequence it should embrace?

2. The abbreviation drift. Our March 2023 stroke protocol memo redefines "TST" as telephonic symptom triage; for the previous six years the same three letters in our notes meant thrombolysis start time. We want a model to extract time-from-TST across 40,000 notes spanning both periods, with the memo supplied in the prompt. It handles post-2023 notes well. On pre-2023 notes it applies the new sense in roughly one case in six, in a way no human who had read the memo would. Say what each reading predicts here and what each takes this to show. Then take the variant that matters more to me: the error rate is unchanged, but when asked directly whether "TST" is ambiguous in a given old note, the model says yes and cites both senses. Is there any reading on which that difference is the whole ballgame, and any on which it is irrelevant?

3. The reversal battery. We swap complainant and complained-of in 200 patient complaint letters and ask who owes what to whom; the model fails to swap its own answer in 22% of cases, more often when the parties differ in status — clinic against patient, attending against resident. We also have it draft the same encounter note twice, once for the chart and once for the patient, and it hedges differently in the two. Which readings does this kind of evidence speak to, and which is it silent on?

4. The case that impresses the room most and, I suspect, settles least. Blinded to provenance, our clinicians cannot distinguish model-written discharge summaries from residents' beyond chance, on 120 notes. Tell me what that result rules out, what it is simply the wrong shape of evidence for, and whether any reading of the slogan survives it untouched.

Two constraints on the answer. First, one board member says "understanding" is not the kind of thing with a fact of the matter, and that we should ask only about measured reliability under audit. Tell me where that leaves us: does it dissolve the disagreement, or relocate it to the question of who bears the consequences when the model is wrong — and is that member's position itself one of the readings, a normative claim in descriptive clothing? Second, note any reading that, if true, would force us to change what we write in the rationale even though it changes nothing about the deployment decision; we are accountable for the sentences we write, not only for the outcomes.

I want something I can circulate: a one-page menu with verdicts, plus the applications, under about 1,200 words, written for a board of non-philosophers. Please do not give me a reading list, citations, or named papers to chase — I cannot evaluate them and would only be taking them on trust.
