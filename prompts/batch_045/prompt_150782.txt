Hey — I run a tiny philosophy reading group (four of us, way too much cheap coffee) and we've blown about a semester on vagueness without converging. It's basically split into two camps: me, defending epistemicism (every vague predicate has a sharp cutoff somewhere; vagueness is our ignorance of where it falls, and the ignorance is principled rather than accidental, roughly because knowledge requires a margin for error), and my friend, defending supervaluationism (vague terms admit many admissible precisifications; a statement is true iff true on all of them, false iff false on all of them, so borderline claims are neither; and validity gets defined as preservation of truth across all precisifications rather than as preservation of truth simpliciter). We've finally agreed to stop re-running the sorites at each other and instead produce the adversarial-collaboration document we should have written in week two. Can you draft it?

Here's what I'd like in it. First, a numbered section of claims we can both sign — the genuine common ground, including the bits that separate us jointly from degree theorists and from people who think the way out is just to stipulate sharper terms. Some candidates I'd guess belong there, but cut or reword any that are vacuous: neither of us wants degrees of truth or a third truth value; both of us hold that borderline-ness is itself borderline, so higher-order vagueness is real and has to be handled rather than explained away; both of us accept that the sorites has premises we're strongly inclined to assert and a conclusion we reject, so something has to give somewhere; and both of us think the "definitely" operator iterates and that any decent semantics for vague language needs it made explicit. Then a disagreements table: one row per real crux, the crux stated in neutral terms, each of our two positions given in wording the other would accept as a fair statement of it, and — the part I care most about — a case, argument, or piece of evidence we'd both agree bears on that crux, with each of our predictions written down in advance.

Two contrived cases we came up with in session, which you should use if they earn their place and replace with better ones if they don't. First, the envelope predicate: a predicate introduced into a language whose extension is stipulated to be fixed by whether a coin flipped in a sealed room and written inside an unopened envelope landed heads — so it has a sharp, fully determinate, but genuinely unknowable boundary. Everyone in the group agrees that predicate is sharp-but-unknown; the fight is whether the epistemicist is committed to treating "tall" or "bald" as relevantly like it, differing only in how the cutoff got fixed, and whether the supervaluationist has any non-question-begging way of marking the difference. Second, the split-usage community: half the speakers' practice fixes "tall" at 180cm and the other half's at 185cm, everyone knows this about everyone, nobody is deferring to anyone. My friend reads that as a concrete picture of a multiplicity of admissible precisifications; I read it as just an unusual way for one cutoff to get fixed, or maybe as a case where the term quietly splits in two. What does each view have to say about it, and would the verdict count as evidence for either?
