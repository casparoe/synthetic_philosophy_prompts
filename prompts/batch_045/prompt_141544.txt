I am preparing a short presentation for a graduate seminar on the many-worlds interpretation, and I would like to put what I take to be a solution to the Born rule problem on the table. Before I do that in front of people who know the literature, I would like to know whether it actually works. I am not asking for references or further reading, just for the structure of the objection.

As I understand it, the problem has two parts. First, the conceptual part: unitary evolution is deterministic and every outcome is realized somewhere in the branching structure, so it is unclear what a probability assignment could be tracking, or what it would mean to be uncertain about an outcome that you know for certain will be realized by some successor of you. Second, the numerical part: even granting that probability is self-locating uncertainty about which successor you will turn out to be, counting successors gives the wrong numbers. In a measurement whose two outcomes have amplitudes of root one-third and root two-thirds, there is exactly one successor who sees the first outcome and exactly one who sees the second, so indifference over successors yields one-half rather than one-third.

A lot of the discussion treats the conceptual part as the deep one. I think that is backwards, and that the numerical part is the one that needs new machinery. My proposal is that the successors were never equinumerous in the first place. Call it amplitude-proportional duplication: when an outcome with amplitude alpha is realized, it is realized by a multiplicity of exactly similar successor systems proportional to the squared modulus of alpha, so that in the case above there is one successor seeing the first outcome for every two seeing the second. Copy count and Born measure then coincide by construction, indifference over copies rather than over branches yields the right credences, and because the multiplicity is exact, the ordinary combinatorial argument then shows that across a long run of trials the overwhelming majority of successors observe statistics close to the Born statistics. So the numbers and the observational typicality come together.

Three objections I expect, and my replies. That it is ad hoc: every interpretation adds structure to the bare formalism, and this addition is empirically inert by construction, so it cannot be refuted by experiment. That irrational and continuously varying amplitude ratios cannot be realized by integer multiplicities: fix a large N, let each multiplicity be N times the squared modulus rounded to the nearest integer, and the induced measure differs from the Born measure by at most one part in N, which can be pushed below any experimental resolution. That the conceptual problem survives: this is just the ordinary self-locating uncertainty move that everybody already grants in fission cases, since before the experiment I know I will have successors and do not know which of them I will be.

Where is the argument actually broken? In particular: does supplying a multiplicity of copies that matches the squared amplitudes solve the problem, restate it, or change the subject to a different theory? Is the rounding reply stable once you ask the multiplicities to be consistent across arbitrarily long runs, across arbitrary observables, and across the tensor product structure, or does it collapse back into needing a measure over infinitely many copies? Is the self-locating step doing more work than I am crediting it with, and would it still be available to me if the multiplicity posit were withdrawn? And is there anything wrong earlier in my setup, in the assumption that a branching event leaves a well-defined finite number of successors to be counted at all?
