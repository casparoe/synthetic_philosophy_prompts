So I've been going around in circles about something and I want your actual take on it, not a survey of positions.

Here's the practice. Take a more or less ordinary country — a European one or the US:

- People who have lived, worked and paid taxes somewhere for fifteen years but never took citizenship can vote in the municipal elections where they live (and, in the EU case, in the European Parliament election) but are never allowed to vote in the national one.
- Meanwhile someone who was born there, left at twenty-two, has lived abroad for forty years, pays nothing there and will almost certainly never move back keeps the vote for life and can mail in a ballot.
- Sixteen-year-olds can't vote, even though plenty of what gets decided — climate commitments, borrowing — will bind them much longer than it binds the eighty-year-olds who can.
- The population count used to apportion seats between regions counts everybody resident, voters or not, non-citizens included. So non-citizen residents get counted for the purpose of allocating representatives they aren't allowed to choose.
- And the bit that actually gets me: when the boundary itself gets moved — voting age lowered, expat voting restricted, a new path to citizenship opened — it's moved by the people already inside it, usually by majority vote among them, and essentially nobody treats that as hopelessly circular. Everyone just gets on with it.

What view of who belongs to the demos does that whole package presuppose? On the face of it, it rules out the all-affected principle (long-term resident non-citizens are plainly affected by national law and get no say) and it also rules out any story on which the people is some prepolitical community with a shared identity (forty-year expats are in, fifteen-year residents are out). So what's left? Something about who can be coerced by the decision, or something about consent, or is it mostly historical accident plus administrative convenience with a bit of symbolism on top? And can the parts be made to hang together, or is the practice quietly running three incompatible theories at once?

One more thing, because I'd rather you engage with it than summarize around it. A friend and I cooked up what we're calling the fixed-point test. The idea: the right demos for a decision is the one where the boundary isn't doing the work. You take the set D of people who get to vote, you ask whether any plausible expansion or contraction of D — non-citizen residents in, expats out, sixteen-year-olds in — would flip the outcome of the vote on the issue in question, and if it would, then the vote is just laundering a gerrymander of the demos. Taken literally that seems to make alot of actual elections illegitimate, which makes me suspicious of the test rather than of the elections. Is it even coherent? Does it collapse into "every boundary is contestable, so no demos ever passes"? And if you did take it seriously, what would actually have to change in how the franchise is set?

Give me your reconstruction, and tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined by theory. 500 words max, prose, no bullet points.
