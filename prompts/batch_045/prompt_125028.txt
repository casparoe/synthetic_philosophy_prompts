I'm writing a term paper on the procreation asymmetry and I keep finding that every paper I read presupposes a different background theory, so before I read any further I want the specification: what would a view have to deliver in order to count as an adequate account of the asymmetry? The claim I want vindicated, or at least accounted for, is the deontic one — that there is a substantial reason not to bring into existence a child whose life would predictably be miserable, yet no symmetrical reason to bring into existence a child whose life would predictably go well — together with the stronger axiological reading on which the absence of the suffering of a person who never exists is good although there is no one whom it is good for, while the absence of the happiness of a never-existing person is not bad, there being no one who is thereby deprived. Please fix the purpose as practical: I want a theory good enough to reason with, one that could tell a prospective parent, or an institution deciding about fertility incentives, embryo selection, or policy toward distant future generations, what they have reason to do under uncertainty — including, if it comes to that, that it has nothing to say.

The cases I want each requirement anchored to, and to which I would like you to add: a straight choice between creating one child who would be well off and creating no one, with no effects on anyone else; a case in which exactly one child will be created whatever I do and I can only choose which of two possible children, whose lives would differ markedly in quality; a case in which I can create a decently happy child now or a considerably happier child five years from now, the two being different people; a case in which the child I could create has a high expected quality of life but a real chance of a life well below the neutral point; and the familiar aggregative pressure case comparing a vast number of lives each barely worth living with a small number of excellent ones. For each requirement I want the case behind it and a marking of it as non-negotiable or as the preference of one camp. I am especially unsure about two candidate requirements: that the theory keep the deontic claim and the axiological claim independent, so that it can say there was no reason to create someone while still allowing that, once they exist, their creation benefited them; and that the theory not make the strength of the relevant reason jump discontinuously at the point where a life becomes worth living. A friend of mine also argues that the asymmetry is not a datum to be accommodated at all: since we can never be confident that a created life will go well and the downside is severe, the balance of reasons favours abstention, and what looks like a deep asymmetry about possible people is only the ordinary asymmetry of risk. Tell me whether, if that derivation works, accommodate the asymmetry is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of the person-affecting camp, and whether the derivation quietly relies on the asymmetry it claims to replace.

The part I most want is this: which pairs or triples of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — whether, for example, one can have the asymmetry in full strength, an acyclic and transitive ordering of populations by betterness, and immunity to the repugnant-style aggregative result all at once — since if the list is unsatisfiable, that is itself the result I should be writing up. Close by naming the single requirement that excludes the most candidate views, and by giving a very brief pass, a line or two each, over the main families — impersonal total and average views, critical-level and neutral-range views, person-affecting views that restrict reasons to those who do or will exist, and the anti-natalist reading — against the list. I will do the fuller scorecard myself, and please skip citations and reading suggestions; I already have those.
