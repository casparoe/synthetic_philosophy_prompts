I am writing the opening section of a paper on the problem of logical omniscience — the section that says what any solution would have to deliver before we start scoring candidate theories — and I want that specification written properly rather than assembled from the standard list of complaints. Please write it for me.

The angle I want taken is this. I do not think the problem is best framed as a single defect of a single theory. I think the accounts in this area are being asked to do two jobs at once, and that the jobs pull in different directions:

1. to serve as the fine-grained input to a compositional semantics of belief reports and to the explanation and prediction of action, which pushes toward contents that are sensitive to how the agent represents things and that do not collapse together everything with the same truth conditions;
2. to serve as the object of normative appraisal in epistemology — the thing you are criticised for not believing, the thing you ought to revise — which pushes toward contents coarse and stable enough for logic to retain authority over them.

My working hypothesis, which the document should test rather than assume, is that some of the demands generated by the first job cannot be met jointly with some generated by the second, and that a good deal of the apparent disagreement in the area is really disagreement about which job matters. If the desiderata list turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, that is a result I want stated as such.

Please write the desiderata. With each, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable, as negotiable-but-costly, or as a preference of one camp rather than a genuine requirement. Some I want considered and either sharpened or rejected:

- the prohibition on cheap solutions: denying closure is not by itself a theory, and a proposal has to explain why closure looked compelling in the first place and how logical failure differs from forgetting, from distraction, and from ordinary factual error;
- the shape of the failure: agents reliably see one-step entailments in a single vocabulary and degrade as inference depth, negation, and vocabulary shift increase, so a theory that predicts uniform or unstructured failure is getting the phenomenon wrong;
- revision without new information: on realising that what one believes entails something one does not believe, one retracts or adds, and this has a different phenomenology and a different normative profile from revising on new evidence; the theory needs room for that difference and needs to say what the agent is criticisable for;
- first-personal corrigibility: logical failure is discoverable by the agent from reflection alone, unlike perceptual illusion, so there must be some standpoint from which the relevant facts are available without that availability reinstating omniscience;
- instruction: an agent can be explicitly and credibly told that her beliefs entail some proposition, still fail to believe it, and still be open to criticism; a theory should predict both that this occasionally happens and that instruction usually works;
- the authority of logic: it must remain available to say that one ought to believe the consequences of what one believes and that failing to is a species of irrationality, and a solution that removes the problem by making logic optional for belief should be flagged as paying a large price;
- the idealisation: first-order logical consequence is not decidable by any algorithm, so closure under consequence cannot be implemented by any mechanical procedure even for an idealised agent, and any theory that appeals to an ideal limit must say what the idealisation is doing and whether it is coherent;
- scope: whether one apparatus covers logical, mathematical, conceptual, and probabilistic consequence, or whether the case in which someone believes the axioms and not the theorem needs separate resources;
- boundaries: the desiderata should be stated so as not to collapse into neighbouring problems — the problem of how to search for a proof, the problem of what to retrieve from memory, and the general problem of how fine-grained propositions are;
- compositionality and action: whatever fine-grained items the theory posits must still feed a compositional semantics for ascriptions and must still underwrite planning and action explanation, including across contexts in which substitution intuitively does go through;
- level of fix: for each desideratum, say whether it constrains the metaphysics of belief states, the semantics of belief ascriptions, or the norms governing belief, since a proposal that solves the problem at one level may leave it untouched at another.

Do not give me a literature review or a bibliography; name positions by family and argument, not by author, and I will supply references myself. At the end, and briefly — no more than a few sentences each, not a survey — score the main families against the list: hyperintensional or fine-grained contents such as structured propositions and impossible worlds; sentential, syntactic, or language-of-thought accounts; awareness, attention, and resource-bounded approaches; fragmentation or compartmentalisation views; and the strategy of denying that logic constrains belief in the first place. Then tell me which single desideratum excludes the most candidates and which pair of desiderata you think cannot both be met.

Aim for roughly 1200 to 1500 words. A table is acceptable for the list if you keep the reasoning about conflicts and about which demands are mere camp prejudice in prose.
