My supervisor has this party trick: whatever case about minds you put to her, she answers by saying how many conscious subjects are in the room. Ive been keeping a list of her verdicts and I want to work out what rule she's actually running, because next supervision I want to ambush her with a case where the plausible rules come apart.

Her verdicts so far (all of them about how many subjects, not about persons, selves or moral responsibility — she's stiff about that distinction):

- ordinary adult human: one, obviously.
- standard adult commissurotomy patient, the full callosal section with all the textbook effects (left hemisphere talks, right hemisphere answers by pointing with the left hand, nothing transfers): two. When I pushed her on whether it's really two, she said the "-ish" I wanted to add is about language, not about the count.
- adult hemispherectomy patient, one cortex gone, apparently normal-ish cognition: one. She gets irritable at the suggestion that this is half a subject.
- the dicephalic conjoined twins — two heads, one body, each brain running its own side, coordinating well enough to drive a car: two. "Two, and the coordination is not the interesting fact."
- someone mid-Wada test, one hemisphere pharmacologically knocked out for a few minutes: one. "That's just a lesion. You've got one temporarily damaged subject."
- someone born without a corpus callosum who tests and reports completely normally: one — and she says this is exactly why the callosum itself can't be where unity lives.
- a dolphin in unihemispheric slow-wave sleep: one, including while it's happening.
- a brain-emulation upload that gets copied, original and copy both running: two, and they aren't the same one.
- the one I find most diagnostic: a long-term commissurotomy patient who, after years of cross-cueing, says he feels entirely ordinary and unified and notices nothing missing: still two. She says the report is one hemisphere confabulating, and the confabulation is itself evidence for two.

I've already checked that she isn't running "one organism, one subject" or "one brain, one subject," so don't give me those.

The case I want to spring on her. Patient M had a complete callosotomy at 20. At 40 he gets an experimental "callosal prosthesis": a device with about 64 channels, against the couple of hundred million fibres he's lost. It does two things. It sends a slow, coarse signal each way — urgency or valence, roughly one second of lag, nothing like the content of an experience, more like a dim alarm or a nudge of mood. And it enforces an arbitration rule on action: either hemisphere can initiate a motor plan, the other gets roughly 300 milliseconds to veto it, and if it doesn't the action goes ahead. Standard transfer tests still fail — no transfer of object identity, the left hand still can't say what it's holding. But the confabulation stops, he no longer produces the classic contradictory double-responses, and when the hemispheres are fed conflicting information he now stalls and then does a single thing. He also starts saying things like "I sometimes know I'm going to be irritated before I know why."

What I want: don't just tell me "it depends what you mean by subject" — I can produce that answer myself and it won't survive contact with her. Give me two or three rules that each generate her whole verdict list above, rules that fit all of it, and say what each one predicts about M. Then give me your best guess at what she'll actually say, with a reason for preferring that rule over the others. Then tell me what further case I should put to her afterwards, one that would genuinely seperate the rules that survive — it's possible M won't. Finally, flag which of the surviving rules is doing real work and which is only relabelling the question. Under 800 words if you can.
