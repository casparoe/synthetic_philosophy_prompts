Hey — I'm running a philosophy-and-film night at a pub near campus and we're doing Groundhog Day next month. I want to run it through an idea I've been chewing on instead of the usual "is he free or isn't he" hand-waving, and I'd love a reading that takes both the film and the idea seriously.

The idea: there's an old anectode about a digger wasp. She drags her paralysed prey to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out and hauls the prey in. If you nudge the prey a few inches while she's inside, she comes back out, drags it back to the threshold, and goes in to inspect again — and people say you can keep that going for as long as you like. The jargon word is sphexishness, and the usual moral is that the behaviour looks purposeful but is really ballistic: a subroutine fired by a fixed releaser, with no room to notice that the world has changed.

What I want out of the film is finer-grained than "rigid = unfree, flexible = free". Because Phil gets flexible pretty early. By the time he's using what he's learned to chase Rita, or lifting the money from the armoured car, he's enormously sensitive to fine features of the situation and wildly inventive — and he's still stuck. That looks like flexibility downstream of a rigidity: the goal fixed, the policy fixed, only the search improved. So I'd like to pry apart (i) responding to more of the environment, (ii) being able to revise what you respond to — new releasers, new ends — and (iii) responsiveness to reasons where the "why" isn't itself another fixed trigger, and ask which of the three the film thinks freedom needs, which one Phil actually acquires, and when.

Two things I really want looked at. First, Ned Ryerson: Phil's reactions to Ned are the most mechanically stereotyped thing in the film and they stay stereotyped the longest, even as everything else about him changes. Second, the ending: he gets out on the day he stops treating the day as a problem to solve. Is the film smuggling in something like (iii), or is the release condition just one more releaser — which would make the happy ending quietly undercut the point the rest of the film is making? (Bonus question: Punxsutawney Phil is an animal whose entire cultural job is a rigid stimulus-response routine. Is the film winking at itself there, or am I reading too much into a mascot?)

Also tell me where the reading fails. I can see it flattening the scene where he starts learning the piano and ice sculpting — that looks like genuine growth inside the loop, and if he can genuinely learn and change while the world resets, then maybe the loop was never about determinism at all. I'd rather have that than a tidy reading, and I'd like to know which two scenes to show in the pub if I've only got twenty minutes of discussion afterwards.
