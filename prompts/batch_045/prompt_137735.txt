Hi — I'm TAing for an undergrad political philosophy course and I have a big pile of answers to get through for the distributive justice section. I wrote the question myself; the rubric the lecturer handed me is pretty thin (basically "do they use the brute/option luck distinction correctly"), and a good chunk of the class wrote something close to the answer below, so I want to get one case right and then be consistent. Assume the students had one lecture on luck egalitarianism and one on its critics.

The question (20 marks):

Two PhD students on identical stipends, Iris and Mo, are each offered a choice by their department for the coming year: (i) a teaching job paying £12,000 for sure, or (ii) a research year with a shot at a £30,000 prize. The departmental handout states the prize probability as 0.6; unknown to the students, the committee has revised it to 0.45 and never updated the handout. Iris teaches. Mo does the research year and wins nothing. Assume both are risk-neutral, and assume Mo says afterwards that he would still have taken the research year at the true probability. The department then debates paying Mo a £12,000 hardship payment, financed by an equal-percentage levy on all staff pay.

(a) How would a luck egalitarian decide whether the resulting inequality — Iris on £12,000, Mo on £0 — is unjust? Answer using the brute luck / option luck distinction. (8 marks)
(b) Does the undisclosed revision from 0.6 to 0.45 change that verdict? Say exactly what it changes and what it does not change, and why. (7 marks)
(c) One critic argues that luck egalitarianism demeans the people it aids and abandons those whose predicament is traceable to their own choices. Explain that objection, and one reply available to a luck egalitarian. (5 marks)

The answer I want you to look at:

(a) Luck egalitarians hold that inequalities are unjust when they come from circumstances people didn't choose, and fine when they come from the choices people make. Brute luck is chance that befalls you regardless of what you choose — being born with a disability, getting hit by lightning. Option luck is the result of a gamble you deliberately took. Mo chose the research year knowing it might come to nothing, so his £0 is option luck, and Iris's £12,000 is the product of her own deliberate choice. So the inequality is not unjust, the hardship payment isn't required, and levying Iris to fund it would be unjust as well, since her £12,000 is exactly what she chose to have.

(b) The undisclosed revision changes everything. Mo agreed to a gamble at 0.6, not at 0.45 — he never consented to the lower odds, so the outcome is no longer chosen by him at all and becomes brute luck. Once it is brute luck, the luck egalitarian has to neutralize it, so Mo is owed the full £12,000 he would have had from teaching, and the levy is now justified, because Iris's position is still the result of her own choice and involves no brute luck.

(c) The critic's objection is that luck egalitarianism is demeaning because it hands people money on the basis of their bad luck, which treats them as objects of pity rather than as agents. A luck egalitarian can reply by giving up on working out whether people are responsible for their situation and simply topping everyone up to the same income; then nobody has to prove anything and nobody is demeaned.
