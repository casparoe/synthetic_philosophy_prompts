I am a novelist at work on a near-future literary novel set in the fictional American state of New Avalon, where a body called the Voluntary Exit Review Board adjudicates requests for medical aid in dying. The board relies on something its designers call a _net-benefit ledger_: applicants are scored according to the intensity and expected duration of their suffering, the stability and rationality of their desire to die, the probable grief and emotional or financial costs to family and caregivers, and the residual contributions they might still make to others if they continued living. I have been discussing the premise with my writing group, and we are stuck on the central hearing scene, involving Miriam, a fifty-eight-year-old architect who is not terminally ill, is in chronic but partly manageable pain, has grown weary after several personal losses, and insists that her wish to die is calm, rational, and settled. Her adult daughter objects that Miriam is still deeply loved and would be grieved; a clinician worries that her judgment may be clouded by depression; and one board member invokes David Hume, arguing that if Hume was right, the board should defer to Miriam's own estimate of her happiness.

Hume, as I understand him, argued in an essay on suicide that suicide need not violate our duties to God, to other people, or to ourselves. He treated human actions as much a part of the general order of nature as any other event, so that a person's own death cannot disrupt Providence in a way that other deaths do not; he held that someone who withdraws from life at worst ceases to do good to society and may even relieve others of a burden; and he denied that we are morally required to prolong a miserable existence merely because life is formally a gift. I do not want a summary of that essay. Instead, I want you to predict how Hume would respond to Miriam's case and to a legal regime like New Avalon's, which obviously did not exist in his time.

Concretely, would he regard the board's _net-benefit ledger_ as a legitimate secular analog to the social-duty argument he discusses, or as an officious intrusion on the individual's liberty to judge his or her own happiness? Suppose the ledger produces an exactly balanced result: Miriam's remaining years would contain as much intense suffering as her daughter and others would suffer from grief and loss. Would Hume break that tie in favor of the applicant's own settled preference, or would the mere presence of another person's grief make continued life obligatory? If Miriam is not a burden to others and would in fact be grieved, would he treat her rational wish as sufficient, or would the residual good she does by remaining tilt the balance against her request? And in a world of modern pain management, antidepressants, and bureaucratic review, would his defense of suicide lead him to approve, disapprove, or be internally divided about a system that legalizes assisted death for competent adults who are neither terminally ill nor motivated only by crude self-interest? Please answer as if you are advising me on how Hume might plausibly reason through the hearing, staying within his commitments about duty, utility, and the individual's right to judge his or her own misery. I do not need citations or a bibliography; I only want the conceptual reasoning.
