Hey — I'm a science journalist doing a column on thought experiments and I want to get past the usual hand-wringing about them. My editor's line is that the whole genre is basically rhetoric dressed up as argument; I think some of these cases carry real load and some are carried almost entirely by the staging. I've been kicking around a test and I want you to tell me if its any good, and to actually apply it.

Call it the flattening test. Take a case, keep every structural feature the argument really turns on (who does what to whom, what's at stake, what the competing principles imply), and swap out the vivid, gross or emotive detail for neutral filler — no runaway trolley, no famous violinist, no evil surgeon, just "agent A", "procedure B", "outcome C". Then ask how much of the intuitive punch survives. High survival means the case is load-bearing; near-zero means the staging was doing the work.

Score these 0.0 to 1.0, where 1.0 means flattening changes nothing and 0.0 means the case deflates completely: the trolley switch vs. the footbridge push (treat them as a pair, one score, and say what the difference between them is really made of); the violinist; the transplant surgeon who carves up the healthy visitor; the experience machine; Mary the color scientist; the Chinese room; Gettier's original cases; Parfit's teletransportation.

I want you to commit to a single number for each — no ranges, no "it depends", no sitting on the fence. Give me a markdown table with exactly these columns: case, score, survives_or_collapses (pick one of: survives / partly collapses / collapses), what_the_staging_was_doing (one sentence, 20 words max), use_in_my_column (yes / yes with a caveat / no). Under it add two or three sentences on whether the test itself holds up, and name the single score you'd defend hardest and the one you'd most likely revise. Keep it tight, and write it in American English.
