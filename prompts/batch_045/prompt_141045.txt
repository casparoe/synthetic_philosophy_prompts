I am in my final year of secondary school and I help run our school's philosophy club. Each term we produce a single page on one debate, and this term I have taken on the question posed by John Taurek's 1977 paper in Philosophy and Public Affairs: a rescuer can save either one stranger, David, or five strangers, but not both, and whoever is not saved dies. Members arrive either certain that saving the five is required or certain that saving the one is permitted, and almost nobody can say more than that. So instead of another summary of the argument, I want to build a decision tree that sorts people by what they think about cases rather than by the side they walked in on, and that they can walk through in about ten minutes.

What I would like from you is a tree built from the logical space first and from names second. Give me the small set of questions that actually do the separating work, each phrased as a concrete case or claim that someone with no training in ethics could answer without recognising a label; then show which combinations of answers are available; then locate the real positions in those cells, naming a leaf only where a settled label exists and writing something like "no settled name" where it does not. Cells that nobody occupies should stay on the page with one line on why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unvisited.

These are the distinctions I want the tree to run through, at minimum:

1. Whether the fact that there are five rather than one bears on what the rescuer ought to do at all.
2. If it bears at all, whether it settles what she must do or only what she may do.
3. Whether the numbers do their work through outcomes, through each person's chance of survival, or through something else.
4. Whether it matters that reaching the five involves letting David die rather than doing something to him.
5. Whether it matters that all six will die soon anyway, or that David is already worse off than the five.
6. Whether the numbers still carry weight when what is at stake differs in kind across the groups, as where five people would each lose a finger and one person would lose his life.
7. Whether the extra four matter only through their relations to each other and to David, or whether they matter as bare additions.
8. Whether someone who will face a hundred such choices over a career has to distribute her choices, or may save the one every time.

I also want one case of my own tested, because I think it separates two things my club keeps running together. Maya can either pull one person from a burning room with certainty, or operate a device that brings five people out if it works and nobody out if it fails; everyone agrees each of the five has an eighty per cent chance, and the five chances are perfectly correlated. One life for certain against an expected four lives in which no individual's chance is as high as the lone person's is in the first option. I suspect this pulls apart "add up lives" from "add up chances", and judging a choice by its expected outcome from judging it by what actually happens, but I am not confident, and I would rather the tree told me than that I assumed it.

The leaves I want to be certain the finished tree contains: one in the neighbourhood of the numbers counting and settling the matter; one on which they count only between otherwise equal claims; one on which they do not count at all, so that each of the six gets an equal chance; one on which each group gets a chance proportional to its size; and one on which the numbers count but with diminishing weight as the group grows. Eight to ten leaves in total, each with one line on the most uncomfortable thing that position has to swallow.

Finally, three things I would find most useful of all: tell me where two leaves cannot be separated by any question in the tree, and give me one further case apiece that would prise them apart; tell me which of the several questions the tree as a whole is answering, among what the agent ought to do, what she may do, and what it would be best that she do; and tell me which branches would have to be redrawn if the tree were answering a different one of those.
