I teach an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of mind, and I'm rewriting the final; I want one essay question on Moore's paradox that students cannot pass by reciting the two moves everyone recites — that "It's raining but I don't believe it" is not a contradiction because it could be true, and that whatever is wrong with it shows up in assertion and judgment rather than in truth-conditions. I want that contrast to be the starting line rather than the finish line. The topic has also become strangely personal: someone close to me, recovering from a neurological episode, keeps producing reports of exactly this shape — "I can see the door is locked, I checked it myself, but I don't believe it" — and every time she says it I can't tell whether I'm hearing something absurd, something merely heartbreaking, or something that suggests the literature has been describing the wrong phenomenon all along. So I want the question built around a case in which the subject's self-ascription of disbelief is not grounded in introspection at all.

Here is the case I have in mind. A subject has an implanted neural monitor — call it a doxastic readout — that infers her current credence in whichever proposition she is attending to and displays a number she can see. It is reliable but not infallible; she knows its error profile; and crucially she has no introspective access to the states it tracks, so the display is her only route to them. She looks out at heavy rain while attending to the proposition that it is raining, the display reads 0.2, and she says, sincerely and after reflection, "It's raining, but I don't believe it." A second variant: same set-up, but she says, "It's raining — though my readout tells me I don't believe it, and it's usually right." Does the second remark retain whatever is defective about the first? I want students to have to answer that, and to have to say whether their answer changes if the subject is a collective agent rather than a person — say, a forecasting team whose official output is "rain" while its own internal confidence register, computed from the same members' credences, reads "not believed."

Please write: (1) the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, for a closed-book, 60-minute in-class essay with an 800-word ceiling, phrased so that it obliges students to state one general principle of the form "a subject's sincere first-personal present-tense self-ascription of disbelief alongside her judgment that p is defective if and only if …, because …", and then to test that principle against both variants; (2) a model answer of about 700 words that takes a stance among the three families of account I have in mind — the view that locates the defect in a clash of methods for answering "do I believe that p?", the view that locates it in belief's being constitutively self-intimating or essentially tied to its own avowal, and the view that locates it in what assertion or judgment commits you to — and that says which of these predicts the readout case correctly and which does not, and why; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points at four marks each, stating what separates an A answer from a B answer, and giving a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong. The model answer should earn no credit for name-dropping: refer to positions descriptively rather than by author or title, and please make sure the scheme says explicitly that summary moves — the tautology about possible truth, the remark that the sentence is fine in the past tense or in the third person — are worth nothing on their own.
