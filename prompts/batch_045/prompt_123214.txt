I've been asked to run a 45-minute internal session on the eng side of the company about why our risk score can't be fair in every way at once — the kind of result where you show that calibration within each group and equal false positive / false negative rates across groups can't both hold when the underlying outcome rates differ, unless the model is already perfect. I don't need the explanation for me, I need a plan for explaining it to them, and I need the plan to survive contact with this particular room.

Who's in the room: about twelve people, mostly backend engineers plus two data scientists, stats knowledge stops at "I once fit a logistic regression", zero philosophy, and zero appetite for being told they're bad people. Two of them built the model we'll be talking about. One guy has definately read a pop writeup of the recidivism-scoring controversy and will bring it up in the first five minutes if I don't get there first.

The model: we score applicants for a subsidised job-training program, the score is their predicted chance of finishing the course, the top scorers get offered a seat. And here's the wrinkle I don't know how to place: we only observe whether someone finishes for the people we actually admitted, so our historical outcome rates are partly an artifact of last year's admission policy rather than a fact about the world.

What I want from you:

- The order the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what. My instinct is that calibration has to land before error-rate parity, because calibration is the thing they already believe in ("of course a 0.7 should mean 70%"), but I'm not sure, and I don't know where the idea of a score as a probability at all has to be nailed down or whether I can just assume it.
- The one example that should carry the whole session, built on our own model or something close to it, and what that example has to have and not have. I'm fairly convinced I should NOT use a criminal-justice case — they'll spend twenty minutes arguing about crime and race and I'll lose the room — but tell me what else it needs: does it need numbers small enough to do in their heads, does it need the stakes to be a benefit being handed out rather than a punishment being imposed, does it need the two groups to be a protected attribute or should I use something blander like referral channel.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually form them, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. The ones I'm expecting: "just delete the group column and we're fine"; "this is only the accuracy-fairness tradeoff dressed up"; "it's a knife-edge result, approximate fairness is fine"; "our outcome rates differ only because of past discrimination, so the fix is to equalise the scores"; "randomising at the threshold fixes it". Tell me which of those I've got wrong, which ones show up later than I think, and the one I'm not anticipating.
- Where in the arc the selective-labels thing should go. If I open with it I think they file the whole session as a data-quality problem; if I close with it I think they conclude the theorem was beside the point. So: where, and in what form.
- What I should flat-out refuse to get into at this level, and say out loud that I'm refusing. I'm guessing the actual proof, anything causal, and which criterion is the morally right one — but tell me if I'd be cutting something I shouldn't.
- How I'll know at the end whether it landed. I don't want a show of hands on "does everyone get it". Give me something they have to produce. My idea was to hand them a variant where the two groups do have the same outcome rate and ask them what's still impossible — but I'm not sure that tests the right thing.

Last thing: they are going to ask, in the final ten minutes, which criterion we should actually ship. Tell me how the sequence should set that up so it comes out as a decision with an owner rather than a question I answer on the spot, because I really don't want to be the person who picked.
