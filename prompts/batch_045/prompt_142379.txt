I'm giving a ninety-minute session at a writers' conference next month — about twenty novelists, no philosophy background, all of them convinced they're already strong on character — and the topic is sphexishness: the way a stretch of behavior runs to completion regardless of what changes around it, and what would have to be true for a character to count as flexible rather than just noisier. I dont want the idea explained to me; I want a plan for how to get them there. What has to land first for the rest to have somewhere to sit? (My guess: that rigidity isn't repetition, it's insensitivity to the variable that matters — and that before that they need to see a fixed pattern that looks varied on the surface.) Which one example should carry the whole session, and what does it need to have and lack? I was going to use a scene of my own where a character keeps walking through a door she no longer has any reason to want, but I worry its too writerly — tell me if that's the right horse. Misconceptions I'm expecting: that flexible means unpredictable, so a coin flip or a random urge makes a character free; that the opposite of mechanical is "doing it for a reason"; that if it's sphexish then it isn't really her acting. Where in the sequence do I head each off, and which one am I not seeing? I also want to work in something I keep chewing over: that sphexishness might be grain-relative — you can be flexible about strategy and rigid about tactics, or the other way round, and whether a bit of behavior counts as rigid depends on the grain you look at it in. Does that go in early as a tool or late as a complication? Give me one thing for the board at the end that tells me they've got it rather than just agreeing with me. And tell me what to openly refuse to get into — I am not spending the last twenty minutes on free will.
