You are a professional philosopher of science with research expertise in statistical methodology and meta-research. Please answer carefully and rigorously. Before writing anything polished, begin with a short unstructured brainstorm — five or six rough bullet points in which you try out candidate diagnoses of the argument below and throw out the ones that don't work — and only then give me your considered answer in continuous prose. Please write in British English throughout, and assume I have A-level Further Mathematics and basic probability, but no measure theory.

I am in the upper sixth and writing my extended project on the replication crisis in psychology. I built the following argument out of a couple of meta-science papers my supervisor gave me, and I am now fairly sure it is broken somewhere, because it seems to prove far more than anyone — including the people who taught me its first premise — is willing to accept. I cannot find the break, so I would like you to find it for me.

(1) In any field where (a) most of the hypotheses researchers test are false, (b) studies are typically underpowered, and (c) researchers have considerable freedom over which outcomes to measure, which cases to drop, and when to stop collecting data, most published positive findings will be false positives. This is not an accusation of fraud; it is an arithmetical consequence of applying a 5% false-positive rate to a very large number of false hypotheses.

(2) I built a toy model in a spreadsheet to convince myself. First run: 1,000 hypotheses tested per year, of which 100 are true; power of 20%; significance threshold of 0.05. That gives 20 true positives and 45 false positives, so roughly 69% of significant results are false. Second run: identical, except that analytic flexibility raises the effective false-positive rate from 5% to about 14% — as it would if each hypothesis got up to three effectively independent chances at significance — while I hold power fixed at 20% in order to isolate the effect. That gives about 129 false positives against 20 true positives, so roughly 87% of significant results are false.

(3) So in a field of that kind, concluding that a hypothesis is true on the basis of a single published significant result is irrational, however small the reported p-value.

(4) Psychology appears to be such a field: large multi-laboratory replication projects have repeatedly recovered effects much smaller than the originals, or nothing at all.

(5) But the empirical case for (4) is itself made by the same kind of people, using the same kinds of tests, published under the same incentives, in the same kind of journals. More importantly, the meta-scientific estimates of power, flexibility and bias are themselves calculated from the published literature — the very body of work that (1) tells us to distrust.

(6) So the evidence for (4) is infected by the unreliability it alleges.

(7) If the evidence for a claim has been produced by a process we have good reason to regard as unreliable, we should suspend judgement about that claim.

(8) So we should suspend judgement about whether psychology is in a replication crisis.

(9) And nothing in (5) to (8) is specific to psychology. Every field-level empirical claim — that a drug works, that smoking causes cancer, that the climate is warming — rests on a literature with the same three features: false hypotheses, low power, analytic flexibility. So by parity of reasoning we should suspend judgement about all of them, and about the meta-scientific literature's own claims about bias, including (1).

(10) That is absurd. I do not believe it, and neither do the reformers who cite (4) while telling everyone to pre-register their studies. So the argument has gone wrong somewhere.

A friend who does Bayesian statistics says the whole thing dissolves at (3): nobody is obliged to treat "significant" as a verdict, because on her view you simply take the likelihood ratio the data give you and update your prior, so a failed replication nudges your confidence down and a successful one nudges it up, and no single study ever has to be believed or disbelieved. My worry is that this only relocates the problem, since the priors we assign to a field's hypotheses are usually informed by what is already in the literature, and the literature is biased — so the posterior is contaminated too, and we have moved the circularity into the prior where it is harder to see. I do not know whether that worry is fair.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look acceptable, and what does rejecting it cost me? If more than one step is faulty, tell me which one is load-bearing and which are cosmetic. Lay out the main alternatives — what it would cost me to give up (1), to restrict (7), to reject the move from (6) to (8), or to deny the parity claim in (9) — and say which option a working statistician would actually take, and where a frequentist and a Bayesian would part company. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1), (2) and (3) than accept (9); but I do not fully trust that instinct, so please tell me if I am protecting the wrong premise, or if my toy model in (2) is smuggling in something it should not.
