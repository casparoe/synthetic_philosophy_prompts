I am marking problem sets for an intermediate game theory course and would like a careful second reading of one answer before I finalise the marks. Below is the question as I set it, followed by the student's answer exactly as submitted.

The question:

Two players alternate moves in the centipede game, played on a finite tree of N decision nodes. At each node the player whose turn it is may Take, which ends the game and gives her the larger share of the current pot while the other receives the smaller share, or Pass, which enlarges the pot and hands the move to the other player; if the final player passes, the game ends in a fixed split in which she receives less than she would have received by taking. Assume throughout that at every node the mover's take-payoff exceeds what she would receive if the other player took at the next node.

(a) Set out the backward induction argument for the claim that the first player takes at the first node.

(b) Explain in what sense this is a paradox. Distinguish carefully between a defect in the validity of the argument, the falsity or implausibility of its premises, and the mere implausibility of its conclusion.

(c) Laboratory experiments reliably find that subjects pass repeatedly before someone takes, that both players typically earn more than the immediate-take payoff, and that longer games produce more passing. State what this evidence does and does not establish.

The student's answer:

Backward induction starts at the end. At the final node the mover compares taking with passing: passing ends the game and pays her the smaller amount, so she takes. At the node before, the other player can run exactly the same comparison, knows that passing would hand her opponent the last move, and therefore takes too. Carrying this reasoning back one node at a time, every mover takes, so Player 1 takes at the first node. The argument uses nothing beyond the assumption that each player prefers a larger payoff to a smaller one: at every node taking yields the mover more than passing yields her, so nothing here depends on how fast the pot grows or on how many nodes there are, as long as the number is finite and known to both.

The substantive premise is rationality. Each player must be rational and must know that the other is rational, but two levels are all that is needed: Player 1 has to expect Player 2 to take at node 2, and Player 2 has to expect Player 1 to take at node 3, and the same two levels repeat at each step. The argument is therefore fairly robust, and survives even if the players are only moderately confident in each other's rationality, since all it requires is that each believe the other more likely than not to take when it is her turn.

The paradox is that the outcome is bad for both of them. Had they both passed for a few rounds, each would have received more than the immediate-take payoff, because the pot grows at every step. This shows that the fault lies in the solution concept itself rather than merely in the surprisingness of the prediction: the rationality requirement defeats the point of being rational, so backward induction cannot be the right solution concept for sequential games.

The experimental evidence settles the matter. Players in the laboratory pass repeatedly and earn more than they would have by taking at the first node, so the immediate-taking prediction is false. Since the reasoning is deductive, the experiments must be telling us that one of the premises is false in practice: subjects are not fully rational, or are not sufficiently certain that the other player is. And the fact that passers earn more shows that passing is what rationality really recommends here.
