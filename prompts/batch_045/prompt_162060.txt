Last night I had a long argument with a friend about Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, and I would like an adjudication from someone who has genuinely thought the argument through. Both of us did graduate work in early analytic metaphysics, though neither of us works on it now, so I already know the standard dialectic and I am not asking for a summary of it.

Her position, as I understood it: the regress shows only that a relational account of the proposition cannot give a compositional analysis of unity in finitely many steps. But, she said, nothing requires the hierarchy to be completed, or completed in thought, so the regress is benign — structurally like the endless hierarchy of iterated truth ascriptions, where every level is licensed by the same rule and no level is doing explanatory work that any other level does not also do. She added a second, stronger point: let the relation be an unrepeatable relation-instance, a trope numerically tied to this very proposition and ontologically dependent on its relata rather than independent of them. Then there is nothing at the second level left to relate. The relation-instance is already inside the fact, so no further entity and no further relation is needed, and the regress never gets started.

I pushed back with two claims, and I am not confident either was fair. First, that presenting the argument as a regress of composition understates it, and that its real force appears only when it is reformulated as a regress of ontological dependence: for the unity at one level to obtain, a further obtaining must already be in place, and that further obtaining is itself something whose obtaining stands in need of explanation. On that formulation the hierarchy is not merely endless but top-down — every level borrows from the next the very thing it was supposed to supply — which looks less like a harmless infinity and more like a circle stretched out over infinitely many steps. Second, that her trope-theoretic move does not escape this: if the relation-instance depends on its relata, and the relata of a second-level relation-instance include the first-level unity, then the dependence runs backwards, and we have not explained unity but presupposed it. We went back and forth on this for two hours and got nowhere.

Please act as a professional philosopher — a metaphysician who has published on propositional unity and on grounding — and think about this carefully rather than quickly. Before you commit yourself, work through the strongest steelman of her trope and dependence-based reply, and of any third position neither of us considered (a non-well-founded or looping grounding structure, an operation or activity conception of relations, a priority-of-facts view), and only then decide. I do not want a literature survey, and I do not want citations or reading suggestions: I want your own reconstruction and your own verdict.

In your answer, please cover: (1) the tightest formulation of the argument you can construct, whether compositional, dependence-based, or something else, together with an explicit test for when an infinite regress of this shape is benign rather than vicious, and whether this one passes it; (2) whether the unrepeatable, dependent relation-instance reply is an escape, a relocation, or a restatement of the problem; (3) whether recasting the regress as one of dependence turns it into a circularity argument, and whether that makes it stronger or weaker; (4) whether the epistemic version of the worry — that a finite thinker would have to grasp infinitely many things — is an additional problem or a distraction from the metaphysical one; and (5) what exactly has been established if the argument succeeds: does it positively support a non-relational account of unity, or does it only eliminate the relational one and leave quietism and other options untouched?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single claim you would defend if pressed — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of my question, and no hedging into a survey of what various people have said. Where you think my friend was right and I was wrong, say so plainly; where you think we were both confused, say that too.
