I've been reading about game theory on my own — no formal training in it, so I'd rather have the reasoning in plain language than in notation — and I've talked myself into an argument I'm confident is bad, because it doesn't just settle one puzzle; it seems to pull the ground out from under everything built on what rational agents can know about each other.

(1) In the centipede game — two players, one pot, a long but finite chain of say a hundred nodes, the pot doubling at each pass, the mover at each node either taking slightly more than half or passing and letting it grow — backward induction says the last mover takes, so the mover before her takes, and so on all the way back to the first node, where the first player takes at once.

(2) Every step of that needs the mover at that node to be rational, and the usual way to supply it is common knowledge of rationality: everyone maximizes, everyone knows everyone maximizes, and so on without end.

(3) But if play actually reached a late node, something has happened that common knowledge of rationality rules out. Whoever sits at that node has therefore lost the assumption that her opponent maximizes — which is exactly the assumption the step at that node requires.

(4) So at every node after the first, arriving destroys the premise the step needs.

(5) And the verdict at the first node is nothing but the chain of verdicts at all the later ones, so (4) takes out the first node's step as well: the argument dismantles every step it leans on.

(6) This is not special to the centipede. Whenever a prediction is derived from common knowledge of rationality, that prediction rules out certain behavior; if that behavior occurs, the assumption behind the prediction collapses; and the assumption is only ever needed precisely in the situations its own prediction excludes. It is unavailable exactly where it would do work.

(7) So common knowledge of rationality cannot support anything — not the no-disagreement results for agents who start from a common prior, not the usual epistemic arguments that rational players end up at an equilibrium. That can't be right; those results are load-bearing and I don't think they are empty.

Of the steps, I'm most attached to (3), which looks to me like nothing more than treating what has happened as evidence. If keeping it costs me the standard results, say so plainly. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to elsewhere?

One more thing I built to test myself, and I can't tell whether it is relevant or a red herring. Same payoffs, same hundred nodes, but each node is decided by a fresh player who is told only that she is at that node, what the payoffs are, and that everyone maximizes — she is not told how play got there. Here arriving at a late node seems to carry no evidence that anyone failed to maximize, so (3) never fires, and yet the reasoning still ends at "take at once," and in this version that conclusion feels much more solid to me even though nothing about the payoffs changed. Whatever step you reject, say what it does with this second version and whether the two versions really differ.

Finally, please separate what is settled — definitions, mathematics, things nobody argues about — from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and flag it when you are giving me a contested line rather than the standard one.
