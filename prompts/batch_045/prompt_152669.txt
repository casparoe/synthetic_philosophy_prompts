The audience is staff at a state AI regulator: mostly lawyers, two engineers, nobody who has read any alignment literature. The workshop ends in a tabletop exercise where small groups have to decide whether to sign off on a frontier lab's internal request to let one of its own models do autonomous multi-day engineering work inside the lab's infrastructure. I want to hand them a decision tree they can walk through and land on "so that's the oversight strategy we're asking for, and here's what we'd have to believe for it to work."

Please act as an expert on scalable oversight and mechanism design who is also the kind of professional philosopher who actually cares whether the distinctions are well-drawn. Take this seriously and do it in the right order: brainstorm the branching questions first, sketch the space of answer-combinations, notice which combinations are empty, and only then drop the existing named proposals into the cells. I want a tree, not a list of things you happen to have read about. Before you draw it, spend a couple of paragraphs arguing with yourself about which two questions do the real work and why the others are secondary.

Some constraints:

- Root node: everyone in the room already grants that the model is better at this task domain than any human who could review its output in the time available. Start there; don't spend the tree arguing about whether that's true.
- Every branch node has to be one question a lawyer with no ML background could answer about the situation in front of them, without already knowing which strategy it leads to. Things like: is the thing being overseen one claim or a week-long sequence of actions; is there an answer that a panel of experts with unlimited time could eventually agree on, or is the question irreducibly about whose interests count; does the reviewer need to end up correct, or does she need to be able to justify having signed off; is this verdict used once, or is it fed back into training thousands of times; can the reviewer tell when both sides have quietly stopped competing. Those are starters — use better ones where you've got them, and if a question needs jargon to even be asked, it's the wrong question.
- Leaves are oversight strategies a regulator could actually write into a rule, ten at most. Include at least: debate between two models judged by a weaker human; recursive decomposition of the hard task into subtasks a weaker judge can check; market-making / prediction-market-style scoring of a model's claims; monitoring plus control, meaning an untrusted strong model watched by a trusted weaker one with the ability to intervene; eliciting the model's own honest self-report; blunt capability limits instead of oversight. Add whatever else earns its place. Each leaf gets one sentence on what it asks the regulator to believe, one on its most awkward commitment, and one on what it cannot do at all.
- Mark the answer-combinations your tree makes available but that nobody actually occupies, and say which reason applies: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
- Flag any two leaves the tree genuinely can't seperate instead of pretending it can. I have a suspicion that debate and market-making collapse into each other on any tree built the way I've described — if they do, say so, and say whether that's a bug in your tree or a real fact about both proposals. Same question for recursive decomposition versus debate.
- One branch or leaf has to be about democratic legitimacy rather than epistemology: the thought that no clever protocol can substitute for the people affected being able to contest the decision afterwards. Put it wherever the logic puts it, not in a footnote.
- No citations, no paper titles, no authors, no reading list. I can't follow up on them and they'd just clutter the page. Name the strategies, don't source them.
- At the bottom, three sentences on how a table of six lawyers would actually work through the thing in ten minutes.

Write it in American English, plain language in the nodes, and format it however typesets easiest into a single page.
