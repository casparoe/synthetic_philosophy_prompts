So I keep bees — nothing serious, two hives at the bottom of the garden — and the other night I got into an argument with a mate who's halfway through a physics PhD. He was saying that in the end everything about living things, and about conciousness too, is going to get explained in terms of the physics of the small stuff, and that biology and psychology are just convenient shortcuts we use because we can't do the real calculation. I said, yeah, but the whole is more than the sum of its parts. He said that sentence is either trivially true or plainly false and I should try saying what I actually mean by it. I couldn't, and it's been bugging me.

Here's the bit from my own hives that I was reaching for. A colony keeps its brood at roughly 35 degrees even when it's near freezing outside, by clustering, shivering their wing muscles, fanning at the entrance, bringing in water, whatever the weather calls for. No single bee does that. No single bee even seems to know what the temperature of the nest is; each one is reacting to something local. And once the brood is warm enough, they stop shivering — so the temperature of the whole colony seems to be acting back on the bees.

The work I wanted the slogan to do was: you could have a complete list of what every bee is doing at every moment and still be missing something, or at least still not have explained the thermoregulating. And I was kind of hoping the same thing would be true of someone's mind and their neurons.

So can you lay out for me, as a list from boring to wild, the different precise things "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" might be claiming? Like: the whole has properties no part has; you can't predict the big stuff from the little stuff without just running the whole thing; you can't derive or explain it even with unlimited computing power; the big stuff has causal powers of its own that push the little stuff around; the big facts aren't fixed by the small facts at all; there are genuinely new laws at the new level. Whatever the real options are — I've seen the word supervenience thrown around and I think it's meant to mark one of these lines, but I'm not sure which.

For each one say whether it's a platitude, something substantive and defensible, or actually false or muddled. Then: which one do I need for my bees, and does the bees case really support it? And which one would I need for the bit about minds, and does my mate's claim that physics will explain everything survive if that version is true?

Two things I keep tripping over and want you to deal with rather than dodge. First: the arrangement. The way the bees are positioned and wired up isn't one of the parts, so if I say "more than the sum of its parts plus how they're arranged", most of the mystery seems to drain out — so is the slogan secretly about arrangement, or about something over and above that? Second: if the colony's temperature makes individual bees stop shivering, aren't I counting the same causation twice, once at the bee level and once at the colony level? If that double-counting worry is right, does it kill off the wilder readings, or only some of them?

Also tell me which reading people normally mean when they say it, and whether that's the one that would do the job I needed. Please don't point me at papers or name-drop philosophers — I don't have access to any of that, and I'd rather you just reason it out than tell me who to go read.
