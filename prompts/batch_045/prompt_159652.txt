ok so a friend has been running this on me for weeks and I think the conclusion proves way to much, but I can't locate the bad step.

The setup is Hume's argument against miracles, section X of the first Enquiry (1748), roughly: a miracle is a violation of a law of nature; laws are established by uniform, exceptionless experience, so there's a proof against any miracle from the very nature of the fact; and since our only warrant for trusting testimony is experience too, the two have to be weighed against each other and the proof always wins. Hume does leave one escape hatch — testimony could establish a miracle if the falsehood of the testimony would itself be even more miraculous than the event. My friend says he can close that hatch too. His cleaned-up version:

(1) Take any well-confirmed regularity R (dead people stay dead; animals of a species declared extinct don't turn up). Given everything else we know, the probability that R has an exception on any given occasion is at most ε, where ε is very small.

(2) Testimony is itself a natural event. Our warrant for trusting it is also just experience, so no report, and no pile of reports, can deliver a probability of 1. There's always some residual chance of error, coincidence, hoax, or misdescription.

(3) So the most any body of testimony can do is multiply the odds by some finite factor, while (1) divides them by a factor that is, by hypothesis, enormous. Call the result the ceiling: for any reported violation of R there's a posterior probability that no amount of further testimony can push past.

(4) Nothing in (1)-(3) mentions God, revelation, or the supernatural. It's only: prior improbability from uniform experience versus finite evidential force from testimony. So the ceiling applies to any reported exception to any well-confirmed regularity whatever.

(5) Hence no number of independent witnesses could ever make it rational to accept, on their say-so, that a species thought extinct has been sighted, that a terminal patient spontaneously recovered, that a lab across the world registered something a settled theory says cannot happen, that a fortress everybody thought impregnable fell in a night.

(6) But we do accept things like that on testimony, and science seems to require taking other people's anomalies seriously — if nobody ever credited a reported counterexample they didn't witness themselves, theories would basically never get overturned.

(7) So the argument proves too much and something in (1)-(4) has to go.

Then he adds a reinforcement for (3) that I think makes it worse rather than better. Call it corrosion: any procedure we'd use to check a miracle — a body, a clock, a camera, a chain of custody, a memory — works only because the regularities hold. If the miracle happened, the machinery that produced the evidence was operating in a region where those regularities were suspended, so on the very hypothesis we're testing, the evidence can't be trusted to track what occurred. That closes Hume's escape hatch: the falsehood of the testimony never has to be miraculous, because the testimony's reliability is undercut precisely when it matters.

His concrete case: sixty field stations, mutually isolated, no contact with each other, no recordings, each with something like a 0.95 track record on ordinary matters, all reporting that at 03:00 on the same night they heard the call of a frog species declared extinct thirty years ago. My gut says at sixty you believe it. His formula says you don't, and if you push the number to six thousand he still says you don't.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) — proportioning belief to evidence, and not treating testimony as infallible, both seem obviously right to me — so I expect I have to pay somewhere else, and I want to know the price before I pay it. Specifically:

- Is the problem that "proof" is doing work it can't? That uniform experience supports R to something like 0.999 rather than 1, so the ceiling is really just "very low" and enough independent testimony can in principle outrun it? Or is that just restating the disagreement in different words rather than finding a flaw?
- Does corrosion rescue (3), or does it prove too much on its own? It looks like it would also show we can never learn about any anomaly from instruments, since the instruments registering an anomaly are the ones the anomaly calls into question. And doesn't it also cut against the friend: if corrosion is right, the argument shows at most that we could never know a miracle happened, not that one didn't?
- If the honest fix is to reject (4), the scope move, what's the principled difference? My guess is dependence: miracle reports cluster around what people already want to believe, so sixty witnesses to a resurrection are not sixty independent draws the way sixty stations hearing a frog are, and the aggregation stalls. But if that's the fix, the conclusion becomes contingent and sociological rather than the knockdown a priori bar Hume advertised — you'd have to go out and check the correlation structure case by case. Is that the real price?

Give me the diagnosis: name the step, say why it looked fine, and price the alternatives, including what a surviving, still-interesting version of the ceiling would look like.
