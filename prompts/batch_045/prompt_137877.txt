I work in health economics — mostly cost-effectiveness modelling for a national HTA body, and I sit on a committee that spends its life arguing about ICERs against a threshold, equity weights in distributional CEA, severity modifiers, person-trade-off exercises, capacity to benefit, discount rates, opportunity cost, the fair-innings argument. I'm fluent in all of that, so please don't spend any time explaining it to me. Where I'm out of my depth is a fight I got into at a dinner party with some people who work on animal welfare and longtermist stuff. They were claiming that whether brain size (or neuron count, or something in that neighbourhood) is morally relevant is a live and important question, and I kept trying to translate it into my own terms and failing. Rough facts I'm working from, correct me if any are wrong but don't go looking things up: the adult human brain weighs about 1.3 kg, our cerebral cortex has on the order of 16 billion neurons, elephants and some whales have much heavier brains but on the counting methods people use now fewer neurons in the forebrain than we do, because a huge share of an elephant's neurons sit in the cerebellum; and there are humans born with microcephaly whose cortical volume is drastically reduced.

Here's what I want you to do. Take the debate about whether brain size is morally relevant and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use — QALYs, welfare weights in a social welfare function, inclusion and exclusion thresholds, severity weights, capacity to benefit, opportunity cost — and take it apart into the different claims it might be making: is it a claim about who gets counted at all, a claim about how heavily each unit of welfare counts, a claim about how much welfare a given event produces, or a claim about something that isn't on a QALY scale in the first place? For each of those readings, tell me what the mapping is and where you had to fudge to make it fit. Concretely: someone at the table proposed what I'll call the neuron-scaled QALY, where a being's QALYs get multiplied by its forebrain neuron count over the human number, raised to some exponent, with the exponent set to zero for species-blind, one for linear, and above one for superlinear. I want to know what in my framework would even pin that exponent down, and whether the multiplier is doing the work of an equity weight, of a capacity-to-benefit adjustment, or of something my models have no slot for at all.

Then give me the residue report. Three cases I invented that I think break the translation, but you tell me: (1) the crowding case — a species whose fixed skull volume contains two architecturally separate conscious streams; is that one patient or two, and what does my framework even say about one body with two utilities inside it? (2) the frame-rate case — a small-brained species whose experience runs at a much finer temporal grain, so that per wall-clock second it packs in many more discrete experiences; QALYs are denominated in wall-clock time, so I suspect my framework is quietly assuming a shared time-grid, and I want to know whether that's a fixable bookkeeping choice or a sign that the brain-size claim is really two different claims, one about the intensity of experience and one about its number. (3) the marginal-cases case — if the multiplier is a function of an individual's neuroanatomy rather than it's species, then humans with much smaller cortices get smaller weights, and my corner of economics has a very deep norm against counting one person's QALYs for less than another's; is that norm something the framework can't give up without ceasing to be itself, or is it just an accident of the fact that we've only ever priced human health? And the part I actually care about: for everything that does not survive the translation, tell me case by case whether it's a blind spot in health-economic vocabulary — something true that it simply has no words for — or a sign that brain size is morally relevant was never one well-formed claim to begin with. Please don't smooth the differences over, and don't smuggle in your own moral assumptions and call them translation; I'll notice.
