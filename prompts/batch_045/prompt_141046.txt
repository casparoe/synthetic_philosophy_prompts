I am reporting a piece on how governments value projects whose benefits arrive a century after the spending, and I keep hitting the same impasse. Every economist and philosopher I interview says two things: that it would be indefensible to count a person's suffering as less important merely because they were born later, and that some positive discount rate has to appear in any serious long-run cost-benefit analysis. What I cannot get anyone to say is where, exactly, the rate attaches.

The puzzle comes out like this. If it attaches to dates, so that every unit of well-being is weighted by when it is experienced, then contemporaries are treated evenhandedly, which is what people want, but the same weighting says that a bad year at eighty counts for much less than an identical bad year at thirty, and my interviewees deny that, sometimes heatedly. If instead it attaches to birth cohorts, so that each person's life as a whole is weighted by when they were born, then nothing is discounted within a life, but two people alive in 2050, born thirty years apart, have their simultaneous and otherwise identical experiences counted unequally, which nobody defends either. Asked whether the rate might apply merely as between generations, one economist could not say what that would mean if not one of these two. Another said the rate is a price rather than a judgment about how much anyone matters, which I did not follow.

I have come across temporal impartiality and pure time preference, but neither seems to name this choice. Is there an established way of carving it: do the two options really exhaust the space, is there a third attachment that avoids both awkward implications, and if the choice is forced, which cost should a defender accept? I don't want a label on its own; I want the distinction and its edges. Tell me how confident you are in what you say, and name the single claim you would least like a hostile referee to challenge. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables.
