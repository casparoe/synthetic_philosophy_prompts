I am working through the epistemology of testimony and have hit an argument I cannot locate the fault in, though I am confident there is one. I would like a diagnosis of the reasoning itself rather than a survey of who holds what.

The dispute is the familiar one. Reductionists hold that a hearer's belief is justified by a speaker's say-so only if the hearer has positive reasons of her own — reasons not themselves supplied by the testimony — for taking that speaker to be a trustworthy indicator of the truth in the matter at hand. Anti-reductionists, in the Reidian and Burgean vein, deny this: testimony is a basic source, and a hearer is entitled to accept what she is told in the absence of defeaters, without any such reasons.

A friend argues that the reductionist position collapses, and that the collapse is not special to testimony. Her argument, as I have reconstructed it:

(1) Call the reasons a hearer has for trusting a speaker her vouching reasons. Reductionism requires vouching reasons; anti-reductionism requires none.

(2) Vouching reasons must be independent of the testimony they support. A consideration that just is the say-so one is trying to validate cannot be what makes that say-so credible. Call this the non-circularity constraint.

(3) Vouching reasons are themselves beliefs that stand in need of justification, and the constraint in (2) applies to them in turn: no link in the chain of a hearer's support may rest on the testimony it is supposed to underwrite.

(4) The reliability belief that does the vouching — that speakers of kind K are trustworthy about matters of kind D — is a belief about a population of cases, about how often assertions of that kind have turned out true. Assembling and evaluating that population — deciding which past utterances count as belonging to kind D, which of them were true, how the sample should be weighted — is not something a hearer can do without drawing heavily on testimony, at any rate not for the domains that matter most. Call such a hearer a dependent hearer.

(5) For a dependent hearer, then, the vouching belief is itself substantially testimonial, and by (2) it cannot do the vouching work. Her testimonial beliefs are unjustified.

(6) But dependent hearers plainly do acquire justified belief this way. Children acquiring a language, non-specialists taking a result on trust from people whose work they could not check, readers of history: most of what any of us knows arrives like this.

(7) So reductionism is false, and anti-reductionism is true.

(8) Now run (2) through (7) again with any other source substituted for testimony. Checking whether your perceptions are reliable requires using perception; checking memory requires memory; vindicating induction requires induction. In every case the vindication of a source draws on the source. So by the same reasoning no source needs vindication from outside itself, and every source confers default entitlement on its deliverances.

(9) That conclusion is unacceptable, or at least far stronger than anyone should want. It makes the default view unfalsifiable, and it would entitle the deliverances of any belief-forming process that happens to resist external monitoring, including processes no one takes to be sources of justification at all.

So something in (1) through (8) has gone wrong, and whatever it is, it cannot be a fact specifically about testimony, since the argument is supposed to generalize.

What I want from you:

(a) Identify the step or the inference you would reject, and state precisely what is doing the work there — whether it is an equivocation between two readings of a constraint, a scope shift, a false dichotomy, or something else.

(b) Explain why that step looked innocuous. I want to know why it survived my scrutiny and my friend's, not just that it fails.

(c) Tell me what rejecting it commits me to. I would most like to keep (6), and I would like to keep the substance of (1), since the thought that trust needs some backing is what drew me to reductionism. If those two cannot both be kept, say so and say which way the cost falls.

(d) Price the alternatives I have not mentioned. In particular: what would it cost to reject (6) rather than anything earlier; what would it cost to reject (8) and insist on a principled asymmetry between testimony and the other sources, given that default theorists tend to argue for testimony by analogy with perception; and is there a position between reductionism and anti-reductionism that blocks the inference from the failure of (5) to the vindication of (7)?

Please be concrete about which claim each option sacrifices, and do not settle the matter by telling me what the literature says — I want the structure of the argument exposed.
