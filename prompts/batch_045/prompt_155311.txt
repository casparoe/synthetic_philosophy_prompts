Hey — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, fifteen years in, mostly long-form, and a lot of what I actually do is decide who to believe: patients, whistleblowers, community groups with local knowledge, sources inside institutions, the guy who emails the newsroom at 2am. I've absorbed enough of the philosophy to know the vocabulary — testimonial injustice, where a hearer gives a speaker less credibility than they're owed because of some identity-linked prejudice; hermeneutical injustice, where the shared concepts just aren't there yet so the experience can't get said — but I don't want a tour of the literature and I can't check citations anyway. What I want is my own gut sorted out, because my gut is handing down verdicts that I can't fit into one principle.

Here's where I land (all of these are composites of stories I've actually worked on):

1. A nurse in a dialysis unit notices a cluster of infections, raises it internally, gets brushed off. Even when I interview her, I notice myself mentally weighting the hospital's statement above hers until I see the raw numbers. That's injustice, and some of it was mine. Definitely one of the clear ones.

2. I discount press releases from a certain class of company by a fairly fixed factor, because historically they oversell by about that much. Just calibration. Fine, no guilt.

3. The pair I keep going back and forth on. (i) I have a standing rule: ignore unsolicited technical claims from people with no affiliation I can check, because I get 200 a week and can't verify them. The rule has obvious disparate impact — it favours people with institutional affiliation, which correlates with class, education, country, fluency. I don't feel bad about it. (ii) A community meeting in a town near a smelter, residents describing an illness in their own words; I run roughly the same filter and miss the story for two years. That one I still feel bad about, and I call it injustice. These look like the same move to me — a filtering rule with disparate impact — and I don't know why I acquit one and convict the other.

4. The mirror image. I once massively over-credited a polished, well-accredited academic talking about a field that wasn't his, mostly because of how he sounded and where he was from. Him? I don't think he was wronged. But the woman who actually knew that field, whom I never called because I already had my expert — her I think I did wrong, and I'm not sure whether that's the same kind of thing or a different animal entirely.

5. The one that bothers me most, and I'm not sure why. A source in a citizen-science network — a farmer, no institutional backing — gives me the key observation. I believe her completely, I print it, but I attribute the explanation to the university scientist I also called, because I know my readers won't take it from her and I don't have column inches to establish her. I believed her and still took something from her. My gut says this is worse than the cases where I disbelieved someone — but it might just be that it's the one I'm still in a position to keep doing.

6. Nobody's culprit. Non-English-language research basically never enters my newsroom's verification pipeline; no individual makes a bad credibility judgment; the upshot is that whole bodies of evidence never reach me. Injustice, but I can't locate the hearer.

7. Not prejudice at all. An engineer flags a safety problem; management discounts him not because of what he is but because believing him costs them. My gut says this is the sharpest item on the list, worse than 1 — and yet it doesn't seem to be what the philosophical term picks out, since there's no identity prejudice in the hearer.

8. Time travel. I reread my own 2009 coverage of people describing a set of symptoms that didn't get a clinical name for another decade. I didn't have the concept, couldn't have had it. Harm was real, but I don't think I did anything wrong, and I notice I resist calling it injustice — which sits oddly with how readily I say it in 6, where there's also nobody to blame.

So: is there a principle that gets these, or most of them? I'd like it stated clause by clause with the case that motivates each clause. I want to know honestly which of my verdicts it makes me give up — I suspect 3(i) and 3(ii) can't both survive, and suspect something in the 4-vs-7 comparison has to go too. Whether the harm has to land on someone's standing as a knower, or whether the third-party and distributional stuff in 4 and 6 counts as the same thing. And then two cases I haven't made up my mind about, please: (a) I now deliberately adjust my credence upward for sources from groups that get systematically under-credited — is that repair, or is it a new epistemic sin where I'm managing people rather than trusting them; (b) my newsroom is piloting a tool that scores source reliability from how often past sources with similar profiles panned out — does your principle say anything about it, and if it's fine, is that for the same reason 2 is fine or for a different one?
