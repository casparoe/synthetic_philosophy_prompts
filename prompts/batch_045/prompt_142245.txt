I'm a senior at a big public high school in central Ohio, and I run our Philosophy Club's "unresolved disputes" series: two members spend a month on one question, argue past each other, and then have to produce a written summary of where they actually landed. We ran Kavka's toxin puzzle last semester and it went exactly as you'd predict — both sides got better at stating their own case and neither moved an inch — so I want to do the format properly this time, and I'd like you to draft the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the two camps would have produced.

The case, stated cold: an eccentric billionaire puts a vial in front of you and offers you a million dollars on the condition that at midnight tonight you have formed a settled intention to drink the contents tomorrow afternoon. The vial makes you painfully ill for about a day and has no lasting effects; you need not drink it to be paid, and in fact the money lands in your account hours before the drinking would happen, so you are entirely free to change your mind. Assume the billionaire has an unerring way of telling whether the intention was formed, so nothing here turns on his being fooled, and assume you are not the sort of agent who has somehow lost the capacity to make plans. The club split into two camps. Call them the Inaccessibilists: they say the intention is not available to you at all, because intending to do something is constitutively tied to your own take on your reasons for doing it at the time of action, so an agent who now knows she will have no reason whatsoever to drink tomorrow cannot now intend to drink, and the offer is one that in principle nobody can collect on. Call the others the Accessibilists: they say there is no in-principle barrier, that an intention can be adopted now for reasons that need not be the agent's reasons at the time of action (that is largely what planning is for), and that what actually blocks you is something else — the awkwardness of intending at will, or the fact that your intention would be evidence about what you will later do, or an unwillingness to engage in something close to self-deception.

What I want is the joint statement both camps could sign. Give me, first, a numbered section of claims both can accept — including about the setup, about what an intention would have to be like if it were formed, and about which moves are cheating (for example, I suspect both sides should agree that you can't collect by intending to drink in order to preserve your self-conception as someone who follows through, since that supplies a reason at the time of action and thus doesn't engage the puzzle — but if one side would reject that, say so). Second, a disagreements table in which each remaining dispute is stated twice, once in wording an Inaccessibilist would accept for her own view and once in wording an Accessibilist would accept for his, with a note on whether the dispute is genuine or merely verbal — and if part of it is verbal, I want to know which part and why, since I have a suspicion that at least one of our fights was really about how to use the word "intention." Third, for each crux, one concrete case, argument, or finding both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Cruxes I'd guess at, to be confirmed, corrected, or replaced: whether intending requires believing (or at least not disbelieving) that you will do the thing; whether intending is all-or-nothing or admits of degrees, and whether the puzzle quietly assumes it is binary; whether the barrier is the disappearance of reasons or merely the unpleasantness of the act — testable, I think, with what I've been calling the grape variant, in which you must intend to do something harmless and pointless tomorrow, like eating one grape at 6 a.m., knowing you will then have no reason to; whether a trivial independent reason at the time of action (the billionaire quietly adds a five-dollar payment for actually drinking) unlocks the intention, which would suggest the real argument is about how much of a reason is needed rather than whether any is; what to say about the iterated version, in which this happens a hundred nights running and you acquire a track record of having formed such intentions; and whether an intention counts as formed if it can only be produced by a route that bypasses your judgment, such as a pill, a fit of pique, or post-hypnotic suggestion. Please also tell me plainly which crux, if settled, would actually move at least one camp, which are empirically tractable in any way at all, and whether either view has quietly insulated itself by building its conclusion into its definition of intention.

Two constraints: our school has no database access, so please don't cite papers, name the philosophers associated with each position, or give me anything to read — I want the reasoning, not sources, and I'd rather you argue the positions out under the descriptive labels above than lean on authority. Format: the numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then the crux-and-prediction section, and finally a single valid JSON object (no trailing commas) summarizing the whole thing with these fields:

- "agreed_claims": array of objects with "id", "claim", and "why_both_sign"
- "disagreements": array of objects with "id", "one_line_summary", "inaccessibilist_wording", "accessibilist_wording", "genuine_or_verbal", "shared_test_or_case", "inaccessibilist_prediction", "accessibilist_prediction"
- "highest_leverage_crux": the id of the crux whose resolution would most move the debate
- "empirically_tractable": "yes", "no", or "partly", plus one sentence of justification
- "largest_verbal_component": one sentence identifying the piece of the dispute that was really about words
