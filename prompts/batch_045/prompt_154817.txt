I write novels — the secondhand-bookstore kind, mysteries with a literary bent — and I have been having the same argument with my workshop and my readers for about three years now, about a character I can't seem to fix or drop. She's the one who gives everything away. The money, the promotion, the relationship, the kidney; she takes that pond argument literally and just never stops. I didn't set out to write a saint.

The reactions split in a way I still find weird.

Some readers: she is the best person in the book and the discomfort is the point, we're supposed to feel pricked. Others don't say unrealistic, they say drained — her life looks narrow and gray, they feel sorry for her, and they think whatever is impressive about her isn't the same thing as her life going well for her. Then there's a third group who cheerfully grant that she's morally flawless and say they wouldn't want to know her and feel nothing like admiration; to them it reads as a failure of character rather than an excess of it.

What drives me up the wall is that these three get traded back and forth as if they were one argument, and nobody moves an inch. My hypothesis, which I'd like you to shoot down if it's wrong, is that the question everyone thinks they're arguing about is really three questions in one coat: how much morality actually demands, what makes a life go well for the person living it, and what we're even doing when we say we admire someone, if that's one attitude and not several with different objects. Or maybe it's verbal and everybody is using good person to mean something different.

But here's the bit that makes me distrust my own intuitions and everyone else's. In a novel I control the camera. If I put the reader inside the cost — the missed trains, the calls not returned, the daily grind of it — and never show them the people who are better off because of her, they come back saying her life is sad and thin. If I show the beneficiaries, even in glimpses, they come back saying she's admirable and they feel ashamed. Same character, same behavior. I can flip the verdict by moving the point of view. Which makes me think a chunk of whatever is being treated as data in this argument isn't a judgment about moral sainthood at all, it's an artifact of how the example gets framed — and if the standard examples have a built-in framing asymmetry, the debate might be leaning on contaminated evidence.

So why has this stalled? I've been pointed at the essay that kicked it off in the early eighties and at the literature on how demanding morality is allowed to be, and as far as I can tell everyone has read everyone and the positions haven't moved in forty years. Diagnose it, using what each side actually claims: verbal or substantive, a bedrock clash about what a good life is, a hidden empirical question nobody has bothered to check (would a real person who gave all that up actually end up depleted, or fine?), several questions under one heading, or a mix where different parts stall for different reasons. Tell me which pieces are seperable — could one get settled without the others — and be straight about what would count as progress here and whether anything realistically will.

Two housekeeping things. Please keep clear which claims are settled among people who actually work on this and which are still a live fight; don't hand me one side's position wearing a consensus costume. And American English, please. Also, where you're guessing about my character rather than about the argument, say so — my workshop already has alot of opinions about her.
