I am the researcher-writer for a six-part documentary series on Jesus and first-century Galilee, and over the next three months my team must assign on-screen wording to roughly forty individual sayings and episodes. For each item we choose between presenting it as something Jesus said or did, presenting it as what the earliest followers came to believe about him, or flagging it on screen as contested. There are no footnotes, narration has to sound settled without claiming false precision, and a three-person scholarly advisory panel — one fairly conservative, one skeptical, one who works mainly on the social world of Roman Palestine — must sign off on the whole list. That gives us about fifteen minutes of argument per item and rules out any verdict only one of them can live with.

Please build me a decision procedure we can actually run in that time. Start from the familiar criteria — multiple attestation, dissimilarity from both surrounding Judaism and the early church, embarrassment, coherence with what is already established, Semitic-language phenomena, and plausibility in a Galilean or Judean setting — but do not let them stand as a checklist, since each one only grades a tradition relative to a background assumption about how the tradition grew. I need at least four additional moves built in: an audit of how the sources could be genealogically related, so that we count independent lineages rather than independent texts; a test in which any verdict is rerun against at least one rival model of transmission and downgraded if it flips; a rule against counting the same evidence twice under two criteria; and a warning flag for the selection effect built into the dissimilarity criterion, which by construction certifies only a figure unlike both the Judaism of his time and the movement that followed him. Say what counts as a sufficient answer to each step, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is editorial rather than factual, and where judgment genuinely cannot be eliminated.

Then run it on three items: the baptism by John, the saying on divorce as it appears with different exceptions in different sources, and the empty-tomb tradition, which is the one I expect you to take away from me and which my narration currently depends on. Show the steps, and close with an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do — in particular whether any set of criteria can establish who Jesus was as opposed to what was being attributed to him within twenty years of his death.
