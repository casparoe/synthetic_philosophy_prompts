I am finishing a novel whose set-piece is a hearing before a fictional international commission on the instrument that gives the book its title: the Reflection Treaty, a binding hundred-year moratorium on the two technologies that in my world, as in ours, are thought to open the rest of the galaxy to settlement — recursively self-improving machine intelligence and the industrial base needed to turn it into a colonization program. I have deliberately stipulated away the easy escapes. A decade before the novel opens, the in-world physics community establishes what everyone calls the Terminus: usable free energy runs out at a fixed date roughly a hundred billion years off, the date does not move with when we begin, and no reachable technology extends it — so the window is finite and value not realized inside it is destroyed rather than merely deferred. My protagonist speaks for the treaty. Its opponent is a strong longtermist named Merel Kast, a former astrophysicist who holds that shaping the long-run future is not one key moral priority among others but the key one, that human extinction or permanent civilizational collapse would destroy nearly everything that matters, and that there are exactly two levers on the future: how long it lasts and how good it is while it lasts. I want his speech to be genuinely formidable, because the book only works if my protagonist has to earn her reply. Here is the argument as I have him give it. (1) The value of the future is, to a first approximation, the number of lives it contains multiplied by their average quality, and the window could hold vastly more lives than have lived in all of history, so nearly all the value ever at stake lies downstream of us. (2) Because the window is fixed and finite, every year of delay permanently deletes a year of that flow: the loss is real, not a deferral. (3) A century-long moratorium therefore costs a century of the flow, an astronomically large quantity of value, larger than everything human history has so far contained. (4) Measured against a quantity that large, any finite near-term cost is negligible — a war, a famine, the suppression of a liberty are rounding errors next to a decade of delay, let alone a hundred years. (5) Nor can the treaty's defenders shelter behind the small probability that any single commission or any single vote changes the timing at all: a modest probability multiplied by an astronomical stake still swamps the ordinary near-term certainties we use to comfort ourselves. (6) And reflection cannot rescue the treaty: either a century of deliberation lands on the values we already hold, in which case the century was pure waste, or it lands somewhere else, in which case we have no reason to think the destination is better; in neither case does it add a single life to the window, which is the only currency that counts. (7) So the treaty would be the largest crime in human history, the right policy is maximum speed secured by compulsion if the democracies will not vote for it, and — since Kast is nothing if not consistent — the minutes spent delivering this speech are themselves a cost, and the commission should adjourn now.

I cannot let it stand as the last word, but I cannot find the joint either: each step looks like something a serious person has defended, and I need my protagonist to reject the conclusion without sounding like a sentimentalist who simply declines to do arithmetic. Two things I noticed while drafting make me suspect I have built the trap in the wrong place. First, when I tried to put numbers to it — suppose the window holds something like 10^23 lives, so that a century of delay removes something like 10^14 of them — the loss is unimaginable in absolute terms and roughly one part in a billion of the total; and by Kast's own accounting, a permanent collapse forecloses the whole remainder, so a reduction of extinction risk of even one part in a billion pays for the century, and the treaty seems, on his framework, to buy itself. Second, step (5) looks like the premise that does all the work and no one would own: if a small chance at an astronomical stake always dominates, then every longtermist should abandon the hearing for whatever speculative scheme has the largest tail, and the argument has proved far more than Kast wants.

So: tell me which step of the seven to reject, why that step looked sound enough to survive his saying it out loud, and what rejecting it costs him — I need to know what he is left holding afterward. I want to keep premises (1) and (2) if at all possible, since the Terminus is settled fact in my world and I do not want the novel to turn on a physics dispute, and I want to keep the claim that extinction would be uniquely bad, since my protagonist believes it too. I also want to know how much weight to put on a wrinkle I cannot evaluate: the whole calculation seems to assume that the rate at which the window is filled is independent of when we start, which is exactly what the treaty's supporters deny, since a later departure might be a faster and better one. And finally, dramatically: should my protagonist concede the argument and oppose the treaty on independent grounds — that what is really at stake is the quality and lock-in of a future rather than its length, or that no one in that room can know the difference their vote makes — or is there a premise she can break instead? I do not need references or reading lists; I need the diagnosis worked out in my own terms, well enough that I can write her side of the scene without cheating.
