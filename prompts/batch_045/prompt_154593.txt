I'm a backend engineer, been doing this maybe nine years, no philosophy background beyond one required course I mostly slept through. I own the telemetry pipeline at a ~250 person company that makes scheduling software. Concretely there are three groups in our data: admins at big hospital systems (paying, loud, they have an account manager they can email), the front-desk staff who actually sit in the UI eight hours a day, and the patients who get reminder texts and show up in our tables as a phone number plus a boolean for whether they no-showed.

I got into this because of a thing that happened last quarter. We shipped a change that silently broke reminder delivery for a chunk of patients; it took six weeks to notice; and the only signal we had was a regional bump in the no-show rate, which in the incident review got described as those patients being flaky. Nobody had lied, the data just had nothing else to say. Around the same time I stumbled into reading a couple of things about archives and power — the gist I got out of it was that an archive isn't a neutral pile of what happened, it's shaped by who was in a position to write things down, and that the gaps are made rather than just found. And now we're about to train a "smart scheduling" model on two years of these logs, so whatever we happened to record is about to become a pretty hard definition of who our user is.

What I want from you is a procedure, not an essay. I run an instrumentation review maybe twice a sprint, plus a quarterly retention pass, so this is a recurring decision, not a one-off. It needs to be runnable in fifteen or twenty minutes by an engineer who has never thought about any of this, and it needs to fit on one page.

So: the questions to ask in order, and what actually counts as an acceptable answer to each rather than hand-waving. Where it hard-stops with a "no, don't ship this event / don't make this change." Where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is really about values, and which person — me, legal, the PM. And what gets written down afterwards and where, because I want some kind of absence register: a place that says we chose not to record X and why, so that in two years someone doesn't read the gap as nothing having happened.

A few things it has to be able to handle, because these are the ones I keep hitting:

- People who only exist as a leftover. Total sessions minus sessions we can attribute, that kind of thing. I've started calling them ghost users. There should be a question that surfaces them and asks what it would cost to make them visible at all.
- Silences that travel. I made up the term "silence inheritance" for when the warehouse inherits a gap from the client SDK, so no amount of reprocessing downstream fixes it and everyone assumes it's fixed. The procedure should catch that whole class, not just the obvious "we never logged it."
- The fact that some absences are load-bearing and good. I really don't want a procedure whose output is "log more," because we're under a GDPR-ish regime, deletion requests are real, and some of what we hold we probably shouldn't hold: the free-text "reason" field on the patient-facing cancellation form sometimes contains why someone can't make an appointment, which is close to medical detail. So there has to be a step that says don't record this, and a way to mark that down so people don't infer from the blank that nobody ever gave a reason.
- Deletion asymmetry: the people most visible in the data are also the ones who know how to get themselves deleted, and the people who are only a residual don't know we have a record and have no way to contest what it says. That should be a question too.
- And be honest about what the procedure can't do — which steps are judgment calls wearing a checklist as a costume.

Then run it on three cases. One: a new event for a "bulk reschedule" button that only enterprise admins can even see. Two: a cost proposal to sample free-tier sessions down to two percent and drop a few fields, which I expect to be a no — I want to see exactly where it fails and what the cheapest thing that passes would be. Three, the one I'll like least: we currently log that free-text cancellation-reason field at full fidelity, forever, and it feeds three dashboards, one of which a VP looks at weekly. I'm fairly sure the procedure is going to tell me we shouldn't have it and I do not want to be the one who says that out loud, so tell me plainly what it says, what complying costs us, what we'd actually lose the ability to know, and if it turns out the procedure can't settle it and it's just a values call for a person, say that instead.

Assume I'm smart about systems and dumb about theory: translate, don't gesture. And please write it in plain language — if a step needs a concept, define it in the step.
