Hi there. I'm retired now — forty-odd years as a field ecologist, mostly survey work and a lot of arguing about detection bias — and I've drifted into reading about archives and historiography, which I'm definately enjoying more than I expected to. I keep banging into what looks to me like exactly the problem we had with unseen species: you can't estimate what you never detected from the sample you did detect without some assumption, and the assumption is doing nearly all the work.

So. I'd like a number, and I'd like to see the workings.

The claim: of the things a historian working in, say, 2125 will want to know about ordinary life in Britain in the 2020s but cannot recover from surviving records, the largest share is missing because no record was ever created in the first place — as opposed to records that were created and then lost through destruction, decay, format obsolescence, deliberate deletion, deaccessioning, or plain mislaying.

Give me your credence in that, decomposed:

- Pin the proposition down first if you think it's sloppy, and say how you'd settle it. My own suggestion is an imaginary well-funded audit that takes the questions historians actually ask in 2125, isolates the ones that can't be answered, and traces each to its cause — but overrule me if that design is bad, and say why.
- Then the sub-questions: how much of social life leaves any durable trace at all; whether digital abundance raises the creation rate or mostly raises the loss rate; the rough base rates of decay, obsolescence and deletion; whether what counts as a record is doing hidden work; and whatever else you think belongs. A number and one line of justification each.
- How they combine, and a number at the end, with error bars if you can give them honestly.
- Which single term you are least confident about, and what evidence or argument would move it most.

Two things I've been chewing on that you should fold in rather than talk around. First, a category I've invented for myself and have started calling retrieval silence: material that was created, is preserved, is catalogued, and is in principle open — but never surfaces, because what gets found is shaped by what has already been quoted, indexed, linked and linked again, so the finding process has its own rich-get-richer dynamic. In my old field we'd have called it a sampling bias that isn't in the detection model at all. Is that a genuine additional cause of silence, or just non-creation at one remove, and where does it go in the arithmetic?

Second, a worry that the proposition may be quietly circular. The questions a future historian asks are partly selected by what the archive happens to contain, so "what they will want to know but can't" may already have been trimmed to fit the surviving record. That should bias the answer one way or the other and I can't work out the sign. Tell me what it does to your number.

For what it's worth, my brother-in-law was a county archivist and is utterly convinced: 0.9 that non-creation dominates, mostly on the grounds that most of life is talk and nothing else. I'm at about 0.55, because I think we generate records at a ludicrous rate and I suspect were losing them faster than we think. Give me your own number, but more usefully tell me where that disagreement most likely lives — the creation rate, the loss rate, the definition of a record, or something I haven't separated out.

Please write in British English; I can't be doing with American spellings.
