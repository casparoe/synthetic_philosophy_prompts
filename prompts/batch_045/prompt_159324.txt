I was arguing with my friends the other night about whether you can really own an idea, and i realized we kept going in circles because of a move I can't put a name to. Here's the pattern: sometimes someone makes something very simple or tightly constrained—a short plain instruction for doing a common task, or a basic shape that's just about the only practical way to solve a tiny physical problem—and when another person comes out with the same thing, the owner says that in this case the general thing and the specific thing have collapsed into each other, so copying the specific item counts as taking the protected thing. But then, in a totally different case, when the thing is very broad or functional—a general method for doing business with a computer, or the discovery that a natural substance has a certain effect—the owner says something like, what's really protected is the general method or principle itself, not just one particular version, so anybody who implements it in any way is using their property. Its like the line between the abstract idea and the concrete expression stretches and shrinks: when the idea is thin, the owner says the concrete version is protected because there is no other way to do it; when the idea is broad, the owner says the abstract idea is protected because it is the real invention. The direction always seems to be the one that gives the owner more control.

To make it concrete, think of a small ornamental hook whose shape is almost forced by what it has to do, and a competitor makes an identical hook; the argument is that shape and function have merged, so the competitor copied the thing that is owned. Then think of a company getting an exclusive right to a very general 'do X with a computer' method, and arguing that even a totally different piece of software that does X is just an implementation of their general invention, so it is covered. The same tradition seems to appeal to 'when it is thin, all versions are the same' and 'when it is broad, the idea itself is the invention,' depending on which way helps.

So my question is: does this exact whipsaw have an established name in philosophy or legal theory, or is it just a family of separate tricks? I know there is a notion for the thin case, where the idea and its only real expression become indistinguishable, and I know there is a background rule that purely abstract ideas are not supposed to be ownable, but what I'm after is the full pattern: the elastic use of the abstract/concrete boundary to expand protection when the subject is broad and to collapse it when the subject is thin. Please don't just hand me a bare label; I have no web access and I won't be satisfied with a name alone. I want the carving: if there is a nearest concept, tell me what it is, how my described phenomenon differs from it and from the nearby notions (including the thin-collapsing one and the no-bare-abstract-ideas one), and which fits best, if any; if there is no established concept, coin a term, define it sharply, and give boundary cases (for example, what if the idea is only moderately thick, or what if the owner deliberately designs it to be thin so the collapse triggers). Please write your answer in American English.
