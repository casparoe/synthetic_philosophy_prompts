I am a finalist on the Philosophy, Politics and Law programme and I am taking the jurisprudence option this term. The block I am finding hardest to revise is the Hart–Fuller debate, because everything I have written about it so far degenerates into a restatement of the separation thesis followed by a list of Fuller's eight principles, and I cannot tell from my own notes whether I actually understand either position. I run a fortnightly revision session for about a dozen people in my cohort, and I want to use the last session before the exam as a timed mock that we mark ourselves. Please help me build it.

What I am asking for is one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The question should be set at final-year undergraduate level, answerable in forty-five minutes, and marked out of twenty. It must be constructed so that the standard revision material is worth almost nothing: a candidate who recites the separation thesis, ticks through the eight principles of legality, or summarises Hart's minimum content of natural law should be able to reach only a low mark. The question may include a footnote setting out the eight principles, precisely so that no credit attaches to reproducing them. I also want the question to force a judgement about a particular crux rather than a balanced survey, and to leave the candidate with something to argue for rather than something to describe.

Here is the case I want the question built around, which I have invented for the purpose, so please tighten it where it needs tightening rather than replacing it with a famous historical example:

In the Republic of Verraine there is no standing legislature. Every five years five hundred citizens are chosen by lot from the electoral register; they sit for ninety days, deliberate in private, and produce a complete criminal code, after which they disperse permanently. The code is the sole source of criminal prohibition for the next five years. No body whatsoever — not the courts, not the ministries, not the selectors themselves — has power to amend, repeal or dispense from it during that period. It is promulgated in full six months before it takes effect. Independent reviewers confirm that it is general in form, intelligible to a person of ordinary education, internally consistent and entirely possible to comply with; the courts apply the enacted text exactly, and there is no measurable gap between the rules as declared and the rules as administered. The selectors are subject to the code for the five years and are prosecuted under it like anyone else. No reasons for any provision are published, nobody is answerable for the code's content after the selectors disperse, and there is no institution whose continuing job is to keep the law in touch with social practice. The current code contains the ordinary prohibitions on violence, theft and breach of contract, enforced impartially between private citizens, and it also makes it an offence to practise, without a licence granted by a state church, any religion other than that church's.

The crux I want the question to aim at is this: each of Fuller's eight principles appears to be satisfied, so a list gets you nowhere, and yet it is arguable that the enterprise conception of law that those principles are supposed to serve — a continuing undertaking by a lawgiver who is bound by the rules it addresses to its subjects — has no foothold here, because after the selectors disperse there is nobody left to hold to the undertaking. If that is right, then Fuller's list and Fuller's theory come apart, and the interesting question is whether the disagreement between him and Hart was ever really about the criteria of legal validity at all, or about what kind of thing a legal system is supposed to be. The religious-provision clause is there for a second reason: neither theorist has an obviously distinctively legal complaint about it, and I want candidates to have to say why the structural defect and the substantive defect are not the same complaint.

Please give me, in this order:

1. The question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including any footnote.
2. A model answer of seven to nine hundred words. It should commit to a verdict and defend it, should deal with the strongest reply available to the theorist who loses, and should distinguish Fuller's procedural complaint from any substantive one.
3. A marking scheme out of twenty built around five must-hit moves, with the marks attached to each, and a one-line note after each on how candidates in my position typically get it wrong.
4. A short note on what separates a first-class answer from an upper second, and a list of the moves that should earn no credit at all, however well written.

Finally: I am revising from my own notes and our library catalogue is unavailable to me this week, so please do not direct me to further reading, page references or particular essays, and do not assume I can look anything up. Please write the whole thing in British English — spelling, punctuation and idiom — since that is what the examiners expect of us.
