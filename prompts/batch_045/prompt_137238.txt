I've fallen down a rabbit hole and I need someone to argue me out of (or properly into) a position on it. It started at a family barbecue when my brother-in-law announced that if you "uploaded" your brain into a computer the real you would just die and the thing in the machine would be a copy, and somehow that led me to this old thought experiment by the philosopher Daniel Dennett. Laying out the setup in case you haven't got it memorised: a man's brain is removed from his skull and placed in a vat of nutrient fluid in a lab in one city, kept alive and hooked up by radio to his own body, which is in another city. All the brain's sensory input arrives over the radio from the body's eyes and ears and fingertips, and it sends its movement commands back the same way, and it works seamlessly — the man carries on with his life, chats with people, shakes hands, writes letters, and reports having experiences as of being in the room where the body is standing. The question the whole thing is built around is: where is he? In the city where the body is doing the talking, or in the lab, floating in a jar? Everyone I put this to has an instant intution and then can't really say what it rests on.

What I want is a proper essay — real prose, not a list of bullet points, call it 1200 to 1800 words — that takes a side on whether "where is he?" has a determinate answer here at all, and if it does, what makes it true. State your verdict early, in the first paragraph or two, then spend the rest defending it rather than building up to it. I don't want a survey of the options with a shrug at the end. The candidate answers I keep going back and forth between, which you should take seriously and then argue against if you're rejecting them: he's where his brain is; he's where his body and his agency are, since that's where he acts and gets acted on; he's wherever his conscious experience is happening; the question has no single right answer because it's really several different questions (legal, moral, practical, medical) that happen to get phrased the same way; or the question is just empty and the puzzle is a confusion we should dissolve rather than answer. Whichever way you go, you have to test it against three variants I made up myself, because these are what broke my first answer.

Variant one, the sleeping brain: same setup, except that once the brain is settled in the vat it's put into a deep, dreamless sleep for a week — anaesthetised, whatever — and while it's out, a computer that has been trained on that brain's own response patterns runs the body instead. It runs it so well that the body goes to work, has an argument with its wife, and nobody notices anything. Then the brain is woken up and remembers none of the week. Where was the man during that week? If your answer changes between this case and the original, I want to know exactly which feature did the work — and whether you're now committed to saying the computer-driven body was a different person for seven days. Variant two, the slow move: this time nothing is moved all at once. Small bits of the brain, or if you prefer small clusters of function, are transferred into the vat one at a time over a week, each one replaced in the skull by a tiny synthetic stand-in that does the same job, so the man stays awake and functional throughout and notices nothing. On Monday he's entirely in his skull; on Sunday he's entirely in the vat. If you say he's where his brain is, when exactly did he move cities, and what do you say to someone who claims there's no such moment and hence no fact about where he was on the Thursday? Variant three, the two bodies: one brain in the vat, but now there are two bodies, one in each city, and the brain's outgoing commands are broadcast to both, so they move in perfect lockstep. Only one of them sends sensory signals back to the brain at a time, and a switch can flip which one that is, even in the middle of a sentence. While the switch is set to the Houston body, where is the man? Does your answer change the instant the switch flips? And does it matter that he can only ever see out of one body at a time, or that both bodies are doing exactly what he's trying to do?

A few last things, since I want to be able to actually read this out loud to my brother-in-law. Please don't try to cite papers or quote anybody — I can't check any of it, and I'd rather have your own reasoning than half-remembered quotations. Argue from the cases. Somewhere in there, name the single intuition your verdict leans on hardest and say what would have to be true for you to abandon it. If you end up saying there's no fact of the matter, you owe me an account of why that isn't just giving up, why it doesn't follow that we can say anything we like, and why people argue about it so confidently anyway. And finish with one or two sentences: the strongest thing you'd say to someone who still insists the man is obviously in the other city from the one you picked.
