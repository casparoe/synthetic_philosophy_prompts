I commission history documentaries for a public broadcaster: around a dozen pitches a week, and lately maybe a third of them are alternative-chronology or ancient-astronaut material — Atlantis pinned to a specific site off a sonar anomaly, megaliths attributed to visitors from elsewhere, or the claim that long stretches of the documented past were fabricated by later chroniclers, so that one well-known empire is really a duplicate of another. Each pitch has to be routed to one of three outputs — commission as history, commission with an on-screen contested-history framing, or commission as labelled entertainment — and my written rationale goes to an editorial standards committee that has to defend it to the regulator if a complaint lands. I have about twenty minutes per pitch and no specialist on staff.

I tried operationalising the obvious demarcation toolkit — falsifiability, and the progressive-versus-degenerating contrast for research programmes — and it misfired in both directions at once. The fabricated-chronology pitch scored better than a mainstream-revisionist pitch from a working archaeologist, because the former stated bold checkable predictions and the latter hedged everything in confidence intervals; and a pitch about an apparently anachronistic artifact was flagged purely for contradicting consensus, which is close to how archaeologists embarrassed themselves by dismissing anomalous sites that later turned out to be genuine. So I need something more robust than a falsifiability checklist.

Please write me a decision procedure my producers can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts (publicity, tone, whether a framing is condescending). It should be runnable in twenty minutes by a generalist, and should say what to do when an answer can't be obtained. In particular I want it to handle three things a naive version gets wrong: claims that must rewrite well-attested records — and so owe some account of why the records look the way they do — carry a different burden from claims that merely add an undocumented event; a claim's evidential bar should be tested for drift, i.e. whether it moves after an objection is answered; and the procedure needs an explicit guard against its own conservatism, so that surprise and conflict-with-consensus aren't treated as evidence of crankery.

Then run it on three pitches: one arguing that a familiar empire is a duplicate created by chroniclers, with a stated prediction that no datable layer will be found at a named site between two centuries; one arguing that a megalithic alignment is too precise to be chance and that mainstream archaeology cannot explain the site at all; and one locating Atlantis at a specific place and date, whose proponent has publicly staked his reputation on a sediment core. My own hunch is that the third is the most respectable of the three, since it at least sticks its neck out — if the procedure disagrees, say so and tell me which step kills it.

Finally: which distinctions have to survive the simplification into a checklist and which can safely be dropped, where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment, and what it cannot do at all.
