I would like a case analysis, in British English, of the anomalous perihelion advance of Mercury through the lens of the underdetermination of theory by evidence.

Take these as the given facts. By the late nineteenth century Mercury's perihelion was advancing a few tens of arcseconds per century more than Newtonian theory allowed — about 43 in the later reckoning — and three broad kinds of fix were available: undiscovered matter inside Mercury's orbit, whether a single planet along the lines of the hypothesised Vulcan or a swarm or ring of smaller bodies; a diffuse cloud or ring of dust close to the Sun; and a revision of the gravitational law itself, either a small change to the exponent in the inverse-square relation or, later, the general theory of relativity, which disposed of the anomaly in 1915.

Please keep three readings of underdetermination separate throughout, and tell me which of them, if any, the episode actually instantiates: the temporary or transient thesis, that the evidence available at a time does not decide but later evidence does; the holist thesis, that no hypothesis faces the tribunal of evidence alone and any one can be shielded by adjusting auxiliaries; and the radical or permanent thesis, that rivals exist which no possible evidence could separate.

Run the analysis in both directions. Theory to case: for each of the three fixes, set out the auxiliary commitments it carried, what would have counted as evidence for or against it independently of the Mercury data itself — direct telescopic searches, transits across the solar disc, the zodiacal light, the motions of Venus and the Earth, the Moon's motion — and say whether the underdetermination was genuine or merely apparent, and for whom. Case to theory: say what the episode shows about each of the three readings. Is this a good illustration of holism, or does it rather show holism to be a claim about logical space rather than about scientific practice, given that the auxiliaries which would have shielded Newtonian theory were themselves independently testable and were in fact tested and failed? And does the later vindication of relativity by novel predictions bear on underdetermination at all, or does it only confirm that whatever underdetermination there was had already lapsed?

Where the concept is silent, say so rather than pressing it into service. I would particularly like your verdict on one closing question. Suppose a rival to general relativity that reproduces every gravitational observation available in 1920 — the perihelion advance, light bending, the redshift — while diverging from it in the strong-field regime, and suppose the strong-field evidence does not arrive until the 1970s. Is the situation in 1920 best described as a case of underdetermination whose protagonists were being irrational, a case in which suspension of judgement was the reasonable response, or no interesting underdetermination at all but only a remark about how little data there was?
