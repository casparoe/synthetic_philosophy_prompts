I've been reading the rationalist blogs and their comment sections for something like fifteen years, mostly lurking, and the position I've ended up on in the worst-case versus average-case argument about alignment is one I can defend fine in a comment thread and have never had to defend out loud. Next month I have to: twenty-five minutes plus twenty minutes of questions, at a small invitation-only workshop of about fifteen people. The room is roughly this: four people from formal verification and programming languages who think in terms of proofs over artifacts rather than claims about the world; three from robust control and robotics, who think in terms of bounded disturbance sets, uncertainty sets, and stability margins; two interpretability researchers who are sympathetic to me but not formal; one decision theorist who thinks in expected utility and will be annoyed by anything that smells like new notation for an old idea; and a program officer from a funder who mostly wants to know whether any of this could ever be written into a regulatory standard. My thesis is not that worst-case work is worthless, it's that it is mispriced. Concretely: every safety case has two components that almost never get reported separately — a behavioral claim that holds on some set S of situations, and an implicit estimate of how much of the deployment distribution actually lands in S — and the universal quantifier of a worst-case guarantee is bought either by shrinking S until it describes a system nobody deployed, or by inflating the uncertainty set until the guarantee is vacuous. The alternative I want to push is what I've been calling coverage accounting: report the pair (S, ĉ) explicitly, where ĉ is an estimate of the deployment mass on S with a confidence interval attached, and spend an explicit residual risk budget on the complement — bound the harm you are willing to accept from the uncovered tail, and argue that the tail's mass shrinks faster than the system's capability grows. My slogan is that every universal quantifier hides a coverage estimate. Note that this is deliberately not the kind of marginal-coverage guarantee you get out of conformal prediction: that guarantee is over exchangeable draws from a fixed distribution, and the whole point of the setting I care about is that the distribution is non-stationary and partly endogenous to what the system does. My argument in four steps: (1) worst-case bounds are proved over a model, and the model is always of a smaller system than the deployed one; (2) so the quantifier ranges over a set that is not the deployment set; (3) the gap is currently filled with informal engineering judgment and vibes; (4) it should instead be filled with a number, however bad, that somebody else can audit and argue about.
