I'm writing a novel and I've painted myself into a corner. There's a scene — a card-room grifter I call the Envelope Man runs a little con on a retired actuary: two envelopes, one contains twice what the other does, you pick one, don't open it, and then he offers you the swap. The actuary is supposed to be the smartest person in the room, and I want what she says next to be a real move in an actual argument, not the kind of pseudo-profound line that sounds clever to a reader who's never thought about it. So I need the map before I can write the dialogue.

What I want from you is a taxonomy / decision tree of the positions on the two-envelope puzzle — the version where you have NOT opened your envelope. Not an essay, not a survey of who said what; I need a classification instrument. Start everyone at the same place (one envelope has twice the other, you've picked one, you don't know what's in it, the swap is free, is there a reason to take it?) and branch from there.

How I'd like it built:

First give me the branching questions themselves, before any names. Each node should be a claim or a mini-case someone could answer cold, without knowing which position it leads to — e.g. whether the reason to switch depends on the actual amount in your envelope, whether an expectation over an unbounded range is even well-defined, whether the way the envelopes were filled matters or only what you know about the ratio, whether being indifferent is the same as having no reason, whether a switch you'd want to take twice is a reductio or not. Phrase them as questions a smart reader can say yes or no to. Six to nine nodes, whatever the logical space actually needs.

Then put the real positions at the leaves. I need at least these as leaves, but add more if the space has room: pure symmetry / no reason to switch; the always-switch argument from the 1.25-times-my-amount calculation; switch-and-keep-switching as a reductio of that calculation; a proper-prior Bayesian who switches on some amounts and not others; the view that there's no well-defined expected value here at all so the question doesn't arise; a causal-or-filling-process view where the answer depends entirely on how the trickster chose the amounts; and a finite-bankroll view where the answer flips for the top observed amounts. Give each leaf a name if it has one I'd plausibly have heard of, and if it doesn't, coin a plain descriptive label and say it's your coinage. Don't attribute anything to papers or philosophers — I half-remember that there are several named variants and I'd only garble it, and you can't check offline anyway.

Then, the part I think I'll actually use: for each unoccupied cell in the space — the answer-combinations nobody defends — one line on why nobody's there. Incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unvisited? And flag any pair of leaves your tree can't separate rather than pretending it can; if two positions make the same prediction for every case I could put in a scene, say so and say what would tell them apart.

Two wrinkles specific to my book that the tree has to handle, so it can't just be lifted from an encyclopedia:

1. My Envelope Man has a hard cap — the house can't cover more than $1,024 in either envelope, and the actuary knows that. So the amounts are bounded. One character insists that even with a known finite cap there's still an argument for switching, and I can't tell whether that's a serious position or a dodge. Give it a leaf or tell me it collapses into one you already have.
2. The swap is offered as a button you can press as many times as you like, each press costing a penny, with the envelopes shuffled behind the grifter's back between presses. (He's a showman.) Does that change which leaf you land on, or is it just a vivid way of asking the same question?

Since this is going into dialogue: for each leaf, one sentence of what a character holding it would actually say out loud in the scene, and one sentence of the strongest reply from a different leaf. That's the bit I'm stealing.

Last thing: be honest about your own confidence. Mark which leaves you're solid on and which ones you're reconstructing, and if the whole setup is confused — if there isn't one "the paradox" but a family of puzzles that shouldn't share a taxonomy, or if my cap-and-penny version is a different problem wearing the same hat — say that plainly instead of politely building the thing I asked for. I'd rather find out now than after I've written three scenes on top of it.
