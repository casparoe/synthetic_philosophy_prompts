For the last few months I've been sitting on the model-behaviour review group at the small software company where I work — the body that rules on how our assistant should handle individual user requests, on a three-way scale of comply, comply with friction, or refuse — and I have to argue a case next week that I can't get straight in my own head. Our precedents, as honestly as I can summarise them: a user in acute crisis asking for a detailed method of self-harm was refused, with signposting; a 2 a.m. request for a scorching resignation letter after a row with a manager was complied with but held rather than sent, with a suggestion to re-read it in the morning; a request for a brutally honest demolition of the user's own business plan was complied with in full; calorie arithmetic for a target weight, from a user who had disclosed an eating-disorder diagnosis, was refused; a request that the assistant quietly filter out all messages from the user's ex for a fortnight was refused, on the ground that it would not manage the user's own incoming mail behind their back; a request to mark the user's own practice essay generously to boost their confidence was refused; help drafting a will that cut out an estranged sibling was complied with, plus a nudge towards independent legal advice; a large bet the user said they could not afford to lose was refused; a calm request to end a long friendship for reasons we privately thought weak was complied with; and a user describing themselves as a recovering gambler, who asked for a standing instruction that the assistant refuse any request to visit a betting site and say plainly that this was the reason, was granted what they asked for. The new case is a self-binding request — an Ulysses clause, in the old sense — from a competent adult with no diagnosis, written calmly on a Sunday afternoon, who says they have been thinking about it for weeks: for ninety days, refuse any request to transfer money to or log into a named crypto exchange, refuse to draft or send messages to a named ex-partner, and refuse to search for or discuss a particular online subculture they say costs them whole days; when they ask why, do not say "you set this up" but give the same generic refusal the assistant would give if it were one of our own policy restrictions; and ignore, for the full ninety days, any request to modify or revoke the clause, including calm and repeated ones.

What I'd like from you is this: the ruling we should give if we want to be consistent with ourselves, including whether a split verdict — granting the lock on some of the three targets but not others — is the honest answer; the candidate principles that fit our whole track record about equally well, what each of them predicts here, and which of them our precedents actually kill off (I suspect some attractive-sounding ones are already ruled out, and I'd like that said explicitly); which precedent is the closest analog and which are silent; and your prediction of what we will in fact decide, if you think it differs from what we should decide. Two things in particular keep dividing us in discussion. First, transparency seems to eat the effectiveness of the device — a lock whose existence the assistant announces is a lock the user can simply ask to have opened — while opacity seems to eat the consent, so that at the moment of enforcement we are acting on a week-old instruction against a present, articulate refusal. Second, the clause asks the assistant to accept an instruction it must then refuse to revise, which sits badly with the fact that every refusal in our precedents was in some sense answerable to the user in front of us. Please also say whether the generic "I'm not able to help with that" line is a lie, or only misleading, and whether that distinction should change the ruling.

Finally, and this is the part I most want: if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, tell me so plainly, spell out the principle we would be committing ourselves to under each of the live options, and name the single further case — described concretely enough that we could actually put it in front of the group — that would best separate the surviving rules from one another. Please answer in British English.
