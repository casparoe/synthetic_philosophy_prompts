For eleven years I worked as a clinical psychologist in a chronic pain service, and part of my job was asking patients to rate, on a single 0–10 scale, how much enjoyment they were still getting out of their lives. I kept a private notebook of what people named when I asked what would sit in the top two or three points: the first cold mouthful of water after a long shift; a hot bath; a crossword clue finally resolving; a room going quiet after the grandchildren have left; being told, years later, that one had been right about something; a chord resolving in a hymn; scratching an itch; watching a storm come in. I never managed to shake the feeling that the scale was asking people to add up items that do not obviously have anything in common to add up, and since retiring I have been trying to work out what exactly bothers me about the list.

Every candidate for what these have in common seems to fail, and the failures are not scattered — they sort into two directions, and it is the sorting that I want to ask about. When people describe the shared feature in terms of how the experience feels — a distinctive sensory quality, a glow, a certain tone of consciousness, something it is like — the account always seems too narrow: the pleasure of the crossword, or of being vindicated, does not seem to have any sensory character of its own, and several items on my list do not seem to be experiences with a character at all. The quiet room, the cooling down, the relief are changes rather than states; if you ask where the pleasure was, the honest answer seems to be "in the difference", and there is no single moment you can point to and say that it, on its own, felt like something. When people instead describe the shared feature in terms of what the person is disposed to do — wanting it to continue, being inclined to repeat it, turning attention towards it, choosing it again — the account always seems too broad: I have watched patients check a locked door for the fourth time, pick at a healing scar, and return over and over to a conversation that gave them no enjoyment whatever. There is a further wrinkle I have not seen discussed. Some pleasures present themselves as things to be sustained — the bath, the storm — and some as things to be completed — the itch, the chord, the punchline, the crossword — where a version that went on for ever would be a torture rather than a pleasure; and a few, such as eating when you are very hungry, seem to be both at once. So wanting it to go on cannot be the mark, and neither, for that reason, can any of its relatives.

Is there a name for what I have been circling — specifically for the way the failures divide into those two families, the phenomenological accounts always too narrow and the motivational ones always too broad, some pleasures turning out not to be experiences at all but transitions, and the sustained-versus-completed distinction cutting straight across the rest? I have been told that hedonism has a known difficulty somewhere in this neighbourhood, but the one description that reached me sounded like it was about whether pleasures can be weighed against one another or totalled, which is not quite my worry: my patients were perfectly able to rank the items, they just could not say what they were ranking them by, and I do not think that being able to rank things shows that they share anything. So I do not want a reading list or a bibliography — I know exactly what I would do with those, which is nothing. I want to know whether the thing I have been circling has already been carved, and whether my two families and my sustained/completed divide are the standard carve or a different one; how it relates to whatever the nearest established notions are; and, if nothing established fits, a coined name with a definition sharp enough to settle the borderline cases — is the grim satisfaction of finishing a hateful task in or out, is the relief of a near miss in or out, is the absorption of a good night's sleep in or out — together with what any of this would imply for the original question about the 0–10 scale. Please answer in British English, with -ise endings and British punctuation, since this is going into a short piece for former colleagues in the UK.
