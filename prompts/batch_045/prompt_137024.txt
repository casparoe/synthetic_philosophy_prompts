I'm writing a novel — mostly set in a hospital records office, one of those slow-burn things where not much happens except to a person's confidence — and one of my characters is a clinical coding auditor who has, over a couple of years, quietly become the person who checks what the model produced rather than the person who does the coding. The arc I want is that she slowly realizes she can no longer tell whether the output is any good, even though she's still nominally the one signing off on it. While poking around for background I read a long-form essay on AI and knowledge that someone in my writing group recommended, and I'd like to use it as the skeleton for her internal argument, but I can't tell what's solid and what the writer oversold, and I'd hate to build a whole book on something a philosopher would wince at.

The essay's central move, more or less in its own words: "Here's the thing about asking a model: you are outsourcing not just the answer but your grip on the question. Understanding means being able to produce the explanation yourself and defend it if pressed; if you got it from a system, what you have is at best a true belief you can't back up, and true belief isn't knowledge — so every time you ask, you trade knowledge for something lesser, and you do it in exchange for convenience. The psychology already confirms it: when people know they can look something up later, they stop storing it, so memory is measurably atrophying, and understanding will go exactly the same way. In one sense this is just the ordinary business of trusting experts, which is fine when the expert is reliable; but a model isn't a person, so there is nobody there to trust, and depending on it is pure loss with nothing on the other side of the ledger. The remedy follows: use these things less, and when you have to use them, make them show their work, since a chain of reasoning you can follow gives back the understanding the shortcut took away."

That last sentence is the bit I'm most suspicious of, because in the scene I keep circling back to my character does read the model's step-by-step reasoning every single time, and it makes her feel better while she sails right past the one mistake that actually matters. I'm sure "showing the work" doesn't fix it, but I can't say why without hand-waving. My own half-formed idea — which I'd rather you stress-test than agree with politely — is that generating and checking are separate capacities and dependence eats the checking one first, invisibly, because the output stays equally fluent whether or not it's right, so the loss doesn't announce itself. Alongside that I keep coming back to an asymmetry: some tasks are much easier to check than to do (run the code, read the translated sentence back) and some are harder to check than to do (is this summary faithful, does this clause actually cover this case), and the model's tone is identical in both, so we smuggle the confidence we earned in the easy cases into the hard ones. Verification asymmetry, if you want a name for it. Is that a real distinction or am I reinventing something that already has a proper name?

So, concretely: could you go through the essay's claims one at a time and tell me which a philosopher working on this would accept, which are overstatements of a more modest and more defensible thesis, and which are flat wrong? I especially want (a) whether "you can't produce the explanation yourself" really shows you lack knowledge, as opposed to lacking some independent grip on it — I've heard experts' testimony defended as a perfectly rational basis for knowledge and I can't see what's supposed to be different here, (b) whether the memory experiments show what the essay says or something narrower that the writer inflated, and (c) whether the "there's nobody to trust" point is the strongest link in the chain or the weakest. And if you think there's a better version of the worry than the essay's — the one my character should actually be haunted by — give me that too, ideally in a form I can put inside a person's head rather than in an argument.
