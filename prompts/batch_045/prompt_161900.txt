I'm on the distributional review panel at a big regional transit authority — we screen every service and fare change before it goes to the board, so three or four proposals a month, decided in about two working days by me and two analysts who have no philosophy or econ training, and whatever we write ends up in the public packet that the board, and occasionally a judge or a reporter, actually reads. So it can't just say "the aggregate benefit outweighs the aggregate burden"; it has to show the work. The thing we keep deadlocking on is basically that old torture-vs-dust-specks hypothetical, except with bus stops: is a change that adds something genuinely trivial to an enormous number of people — 90 seconds to 400,000 daily trips, a fare system that rounds up and overcharges about three cents a ride, a platform relocation that makes 60,000 people walk an extra ninety feet — even comparable to a change that lands something severe on a small number of people, like ending door-to-door paratransit for forty wheelchair users or closing a station that twelve elderly residents depend on entirely? Half the office says obviously the millions of specks win on arithmetic, the other half says obviously you can't add up dust specks, and I dont want to settle it by whoever talks loudest.

What I want is a procedure we could actually run on a proposal, not an essay about aggregation. Concretely:

- the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just returns a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts;
- a rule for the unit of aggregation — per person, per trip, per person-minute? — because multiplying 90 seconds by 400,000 trips gives ten thousand hours, while dividing it among people who each lose a minute and a half gives roughly nothing, and I suspect whether the specks count at all depends entirely on which of those we pick;
- a rule for burdens below the threshold of anyone noticing: a one-in-ten-million increase in lifetime cancer risk from some new cleaning solvent, a three-cent rounding error nobody detects, a delay shorter than anyone registers. Do these contribute their expected value, contribute nothing, or is that exactly the value call I have to make out loud?;
- whether it matters if the trivial burdens pile up on the same people repeatedly or are spread thinly across different ones (our fare change hits the same daily commuters over and over; the platform move hits each rider once or twice);
- how to treat a policy that certainly imposes a serious burden on five identifiable people versus one that imposes a one-in-a-million chance of that same burden on five million people — about five expected people, but nobody identifiable in advance. Does the procedure work in expected value and then shrug at what actually happens, and is that defensible in a public document?;
- what to do when we simply don't have a number, which is most of the time — a rough count, or a count from a five-year-old survey — and how to reason with bounds instead.

Then please run it on three cases: (1) the fare-rounding overcharge, roughly three cents across ninety million rides a year, which everyone here has always treated as paperwork noise; (2) a station consolidation that saves the agency about $600k a year and forces twelve elderly residents with no alternative to add 1.2 miles to every trip; (3) a scheduling change that adds ninety seconds to 400,000 daily trips but gets eight maintenance workers off a genuinely miserable 2am shift. I have a sinking feeling the procedure is going to tell us the rounding thing is the biggest item on the ledger — say so plainly if that's what falls out, I'd rather hear it now. Also be straight about which steps are judgment dressed up as arithmetic, because the board will treat anything with a number in it as objective and I need to know where I'm smuggling in a value choice, and flag the cases the procedure genuinely can't settle so we know to escalate them instead of pretending.
