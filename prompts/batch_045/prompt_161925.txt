I sit on a small committee that allocates a modest philanthropic budget between near-term global-health giving and far-future, existential-risk-focused work. A colleague has been arguing that the Simulation Argument, taken seriously as a claim about what we are, should move us substantially toward the near-term bucket. I cannot tell whether he has found a genuine defeater for longtermist fanaticism or is merely exploiting the fact that any consideration that touches astronomical numbers looks like a defeater. I want a number and the machinery that produces it.

Here is the proposition I want your credence in, stated as precisely as I can manage.

P: An agent who (i) accepts a Bostrom-style simulation argument well enough to assign nontrivial credence to most observers with our evidence being simulated, and who (ii) would otherwise accept longtermist fanaticism — that is, would otherwise allocate the large majority of their altruistic resources to shaping the very long-run future, and would otherwise grant that small probabilities of astronomically large payoffs legitimately dominate expected-value calculations — should, on account of (i) alone rather than on account of any independent worry about fanaticism, mark down the expected value they assign to far-future interventions by a large factor relative to what they would assign if they set the simulation question aside.

My own credence in P is about 0.3; my colleague's is about 0.75. Give me yours, decomposed over the following, with a number and one line of justification for each, then the combination.

A. The base rate. How much credence do you give the simulation conclusion itself, and how is that credence distributed over which kind of simulation we would be in — a short, purpose-built, low-fidelity run versus something open-ended and expensive? I am at roughly 0.15, my colleague closer to 0.4.

B. Truncation. Conditional on our being simulated, what is the probability that our world's accessible future is drastically shorter, smaller, or less populous than the open-ended future a non-simulated civilization at our stage could have? The natural thought is that simulations are run for purposes and terminated when the purpose is served, which makes any random point in a simulation likely to be near its end.

C. Upward leakage. By this I mean any route by which events inside a simulation make a difference to the long-run value of the containing world — the simulators drawing conclusions from what they observe, being influenced by it, or copying outcomes outward. If leakage is even modest, truncation deflates the stakes less than it appears to. Give a probability that leakage is substantial enough to matter.

D. Copy coupling. If simulated worlds are numerous and near-duplicate, then under one view of counterfactuals my choice affects only my own copy, while under evidential or functional views my decision procedure is correlated with, or settles, what the other copies do — in which case the stakes are multiplied by the number of copies rather than divided by the truncation factor. How much of the deflationary conclusion depends on which of these views is correct, and how confident are you that the deflation survives the non-causal ones?

E. Self-undermining. Our beliefs about what simulations are like — how long they run, how cheap they are, how many there are — are themselves formed inside the candidate simulation. If a simulant's beliefs about its container are unreliable, then the truncation estimate in B is unreliable too, and the deflationary argument may partly saw off the branch it sits on. How much probability do you leave for that?

F. Independence from general anti-fanaticism. How much of the deflationary force is genuinely contributed by the simulation consideration, as opposed to piggybacking on standing skepticism about expected-value reasoning with astronomical stakes? If someone already rejects fanaticism, the simulation argument adds nothing; I want to know how much it adds for someone who does not.

G. The target of concern. If simulated observers vastly outnumber non-simulated ones, then the futures that dominate the expected-value calculation may already have been simulated futures all along, so that learning we are simulated changes the size of the object of concern less than it seems. How much does this consideration do?

Three further things. First, tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and which term you are least confident about. Second, if my decomposition is misspecified — if two of these terms are not independent, if one double-counts another, or if a term that matters more than any of them is missing — say so and give me your own decomposition instead. Third, where does the disagreement between my 0.3 and my colleague's 0.75 most likely live: is it a disagreement about the base rate, about truncation, about decision theory, or about whether the simulation consideration is doing any work at all?

Finally, be explicit about the epistemic status of your answer. Tell me which single argument or consideration, if I could settle it, would most change your verdict on P, and identify the place where your own reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong — the step you would inspect first if you later found out you were mistaken.
