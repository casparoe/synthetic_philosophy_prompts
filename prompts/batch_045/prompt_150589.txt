i'm a PhD student in comparative memory studies — my department is half history, half political theory — and i'm putting together a chapter that runs four cases side by side: West German Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan and the Asia-Pacific war, Turkey and the Armenian catastrophe of 1915 plus the imperial collapse around it, and the American South on slavery and the Lost Cause. What keeps happening, in seminars, in the secondary literature, and honestly in my own drafts, is that everyone reaches for therapy vocabulary: repression, denial, working-through, the famous diagnosis of an inability to mourn, a nation that hasn't processed its trauma, a patient who either has or hasn't recovered. My supervisor leans on it too — she'll say Germany has done its mourning and Japan is stuck — and once you say it that way the metaphor starts carrying conclusions: that silence is a symptom, that public speech is the cure, that there's a healthy end state called coming to terms, and that the whole point of the exercise is the perpetrator society getting well again. I have a workshop in two weeks and I want to keep using it only if I can say what it's actually licensed to do.

So: audit it properly. Start with the correspondence table — patient, analyst, the original trauma, the symptom, the cure, resistance, relapse, termination of therapy — and tell me who or what plays each role in the German case, since that's the one the metaphor was built on. Then the disanalogies, and please split them into the ones that matter for the work I just described versus the ones that don't. The ones that feel load-bearing to me: there's no single mind and no unconscious, so repression is a picture rather than a mechanism; the patient isn't the same entity as the one who did the deed, because the generations turn over and the grandchildren inherit a story rather than an act; the party who is supposed to get better is the perpetrator side, but whether the cure has happened seems to depend on the victim side — uptake, or something like acceptance — which no clinical model has a slot for; and therapy has an endpoint and a built-in notion of health, whereas the German fight over normalization suggests the endpoint is exactly what's contested, not what the cure aims at. Also: diagnosis carries an authority with it. Who gets to say that a country is in denial, and on what standing?

The bit I really can't get straight is whether there's a better model for the same job. I keep toying with a legal one — a charge, contested evidence, a statute of limitations, verdict and sentence versus settlement and plea — and with inheritance or estate administration: you didn't contract the debt, you inherited the estate, you can administer it badly or disclaim it, and heirs have duties they never chose. Do either of those dodge the problems above, or do they just smuggle in a different normativity? My hunch is that the legal one re-centers the victim and the obligation where the therapeutic one re-centers the perpetrator's health, and that this is most of the difference between them — but I'd like that checked rather than confirmed. And separately: does the melancholia-versus-mourning distinction fit the South and Turkey better than it fits Germany and Japan? In those two, what's lost is a lost cause or a lost empire, something the group stays identified with rather than something it lets go of, which reads more like the pathological case than like unfinished grief. Give me a verdict on which of the four cases the therapy model fits least badly and where it does real damage. No reading list, please — I'll chase the sources myself, I just need the argument.
