Here is why I am asking. About a year ago I joined the quality-improvement committee of a regional hospital network. We were comparing two care pathways for the same condition, and the numbers did the famous thing: across all patients the newer pathway had the lower complication rate, but within every severity band the older pathway did better. The committee split instantly and along lines that had nothing to do with the medicine. One group said the aggregate is the number that matters because that is what we actually deliver to the population we serve. Another said the stratified numbers are the real comparison and the aggregate is an artefact of who got sent where. A third, smaller group pointed out that our severity score is recorded *after* patients are assigned to a pathway, so severity is partly downstream of the intervention, and that this makes both numbers suspect in different ways. We never resolved it; we published the aggregate with a footnote.

I then went looking for the literature, expecting to find the answer, and instead found a fight that has not moved in decades. Statisticians and epidemiologists tend to say there is no paradox at all: it is confounding, standardize or adjust and the problem dissolves, and the only interesting content is a warning about collapsing tables. The causal-graph and do-calculus tradition says something stronger — that once you ask for the effect of an intervention rather than a conditional probability, the reversal is not a feature of the world but of a badly posed query. And decision theorists treat the same examples as evidence about rational choice, connecting them to Savage's sure-thing principle and to the causal-versus-evidential decision theory dispute. Meanwhile applied papers keep being published as though none of this settled anything, and the two canonical examples keep being wheeled out as if they were the same kind of case when they seem to me to be different kinds of case.
