hi — I write novels, not philosophy, and next month my publisher has volunteered me to interview a philosopher of mind on stage at a literary festival (long story, the audience will be mostly other writers). The guest is someone who's defended the symbol grounding problem for years and who, annoyingly for my purposes, has lately been arguing that large language models don't refute the argument at all — they're the cleanest illustration of it anyone's ever produced. That's awkward for me, because my new book is narrated by a text-trained AI and I'm trying to make it feel like the thing genuinely understands what it's saying, and I'd definately rather find out now if the whole premise is something a philosopher will quietly laugh at.

So: draft me eight to ten interview questions, each with a short note on the answer she's most likely to give and then a follow-up aimed at that specific answer. Things I need covered:

- the dictionary problem — why a chain of definitions can't get you meaning on its own. She'll say something about symbols just going round in a circle, and I need the follow-up for when someone points out that real language isn't only definitions: words are also tied to each other by use, by inference, by the practices of a community. Is that an exit from the circle or just a much bigger circle?
- how much grounding is enough. Is there a threshold, and who sets it? I want to ask her about a system that has all the text plus one lousy sensor — a light meter, say — and whether the meter does any grounding work at all, and if not, why not.
- abstract words. "Justice", "if", "because" don't obviously cash out into sensorimotor categories. The standard move is that you ground a base of concrete categories and define everything else off that base. If that's right, then most of what I understand is ungrounded and I understand it fine — so what exactly is meant to be broken about the machine? Please phrase that as a question, not as me picking a fight from the stage.
- the bit I keep circling back to: there's a subplot in the novel where a woman blind from birth argues with the AI about color words. Everything she knows about red came from other people's testimony, and nobody wants to conclude she doesn't know what red means. Either she's grounded somehow, in which case testimony and social scaffolding count — or she isn't, in which case grounding looks like the wrong requirement. Ask her that, and give me the follow-up for whichever way she jumps. My bet is she says the woman inherits grounding from her linguistic community, and then I need to know why the AI, which inherits everything from a linguistic community too, doesn't inherit it.
- whether the argument is really about meaning or really about consciousness. She'll say semantics, not phenomenology, and that the hard problem is another department. But the argument gets all its force from the feeling that the system has no idea what it's talking about, and I want one question that makes her say whether that feeling is doing any actual work in the argument or is just decoration.
- whether a simulated body inside a simulated world would count. There's a scene where the AI gets dropped into a game engine, and I need to know if I'm cheating.
- what would count against her. If a text-only system started doing cross-modal things — describing scenes accurately enough for a sighted person to draw them, steering a robot arm from language alone — would that move her, or is there always an answer available that leaves the view untouched? I want to know whether she has a falsifier or whether she's got an unfalsifiable one.
- one softer question where she gets to say what she'd want a novelist to get right: how would a genuinely ungrounded system narrate its own uncertainty, without me falling into the "and then it saw a sunset and finally understood red" cliché.

Keep the questions phrased so a room of novelists can follow them without me having to define terms out loud, but don't thin out the philosophy — I'd rather look slightly lost on stage than have her answer "well, that's not quite the argument" to every single one. Also: she has a habit of saying "that's an engineering problem, not a philosophical one." If you can build one question where that answer stops working, please do, that would be my favourite moment of the hour.
