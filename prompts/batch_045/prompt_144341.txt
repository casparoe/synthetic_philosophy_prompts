I'm a senior taking a philosophy elective at my school, and we spent a class on Molyneux's question — the puzzle about someone blind from birth who has learned, purely by touch, to tell a cube from a sphere, and about whether he would be able to say which was which just by looking if his sight were suddenly restored. I'm writing my final paper for the course on this, and I honestly don't understand why it's still treated as an open philosophical problem instead of as a question for ophthalmologists. I've seen it claimed in a few places that people whose sight has been restored after cataract surgery can't do this at first, so isn't the answer just no? And if the answer is no, doesn't that simply settle things in favor of the empiricists: we aren't born with any notion of shape, and everything we know about space has to come from experience? My teacher said that the empirical findings don't settle the philosophical question, which sounded to me like a way of avoiding the answer, and I'd like to understand what she could have meant by it.

She also gave us a variant that I can't stop thinking about, which made me suspect I'm the one missing something. Suppose a person had one eye that was blind from birth because of a cataract and one perfectly good eye, so that he has had completely normal visual experience his entire life but has never received any visual input through the blind eye. If the cataract in that eye is removed, would he be able to tell the cube from the sphere using only the newly opened eye? I said the answer has to be the same as in the original case, because in both cases the eye being used has never seen anything before, and nothing about having another working eye seems relevant. My teacher said the two cases might come apart but wouldn't say why until we hand in the paper. Is she right that they could differ, and if so, what does that difference show about the original question — and about why anyone still argues about it rather than just running the experiment?
