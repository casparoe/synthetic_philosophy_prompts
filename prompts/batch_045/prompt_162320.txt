I'm 71, retired from a materials lab where I spent most of thirty-odd years doing powder diffraction on doped oxides and arguing with furnaces. Since I stopped I've been going to a reading group at the local uni and picking at a bit of philosphy of science on my own, and I've got slightly obsessed with the reduction and emergence business. I can never remember who argues for what, so please don't give me a list of names unless a name really carries weight — I want the ideas, not the map of who said them.

Here's where I've got to. It seems to me people use "emergent" for at least three different things and then talk past each other:

(a) stuff that in principle follows from the micro-story but that nobody can actually get out of it — the simulation is too expensive, you'd need to know too much, the error bars eat you alive. Emergence as a fact about us, not about the world.
(b) stuff that's robust across wildly different micro-stories: you change the details at the bottom and the macro law doesn't budge. This is why I could predict things about compositions I'd never measured, and it always felt like more than book-keeping.
(c) the one I can't shake. As I understand it a sharp phase transition strictly speaking doesn't occur in a finite system — you need the infinite-size limit for the singularity to be there at all. Which means there is no finite micro-configuration for the transition to be identical to, so it isn't just hard to derive, there's nothing obvious for it to be. I genuinely can't tell if its a deep point or a philosopher's trick.

My hunch, which I can't defend, is that most of what my old field called emergence was really (a) wearing a metaphysical hat, and that (b) is just good phenomenology. But I'd like that hunch tested rather than flattered.

I've also got one idea of my own that I've started calling leaky reduction, and I want it in the story: the derivation from the micro model to the macro law works, but somewhere in the set-up — the ensemble you picked, the boundary conditions, the fact that you already knew the phase was there and so knew where to look — you've quietly imported a macro-level assumption. So the reduction succeeds, but only by smuggling in what it was supposed to derive. I've no idea whether anyone has said this properly.

What I'd like is a short story, 1200–1800 words, that puts this to work instead of explaining it.

Set it somewhere I'd recognise — a beamline at four in the morning, a group meeting, a grant panel, a lab with one instrument everybody's afraid of. No allegory: no one called Dr. Whole or Ms. Part. Give me people with their own stakes. Say, a senior person whose whole career rests on a phenomenological model, a junior person who does everything bottom-up on a cluster, and a technician who's the only one who knows what the machine actually does.

The plot should turn on the crux. There has to be a decision — whether to pull a paper, whether to put the group's remaining money behind the bottom-up programme, whether to take a job — that would plausibly go the other way if you thought a different one of (a), (b), (c) was the right account of the case. Concretely: let the macro model predict something, let the simulation reproduce it only after the infinite-size extrapolation (the finite runs never show it), and then let somebody notice that the same macro prediction is also being used to pin down a parameter in the micro model, so the dependence seems to run both ways and it isn't clear which level is the "fundamental" one. Don't settle it. I want to feel the pull of both readings.

Two more things. Don't write to my hunch: give the senior character better arguments than the ones I've managed to come up with, and show me what would have to happen in that lab for me to change my mind. And no character may give a little speech explaining the philosophy — if I can tell which side you're on in the last paragraph, it's gone wrong. If you invent an instrument or a material, fine, but say so in the note. And please get the lab detail right where it matters; if you can't, put the scene somewhere where nothing technical has to be named. I will spot a fake diffractometer.

After the story, 250–300 words: (i) which of the claims the story leans on are actually settled among people who work on this, and which are contested — I want the contested ones labelled as contested, not quietly presented as what everyone thinks; (ii) where the story takes a side or leans on a reading a decent chunk of the field would reject, including anything you did with my leaky reduction idea; (iii) anything you're guessing at or that you'd expect a specialist to roll their eyes at. No references or further reading — I can't chase them and I probably wouldn't read them anyway.
