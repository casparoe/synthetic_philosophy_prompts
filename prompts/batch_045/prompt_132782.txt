i keep starting papers on anthropic reasoning and then abandoning them, because i can never tell whether a proposed theory is actually solving the problem or just gerrymandered to kill a conclusion somebody didn't like. So before i read any more of it: give me the spec.

Purpose, so that adequacy has something to be relative to: i want a theory of self-locating / observation-selection reasoning that's good enough to plug into a real decision — specifically, to feed an expected-value calculation about how likely it is that humanity has a very long future, the kind of calculation people use to argue that reducing existential risk is astronomically valuable. Good enough to bet money on, not just good enough to defend for an hour in a seminar.

Deliverable: the list of requirements. For each one, the case or consideration that motivates it, and whether you'd call it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Cases i already want on the list, with the worry attached:

- Sleeping Beauty, plus the further question of whether the theory's verdict there is even the same object as the thing that should guide her betting.
- the doomsday argument itself, plus the meta-question of whether "it lands a doomsday shift" is a cost or a requirement, and the related one: if adopting a theory now makes you expect to abandon it after the next birth, is that a defect?
- fine-tuning versus the presumptuous-philosopher move. Some people think getting an a priori boost for big universes is the theory doing its job; others think it's a reductio. Which is it?
- Boltzmann brains. The evidence there is subjectively identical to yours, so the theory has to say why the inference doesn't go through, without just stipulating that they don't count as observers.
- two i made up and genuinely can't tell are real desiderata or just pet peeves, so adjudicate them and kill either one if it's confused: (1) vague-boundary sensitivity — if the reference class is picked out with a vague predicate, tiny changes in where you draw the line shouldn't produce huge swings in the resulting credence, the way they do if you move chimpanzees, or a slightly demented version of yourself, in and out of the class; (2) regularization independence — in an infinite universe where every observer-type occurs infinitely often, the answer shouldn't depend on which limiting procedure or measure you use, and if in fact every available theory's answer does depend on that, then say so as a result about the desiderata themselves rather than hiding it.

Also flag anything big i've left off. And the part i most want: which subset of the requirements can't all be satisfied together, with the argument for why — i'd rather discover the list is jointly unsatisfiable than get a tidy list. And which single requirement is doing most of the work, i.e. knocks out the most contenders?

Then, briefly — a table, six or seven rows is plenty — score self-sampling, self-indication, full non-indexical conditioning, and the "nothing special here, just conditionalize on the centered evidence" view. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are where i want the thinking.
