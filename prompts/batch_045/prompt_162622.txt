Design a case in which a piece of already-known evidence supports a new theory through theory structure rather than through likelihood, and show that it does.

Constraints on the case. Set it in an ordinary empirical investigation — an epidemiological inquiry or a reliability-engineering failure analysis, say — with agents no more capable than careful working scientists: no logically omniscient Bayesian, no demon, no prior supplied by fiat. The evidence E must be a statistical regularity already known and effectively certain before the new theory T is proposed. T must be genuinely novel in the demanding sense that its central quantities were not expressible in the old theoretical language, so that its prior cannot be inherited from anything already on the books. T must have no confirmed novel predictions: old evidence is all the empirical support it has. Its live rival must be a package of the standing theory R together with an auxiliary A, where A was introduced for reasons independent of E but does in fact accommodate E — arranged so that the likelihood of E comes out roughly equal under T and under R∧A, as a consequence of A's independent motivation rather than by stipulation. And the T-to-E connection must be probabilistic, so that deductive bootstrapping cannot get started: no instance of T is deducible from T together with E alone.

What I want back. First the scenario, in one paragraph. Then the check, derived rather than asserted: why ordinary conditionalization at the moment T appears gives T no boost; why the hypothetical-prior fix — re-running the update on a prior from which E has been deleted, the move associated with Garber — also gives no boost, given the tied likelihoods; why Glymour-style bootstrapping is unavailable here. Then the hard part: the episode must be one in which the scientist who cites E as a reason to prefer T over R∧A is not merely confused. Locate where the support actually lives — in a prior over theoretical packages that charges R∧A for its extra machinery, in a unificationist or explanationist relation between T and E that is not a likelihood relation, or somewhere else — and derive the verdict from the account you choose rather than stipulating it. Close with the diagnosis: which feature of the case does the work, what happens if that feature is removed (if T needs an auxiliary of its own, or if A is dropped and R alone faces T, where the marginal likelihood of E under R is lower), and how the defender of the account that comes out worst — the strict conditionalizer, or the likelihoodist who denies that parsimony and unification are evidentially relevant — would attack the case. The point of the case is to settle whether the problem of old evidence is really about how old evidence bears on a theory's likelihood, or about how theories get assessed structurally when no new prediction is in the offing; a case that simply stipulates that E confirms T, or that hides the work inside an unexplained prior, does not settle it.
