Here's where I've ended up. I've more or less talked myself into a fairly strong response-dependent reading of Strawson's reactive-attitudes material. Roughly: for someone to be morally responsible for something is for reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, guilt, the whole family — to be warranted toward them on account of it, where "warranted" bottoms out in the attitudes themselves rather than in consequences, in what's fair given the agent's history, or in some metaphysically loaded "could have done otherwise." Whatever fills the warranted slot has to be spelled out from inside the practice, not by something standing outside it. I also want the strong inescapability claim: we can't step outside the participant stance wholesale and still be doing anything recognizably like interpersonal morality, so the question "is the practice justified, from outside?" isn't one that gets a yes or a no.

What I want from you is what would count against *that* version specifically, not against some cartoon where Strawson is just saying responsibility is whatever people happen to feel. A few things I want you to deal with, and please add ones I haven't thought of:

- There can be reasons for an attitude that have nothing to do with what makes it fitting — say a demon will punish you unless you feel gratitude, or you have excellent prudential reasons to work up some resentment even though the person did nothing wrong. If that's right, the view can't just read warranted-ness off the attitudes, and it needs a seperate story about which reasons are the relevant kind. Does supplying that story quietly turn it back into a quality-of-will theory, or into the kind of view it was supposed to replace?

We resent people partly *because* we take them to be responsible agents, so defining responsibility in terms of the attitudes looks circular.
