I've been reading the alignment literature for years — the old MIRI-flavored material on security mindset and worst-case reasoning, and the newer stuff on evals, control, and scalable oversight — and I keep getting stuck on a mismatch between what that literature says it's doing and what it actually does. The rhetoric is worst-case: alignment is a security problem, adversarial optimization finds the failure you didn't test for, you don't get to assume the distribution of inputs is friendly, and so on. The practice is almost entirely average-case: sample a benchmark of prompts or environments, measure a failure rate, red-team until the rate goes down, extrapolate to deployment. Everyone gestures at the gap; I haven't seen anyone say precisely what would have to be true of a deployment setting for the average-case evidence to carry the same decision-theoretic weight that a worst-case guarantee would carry.

My half-formed idea is that what licenses average-case reasoning isn't optimism about the world, it's a structural feature of the setting: you get many independent, cheap, *recoverable* trials, so mistakes are informative and the law of large numbers does real work. In ordinary ML that's just true — i.i.d. draws, errors are survivable, you can run the experiment again. In a one-shot deployment with irreversible failure, even a perfectly finite expected loss may be unestimable from any data gatherable beforehand, and unlearnable from afterwards; the failure mode isn't "high variance," it's the absence of the repeatability that makes averages meaningful at all. If that's right, the interesting question isn't "worst-case or average-case?" but "which of the missing structural features can be *manufactured?" — staged autonomy and capability ramping as a way of getting many small trials instead of one big one, tripwires and interruptibility as a way of buying recoverability, honeypots and model organisms as substitute trials, tail-index bounds or 'guarantee conditional on not having taken an irreversible action' as ways of getting a quantile-style guarantee instead of a mean. I also want the reverse direction taken seriously: maybe insisting on worst-case has its own pathology (paralysis, capability taxes that push deployment to worse actors), and maybe heavy tails cut against the worst-casers too.
