I'm retired now — forty-odd years in experimental geophysics, mostly seismic inversion, which is a fancy way of saying I spent my career deciding what was down there from wiggles I could actually record. That habit is probably why I keep getting stuck on the same point in the philosophy reading group I've joined at the university. There's a retired astronomer there, Frank, who calls himself a relationist about spacetime, and I've been putting cases to him over coffee for a few months and I honestly can't tell what rule he's running. His verdicts so far:

- Two ships drifting past each other in otherwise empty space, each saying the other is the one moving: no fact of the matter.
- The whole material universe shifted three metres east, or started up three minutes later, with every internal relation unchanged (that's Leibniz's move, I think): no difference, same world, no fact about where or when it is absolutely.
- The hole-argument pair — two models of a collapsing star that agree everywhere outside some empty region and inside it differ only by a smooth relabelling of which point is which: no difference, there's nothing to decide about which point is which.
- The doubling story (Poincaré's, I believe) — an infinite, spatially flat universe where overnight everything, rulers included, doubles in size: no difference. He did mutter that he wasn't sure whether it matters that the universe was infinite in that one, and he never did get back to me about the compact version.
- Newton's bucket with the rest of the universe deleted: there is a fact of the matter about whether the water is spinning, the surface goes concave and that's the end of it, no other matter required. He's definately not a Machian about rotation.
- Whether there are galaxies out past our particle horizon: yes, there's a fact, we just can't get at it.

That last one is what makes me suspicious that no-possible-observation-could-tell-the-difference isn't really his test, whatever he says it is.

The case I want to put to him next: a universe with no matter in it at all, ever. A vacuum solution, but not a flat one — there is a pulse of gravitational radiation crossing it, with nothing needed to source it, since the vacuum equations permit waves on their own. Compare it with a universe that is the same in every respect except perfectly flat and empty. Is there a fact of the matter about whether the pulse is there, or are these the same possibility described two ways? And if he says there is a fact that there is a pulse somewhere, would he also say there is a fact about where it is — because given what he said about the hole case I'd have thought that was a seperate question.

So: what will Frank say, what rule does he seem to be running, and what other rules fit those six verdicts just as well? Which of those rules come apart on this case, what does each of them predict, and which one is really his, with your reason for preferring it? Then tell me the case to put to him after that to settle it. My candidates are the compact version of the doubling one he ducked; the same question in a universe with twice as much matter in it, all of it forever beyond everybody's horizon; and a single point particle accelerating alone in an empty universe rather than a whole planet. Pick one, or tell me something better.

For what it's worth, my own instinct is that if nothing, anywhere, ever, could even in principle register the difference, then it isn't a physical difference, and I'd like to know whether that instinct survives the rest of what he's already committed to.

Give me your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, and please don't restate the question back at me.
