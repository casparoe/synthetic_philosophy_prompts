You are a philosopher of law and constitutional theorist who also knows the formal literature on self-reference in normative systems. Think carefully before answering: start with a short unstructured brainstorm on what an analogy of this sort can and cannot establish, then give me the audit.

In a reading group on constituent power we keep settling a dispute with two competing pictures, applied to a test case we have been calling the Escalator. A constitution's amendment rule provides: (i) any provision may be amended by a legislative supermajority; (ii) each amendment enacted raises the supermajority required for every later amendment by five percentage points; (iii) the amendment rule itself may be amended, but only under the threshold in force at the time. One of us reads the Escalator through Theseus' ship: every plank is replaced by a procedure the ship itself supplies, so the constitution survives unlimited self-amendment, can never legally abolish itself, and the rising threshold is self-validating all the way up. Another reads it through the game whose rulebook contains a rule letting a majority of players change any rule: keep voting and eventually there is no game left, so the power to amend cannot coherently include the power to abolish the power to amend, and the Escalator is self-undermining.

Audit both analogies against this test case. Give me the correspondence table for each — for the ship, what plays the ship, the planks, the shipwright, the identity of the ship and the harbor; for the game, what plays the rules, the players, the game itself, the umpire, and the moment at which the game ends. Then separate the disanalogies that matter here from those that do not: note, among other things, that the ship picture was built to answer a question about identity over time rather than about the validity of a change, and that the game picture may be quietly importing an external umpire or a stock of invariant constitutive rules that a legal order need not have. Tell me whether either analogy can actually carry the conclusion its user wants about the Escalator, and whether a third comparison — a self-modifying program, a compiler bootstrapping the language it is written in, an agent binding its own future self — does better. If neither picture settles the question, say what the Escalator really turns on.
