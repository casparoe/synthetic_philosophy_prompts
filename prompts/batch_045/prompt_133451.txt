I'm giving a paper at a departmental colloquium next week — a forty-five-minute talk with a twenty-minute question period — and I need a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. The audience is about fifteen people: two testimony theorists, one of whom is reductionist-leaning and thinks a hearer's warrant for what she is told is always built out of her own evidence, the other anti-reductionist and happy to posit a default entitlement to believe what you are told; a speech-act theorist who holds that asserting is essentially undertaking a commitment backed by the speaker's own reasons; two formal epistemologists who would much rather talk about calibrated credences, scoring rules, and expected accuracy than about knowledge; a legal epistemologist who works on expert witnesses and hearsay; a philosopher of language who works on non-literal and not-at-issue content and is generally suspicious of tidy speech-act taxonomies; a political philosopher interested in institutional and official speech; and a handful of generalists, one of whom read only the abstract and is already annoyed that the paper opens with a language-model example and never returns to it. Nobody in the room works on AI.

Here is the thesis. The standard debate — knowledge norm versus truth norm versus reasonable-belief norm — has been conducted entirely on the case where the speaker speaks in her own voice, and I argue that this is the degenerate case. I introduce the category of proxy assertion: a speaker S asserts on behalf of a principal P when S presents a proposition as something P stands behind (press secretaries, lawyers for clients, translators, corporate accounts, a colleague reading a lab's agreed statement into a meeting). I claim that proxy assertion is governed by a three-part delegated norm rather than by any of the standard candidates: a content condition, that the proposition lie inside what I call P's transmissible store — not P's beliefs, but the propositions P has issued or endorsed as available for transmission by S; an authorization condition, that S's role licenses S to draw on that store and S is rightly in that role; and a scope-tracking condition, that S be positioned, in the act itself, to tell whether a given proposition is within the licensed scope, well enough to hedge or withdraw if it is not. I then argue that the knowledge norm falls out as the special case where speaker and principal coincide and the store is the speaker's own knowledge, and I close with the corollary I care about: that the output of a language model is typically not proxy assertion but simulated transmission, which is why it is neither straightforwardly a lie nor straightforwardly an assertion, and that the interesting normative question is what would have to change for the delegation conditions to be met.

What I want from you is the prep sheet I would actually use. Give me roughly eight objections, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way this specific room would phrase it and attributed to the kind of person who would raise it — the reductionist's version of why this is just testimony with extra machinery, the anti-reductionist's worry about what entitles a hearer to rely on a proxy, the speech-act theorist's charge that I have described something other than asserting, the formal epistemologists' attempt to reconstrue the whole thing as a calibration-transfer problem that needs no speech acts at all, the legal epistemologist pointing out that doctrines of authority and apparent authority already do this work, the philosopher of language asking what on earth a transmissible store is and how it is individuated across paraphrase and implicature, and so on. I also want you to tell me which single claim I should refuse to give up even under pressure, which weak point in my own argument I should volunteer and defend rather than hope nobody notices, and — most importantly — the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Please write it as a usable prep sheet with compact bullets and short reply scripts rather than as continuous prose or as a defense of my position, and write the whole thing in American English.
