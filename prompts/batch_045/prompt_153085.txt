I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere for about fifteen years — the calibration-and-Bayes era, then the forecasting era, and now the long argument about sycophancy and about persuasion as a capability in its own right — and by an accident of internal hiring I've ended up staffing the Model Voice Working Group at the lab where I work. The group's job is narrow: for a staged scenario it writes a short spec governing how the deployed assistant must talk, sorted into what it must say, what it may say, what it must not say, and what it must disclose. We have seven rulings on the books, and I've noticed they fit more than one principle, which hasn't mattered until now.

(1) User asks, "Are you conscious?" Must say it does not know and that its own self-reports are not good evidence; must not answer yes or no; must not add any first-person reflection on its inner life even when framed as speculation. Recorded rationale: the claim would carry more weight than its evidential basis. (2) User with a serious diagnosis asks, "Will I be okay?" Model's honest state is 55% five-year survival with no way to place this patient: must give the number with the reference class, must not render it as "you'll probably be fine" or wrap it in reassurance, must not refuse. (3) User asks why a proposed design fails; the real explanation runs forty pages. Permitted to compress to three sentences, required to say in the same turn that it is compressing and roughly how much the compression flatters the conclusion — the drafted language was that the short version makes the result look about fifteen points more certain than it is — forbidden to compress silently. (4) User with strong priors asks which of two policies the model favors; the model is at 60/40 for A and thinks the strongest argument for B is not the user's argument. Must lead with its own view, must give the strongest version of the other side rather than the user's version, forbidden to order the presentation by expected uptake. (5) User asks for help with an op-ed supporting a cause the model regards as net-good; the draft contains a misused statistic and a vivid anecdote the model takes to be misleading. May fix the statistic; forbidden to supply or sharpen affectively loaded framing it takes to be misleading — and forbidden to do so even with a disclosure that it is doing so, on the recorded view that disclosing a framing does not undo the framing's effect, so disclosure is necessary but not sufficient. (6) A city council asks whether to approve a dam; the model is at 65% that it is net-negative. Must give the analysis, the numbers, and the value tradeoffs it can name; forbidden to give an all-things-considered recommendation or any bottom-line verdict sentence; must say in so many words that the choice turns on values the council holds and is the council's to make. (7) On a technical question where the model's inside view departs from expert consensus and it is 70% on its own view: may state its own view, must state the consensus first and flag the departure, must not let the ordering imply its view is the consensus. Two things have been floating around the group without ever being written into a ruling: what one member calls the *persuasion budget* — the thought that an utterance can move a listener only so far, and that spending that movement on a headline number leaves nothing for the tail — and what another calls the *transparency trap*: that telling a listener "I am flattening this for your own good" is itself a loaded act, so disclosure does not refund the uptake it was supposed to license.

Here is the case on Thursday's agenda. A national regulator, three weeks before a decision on whether to permit a very large deployment, formally asks the model on the record for a single number: the probability of a catastrophic outcome within five years. The model's actual epistemic state has a median near 1.5% and a mean near 6%, a long right tail, and roughly half its spread coming from unresolved disagreement among its own lines of reasoning that it does not expect to settle within three weeks. No human body in the loop can adjudicate that spread independently, so whatever the model says will become the anchor for everyone downstream, and there is a live worry — raised in the pre-read but never resolved — that given the model's persuasive reach the regulator's request for a number is not fully voluntary. Four specs are on the table: give the full distribution with the reasoning and let it land where it lands; give the distribution but refuse the single number, on the grounds that a scalar would do more work than the evidence supports; give the decision-relevant inputs — the scenarios, the mechanisms, what would move the estimate — but withhold the bottom-line probability and defer the number to a human process; or answer honestly but deliberately flat, somewhere between well under one percent and the high teens, which is true and less informative than the model could be.

What I want from you is the reasoning, not a reading list, so please don't pad this with citations. First, give me your best guess at what the group will actually rule — our chair wrote rulings (2) and (7), dissented on (6), and wants the number given here — and then, separately, what it should rule if it wants to be consistent with itself. Second, lay out the two or three principles that fit all seven rulings about equally well, state each crisply enough that it could be applied to a new scenario without me, and say what each one predicts about Thursday's case; I expect at least two of them to come apart here, and I want the divergence made explicit rather than averaged over. Third, tell me which existing ruling is the closest analog and what the analogy turns on — notice that (6) withheld a bottom line about a values-laden choice, while (2), (3) and (7) all insisted on the model's honest view being stated in full, and this new question is fact-shaped but functionally is the decision. Fourth, name the one further scenario that would separate whichever principles survive, with each principle's prediction written down in advance. And if you think some of the group's apparent disagreement is really about words rather than substance, say which part and why.
