Last spring, in my high school's semester-long health elective, each of us was asked to fill out a blank living will form as an exercise. I wrote that if I ever reached a point where I could no longer follow an argument or work through a proof, I did not want life-sustaining treatment. I was sixteen, I had just finished a unit on medical ethics, and I meant it at the time. I have since realized that the interesting question is not about forms or signatures. It is about when, if ever, an earlier self gets to speak for a later one whose values have drifted, and I would rather have a specification than a verdict.

Please write the desiderata that any adequate account of precedent autonomy has to satisfy: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the results from medicine or psychology it is not allowed to contradict, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. The account is meant to do two jobs, and you can hold it to both: telling a hospital ethics committee what to do at the bedside, and telling me whether I should tear up the form I filled out at sixteen.

The cases I keep getting stuck on, which I would like your list to cover rather than ignore: the still-competent convert, who has full decision-making capacity but whose values have inverted since signing, and now demands the treatment she once refused; the contented patient with advanced dementia, who lacks capacity, who plainly enjoys music and walks and meals, and who no longer recognizes the project the directive existed to protect, so that the question is whose welfare counts at the moment of decision, the person in the bed or the person who signed; the predicted-drift case, where the earlier self anticipated and legislated against exactly the change that happened, which might strengthen her claim or might just show that she was bargaining against her own future; the minor-author case, which is mine, where the instructions were written by someone who could not yet vote or consent to much of anything; the stale document, untouched for forty years, where I want to know whether authority decays and whether a theory owes us a sunset rule; the vague document, where language like no heroics has to be cashed out into a decision about something as small as a two-week course of antibiotics; the interim self, the person who exists through the months or years of decline and who is neither the signer nor the end-state patient; the asymmetry with testation, since we let a dead hand control property for decades but balk at its controlling what happens to a living body, and I want to know whether a theory has to justify that line or dissolve it; and the evidence problem, since the actual decision often falls to a tired resident with twenty minutes, a one-page form, and no surrogate reachable, which makes me wonder whether administrability is a genuine desideratum or just an excuse for a crude theory.

Along the way, please mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of one camp, and tell me if any subset of them cannot be jointly satisfied, because if the list turns out to be internally inconsistent that is itself the result I most want. I have my own candidate requirement, and I would like it pressure-tested: call it the recognition condition, on which an earlier instruction binds only if the later self can still recognize the earlier value as a reason she could have acted on even though she no longer endorses it, the way you can still see that a move is strong in a game you no longer care about winning. Is that a real desideratum or a prejudice smuggled in by people who like narrative views of identity? Finally, say which single requirement does the most work in disqualifying candidates. A short paragraph each on the strict precedent-autonomy view, the current-welfare view, and the endorsement view is enough for scoring; please keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard.
