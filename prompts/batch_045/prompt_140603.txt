I help run a philosophy of physics reading group — 90 minutes on Thursday evenings, usually 12 to 16 people, roughly half physics majors who've taken a real QM course and half philosophy people who mostly know the two-slit thing from popsci, plus one or two who drift over from math. Nobody is a foundations person. We're doing a few weeks on Everett / many-worlds, and I want one whole session on the probability problem: if the theory says every outcome of a measurement really happens, somewhere, then it isnt obvious what it even means to say one outcome is more probable than another, or why a rational agent in such a world should place bets in proportion to the squared amplitude rather than just counting how many copies of herself end up winning. I specifically do not want to write that rule on the board at the start — last time I tried something like this a physics guy just said the formula out loud in minute three and the whole discussion collapsed into him explaining it. So the session has to be built so that move isnt available, at least for the first hour.

What I've been sketching is a casino, and I'd like you to build it into something actually runnable. Participants get a bankroll of tokens — real but tiny stakes: the team with the most tokens at the end is excused from bringing snacks next month, or there's one shared bag of genuinely good chocolate, whatever you think will make people care a little — and they bet on the outcomes of a device, which is really just me with a deck of cards or a spreadsheet I prepped in advance. Here's the trick: the frequency with which each outcome appears across the room comes apart from a number I print on each card, an abstract bare magnitude with no physics vocabulary attached to it. The outcome that shows up in most of the branches is the one with the tiny number, and vice versa, so betting by counting copies loses and betting by the printed number wins. Then I want two wrinkles, which I think are the interesting part. First, a dial: the magnitude is set continuously, so the question "how many branches are there" has no answer at all unless somebody picks a grain size, and I want them to notice on their own that they have no idea how to count. Second, my favorite: the erasure case. After bets are locked but before payout, I sometimes announce that the measurement is going to be reversed — the branches get recombined, so the many copies who won and the many copies who lost all get merged back into a single person who collects whatever is left. Should anyone want to change their bet? Should it matter whether they think the technician will actually do the reversal? I want the room to feel that something is wrong with any rule that says "care about how many future copies of you there are," because that rule now seems to depend on what happens after the bet is placed.

So give me the real thing: full rules, any roles worth handing out (I was thinking of assigning a couple of people to be "the low-weight continuer" who has to argue for decision rules that dont write her off — good idea or does it backfire?), materials and prep, a 90-minute timeline, and a payoff scheme whose payoffs make the different candidate rules come apart sharply instead of all doing about equally well. Then the debrief: map specific moments in the game onto the parts of the philosophical problem — self-locating uncertainty before a branching versus the branching structure itself, why a frequency-among-branches story seems to fail, why counting branches needs a preferred grain, why a betting policy might have to be argued for from something like a rational-agent or symmetry assumption rather than derived from the branching alone, and what the erasure case does to each of those. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it: I'm guessing somebody goes fanatical and says all that matters is whether there is a winning branch at all, somebody else says the numbers on the cards are made up so who cares, and the physics people get impatient. And be honest about what the game can't do — I assume it can't get at why the quantity in question is that particular function of the amplitude rather than, say, its fourth power or its absolute value, and it probably can't make vivid why this is a special problem for a no-collapse theory as opposed to one where measurement actually settles things. Tell me what I have to supply myself at the end, and how to do it in about five minutes without turning the last five minutes into a lecture.
