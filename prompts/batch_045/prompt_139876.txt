I am a retired physical chemist — thirty years in industrial catalysis — and since retiring I have been sitting in on philosophy-adjacent discussion groups, and have now agreed to run one evening myself. Our library's Wednesday series draws about twenty-five people, mostly retired engineers, a physician, a lawyer, a high-school mathematics teacher, and one retired operations-research analyst who is sharp, friendly, and will tell me inside ten minutes that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma defection strictly dominates. Last month another presenter did the iterated version and everyone left content that reciprocity and reputation explain cooperation there; my job is the harder case. I have ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and no way to assign reading in advance, so please do not hand me a bibliography or a list of essays — I cannot easily reach a university library, and in any case I want the plan, not the sources.

The notion I have to teach is superrationality: the claim, associated with the popular-science essay that introduced the term, that two agents who are genuinely running the same reasoning procedure should treat their choices as correlated rather than independent, so that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma — no repetition, no reputation, no communication, no enforcement — cooperation is nonetheless the rational output. Please do not explain the idea to me; I can follow the argument. Plan how I should explain it to that room. I want the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what: does the dominance argument have to be stated and fully conceded before the correlation move can even be heard, or does conceding it first hand the operations-research analyst the whole game? Does Newcomb's problem belong early as an intuition pump, late as a diagnostic, or nowhere? Where does the shift from choosing-as-one to choosing-as-one-of-a-kind have to be made explicit, and what has to be in place before it? I also want one case to carry the session, and I am uneasy with the standard stagings: a player facing an exact copy of themselves invites the response that this is just one person in two places, and the eccentric-benefactor-with-twenty-finalists version invites the response that it is a rigged parlor puzzle. I would rather build on two agents who are not literally the same agent and not guaranteed to reason identically — two independent implementations of the same published algorithm written by different teams, or two strangers each of whom has read and been convinced by the same argument. Tell me what such an example must contain and must lack if it is to bear the weight.

There is one wrinkle I insist on including, because it is where I suspect the idea either survives or dies and I do not know which: a tremble. Let the other agent be an ordinary defection-bound reasoner with some small but non-negligible probability rather than a superrational one. What becomes of the superrational recommendation, at what point in the sequence should I introduce the perturbation, and is the honest answer that the prescription is discontinuous in that probability — which, if so, I would rather say out loud than paper over? I also want the misconception map with each item placed: that this is just the golden rule in mathematical costume; that it assumes some kind of causal leakage or telepathy between the agents; that it is refuted in advance as a variant of evidential reasoning; that it requires believing the other agent literally is you; and the subtler one, that it amounts only to cooperating when your estimate of the other's cooperativeness exceeds a payoff-derived threshold, which misses the dependence claim entirely. My mathematics teacher will want to say that symmetry means we both do the same thing and so we should simply pick the better of the two joint outcomes, and the operations-research analyst will say that this merely selects an equilibrium by stipulation; I would like to know which of them is closer to the truth and how to let that exchange happen in the room without losing the thread. Give me one question to end on that would show me the idea itself had landed rather than that people had been persuaded to be nicer, and tell me what I should openly decline to explain at this level. Finally, and this matters to me as a scientist who has sat through too many confident talkers: mark clearly for me, in the notes rather than on the board, which claims are settled — the payoff structure, dominance, the equilibrium facts — and which are live disputes among people who work on decision theory, such as whether superrationality is a genuine position distinct from the familiar causal and evidential approaches, whether it collapses into one of them or into reasoning as a group agent, and whether it is a normative claim at all; I do not want to present a contested view to that room as though it were the consensus.
