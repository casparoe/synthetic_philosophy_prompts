Hi — I write a science-and-ideas column, and I'm finally doing the piece on whether religion actually makes people more moral. I've read enough of the usual back-and-forth to know that both sides always seem to be measuring something slightly different, and I want to get properly clear on what's even being claimed before I talk to anyone. So I'm going to hand you a stylised version of the evidence and a list of candidate readings, and I want you to grade them. Please don't give me citations, reading lists or "see so-and-so (2013)" — I've got no library access on this laptop and I'm not going to chase references. Just the reasoning, in British English please (behaviour, sceptical, that sort of thing).

Here's the stylised evidence pattern I'm working from. Treat it as given, even if you think the real data are messier — I want to know what follows from it, not what the actual numbers are:

- On self-reported giving and volunteering, religious people come out well ahead.
- In anonymous economic games the gap nearly vanishes, and what's left of it is mostly giving directed at co-religionists, or triggered by religious primes in the lab.
- On ordinary dishonesty and property crime within a country, religious people score a bit lower — but the size of the gap tracks how observed people feel more than what they believe.
- Cross-nationally, the most secular countries sit at the top on trust, low corruption and low violent crime. They're also, without exception, countries that secularised slowly on top of a long religious history.
- In the handful of places where religion fell off a cliff within about two generations, trust didn't collapse, but it didn't improve either.

Now the eight readings of "makes people better" that I keep seeing smuggled into this argument. Someone says "religion makes people better" and they might mean any of these:

1. They help strangers more, at real cost, with nobody watching.
2. They help more, but mainly inside their own community.
3. They cheat less when cheating is cheap and undetectable.
4. They punish norm-violators more readily even when punishing costs them personally.
5. They score better on the grab-bag of vice indicators: crime, addiction, family breakdown.
6. There's a tighter fit between the principles they state and what they actually do.
7. They extend cooperation and trust to out-group members, not just to people like them.
8. They have a stronger and more reliable moral self-conception — they're more sure of what's right and more committed to doing it, whatever the behaviour actually looks like.

What I want from you:

Give each of the eight a score out of 100 for how much a clear win for religion on that reading would actually vindicate the claim as it's used in ordinary public argument — where 100 is "if this came out positive, the question is settled and I should write the celebratory version" and 0 is "even if this came out positive it settles nothing anyone was arguing about." Sort them into tiers rather than just listing numbers: which ones really carry the argument, which are consolation prizes, which are rhetorical decoys. One sentence of justification each, max.

Then, three more things. First: my own hobby-horse. Call it the echo hypothesis — the idea that high-trust secular societies are running on norms that were installed and enforced at huge cost under religion, and that they're spending down inherited capital rather than demonstrating that religion was never load-bearing. Rate separately how damaging that hypothesis is to the secular-society side of the story, and give me a single number: what fraction of the "we clearly don't need religion for trust" claim survives once the echo hypothesis is taken seriously? A number between 0 and 1, with the reasoning.

Second: for each of the eight readings, say how the direction of the result would cut. Which one, if it came out negative for religion, would most damage the religious side; and which one, if it came out positive, would most damage the secular side. I suspect they aren't the same reading, and I want to know why.

Third: if you were advising each camp on which single reading to insist the whole debate be judged by, and which one they'd quietly try to swap in at the last minute — what's on each list? I want the tell.

Definately don't hedge everything into a mush of "it depends"; commit to numbers and tiers, and tell me where you're genuinely torn.
