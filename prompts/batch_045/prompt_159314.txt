Two researchers have agreed to end a long-running dispute with an adversarial collaboration and to publish only what both can sign. One of them, call her G, is a committed geometric rationalist: she holds that the object a rational agent should maximize is the time-average growth rate of whatever quantity it compounds — equivalently, for multiplicative gambles, the expected logarithm of wealth — and that this is a requirement of rationality rather than a statement of taste. The other, call him E, is a committed expected-utility theorist: he holds that rationality is maximization of expected utility under the agent's actual utility function, that the ensemble average over all possible trajectories is the right object, and that taking logarithms is one preference among others which has been smuggled in wearing the costume of a norm.

Write the joint statement they would both sign.

Ground rules for the document:

- Adjudicate nothing. The text is not a verdict on which side is right; it is a construct both can endorse, with the residual disagreement made exact.
- Settle every point by argument or calculation. No citations, no named authorities, no bibliographies, no appeals to what the literature says.
- Define your terms once — bankroll, multiplicative gamble, growth-optimal stake, fractional staking, time-average versus ensemble-average growth, ruin — and then use them consistently.
- Distinguish scrupulously between claims that hold at every finite horizon and claims that hold only in the limit. Do not let an asymptotic result do the work of a finite-horizon one, in either direction.
- State the mathematics precisely rather than by rule of thumb: the shape of the growth rate as a function of stake (concavity, unique interior maximum, where it crosses zero, expressed in terms of the payoff odds), the second-order behaviour near the optimum, what happens under parameter uncertainty, and what happens when the dynamics stop being purely multiplicative. If any proposition I sketch below is false or only true under hidden assumptions, do not sign it on their behalf: state the correct proposition and record the correction explicitly.

Structure the statement as follows.

1. Agreed claims, numbered, roughly eight to twelve. These should include, at least: the finite-horizon fact that for independent and identically distributed multiplicative gambles the myopic expected-log rule also maximizes expected log terminal wealth and prescribes a constant fraction of current wealth, independent of the wealth level; the fact that it does not maximize expected terminal wealth, nor expected terminal utility for any utility that is not affine in log wealth, and that against an unbounded utility it loses in expectation by an arbitrarily large factor; the asymptotic dominance result, stated with its exact scope; the fact that under uncertainty about the true probabilities the growth-optimal rule stakes against the posterior-predictive distribution rather than against a point estimate, so that full staking on an estimated edge overbets; and the agreed diagnosis of why fractional staking is attractive, with the several distinct reasons for it kept separate rather than run together.

2. A disagreements table with columns for the crux, G's own formulation of her position, E's own formulation of his, a neutral restatement both accept, and a tag marking each disagreement as factual, normative, or verbal. Each side must be able to recognize its own view in its own column.

3. Between three and five cruxes, where a crux is a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side. For each crux give: the sub-question itself; the case or calculation both agree would bear on it; what G predicts the case will show; what E predicts; and what outcome would count as mixed rather than a clean win for either. Use or improve the following candidates; merge two if you think they are really one, and say so.

   - Preference or constraint: whether maximizing expected log wealth is a specification of the agent's good or a constraint on choice that binds whatever the agent's utility happens to be. Test: an agent whose utility in terminal wealth is explicitly stated, bounded, and saturating — more wealth above a threshold buys nothing.
   - The Deadline case: a known finite number of rounds, after which terminal wealth is converted into utility by a stated bounded increasing concave function. Both agree on the mathematics here; the crux is whether asymptotic probability-dominance carries normative force at that finite horizon, and in particular whether it carries any force at all once utility saturates.
   - The Toll Booth case: the same favorable multiplicative gamble, but with a fixed cost levied each round that does not scale with wealth. Ask whether the growth criterion still yields a myopic, wealth-independent rule, whether maximizing expected log of the next period still coincides with maximizing long-run time-average growth, whether ruin becomes reachable under fractional staking, and whether such dynamics are the normal case or a special one. Both camps should record in advance what they expect the answers to be.
   - The Signal case: a repeated horse race in which a noisy signal about the outcome can be purchased each round at a stated price. One camp prices the signal in bits, the other in units of utility; the crux is whether either framework yields a principled willingness to pay for information, and whether the two agree about how much to stake while still learning.
   - The Federation case: several agents, each caring only about the logarithm of their own wealth, who may either run separate bankrolls or pool into one bankroll governed by a stated rebalancing rule and may or may not walk away with their share after a lucky run. The crux is whether the growth criterion is a norm for individuals or for persisting coalitions and institutions, and whether its dominance argument survives when the coalition can fracture.

4. A section on verbal disputes. Identify which parts of the quarrel you judge to be disagreements about words, say exactly why, and rewrite those disputes so both parties can sign the reformulation. Say plainly which residue remains genuinely substantive.

5. A short appendix titled what neither side claims, listing the positions each camp is often accused of holding but does not, so that neither side is trapped by a strawman in later debate.
