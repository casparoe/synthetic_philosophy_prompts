The topic I want is moral saints, like, whether it's actually desirable to be as morally good as you possibly can be. From what I've read the two camps are pretty stuck: one side says a person who was maximally morally good all the time would be kind of flat and boring, no room for anything that isn't moral — hobbies you're good at for their own sake, loving a subject, friendships that aren't about helping someone — so we wouldn't want to be that person or be their friend. The other side says that just shows we're selfish, and anyway the boring saint is a strawman, since a saint could have all kinds of personal interests as long as they never actually get in the way of what morality requires.

My half-formed idea is that both sides seem to quietly assume being a saint costs you something, that your own projects have to be given up or at least are always at risk. So what about someone who got lucky — whose talents and tastes just happen to be exactly the ones that do the most good? Someone who loves the high-stress job that also lets them give away most of their income, or who'd happily spend all weekend on the boring thing that happens to help a lot of people. They're maximally moral and they're not sacrificing anything. Call it an easy saint.

Here's the question I want stated precisely: is the standard complaint against moral sainthood a complaint about maximal moral goodness itself, or only about maximal moral goodness that costs you your own projects? And if it's only the second, does the debate collapse into something obvious — sacrifice is costly, duh — or does it turn into a different and more interesting question: whether a life can really be a moral saint's life if it's morally perfect by luck, and whether sainthood has to involve some sacrifice or effort to count as sainthood at all?

I'm imagining building three or four concrete cases — the reluctant saint, the easy saint, someone in between whose neutral hobby turns out to be super useful — and using them to test which feature is actually doing the work in the complaint.
