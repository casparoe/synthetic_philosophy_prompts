I've just come out of a long argument with a friend about indicative conditionals and I want to find out whether the position I ended up defending is even coherent before we pick it up again.

We agree on the setup. We both take the Adams equation — that a rational agent's credence in "if A then C" should equal her credence in C given A — to be the central datum any theory has to explain, and we both accept that the Lewis-style triviality arguments show the equation can't hold for *every* credence function if "if A then C" is standing for some proposition whose probability the agent is tracking. We disagree about the moral. She concludes that conditionals don't express propositions at all: the equation is a constraint on what you are in a position to assert, or on some non-propositional attitude, and there is no proposition there to have a probability. I refused to go along. I want to keep the proposition, keep the equation, and say that the triviality arguments overreach. Here, as honestly as I can manage, is the package I found myself defending.

(1) The equation holds as a norm on credence: cr(if A then C) = cr(C|A), for cr(A) > 0. I take this to be non-negotiable datum-level stuff, not a theorem to be derived from a semantics.

(2) Conditionals express propositions, with truth conditions. The left-hand side of (1) is a genuine credence in a genuine proposition, and disagreement about an indicative conditional is disagreement about how things are, not a clash of attitudes. I take the embedding data at face value: what "if B then C" contributes inside "if A then (if B then C)" is the same proposition it contributes unembedded, and conditionals can be negated, conjoined and disjoined.

(3) Semantically I want the closest-worlds account: "if A then C" is true at a world just in case C holds at the closest A-worlds, ties among the closest worlds permitted, with the usual centering assumption. The relevant ordering is fixed by the context of utterance, not by anyone's personal credence function.

(4) My answer to the triviality arguments: they cheat by quantifying over all credence functions, and no agent ever occupies more than one doxastic career. Read the equation *locally*: fix the agent's initial credence function cr0 and require only that, for every A and C and every proposition E in her algebra with cr0(E) > 0 and cr0(A ∧ E) > 0, cr0(if A then C | E) = cr0(C | A ∧ E). Every credal state she could occupy is a conditionalization of cr0 on some total evidence E, so nothing is lost; and derivations that need to compare her credences with those of a different agent never get off the ground. She said the restriction makes no difference and then couldn't produce the argument on the spot, so I don't know whether that was bluster.

(5) I also hold that a rational agent assigns credence zero only to logical falsehoods, so the cr(A) > 0 proviso in (1) is no real restriction and I don't care that cr(C|A) is undefined at zero, because it never is.

(6) Two verdicts I won't give up. First: my credence in a nested conditional and in the corresponding collapsed one should coincide — cr(if A then (if B then C)) = cr(if A ∧ B then C) — and I want this to fall out of (1) rather than be a coincidence. She says this principle, which she called import–export, is invalid on the closest-worlds account I am helping myself to in (3); I don't know whether that's right, and if it is I don't know what it costs me. Second: with a fair coin just flipped and unobserved, my credence in "if it landed heads, it landed heads" is 1 and my credence in "if it landed heads, it landed tails" is 0, and I am happy to call those true and false respectively and to say so out loud.

(7) The thing she pressed hardest, and the one I am least sure about: it follows from (1) and the probability calculus that cr(if A then C) ≤ cr(A ⊃ C) always, with equality only when cr(A) = 1 or cr(A ∧ ¬C) = 0. She says this shows the material conditional is smuggled back in. I say it shows only that the material conditional is a ceiling, and that the gap between the two is exactly where conditionals do their work.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey. Go through (1)–(7) and tell me which commitments are in genuine tension, which pairs only look like they are, and for each real tension what exactly I would have to give up and what giving it up would cost me. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and at the end tell me which single revision would clean up the most, on the assumption that I would like to keep (1) if anything survives. Be explicit about which of my worries *dissolve* on inspection: I would rather be told that an anxiety is unfounded than have it politely counted as a cost. Where a tension takes the form of a derivation, show me the derivation rather than announcing the result — I would rather see the four lines than be told they exist. Assume my algebra is rich enough to contain a shuffled deck and a fair coin, independent of one another, and use concrete instances drawn from them wherever that makes a step legible. And please don't tell me to go and read someone: I want the argument, not the reading list.
