Hey — I run a tiny for-fun reading group (four of us; two are sleep researchers who mostly just roll their eyes at the philosophy) and we have spent way too many evenings on the question of whether dreams are experiences. Half the room is basically Malcolmian: the idea that there is some experience going on in there while you are asleep, which you then describe more or less accurately on waking, isn't a false empirical hypothesis but a kind of grammatical confusion — the criteria that make first-person reports of experience intelligible are tied to waking-ish circumstances, so there is no inner happening whose character a report could get right or wrong, and no "it seemed to him while he slept" that is either true or false. The other half is Dennett-style: this is a perfectly coherent empirical question, and the answer is no — on that picture the narrative-y, scene-by-scene dream we report is not a record of something lived through during sleep, it gets composed very fast at or around the moment of awakening out of whatever sleep-time activity is going on (the cassette idea), and there is no fact of the matter about what the dream really was beyond the brain activity plus the report. Both camps end up denying the naive "you have Technicolor adventures all night and then recollect them" view, but for reasons that don't fit together at all, and they keep talking past each other, so I want the document they would produce if they did this properly.

Write me the joint statement of that adversarial collaboration: a Malcolmian about the grammar of dreaming and a Dennett-style cassette theorist, after a good-faith collaboration. Give me a numbered agreed-claims section they could both sign (including what they jointly want to say to the naive view, and an explicit note that theirs is agreement in conclusion rather than in reason, so nobody in my group mistakes it for a consensus), then a disagreements table: for each disagreement, a neutral formulation plus the wording each side would accept for its own position, then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — and for each crux a concrete case or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I don't want the usual greatest-hits arguments; please actually work through the cases I care about: (1) the sleep-time sensitivity constraint — reports are keyed to what happened during sleep (sounds or names played to the sleeper, a limb cuff, day-residue from that afternoon), so the composition can't be purely post-hoc, and I want to know whether the cassette view has to become a hybrid and what each side then predicts; (2) a slow-awakening protocol — a drug that stretches the REM-to-waking transition over a couple of minutes, with the subject giving reports every thirty seconds while half in and half out, which should tell you whether narrative is coalescing on the waking side of the boundary; (3) lucid dreaming with pre-arranged eye-movement signals during confirmed REM, i.e. the subject apparently does something during sleep that is later confirmed; (4) the individuation mess — a subject awakened once, put back into the same REM period, and awakened again with an incompatible report, or a single dream that keeps getting more detailed across retellings without anyone confabulating; and (5) those flat awakenings where people say there were colours and shapes but no "me" and no story, which may or may not be an experience in the sense either side cares about. Also say plainly which parts of their dispute you think are verbal rather than substantive, and what each would count as evidence against their own view — if the answer is "nothing could", say so.

Practical stuff: aim for something my group could read aloud in one sitting, roughly 800–1200 words; assume we know the background, so don't re-explain either view from scratch; no citations, no bibliography, no "see X 1976" — we do this offline and nobody can look anything up, so argue it out rather than gesturing at sources.
