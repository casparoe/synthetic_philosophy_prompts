I work as a software engineer, mostly on systems that allocate scarce capacity: job scheduling, rate limiting, triage ordering for alerting. A few weeks ago at dinner I said, without thinking, that of course you save the five instead of the one. Someone at the table asked what the "of course" was doing there, and I couldn't tell her. She then sent me a short 1970s paper — I believe by a philosopher named Taurek — arguing that the numbers don't count at all and that the honest response is to give each person an equal chance. I find that I can't refute it and can't accept it either. What I want is the principle I am implicitly using, stated precisely enough that I could implement it.

So: my verdicts. In every case, assume everyone involved is a stranger unless I say otherwise, no one is responsible for the emergency, everyone is equally well off beforehand, the harms I describe are the whole story, and this happens exactly once — I don't want an answer that works by appealing to what would happen if everyone reasoned that way, or to precedent, or to who is at fault.

1. Five contractors in the east wing, one in the west; I can vent smoke one way or the other; either way the people on that side get severe but survivable burns. Verdict: vent toward the one. This is a reflex, not a calculation.

2. Same, but two against one. Verdict: I'd still pick the two, but it now feels close to a toss-up. My confidence does not seem to scale with the ratio, and I don't know what to do with that.

3. One person will die; alternatively five people will each lose a hand (same incident, same mechanism, no other differences). Verdict: prevent the death. I'm uncomfortable with this and can't say why. If it were twenty hands instead of five, I'd start to waver.

4. One person will lose a hand; alternatively a million people will each get a mild ten-second rash. Verdict: save the hand — and a trillion rashes doesn't move me either. This makes me suspect that whatever I'm doing with numbers in case 1 isn't really summing.

5. Five strangers, or my own child. Verdict: my child, with no guilt and no sense that I've done anything requiring an excuse. This sits badly with case 1 in a way I can't articulate.

6. One patient will lose a hand and a foot; alternatively two patients will each lose a hand. Verdict: the single patient is clearly worse off than either of the two, and yet I don't think the outcome containing him is worse than the outcome containing them. I have two reactions here that refuse to fit on one scale.

7. Five against one again, but saving the five requires me to cut power to the one patient's ventilator, and that patient would otherwise live. Verdict: far more resistance than in case 1, possibly to the point of not doing it — and I don't think that's only squeamishness, since the numbers and the outcomes are the same as in case 1 as far as I can tell.

8. A service of mine is running ten thousand identical copies of one person's mind; killing the cluster ends all of them. Alternatively, one unrelated biological person dies. Verdict: I go back and forth. Ten thousand copies of the same mind is not ten thousand times worse than one death, but it isn't exactly one death either. This is the case I most want a principle to speak to.

9. Suppose only one rescue is possible no matter what: I can either rescue the lone person with certainty, or run a fair lottery over all six, giving each a one-in-six chance. Verdict: the lottery feels better. Expected lives saved is one either way, so the rule I thought I had can't even see a difference between the options — which is itself evidence that "count the numbers" was never the whole of my view.

10. Back in case 1, suppose I flip a coin: heads I save the five, tails I save the one, so every one of the six gets a fifty percent chance. Verdict: I hate it. It feels like a dereliction that wrongs the five, while wronging none of the five individually in any amount I can name. Cases 9 and 10 are the pair I can't hold together, since both are defended in the language of giving people equal chances.

My own stab, offered to be tested and repaired rather than endorsed: aggregate over people with sharply diminishing weight per additional person at a given level of harm — I was thinking the square root of the number of people — and treat harms of different kinds as not summable at all, comparing the worst single outcome first and falling back on the aggregate only when the worst individual outcomes are comparable. That gets me cases 1 through 4 and case 10, and I notice it says nothing at all about 5, 6, 7, 8, or 9.

What I'd like back:

- A short menu of candidate principles rather than one answer, each stated precisely enough that I could write it as a comparison over a list of (person, harm) pairs, with the clause-by-clause motivation: which of my cases forces which clause.
- For each candidate, its verdicts on cases I haven't listed — at minimum: a hundred against one with identical harms; five people each losing a hand against one person losing two hands; and one identified person against a large number of merely statistical people I could never name.
- An honest statement of which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to drop, and whether the dropped one is more credible than the principle that drops it.
- Whether cases 9 and 10 can be reconciled at all, or whether I have to choose between an ex ante reading (what matters is the chance each person is given) and an ex post reading (what matters is how many are actually saved).
- If no single rule can be stated, tell me which requirement I should abandon — completeness, transitivity, impartiality, or sensitivity to numbers — and what the resulting failure would look like in production terms, e.g. which inputs would make it cycle, refuse to answer, or return verdicts sensitive to how I partition the people into groups.

I'm not asking for a reading list or for citations; I have no library access and I want the argument rather than the sources. Where a view has a standard name, naming it once is welcome, but please derive the comparisons rather than appealing to authority.
