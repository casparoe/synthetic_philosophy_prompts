Here is the question:

Question: Grice's cooperative principle holds that one ought to make one's conversational contribution whatever is required, at the point where it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the conversation. Grice also distinguishes four categories of maxims: Quality (try to make your contribution true; do not say what you believe to be false or that for which you lack adequate evidence), Quantity (make your contribution as informative as is required, and do not make it more informative than is required), Relation (be relevant), and Manner (be perspicuous: avoid obscurity and ambiguity, be brief and avoid unnecessary prolixity, and be orderly). A speaker may fail to fulfill a maxim in several ways: they may quietly violate it (which tends to deceive), opt out, face a clash, or openly flout it (i.e., blatantly fail to fulfill it), where flouting can give rise to a conversational implicature because the hearer assumes the cooperative principle is still being observed at a deeper level. Conversational implicatures are cancelable (defeasible) and are worked out from what is said plus context. Generalized conversational implicatures are those that arise without needing much specific context, such as the scalar implicature from 'some' to 'not all'; particularized ones depend heavily on specific context. Conventional implicatures, by contrast, are attached to particular words (e.g., the contrast suggested by 'but') and are not derived in the same way from the maxims.

Recently, a commentator has proposed adding a fifth maxim, the maxim of Manifest Relevance (MR): 'Do not answer in a way that is relevant only to a question the speaker did not ask.'

Now consider this exchange. Alice asks Bob: 'Did you water the plants or just move them into the shade?' Bob replies: 'I moved the delicate ferns out of the sun.' Assume it is common knowledge between Alice and Bob that Bob knows there is also a cactus, which was left in full sun, and that Alice's question was about all the plants, not just the ferns.

Using the Gricean machinery above, do the following:
(a) State what Bob says.
(b) Identify what Bob conversationally implicates, if anything, and say whether it is particularized or generalized.
(c) Say whether Bob's reply is best described as flouting Quality, flouting Quantity (specify which sub-maxim), flouting Relation, flouting Manner, flouting the proposed MR maxim, or as quietly violating one of them (or some combination). Explain the reasoning that gets the hearer from what is said to the implicature, and note whether the implicature is cancelable.
(d) Briefly say whether, if Bob had instead quietly and unostentatiously violated a maxim (for example, by lying about watering the plants), there would be the same kind of implicature.

And here is the student's answer:

Student's answer: Bob says that he moved the delicate ferns out of the sun. He does not say that he watered the plants. By answering only about the ferns and not mentioning the cactus, and given the common knowledge that the cactus was left in the sun, Bob conversationally implicates that he did not water the plants (or at least did not water the cactus). This is a particularized conversational implicature, because it depends on the specific context: Alice's question covered all the plants, and both speakers know about the cactus. If Bob had said 'I watered some of the plants,' that would trigger a generalized conversational implicature, comparable to the scalar implicature from 'some' to 'not all,' because it comes from the word 'some' and needs little specific context; generalized implicatures of this sort are cancelable, as when one adds 'in fact, all of the plants were watered.'

The implicature here is cancelable: Bob could continue '...and I also watered the cactus,' and the original implicature would disappear without contradiction. Bob's reply flouts the maxim of Quantity, specifically the second sub-maxim (do not make your contribution more informative than is required), because he gives less information than required, and his partial answer is a prolix way of avoiding brevity, so he is also flouting Manner (iii) be brief. He is flouting rather than quietly violating, because Alice can see that he answered only part of the question; she then reasons that if he had watered the plants he would have said so, given Quantity, so he must not have watered them. The proposed MR maxim is not flouted: Bob's answer is relevant to the 'move them into the shade' part of Alice's question, so it is not relevant only to an unasked question.

If Bob had quietly violated a maxim—say, he knew he did not water the plants but said 'I watered them' while crossing his fingers—then there would be no implicature, because a quiet violation is meant to deceive and the hearer is not supposed to notice the failure. Finally, the implicature is part of what Bob means, not part of what he says, and because it is cancelable it is a conversational implicature. Conventional implicatures, such as the contrast from 'but,' are also cancelable, so the only real difference between conversational and conventional implicatures is whether the cooperative principle and the maxims are involved in deriving them.
