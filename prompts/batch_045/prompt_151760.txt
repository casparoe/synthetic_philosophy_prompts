I am a retired structural biologist — thirty-odd years of crystallography and protein folding — and since retiring I have been attending a philosophy reading group at the local university, where this term we have been working through the material on what the literature calls the strange inversions: Darwin's, in which the competence of organisms to see, fly and evade is explained by a process that has no comprehension of sight, flight or evasion, and Turing's, in which the competence of machines to play chess, translate sentences and now predict protein structures is explained by search and optimisation over an objective that nothing inside the system comprehends. In both cases a piece of reasoning that had seemed compulsory — you cannot have the appearance of design without a designer, of understanding without an understander — is stood on its head. I have drafted an essay for our end-of-term pamphlet and would like your comments on it before I inflict it on the group; my worries are that it may be simply wrong, that it may be a restatement of something every philosopher already knows, and that whatever is genuinely interesting in it is buried in the wrong place. I know the biology and the optimisation theory; I do not know the philosophical vocabulary, so if a move I am making has a standard name, please describe it rather than name it, since I have no way of looking it up.

The essay, in outline, runs as follows. (1) I distinguish two kinds of inversion, which I call output-faithful and ground-faithful: an inversion is output-faithful if the derived competence merely reproduces what the comprehending source would have produced, and ground-faithful if it also reproduces something structurally like the chain of reasons, or the causal sequence of test-and-revision, by which the source produced it. I claim Darwin's inversion is plausibly ground-faithful — differential retention acting on variation recapitulates in the physical world something akin to engineering by trial and error — whereas brute-force search is only output-faithful. I then argue that which kind of inversion you have determines whether the inversion genuinely removes reasoners from the explanation of reason, or merely conceals them. (2) I propose what I call the residual comprehension principle: every strange inversion relocates comprehension rather than eliminating it, and one can always balance the books by finding where the comprehension was prepaid. In Darwin's case the relocation is into the environment, which poses the problems without understanding them, and since environments are not designed this looks like a real elimination; in Turing's case the relocation is into whoever specifies the architecture, the objective function, the training distribution and the stopping rule, so that machine competence without comprehension is really comprehension deferred and concentrated at the framing. (3) I then ask whether a closed inversion is possible: a lineage of systems in which the framing conditions are themselves the output of inverted processes — objectives evolved by selection over objectives, architectures searched by the same search, training curricula generated by the learners themselves, aims arrived at by self-supervision — bootstrapped ultimately from some purely physical criterion such as persistence or thermodynamic throughput. If a closed inversion could still yield competence, my principle is false; I argue it cannot, on something like a conservation principle, namely that any selection regime must be informationally richer than what it selects, and I lean on the no-free-lunch results in optimisation theory as an analogy without being confident the analogy is honest. (4) A coda from my own field: protein structure prediction was solved by systems whose internals nobody understands, and my former colleagues divide between calling this science without understanding and calling it merely better instrumentation. My principle gives a verdict — what has been inverted is the derivation, not the aim, since accuracy against curated experimental structures, and the training set itself, were specified by comprehending people — after which I speculate about a fully closed inversion of science, a research loop whose hypotheses, experiments and even aims are generated by the loop, and suggest that we would not count its output as understanding at all, because the last irreducible residuum of comprehension sits with whoever decides what counts as a good prediction.

What I would like from you: first, whether the output-faithful/ground-faithful distinction is doing any real work or quietly collapses, and in particular whether my talk of recapitulating test-and-revision is too vague to bear the weight I put on it. Second, whether the residual comprehension principle is substantive or merely a tautology dressed up — one member of the group, a retired mathematician, says it is vacuous, since of course someone has to build the machine, and another says it is only the old homunculus objection pushed up one level; tell me which of them, if either, is right, and give me the strongest version of the objection, not the weakest. Third, whether the no-free-lunch analogy can be defended given that those results concern average performance across all problems whereas my claim concerns the framing of a single one, and if the analogy fails, what the nearest honest argument would look like. Fourth, editorial advice: which section is weakest, which is most worth expanding, and whether the coda on structure prediction should be dropped as self-indulgent or promoted, since I suspect it is the only part with anything new in it. Finally, if you think the closed-inversion thought experiment as I have set it up is incoherent or underdescribed, say exactly where it breaks and how you would repair it — I would much rather be told that a step is smuggled than be given a polished rewrite of the whole thing.
