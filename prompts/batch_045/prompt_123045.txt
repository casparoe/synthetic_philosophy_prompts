I teach a 75-minute seminar on decision and game theory for about 18 philosophy undergrads — they've seen expected utility and normal-form games, but nothing epistemic. I want one session that makes them *feel* what's doing the work when we say rationality is common knowledge rather than just known by everybody.

What I have in mind: a sequential take-or-pass game with a growing pot, run twice. First run: pairs play it anonymously a couple of times with random re-pairing, told nothing about their partner beyond the payoffs — the player to move either takes (bigger share for them, smaller for the other) or passes and lets the pot grow and the move go across. Second run: same game, same payoffs, but before play I announce publicly that a small known fraction of the cards dealt are marked "must pass" and those players have no choice; nobody knows whether their own or their partner's card is one, and each player only sees their own. I definately want the contrast between the runs to be the lesson: one sliver of doubt about the other person and the chain of reasoning unwinds the other way. Plus some way to recover from their own slips how many levels of "he thinks that I think" they were actually using, so the debrief has data and not just my say-so.

Give me rules, materials, a points payoff scheme (no money — a coffee voucher for the top scorer), the exact wording of what I say before each run with nothing that names the idea up front, and a debrief that maps moments onto the formal distinction, including the discontinuity: how a tiny departure can flip the whole prediction. What will go wrong the first time I run it, and which part of the idea the game just can't stage? Also say how confident you are and flag the bit you're least sure of.
