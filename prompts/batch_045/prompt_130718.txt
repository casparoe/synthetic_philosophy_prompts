Can you give me an intuitive handle on information cascades? Take the usual toy model: binary state, everyone in turn recieves a private signal that's correct with probability q, sees all the earlier choices but not the signals behind them, then chooses. I can follow the algebra fine — once one action is ahead by two, everyone after ignores their own signal, and the resulting cascade is correct with probability q²/(q²+(1-q)²) — but I dont have a gut-level feel for the two slogans that always get stapled to it.

First: everyone in the chain is best-responding to everything that's public. So in what sense has anything gone wrong, and what exactly is the thing that gets lost — whose information, and where does it physically go?

Second: cascades are supposed to be fragile. But no amount of further private signal can break one, while a trickle of public information can. What is the difference between those two kinds of information that makes that true? If it helps to pin things down, use q = 2/3 and one small worked illustration.

The variant I actually care about: suppose each person can pay a small cost to wait one round before acting, and everyone sees how long each person waited. My hunch is that this dissolves the blockage, because waiting is graded rather than binary and so leaks something about signal strength. But I can't tell whether I've genuinely unblocked the later private information or just pushed the same problem up one level — waiting looks like exactly the sort of thing that could itself cascade. Where does that leave the rational-herding story?
