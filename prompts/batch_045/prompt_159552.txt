I’ve been helping my gran (she’s 84) get her affiars in order, and it’s definately made me think about old age in a way I never had to before. My brother thinks she’s sharp as ever and should keep running her little farm; my sister thinks the forgetfulness is creeping in and we should quietly take over the decisions. I’m supposed to be reading for a part-time Classics course at the same time, and the set text is Cicero’s dialogue Cato Maior de Senectute, usually called On Old Age. I found out it was written in 44 BC, when Cicero was 63 himself, and it’s addressed to his friend Atticus, with the speech put into the mouth of Cato the Elder at 84. In it, Cato goes through the standard complaints about growing old—that it drags you away from active public life, that it ruins the body, that it takes away sensual pleasures, and that it leaves you on the edge of death—and tries to rebut each one. The part that got under my skin is his insistence that old age’s authority isn’t something grey hairs or wrinkles can just grab for themselves; on his view it’s the harvest of a life honourably spent earlier on, and old men can still be genuinely useful to the state by advising and judging rather than by soldiering or dashing about. He also pokes fun at the idea that memory inevitably collapses, joking that no old man ever forgot where he had buried his treasure.

Then I read about this private member’s bill going through our parliament—people are calling it the Censor’s Check. The idea is that once you turn 82 you’d have to sit an annual interview testing what the drafters call ‘deliberative memory’, defined as the ability to recall your own past decisions, feel their emotional weight, and use that felt sense to judge new cases. If you fail, you keep your healthcare and pension, but you lose the right to manage your own property and to vote in constitutional referendums, and instead you’re assigned a state counsellor to help you decide things (with a right of appeal, supposedly). Supporters say it protects vulnerable elders and stops the country being led by people whose minds are stuck in the past; my sister is half for it, my brother thinks it’s monstrous. A pilot for the Check used an app called the Atticus Gauge, which claims to estimate deliberative memory from speech patterns, and the trial threw up a strange pattern: the highest scorers were often the people most stubbornly hostile to any political change, while some participants with mild biological memory loss still scored well because they had kept a strong emotional story about what they achieved in their forties. Nobody can agree what the Gauge actually measures.

So, my question: given what Cicero really argues in Cato Maior de Senectute, how do you think he would have viewed the Censor’s Check? I’m not after a generic ‘Cicero valued wisdom so he’d like old people’ answer. Would he have welcomed a test of deliberative memory as a way of protecting the republic from rulers who have lost their grip on the lessons of their own lives? Would he have been appalled at the thought of a state counsellor overriding an 84-year-old’s management of her property, given that he thinks authority is earned by a whole well-spent life rather than granted by years alone? Or would he have landed somewhere in between—maybe backing the Check for some offices but not others, or only for people he’d judge not to have earned authority earlier on? Please commit to a predicted position and give me the reasoning for it: say which bits of his essay push him one way, which bits pull him the other, and where you think he’d finally come down. Write it in British English, and just as one continuous prose piece, the way you’d write a longish email—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. And please don’t quote passages or give citations or references; I can’t check anything online, I just want your own reasoning.
