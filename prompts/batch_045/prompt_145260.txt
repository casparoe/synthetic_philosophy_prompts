Consider a variant of the surprise examination paradox in which the surprise condition is stated probabilistically rather than as a claim about knowledge.

A teacher addresses a class and says: there will be exactly one examination. Moreover, on the morning of the day the examination is held, your credence that the examination is that day — conditional on every earlier day having passed without an examination, on this announcement, and on everything else you know — will not exceed one half.

Scenario A (bounded): the examination must fall on one of exactly five consecutive days, Monday through Friday, and everyone knows that Friday is the last possible day.

Scenario B (unbounded): the class meets indefinitely, there is no last possible day, and the teacher's schedule assigns probability one to there eventually being an examination, though no particular day is ruled out in advance and no upper bound on the wait is announced.

The claim I want a credence on is the following composite: the teacher's announcement is unsatisfiable in Scenario A but satisfiable in Scenario B, and this asymmetry shows that the familiar backward-induction argument — the one that appears to establish that no examination can be a surprise — draws its entire force from the existence of a known last possible day, and not from anything distinctive about surprise, knowledge, or the self-referential character of the announcement.

Give me a single number for that claim. The number itself is not the point; the decomposition is. At minimum, work through these:

1. In Scenario A, can any probability distribution over days meet the one-half constraint? Note that the constraint governs the students' conditional credence on the morning of each day given that all earlier days were exam-free, and note what that implies about the last day specifically.
2. In Scenario B, does a satisfying distribution exist? If one does, is it unique, and does non-uniqueness matter — for instance, does the constraint survive the students being uncertain about which satisfying schedule the teacher has chosen?
3. How much of the paradox's apparent force rests on modelling surprise as a credence threshold rather than as non-deducibility or inability to know in advance? If surprise instead means cannot be deduced from the announcement together with the known calendar, does the bounded case still collapse, and does the unbounded case still survive?
4. Does the self-referential element do any independent work? The announcement constrains credences that are themselves formed partly in response to the announcement, so there is a fixed-point structure here; is it a genuine obstruction distinct from the backward induction, and is it more severe in one scenario than the other?
5. Is there any reading on which the standard argument survives even in Scenario B — by appeal to the students' knowledge of their own rationality, or to a distinction between probability one and certainty, or to the absence of a uniform bound on the expected wait?

Give a rough probability and a one-line justification for each, say how they combine into your headline figure, and then tell me two further things: which single term your number is most sensitive to — the sub-question that, if resolved the other way, would move the headline figure furthest — and which term you are least confident in, together with what would count as evidence about it.

Tell me which of those numbers you think is closer to right, and, more usefully, which of sub-questions 1 through 5 the two of us are most likely actually disagreeing about.
