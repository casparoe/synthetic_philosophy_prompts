Hey — I'm hoping someone can poke holes in a set of things I've ended up believing, because I've got a nagging feeling that at least one of them is doing work it isn't entitled to, and I can't tell which.

Background: I'm a self-taught programmer, no philosophy or stats training, and I've been reading about Occam's razor and Solomonoff induction on and off for a couple of years (blog posts, whatever bits of the sequences I can follow, stuff like that). Here's where I've landed:

1. I think Occam's razor is genuinely justified, not just a taste for tidy theories. The reason I'd give is the Bayesian-flavoured one: a more complicated hypothesis can fit more possible sets of observations, so it spreads its confidence thinner, so the fact that it fits the data we actually got is less of a point in its favour.

2. I think the right way to make "simpler" precise is Kolmogorov complexity — how short a program can be and still output the data, or still single out the hypothesis.

3. I know that measure depends on which language or universal machine you're measuring in, but I've never really been worried by that. As I understand it, switching machines only shifts the lengths by some fixed constant that doesn't depend on the hypothesis, and constants stop mattering once you've got enough data. So I basically file it under bookkeeping.

4. At the same time, I think the razor earns its keep in exactly the opposite situation: when you've got very little data, or one experiment, and the complicated theory had enough knobs to have fitted whatever happened. That's where I actually reach for it — twenty data points and six free parameters, of course it fits. In the limit of masses of data I don't think you need the razor much at all; the data does the work.

5. I hold up Solomonoff induction as the ideal version of all this: weight every computable hypothesis by 2 to the minus its description length, and your predictions converge on the truth in any computable world. I know it's uncomputable and can't actually be run, but I definately don't think that's an objection — it's an idealization, like a perfectly rational agent, and I think an ideal can still tell you which direction to move in.

6. I also think a lot of the best science deliberately uses models everybody knows are false — point masses, ideal gases, frictionless planes — and that this isn't a failure of rationality but the sensible thing to do when you've got limited time and a limited brain.

7. And I'm suspicious of any attempt to justify the razor a priori. From what I can tell, for any bias toward simplicity you can describe a world where that bias does worse than the opposite bias, so I think the razor has to be vindicated by contingent facts about how our universe actually happens to be, and not by pure reasoning.

So: is this a package or just a pile? I'm fairly attached to all seven, so please don't just tell me to drop whichever one is least convenient — I want to know which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and for each real tension what the cheapest fix is and what that fix would cost me somewhere else on the list. Rank them by how bad they are rather than flagging everything as equally serious, and if there's one revision that would clean up the most at once, say which one it is.

If it's relevant: the place I actually use this stuff day to day is small-sample situations. I do forecasting at work and I'm constantly choosing between two models that both fit the little bit of data I have. I mention that because I suspect it isn't a neutral detail about me — but I genuinely dont know which way it cuts.
