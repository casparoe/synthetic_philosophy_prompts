I'm on a small committee trying to write a restitution policy for our museum, and I've built an argument I like for the first five minutes and then hate, because it won't stop.

(1) A transfer made under credible threat of force transfers no valid title — coercion voids consent.
(2) A title that is void at the moment of transfer can't be repaired downstream: no good-faith purchase, no gift, no bequest, no lapse of time can turn it into a valid one, since you can't hand on what you don't have.
(3) So any object with a coerced transfer anywhere in its custody chain is held right now without valid title, whoever holds it and however long ago it happened.
(4) The Benin Bronzes were carried out of Benin City by British troops in 1897 — coercion about as pure as it gets — so they should go back. I'm happy with that.
(5) But nearly every object that's a few centuries old and has crossed a border has a coerced transfer somewhere in its history: conquest, tribute, pillage, forced sale, tax collected under a regime nobody chose.
(6) So nearly everything in our collection — and the Met's, and the Louvre's, and the National Museum in Cairo's — is currently held without valid title, including items with no plausible claimant that nobody is demanding back.
(7) And by the same reasoning so is most land, most university endowments, and my house: if coercion anywhere in the past voids title, then nothing is legitimately owned by anybody.

I'm confident about (4) and I'm not willing to accept (7). One more wrinkle: the Parthenon Marbles, where the removal wasn't straightforwardly coerced at all — Elgin operated with the permission of whoever was governing Athens at the time, whatever exactly that amounted to — and yet my sense that they should go back is just as strong, which makes me suspect (1) isn't the load-bearing premise anyway.

Wich step is actually broken? I'd like to keep (1), (2) and the conclusion of (4) if I can. What I can't see is whether the honest repair is to bite down on (2) and let time and good faith cure a void title (which sounds like saying that if you wait long enough an injustice quietly becomes a justice), or to say (3) runs legal and moral title together, or to just swallow some mild version of (7). Tell me where the defect is and what each fix costs me — and most importantly, whether any fix that still hands back the Bronzes also hands back everything else, or whether there's a repair that stops at the colonial cases without being obviously ad hoc.
