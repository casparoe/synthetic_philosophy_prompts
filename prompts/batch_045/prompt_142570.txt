I'm taking an upper-level seminar this term on rational choice and the foundations of decision theory, and we recently spent a week on the Allais paradox and the independence axiom of expected utility theory. In the usual presentation, the first pair of options asks us to choose between a certain million and a gamble that offers a ten percent chance at five million, an eighty-nine percent chance at the million, and a one percent chance at nothing; the second pair asks us to choose between an eleven percent chance at the million and a ten percent chance at five million, with the remaining probability in each case giving nothing. Most people choose the certain million in the first pair and the ten-percent chance at five million in the second pair, and that combination is inconsistent with expected utility theory once independence is assumed. Class discussion split along the familiar lines: either the Allais preferences are an irrational bias that normative theory should explain away, or they are a rational response that shows independence is too strong and should be replaced by some weaker, non-expected-utility framework such as a rank-dependent or betweenness-satisfying theory.

I was discussing this with a couple of friends in the philosophy department after class, and we got stuck on what strikes me as an underexplored assumption. Both the defenders and the critics of independence seem to take for granted that the two decision problems are simply alternative descriptions of the same underlying probabilities, so that the only thing the paradox could be telling us is something about attitudes to risk or the weighting of outcomes. My half-formed idea is that the paradox might instead be revealing a failure of what I would call reduction invariance: the assumption that it makes no difference to an agent's evaluation whether a lottery is modeled as a single-stage gamble over final outcomes or as a compound, multi-stage gamble in which an earlier chance node determines which later sub-lottery is played. If agents do not in general reduce compound lotteries in the way the standard formalization assumes, then the Allais pattern might not be a direct strike against independence at all; it might be a strike against a prior and possibly separable structural assumption about how to represent the sequential resolution of uncertainty. I want to write my term paper on exactly this, but I'm not sure whether the question is tractable in one semester or whether it quietly collapses back into the standard debate about whether to keep or abandon independence.

Could you turn this into a proposal for a 5,000–6,000 word philosophy-of-decision-theory term paper, to be written over one semester? I need the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, an explanation of why the usual pro- and anti-independence readings leave it open, and an approach that would actually make progress—ideally a month-by-month method saying what I would do, rather than the obvious alternative of just surveying non-expected-utility theories. Please lay out what the possible conclusions would be and which considerations would decide between them, what would count as the project failing (for example, if the distinction between reduction invariance and independence turns out to be extensionally equivalent after all, or if the question cannot be separated from merely empirical psychology), and give an honest assessment of whether this is too big for a semester and how I should scope it down. Don't bother with citations or a bibliography; I will add those myself, and I don't need you to search for anything. I would rather you work conceptually and tell me plainly if my half-formed distinction is spurious or already implicit in the usual axiomatisations.
