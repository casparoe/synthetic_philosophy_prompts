I hold a reconciliationist reading of Hume's essay on the standard of taste: aesthetic judgement rests on sentiment rather than on the detection of a property, yet there is still a standard, located in the durable, considered verdict of critics who satisfy the usual qualifications — trained discrimination, wide comparison, practice, freedom from prejudice — so that disagreement surviving that filter can be written off as a fault on one side. What would count against this package, and how decisive is each item? Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, separating what a defender of the view should itself concede as counting against it from what it may reasonably dismiss, and take the empirical ones seriously rather than setting them aside: the pattern of belated canonisation, where work now central to a tradition was rejected by the best-qualified critics of its own day, and path-dependence, the fact that a verdict is sensitive to order and contrast of exposure, which makes the joint verdict look ill-defined when the critics are spread across history rather than simultaneous. The case I would personally find most persuasive is one in which qualified critics reverse a verdict with no change in the work and no discoverable defect in the earlier critics — say whether such cases are real, and whether the view can describe them at all. I also cannot settle a dilemma: either the joint verdict tracks something independent of itself, which makes the view more realist than its sentimentalist premises permit, or it does not, and the standard becomes definitional and collapses into the relativism it was meant to answer — which horn is worse, and is there a coherent middle? Finally, say plainly whether the escape hatches (prejudice, want of practice, defective delicacy) have insulated the view into unfalsifiability, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind, and whether the view can explain why I should defer to the joint verdict rather than merely record it. Please write in British English.
