I run a monthly rationality workshop — a Saturday afternoon slot, 18 regulars, 90 minutes, mostly working programmers plus a few philosophy-adjacent people. They've all been through calibration training and at least one Aumann-style convergence exercise (everyone privately estimates a quantity, then shares estimates and revises, everything scored with a proper scoring rule on a leaderboard, no money changing hands). For an upcoming session I want to stage peer disagreement itself: conciliationism versus steadfastness. My main constraint is that participants should feel both pulls before either view is named — no lecture up front, no session that degenerates into an argument about which theory is nicer. They should walk out having noticed that they applied different rules in different cases and not being sure they can justify the difference.

The wrinkle I keep chewing on, and that I'd like the activity built around, is disagreement where the evidence about your own evidence is itself unreliable. Concretely: each participant gets private evidence toward an estimate, and alongside it a noisy reading of how noisy that evidence is — a noise oracle that is right maybe 70 percent of the time — and then confronts a peer whose estimate and whose noise-oracle reading both differ from theirs. Some disagreements should be traceable: the participant can see the specific step where the peer's evidence passed through a corrupted channel, or can inspect the peer's reasoning line by line and locate the slip. Others should be bare: no visible flaw at all, just an equally smart, equally calibrated person who landed somewhere else. My hope is that the same people who happily split the difference on bare disagreements will refuse to move at all on traceable ones, and that we can then ask whether the traceable cases were ever really peer disagreement, or whether conciliation was never about counting heads in the first place. I also want one round that turns the machinery on itself: pairs disagree about how much they themselves should have updated in the previous round, so that whatever rule they endorse has to apply to disagreement about that rule. I've been calling the resulting worry self-erosion: the fact that a conciliationist who meets an equal who denies conciliationism seems required to discount the very principle she was using, and that this looks like a bug to some people and a feature to others. The activity should make that visible without me naming it.

What I need from you: the setup and materials (I can print cards and use one phone for randomization), the pairings or roles, a minute-by-minute 90-minute timeline, the payoff scheme (leaderboard points only), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific moves each view makes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is that at least one participant just treats the noise oracle as one more piece of first-order evidence and the whole distinction collapses, but you tell me — and be frank about which feature of the actual debate the game cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand. Please don't include a reading list, citations, or suggestions for further reading; I have the literature side covered and I don't want to guess at references I can't check. What I do want is a short closing note separating what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is contested, and flagging anywhere your design presupposes a contested reading — in particular on whether peers can be identified non-circularly, and on whether conciliation should extend to higher-order disagreement about conciliation itself — so I don't walk in presenting one side's framing as the consensus.
