I am a novelist, and my next book — two years of work, if I am honest about it — is about a mid-ranking official in the railway ministry of a country I have invented. He hates nobody; he is simply very good at his job, which is moving people from one place to another on schedule. Before I commit to the thing, I have written a short preface setting out a rule of composition I intend to follow, and I would like your comments on it. The draft follows.

[1] Hannah Arendt's phrase has been handled so often that it has gone smooth, and I want to recover its rough edge by moving it. As it is usually received, the banality of evil is a claim about a kind of person: the offender as a dull, cliché-ridden mediocrity, in her words "neither perverted nor sadistic… but terribly and terrifyingly normal". Read that way, the phrase has become a licence for a familiar scene — the functionary eats his sandwich, the reader shivers, the novel has done its work. I want to argue that the interesting claim is not about persons at all. Banality, I suggest, is a property of sentences, not of souls.

[2] I mean something technical. In my drafts I write what I call the throughput sentence: a sentence whose grammatical subject is cargo rather than agent, whose verb is one of arrival, processing, transfer or loss, and in which nothing a reader could call a feeling appears. Of the 4,100 manifested at Vireux, 3,860 went forward on the night service; 240 were held for the morning. My claim is that the moral weight of such a sentence is not in it but behind it, supplied by the reader, and that the prose is under an obligation not to help. The moment I add that most of the 240 were children, I have taken the reader's share of the work away and handed back something smaller in exchange: a shock, and permission to feel that they have done something by feeling it.

[3] If this is right, it re-describes what Arendt was seeing. She called the thing she saw in that courtroom "word-and-thought-defying" — defying, that is, the apparatus of description itself. Officialese was not a mask worn over the man's thinking; it was the shape his thinking had taken. The flatness of my sentences is therefore not a style chosen to portray a flat man. It is an attempt to let the reader occupy the same deficit, and to discover for herself that the deficit is comfortable.

[4] The risk I cannot see my way past, and the reason I am bothering with a preface, is what I call exculpatory flatness. A flat register does not only withhold condemnation; it withholds the materials for it, and it quietly turns my official into a figure of pathos — a tired man with a headache and a train to fill. Pity is the wrong ending, and I think it is a worse wrong ending than horror, because it is quiet, and because it lets everybody off.

[5] So the rule will be broken exactly once. At the very end, one sentence will take a person rather than a number as its subject, and the book will stop. I am not certain this exception redeems the method or simply confesses that the method cannot end a book on its own.

What I would like from you: first, whether my central distinction — banality as a property of sentences rather than of persons — is doing any real work, or is only a relabelling of what everyone already says about this; second, whether I have been unfair to Arendt in pressing her phrase into a claim about prose (I care about fairness to the thought rather than about the wording); third, whether the throughput sentence will actually do what I claim — whether readers really do complete flat administrative prose with horror, or whether a good many will simply read a logistics manual and feel nothing at all.

Please reply as notes keyed to my paragraph numbers, and add one line ranking all five paragraphs from strongest to weakest, plus a single sentence on what is wrong with whichever you place last. The whole response must be in British English and must not exceed 400 words.
