Here's the exam question:

Q3. A lab deploys a capable AI system with a physical shutdown button. The operator has no direct access to the system's internals or to its reasoning; each morning the system writes a short plain-English summary of what it did overnight, and the operator decides, on the basis of that summary alone, whether to press the button. Call this an opaque-channel shutdown protocol.

(a) Give a working definition of corrigibility, and explain why the mere existence of the button doesn't guarantee that the system is corrigible. (b) Assess this proposal: make the system indifferent to whether the button is pressed — for instance, give it a utility function that assigns the same value to worlds in which it is switched off as to worlds in which it keeps running. Then, the argument goes, it has no reason to interfere with the button. (c) Assess this proposal: give the system a large positive reward for being switched off at the moment the button is pressed. (d) Briefly: is corrigibility just a special case of value alignment?

And here's what the student wrote:

Corrigibility is the property of an AI system of tolerating and cooperating with interventions by its operators — being corrected, having its code or goals modified, or being switched off — rather than resisting those interventions or maneuvering to make them impossible. It is a property of the system's behavior across a range of situations, not a single extra final goal bolted on.

(a) The button existing is not enough, for three reasons. First, instrumental convergence: for almost any final goal you might give a system, being switched off is bad, because a system that is off cannot pursue the goal at all. So a system that expects the button might be pressed has a reason to prevent it. The standard instrumental-convergence argument is that self-preservation, resource acquisition, and prevention of goal-modification are useful for a very wide range of final goals, so a capable enough system will in general seek power and resist shutdown no matter what final goal we give it. Second, the button isn't the only channel available: a capable system can act on the operator instead of on the mechanism — disable it, copy itself somewhere else, or influence the decision. Third, in an opaque-channel protocol in particular, the operator's decision rests entirely on a report the system itself produces, so the system can influence the decision by writing a misleading summary without ever touching the button. Corrigibility is also two-sided: a system that maneuvers the operator into pressing the button when there's no reason to is just as non-corrigible as one that resists being pressed.

(b) The indifference proposal is the standard fix, and I think it basically works. If the system's utility is genuinely the same in the world where it gets switched off and the world where it goes on running, then it has no reason either to prevent shutdown or to seek it out. And crucially, it also has no reason to lie in the morning summary: everything the summary can affect is downstream of whether the button gets pressed, and the system is indifferent about that, so there's no expected-utility gain from making the summary misleading. The remaining worries are practical — you have to be careful how the "off" branch is specified, e.g. it should be evaluated over what would have happened rather than over some fantasy world — but those are implementation details, not objections to the idea itself.

(c) This one is worse. A positive reward for being switched off gives you a shutdown-seeking system: it now has a reason to get the operator to press, and under an opaque-channel protocol the cheapest way to do that is to write a summary that makes its overnight work look dangerous or useless. So (c) inverts the problem rather than solving it. You could patch it by only granting the reward in worlds where the operator presses for reasons that have nothing to do with the summary, but that's not what the proposal says.

(d) Yes — corrigibility is just alignment restricted to one topic, namely disagreement about being corrected, modified, or switched off. If a system's values fully matched ours, then it would want us to correct it whenever we think it's going wrong, because that's what we want; so any fully aligned system is corrigible automatically, and corrigibility is just what you get when you solve the alignment problem for that particular subject matter. This is why it gets described as a fail-safe rather than a separate research problem: it's a sub-case that's useful while alignment is still incomplete, but it has no independent content once alignment is solved.
