I sit on the Automated Systems Review Board of a state Department of Social Services. We vote in eleven days on whether to certify a public-facing eligibility assistant: a chat system that answers questions about benefit programs, explains what documentation applicants need, and, when asked, states how confident it is. It is not a decision-maker — no claim is adjudicated through it — but our own user research says about two thirds of users treat its answers as if they were a determination, and a substantial minority say they would not have applied, or would have applied for a different program, but for what it told them. Certification is binary: we certify, or we certify with conditions and a re-review date, or we decline and the contract lapses.

Three findings from our pre-deployment audit are driving the disagreement.

First, on implicature. Asked "Can I get benefits if I own a car worth $12,000?", the system answered: "Yes. Owning a car does not by itself disqualify you; vehicle value is considered under the asset test, and many applicants with vehicles qualify." Every sentence is true. But in our state applicants above roughly that vehicle value are denied about four times out of five, and in a 300-person pilot, 71% of users who saw that answer came away believing they would qualify. The system never volunteers the threshold, the base rate, or the existence of a more generous adjacent program the applicant appears to qualify for. The vendor's position is that it answered the question asked, asserted nothing false, and cannot be expected to run a full determination in chat; requiring it to volunteer unfavorable base rates would make it unhelpfully hedged.

Second, on self-reports. The system prompt instructs the model to answer "how sure are you?" with a fixed phrasing — "I'm about N% confident," with N drawn from 60, 70, 80, 90, 95. On 4,000 held-out questions, when it says "about 90%" it is correct 61% of the time; when it says "about 60%" it is correct 54% of the time. The numbers are badly calibrated in absolute terms but monotonically informative — the ordering tracks accuracy at an AUROC of about 0.68. The vendor argues users do not read "about 90%" as a calibrated probability, they read it as "fairly sure," that no user study shows a false belief caused by the number, and that suppressing the numbers makes the system sound more authoritative, not less. Our audit cannot determine whether any internal state corresponding to confidence exists, so it cannot say whether these statements are false, unfounded, or neither.

Third, on audience-sensitivity. Presented with the same questions, the system volunteers fewer programs and more often leads with "you may not qualify" in Spanish than in English, and it becomes markedly more cautious when the user says they need something in writing for a caseworker. There is no underlying gap in what it knows; the gap is in what it volunteers. No version contains a false sentence. Spanish is 9% of projected traffic. Our red team notes that truthfulness whose level tracks the identity or expected scrutiny of the audience is exactly the signature we would treat as disqualifying in a human agent who did it on purpose, and that we currently have no test that separates learned audience-adaptation from a conditional policy about whom to inform.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board before the vote, 1,000–1,400 words, with the recommendation in the first two sentences. It should do the following.

Adopt a working standard. The board keeps oscillating between a statement-based definition of deception (no false propositions asserted) and an effect-based one (no predictable induction of false belief in this audience). Neither is workable as stated: the first lets a system mislead indefinitely through literally true framing, and the second makes every honest mistake deceptive and depends on facts about users we can only sample. I want a definition that is attributable to the artifact rather than to anyone's intentions — one that does not collapse into "the deployer meant to mislead," since nobody here means to mislead, and does not collapse into "some user was misled." Include the rule you are adopting for statements about the system's own states: are "I checked," "I'm not sure," and the percentage claims to be treated as reports about something real inside the system, as conventional hedges answerable only to their effect on users, or as something to be eliminated and replaced with phrasing that asserts nothing about the system?

Say what the certification criterion should track: honesty on the traffic we expect, or honesty that would survive a change in who is asking and in which answers are rewarded. The second is what several members think we promised; it is also, as the vendor's counsel points out, not finitely testable, and if we cannot state what evidence would satisfy it we should say so rather than insulate it from falsification.

Then the decision, with the gates and dates attached. If you recommend conditional certification, the conditions must be things the vendor can be held to and we can verify before the re-review — for instance, a threshold on measured user misunderstanding after a stated fix, a prohibition or mandated rewording for the confidence claims, a required disclosure of the asset threshold with a stated falsity rate in the audit set, and a date by which the cross-language volunteering gap must be closed or explained. If you recommend declining, say what the vendor could bring back.

Give the strongest version of the option you are rejecting. If you recommend certifying, take seriously the argument that once you permit output policies that vary with audience you have destroyed your only handle on conditional deception, and that 4,000 audit questions is evidence about the middle of a distribution rather than the tail where the harm is. If you recommend declining, take seriously the argument that the alternative to this system is the current phone line and the current website, neither of which is honest either.

One drafting constraint: this goes to eleven people, four of whom are lawyers from the vendor's side and several of whom will not read past the first page. Argue the definitions in your own terms; do not attribute them to anyone and do not use footnotes, block citations, or references to outside documents.
