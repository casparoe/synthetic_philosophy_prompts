I want a map of the exposure of a position I hold about rational disagreement. Take Aumann's agreement theorem as the starting point: agents who share a common prior, whose information is given by partitions of a fixed state space, and who have common knowledge of each other's posteriors must assign the same probability to the event in question. My view is that the theorem is best read as a constraint on information structures rather than as a verdict on real disagreement, and that most persistent disagreement among apparently rational and honest speakers is explained by a failure of the fixed-algebra assumption rather than by irrationality or by divergent priors. The version I have been developing — call it the latent re-description model — says that in an extended discussion the proposition under contention is repeatedly re-expressed over a finer state space, so that at no round are the two speakers conditioning on partitions of a common algebra; the theorem then never gets a grip, and disagreement can persist indefinitely between fully Bayesian agents with a genuinely common prior over the finest description available. Tell me what would count against this. Which formal developments, which stylized cases, and which observable features of actual disputes should move me — described in substance rather than by name, since I cannot look anything up? I especially want to know what would discriminate my explanation from the flat rival that the priors were never common in the first place: what signature would real disagreements have to exhibit for one of these to be the better explanation? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly which ones I, as a defender, should concede rather than reinterpret away. I already suspect my weakest point: if re-description can always be invoked after the fact, the model explains every disagreement and predicts none, and I want to know whether it can be stated in a form that genuinely risks being wrong, or whether that risk is the price of the view.
