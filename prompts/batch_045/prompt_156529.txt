hi — im in an undergrad seminar on historical judgement and moral responsibility, and I tried to build an argument that makes the "man of his time" defensible without making it useless, and instead it seems to eat everything. can someone spot the step that's broken?

(1) Call the man-of-his-time defence (MHOT): a historical figure isn't blameworthy for a moral failing if that failing was near-universal in their society at the time.

(2) Near-universality on its own feels too cheap — slaveholding was normal in Jefferson's Virginia, but plenty of people in his own world (Quaker abolitionists, the abolition societies whose pamphlets he could have read, some of his own correspondents) said it was wrong while knowing roughly what he knew. So I patched it with what I'm calling the Dissent Condition: MHOT only excuses when there was no available dissenter — nobody in the agent's own society, with roughly comparable evidence and education, who publicly said the practice was wrong.

(3) Jefferson fails the Dissent Condition for slavery — he even wrote the bit about all men being created equal — so MHOT doesn't get him off. Same for Columbus: there were absolutely people in that period, including inside the Spanish court, saying the enslavement of the indigenous population was wrong. Good, that's the result I wanted.

(4) But now run it on us. Whatever our descendants pick as our monstrous thing — eating animals, flying, pensions invested in fossil fuels — there are already people right now, with the same evidence we have, saying it's monstrous. So the Dissent Condition is satisfied basically always, and everyone in every era is blameworthy for everything.

(5) That conclusion can't do the work I need: it can't distinguish Jefferson from Columbus from Churchill, and it makes MHOT never available, which collapses into the opposite absurdity where we can never criticise anyone from the past at all.

(6) So one of 1–4 is wrong.

I'd rather keep step 3 than keep whatever generates step 4. Which premise do I give up, why did it look fine when I wrote it, and what does dropping it cost me? (My prof also keeps insisting that what we're actually doing with statues and building names is commemoration rather than blame, and I can't tell if that's a way out or just a distraction.)
