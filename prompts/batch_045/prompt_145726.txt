Bit of a strange one, but I've somehow ended up sitting on my hospital's clinical ethics comittee — it was supposed to be a single meeting, two years ago — and there are two things we do there that I can't get to sit together. I want to know what each of them commits us to about what life *is*.

The first: we certify patients dead on neurological criteria, no brain-stem reflexes and no drive to breathe, whilst the ventilator keeps the heart going, the skin heals, wounds knit, infections get fought off, and in the rare pregnant cases the unit has carried a foetus for months inside somebody we have already signed off as dead. Legally that's a corpse. Biologically it's doing an awful lot of the stuff I put on the first slide of my philosophy of biology lectures.

The second: we run a dead-donor rule. Nobody may take organs from someone who isn't dead, but the organs have to be alive when they come out. So the instant death is declared we switch from "this is a patient, protect them" to "this is a set of parts, keep each one warm and perfused", and in donation after circulatory death we stand about and count off a short no-touch interval before anyone lays a hand on the body. How long that interval is varies by protocol, and everyone in the room knows it's a bit of a fudge, but nobody knows what would settle it.

So: reconstruct the account of life — and of where life is located, organism or part or process — that this pair of practices is running. Is it one account, or is the hospital simply doing different metaphysics depending on whether it's treating or procuring? What I'd like, concretely:

- what the practices strictly presuppose, meaning what would actually break if we gave that bit of the concept up, as opposed to what merely sounds natural alongside it;
- where they're underdetermined, so that several rival accounts of life would license exactly the same behaviour and the practice tells us nothing at all;
- and whether the two halves are even consistent with each other.

I've also been playing with a criterion of my own that I'd rather have stress-tested than indulged. Call it the subsidy criterion: a system is alive iff the work of holding it far from thermodynamic equilibrium is being done by that system rather than by something outside it. On that view the ventilated body doesn't count — the gas exchange, the temperature control, the fluid balance are all being done *to* it — and it fails in the same way a flask of cells in an incubator fails, which is why nobody thinks a cell line that's been dividing for decades is an organism however busily it metabolises. But it plainly over-reaches: someone on ECMO, heart-lung bypass, for six weeks who then wakes up was surely alive the whole time, and a newborn is alive while its mother's body does nearly all of its regulation for it. So I want to know whether the hospital's practice is actually committed to something like the subsidy criterion, in which case my over-reach is their problem too, or whether they're better off saying "no, the body is alive, it's just not a person any more" and getting everything they need out of a completely different distinction — life versus personhood, organism versus agency, whatever it is.

If you take that second route, tell me what would have to change in practice if we swapped the subsidy criterion in instead: would we owe the ventilated body something we don't currently owe it, would the dead-donor rule collapse or become trivial, would keeping a brain-dead pregnant woman going for months become indefensible, and would anything change about the embryos? On which: the IVF side of the same hospital freezes embryos for years and then uses them, hands them to research, or bins them, and there's a legal limit on how long an embryo may be kept growing in vitro. Frozen gets treated as neither dead nor alive. If your reconstruction has a principled story about the thawed embryo, that's a point in its favour; if it just shrugs, say so, I'd rather recieve the bad news.

No citations or reading lists please — I can look those up myself and half the time I can't get hold of the journals anyway. And write the answer in American English; I'm recycling chunks of this for a paper with a co-author in the States and I'm fed up of changing "metabolise" into "metabolize".
