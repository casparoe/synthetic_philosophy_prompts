I chair the responsible-AI review board at a mid-sized software company: six engineers, a product manager, and two lawyers, none of whom work on machine learning, and who have absorbed AI safety mostly from news coverage plus one internal lunch talk on RLHF that left them with the impression that alignment is a training-recipe problem that human feedback essentially solved. I have forty-five minutes with them in two weeks, and what I want them to leave with is an understanding of why scalable oversight is a genuine bottleneck and of what the debate proposal for oversight is actually claiming — not that it works, but what would have to be true for it to work, and how narrow its conditions are. Please don't explain the idea to me; plan how I should present it.

So: what has to be established first, and why does everything else depend on it? My instinct is that the bottleneck has to be felt before the proposal can even be heard — that the moment we are asked to sign off on a system that is better than the board at the task it is doing, judging the output and checking the output come apart, and every oversight intuition they already have (hire better reviewers, make the model show its reasoning and read that, spot-check samples, red-team it) quietly assumes the two are the same thing. Relatedly, one of the engineers has circulated a cheaper variant he calls self-cross-examination: a single model produces an answer, then produces the strongest case against its own answer, and a human picks whichever survives. I want to know where in the sequence I should deal with it, because explaining why it fails might be the cleanest way into why two adversarial debaters are doing any work at all — but I also worry it will look like I am swatting down his suggestion before I have motivated the problem.

I also want help with the carrying example. I was going to use a disputed figure buried in an enormous expense or compliance report: the board cannot recompute the total, but it can check any single line, and disagreement can be narrowed by bisection. Tell me what such an example has to have and, as importantly, what it must lack; why the usual illustrations — judging which of two images contains a given object, or which of two routes is faster — would do less work for this particular audience; and why I should not carry the whole session on the kind of case the board actually cares about, is this deployment safe?, which I suspect has exactly the wrong structure. Then: the misconceptions to expect and precisely where in the sequence to head each off. I am fairly confident about four — that debate is just truth winning out because truth is persuasive; that the judge needs to know nothing at all, so debate removes rather than relocates the need for trustworthy judgment; that adversarially trained debaters will converge on honesty the way markets converge on prices; and, from the lawyer who reads about steganographic collusion, that the two models will simply agree to fool the judge. I am much less sure how to place the worry that the debate itself grows too long or too technical for the judge to follow, since I suspect that objection is different in kind from the others and may be the one that matters most for a board like mine. Finally: the single question I can ask at the end that would show me the structure has landed rather than the vocabulary — my candidate is to hand them a fresh task and ask what, exactly, a weak judge would need to be able to check cheaply and reliably for the protocol to help, treating cannot be named as a good answer — and what I should openly decline to settle in forty-five minutes, saying out loud that I am declining it.
