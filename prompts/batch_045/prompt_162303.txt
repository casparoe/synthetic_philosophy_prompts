I'm a junior majoring in philosophy, and this term I'm in a seminar on race, aesthetics, and politics. We spent most of two weeks on the W. E. B. Du Bois essay about Black art — "Criteria of Negro Art," I think it's called, the one where he says that all art is propaganda and always must be, that he doesn't care about art that isn't used for propaganda, and that this is no license for bad art, because the propaganda has to be beautiful and true to count as art at all. I finished the reading convinced, and then realized that being convinced hasn't changed a single thing I actually do, which makes me suspect I understood it less well than I thought.

Here is what I mean. I sit on the student exhibitions committee for the gallery in our student union: twice a month, in a ninety-minute meeting, six to ten proposals for small commissions — anywhere from $500 to $3,000 — from student and local artists for work to be installed in the building or shown in the gallery cases. We vote, we write a paragraph of reasons that goes back to the applicant, and the gallery director reviews what we recommend and can veto it (she has, once, over insurance and once over a work that would have needed a structural engineer). Our rubric is three lines — artistic merit, message, fits the space — and it is useless, because every real disagreement we have turns out to be a fight inside the first line. I would like to replace it with a decision procedure built honestly out of Du Bois's position: art should be propaganda; propaganda that isn't made well isn't art; and craft that carries nothing is decoration. Please write me that procedure as an ordered set of questions a committee of non-specialists can run in about fifteen minutes per proposal, each with a stop rule: what counts as passing, what counts as an immediate no, and which questions have to be kicked to a person — the director, the artist, the people depicted — because they are disagreements about values rather than facts and no checklist can settle them. Keep the distinctions in Du Bois's view that are actually doing the work and drop the ones that only matter in a seminar paper, and be blunt about where a rubric like this will misfire.

Two half-formed ideas of my own, which you should use or discard as you see fit. The first is a subtraction test: take the political content away and ask whether the object still holds up as a made thing, then take the craft away and ask whether the message still reaches anyone who wasn't already persuaded — so that a slogan on a beautiful surface and a beautiful surface with a slogan stapled to it both fail, for different reasons. The second is that propaganda has an addressee, and it matters who it is: a work addressed to the people it depicts, or to the people it wants to move, seems to me a different animal from a work whose real content is that its sponsor is a decent institution, and I don't know what Du Bois's view implies about that second kind. Then run the finished procedure on three kinds of proposal we get constantly. One: a large, genuinely accomplished mural for the dining hall wall about the scheduling of subcontracted food-service workers, proposed by a student labor group, with a student artist whose portfolio is strong and a facilities office that is quietly unhappy. Two: a polished, professionally produced installation funded by the bank that handles the university's accounts — beautiful, costs us nothing, says that everyone on this campus belongs, and puts the bank's name on the wall label; we currently approve these cheerfully and I want to know whether we should. Three: a stack of photocopied posters from the tenants' union in the neighborhood next to campus, about a rent increase at a building the university's foundation has a stake in — the committee agrees with them completely, the people affected made it themselves, and the design is honestly bad; I suspect this is the case where the procedure says something we don't want to hear, and I want to see whether it has to say it or whether it can offer us a condition instead of a no. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — what it will get wrong, which of our decisions it will not touch, and where it will quietly turn into a vote dressed up as a test. Please write the whole thing in American English.
