I've been reading the rationalist and EA blogosphere for about a decade — the sequences, the Forum, the comment sections — and over the last few years I've drifted toward a suffering-focused account of welfare that I want audited before I write it up as a proper top-level post. I'm reasonably confident in each piece individually; what I can't tell is whether the pieces are jointly tenable. I have a nagging sense that the package either collapses into a version of hedonism with extra steps or quietly relies on something my official metaphysics of welfare rules out, and I can't locate which commitment is doing the work.

Here is roughly where I've landed.

1. *Tranquilism* as a theory of prudential value: the only basic prudential goods and bads are suffering (pain, distress, unpleasant experience) and craving (wanting, aversion, restlessness, boredom, the sense of being pushed). Pleasant experience that is not the stilling of some craving contributes nothing. I bite the bullet that a life of calm contentment and a life of ecstatic joy are equally good for the person living them, provided neither contains suffering or craving.

2. *Structural* craving is what does the real work. I've been using a distinction between occurrent craving — the felt urge you notice — and what I call structural craving: a standing disposition to be pulled toward or pushed away by things, which need never be felt. The contented person who could not sit still in an empty room for an hour; the busy striver who reports wanting nothing and is never at rest. I think structural craving is why the tranquilist critique of ordinary life is more than a quibble about moods, and I count it as bad for the person who has it.

3. *Experientialism* about the substrate: I'm a mental-state theorist in the strict sense — only what is, or is realized in, conscious experience can be good or bad for a person. This is a large part of why tranquilism appeals to me rather than an objective-list or desire-satisfaction view.

4. *Symmetry* about death and about unknown frustration: I accept the Epicurean symmetry argument and hold that death is not bad for the person who dies; likewise, I hold that the frustration of a desire you never learn about — the betrayed wish, the posthumous dishonor — is not bad for you.

5. *Two case verdicts* I'm fairly confident in. (i) A patient in severe pain who, through long training, has no aversion to it — not distressed, doesn't want it to stop, reports no craving — is not thereby made worse off by the pain. (ii) Someone who feels wonderful but is in the grip of constant wanting — the successful addict, the driven striver who enjoys every minute and is never satisfied — is doing badly in the respect that matters.

6. *Practice*: I spend real time and money on pleasant experiences — long dinners, music, multi-day hikes — and I recommend it to others without apology, and I think craving-reduction practice is the highest-expected-value thing I can do for my own welfare.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, ordered by severity rather than listed as equally bad. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix costs me — ideally as a revision to one commitment rather than a new thesis bolted on. Then name the single revision that buys the most consistency overall, and be explicit about what that revision commits me to that I'd find unwelcome. If any of my commitments is better treated as a verbal dispute or an ambiguity to disambiguate than as a substantive disagreement, say which one and why. Please answer in American English, around 900 to 1200 words.
