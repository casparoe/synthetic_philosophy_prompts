I am a PhD student in cosmology, and I am writing a short framing note for a seminar series my department is running next term under the working title "Does philosophy matter for physics?". Before I write it, I want a diagnosis of the dispute the series is named after, not a verdict on it.

The dispute: on one side, working physicists of considerable distinction have said that philosophy has not kept up with science and has sometimes actively obstructed it — Weinberg's polemic against philosophy as a guide to physics, Hawking's announcement that philosophy is dead because it has failed to track cosmological theory, Feynman's remark comparing philosophy of science to ornithology for birds. On the other side, philosophers and philosophically inclined scientists reply that the charge is self-undermining: the claims scientists make about what science can settle are not themselves scientific results, methodological commitments about induction, explanation, and what counts as evidence are unavoidable whether or not one owns up to them, and scientists who disclaim philosophy simply do it badly and unconsciously. That side also points to cases where conceptual work seems to have mattered: the reconstruction of simultaneity in special relativity, the interpretive arguments around quantum theory in its formative decades, and the debates over what a gene is in late twentieth-century biology.

This has now been going on, in essentially this form, for decades. Every move is anticipated, nobody appears to have changed their mind, and both sides can produce examples faster than the other can explain them away. I want to know what kind of stalemate that is.

Concretely, I want you to: separate the questions that are being run together under one heading — a historical-causal one about whether philosophical reflection in fact helped or hindered particular episodes; a pedagogical one about whether training in philosophy makes scientists better at science; a jurisdictional one about whether some questions science must answer are not empirically decidable; and a diagnostic one about whether philosophical training is useful in proportion to how well a field is currently doing; then tell me for each whether the disagreement about it is substantive, verbal, or a matter of the two sides answering different questions under a single label. I also want to know where, if anywhere, there is a genuinely empirical disagreement hiding inside the rhetoric — for instance about whether positivist scruples actually delayed the acceptance of atomism, or whether the conceptual clarifications in early relativity and quantum theory were caused by philosophy or were internal to physics and merely baptized philosophical after the fact.

I have two hypotheses, which I want tested rather than confirmed. The first is an illocutionary mismatch: the physicists' remarks are boundary-work and self-exhortation aimed at allocating prestige, attention, and graduate-student time inside the academy, while the philosophers' replies treat them as truth-evaluable theses to be refuted, so each exchange consists of a refutation arriving where a rallying cry was sent and vice versa. The second is that the marginal value of philosophy to a field is countercyclical — high when that field's basic concepts are coming apart, near zero during periods of consolidated normal science — so that the question as posed has no stable answer that is independent of the state of the questioner's own discipline. The second hypothesis matters to me personally because my own subfield has a live case: with eternal inflation, we do not have an agreed measure over the diverging pocket universes, and it is therefore genuinely unclear whether the theory predicts anything at all, which looks like a conceptual problem rather than a computational one.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Roughly nine hundred words. Do not give me a reading list, bibliography, or citations; I want the argument, not sources. Take my two hypotheses seriously enough to say clearly if either is mistaken or only half right, and close by saying what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a piece of empirical work, a rephrasing of the question — or by saying honestly that no progress should be expected and why.
