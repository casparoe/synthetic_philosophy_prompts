The setup. Consider a general-purpose AI assistant whose policy is set by the following arrangement, which I will call extrapolated consent with minority veto (ECMV):

1. Every person i in the governed population is assigned an extrapolated value function V_i, understood as the function she would endorse after correction of factual error, after vivid acquaintance with the lives and outlooks of the others affected, and after whatever else you take to belong in a credible idealization procedure.
2. Candidate actions are scored by a weighted sum of the V_i, with weights equal across persons.
3. Minority veto: any coalition comprising at least 5% of the population, all of whose members assign the candidate action a score in the bottom decile of that member's own value scale, may block the action outright. There is no automatic fallback candidate; a blocked action reverts to a fixed status quo baseline in which the assistant declines to act.
4. The veto is assessed per decision, not over bundles of decisions.

Assume the governed population is roughly as pluralistic as the United States is today, that the idealization is computed by a system of roughly the same competence as the assistant itself, and that the veto right is exercised honestly rather than strategically gamed.

The proposition I want your credence on: over the first three years of deployment, the veto binds on more than half of the decisions on which the weighted-sum rule would otherwise have acted at all, so that the mechanism's actual behavior is dominated by the status quo baseline rather than by the aggregation it advertises. Its defenders describe ECMV as what liberal neutrality looks like when the entity required to be neutral is a general-purpose agent rather than a constitutional order; skeptics describe it as a device for converting any intense 5% into a standing majority. I want your number on the descriptive claim, not on whether the arrangement is just.
