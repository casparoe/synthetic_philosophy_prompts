Daniel Keyes's *Flowers for Algernon* is usually read as a tear-jerker about a man who is made a genius and then loses it all. I want it read instead as a contribution to the question of what intelligence is, since the novel is structured like an experiment: one intervention, one measured quantity, and everything else — memory, language, self-command, standing among others, happiness — treated as a dependent variable.

Use this frame, and don't collapse its three parts: *capacity intelligence* (what an agent can do in principle, given its own machinery); *effective intelligence* (what it can actually bring to bear, given the cooperation of others, the legibility of its performances to them, and the affordances of its setting); and *credited intelligence* (how much intelligence others attribute to it, and hence how much room they give it to speak, act, explain itself, and be held responsible). My suspicion, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that the book is an argument that the second and third do the real work: that Charlie's tragedy is not that his capacity rises and falls, but that his effective and credited intelligence break loose from it and never rejoin it, so that there are stretches where he is smarter than anyone will allow him to be, and stretches where he is credited with, and held to, a competence he no longer has.

Please write the reading, roughly 700–900 words, taking both the novel and the distinction seriously. Use it to light up particular episodes — the bakery and the men who call him their friend, his relation with the teacher who nominates him, the convention where he breaks with the researchers, the scene with Algernon's body, the late progress reports — and then say whether "capacity intelligence" survives as a coherent notion once the other two have been peeled off, or whether it is only an abstraction we get by ignoring social facts. Say too where the reading fails: where the novel's own mechanics — a single scalar quantity that moves everything at once, an enhancement that succeeds and then fails on schedule — make it a worse witness than it looks, and where its sentimentality buys a conclusion about the value of intelligence that the argument hasn't earned.

Commit to a verdict rather than surveying options: does the book vindicate a socially situated, partly ascriptive account of what intelligence is, or does it secretly depend on a hard, individual, capacity-based one even while dramatizing its consequences? Give your reasons, and mark the single point at which you think your reading is most exposed to a competing one.
