Writing a paper on populism and I keep hitting the same wall: everyone from cable pundits to movement leaders invokes the will of the people, and nothing I have read tells me how you would actually check one of those claims. So before I read any more theories of it, I want the spec.

Fixing the purpose, since I think adequacy depends on it: I need a theory good enough to adjudicate a specific contested claim, not to argue about whether democracy is legitimate in general. The situation is always of the form: some leader says the people will policy X, or says that a parliament, court or referendum result has betrayed the will of the people, and the body applying the test is a constitutional court, an opposition party, or a journalist. Often there is no vote to look at.

So: give me the requirements as a table — requirement | the case that shows why it is a requirement | non-negotiable, or just a preference of one camp. Invent fresh cases rather than recycling the textbook ones. Here are the ones I already think belong on the list; tell me which are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudices of a camp I have been reading too much of:

- it has to be able to say who the people are in the case at hand before it aggregates anything — the boundary problem. My case: a national vote on damming a valley; the valley's 40,000 residents are 0.7% of the country and vote 80% against, the country votes 52% for. Whose will? Also the variant where the flooded valley is downstream in a neighbouring country.
- it has to distinguish the people from whoever happened to be allowed to vote. Call it the shadow demos: several hundred thousand long-term residents with no citizenship, living under the law that is being decided. Are they part of the people whose will is at issue? Does the theory owe an answer, or is silence acceptable?
- it cannot collapse willing into wanting or into what is good for them. Case: everyone in the demos wants X, would be better off under not-X, and says afterwards that they never willed X.
- it has to handle the claim made where nothing was ever expressed: no vote, no poll, just demonstrations and a leader speaking for them. This is most actual populist claims, so a theory that cannot do this is useless to me.
- it has to say whether intensity counts — 51% who barely care against 49% who would riot — and whether a will can be more or less formed, a settled one versus a mood.
- it has to survive the two-referendum case, where a majority backs A, a majority backs B, and a majority rejects A and B together, plus ordinary cycling, without just stipulating that the will is whatever the last vote said — given Arrow-flavoured results about aggregation.
- it has to say something about a will that changes, and about a will directed at the future: today's voters want a pension reform that the people of 2060 will hate.
- it has to be checkable in principle, so that a claim can be called false rather than merely contested — but not so strict that every real-world claim is false by construction, which would just be rigging the test.

Then two short paragraphs, under 200 words for the pair. First: which subset of the requirements do you think is jointly unsatisfiable? My suspicion is that demos-sensitivity, sensitivity to intensity, immunity to agenda control, and always yielding a determinate answer cannot all be had at once, but tell me if I have picked the wrong four. Second: which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and why.

Optionally and briefly — a sentence or two each — score the aggregative majoritarian picture, the deliberative picture, the view that the people form a single subject with a will no vote fully expresses, and the version on which the will is what they would will if properly informed.

Format: the table plus those two paragraphs, hard cap of 900 words for the whole thing. Do not cite anything or suggest reading — I cannot look any of it up, so argue from the cases.
