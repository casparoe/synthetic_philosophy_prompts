At my reading group last week someone shut down an argument by saying the agricultural revolution was the worst mistake in human history. The argument was about whether material and technological progress actually tracks well-being: his move was that the biggest material revolution in our history made people worse off, so we should be suspicious of every story about things steadily improving. I want to know what that one-liner could actually be claiming, precisely, since I suspect it's doing less work than it sounds like it's doing.

Can you lay out the readings as a menu, from trivial to strong? The distinctions I keep tripping over: worse for the people who actually made the switch versus worse for everyone since; average welfare versus total welfare, given that farming supports far more people, which might flip the verdict entirely; welfare versus inequality, hierarchy and property, which is a different complaint dressed in the same words; whether mistake implies somebody made a bad decision, since nobody voted for farming and each household may have been doing the rational thing under population pressure; and the lock-in point I haven't seen anyone make explicitly — once enough people farmed, population grew to the new carrying capacity, so going back meant mass starvation, which makes it irreversible rather than merely bad. Also: what is the original affluent society line supposed to mean, abundant goods, abundant leisure, or just few wants easily met?

For each reading say whether it is near-trivial, substantive and defensible, or probably false, and then the part I actually care about: which reading he would need for his argument to work, and whether anyone has decent reason to accept that one. Please don't get bogged down in the archeology — I want the conceptual map, not a verdict on the evidence.
