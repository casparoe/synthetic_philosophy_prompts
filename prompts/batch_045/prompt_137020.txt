You are a professional philosopher who works on decision theory and who also writes formal verse: you know the Newcomb literature cold, and you care about the difference between a poem that names a puzzle and a poem that gets an argument right. Please work this one out rather than dash it off.

What I want is a sestina — six stanzas of six unrhymed lines, followed by a three-line envoi — to hand out at the close of the last session of a graduate seminar on Newcomb's problem. My students have read the formalism, so the philosophy has to be right; it should also stand up as a poem for someone who has never heard of the problem.

The speaker is the simulation: the model of the chooser that the Predictor ran in order to fill the boxes, speaking to the chooser in the moment before the choosing, and unable to rule out that it is the one doing the choosing. Use the standard setup — an opaque box that visibly holds a thousand dollars, an opaque box holding either a million or nothing, you may take both or only the second — with one added stipulation: the prediction was made and the boxes filled and certified before the chooser was born, and the Predictor is long dead. Nothing in the poem may hint at backwards causation or at the chooser reaching into the past.

The six end-words are TAKE, LEAVE, MILLION, THOUSAND, PREDICT, BOX — the same words throughout, no synonyms, permuted by the rule that the last word of one stanza opens the next, then the first, then the second-last, then the second, and so on, folding the tail back over the head. The envoi takes all six, two per line. The structure should be doing philosophical work: the pattern of end-words is fixed before a single line is written, in the way the contents of the second box are fixed before a single line is spoken, and the music of the form is that six unchanging words keep arriving in an order the speaker did not choose.

The content has to survive a specialist. Specifically:

- Give the two-box argument in its strong form: within either state of the second box, taking both beats leaving the thousand by exactly a thousand, and show that this margin does not narrow as the Predictor's record improves — it is a thousand at every accuracy short of certainty, and at certainty the comparison collapses for a different reason, because one of the states you would need to compare never occurs. Let the argument's quiet premise show too: comparing within states assumes those states are independent of what you do.
- Give the one-box argument in its strong form: your reach is evidence about what the simulation did, the probability that the million is there given that you leave the thousand is high, and evidence about which world you are in is what you act on.
- Give the causal theorist a fair hearing: nothing you do now changes what is in the box, and the relevant counterfactual is that the box would be what it is either way. And let the why-ain'tcha-rich pressure be felt rather than asserted — the one-boxers walk out with the million.
- Do not let the evidential move look like mere credulity about correlations. Somewhere in the poem, put the contrast with the smoking-lesion style of case, where a gene causes both a lesion and a taste for smoking, and where acting on the evidence looks like bad advice; and put the reply that separates them, which is that in Newcomb what your decision procedure outputs is what the simulation showed, while in the lesion case your procedure settles nothing about whether you carry the gene. Call that subjunctive dependence if you like, and let it be recognisable as what functional decision theory tries to isolate.
- Do not adjudicate. The envoi should leave both arguments audible; a reader who knows the problem should be able to say which lines belong to the dominance picture and which to the evidential one, and why they cannot both be the last word.

Avoid the cheap vocabulary — no paradox, no fate, no free will, no quantum, no mysticism — and avoid filler lines that exist only to land an end-word: if a line is there for the word, make the word do argumentative work in it.

Before the poem, give a short planning block: a table of the six end-words and what each stands for, the permutation you are using, and one line on the job of each stanza. After the poem, give an audit of no more than 150 words: name one line a causal decision theorist would reject and say why, and one an evidential decision theorist would reject and say why. If you cannot find one of each, the poem has blurred the disagreement, and you should revise it.
