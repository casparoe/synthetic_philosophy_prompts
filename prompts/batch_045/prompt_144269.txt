So — I'm on our hospital's serious incident review panel, and about six weeks ago my dad died in the same hospital. Sepsis. In on the Tuesday, sent home, back on the Thursday, gone by Friday. Both times my stepmother Marisol was with him — 19 years together, never married, and English is her second language — and both times she told the nurses he wasn't himself, kept asking the same question over, his skin felt wrong. The chart says "no objective findings, partner appears anxious." Nobody acted until it was too late.

Here's what the panel is actually deciding. Our retrospective case-finding runs off a trigger tool that only reads clinician-authored fields, so anything a family member said that didn't get charted as a clinical datum basically doesn't exist for us later. I've declared the conflict and I'm recusing from the vote on Dad's case, but I'm still the one drafting the options paper on whether lay observations count as evidence in review at all.

I've been reading about epistemic injustice, and I get the two standard kinds: the credibility-deficit one, where a speaker gets less credence than she's owed because of who she is, and the shared-concept gap one, where somebody can't get what she's experiencing into words that carry any weight. But I want to push on something I don't see handled well, which I've started calling inscription failure: the wrong where a claim may have been half-believed at the time, but it never got written down in a form that survives into the record, so it can't do evidential work for anyone later, however sympathetic. Two things I want you to actually argue about. First, is that a distinct kind of epistemic wrong or just the downstream effect of the credibility one? Second — and this is the bit I keep circling — the usual remedy is supposed to be a virtue in the listener, some habit of calibrating credence properly, but the listener here has rotated off shift and can't be trained retroactively; if the fix has to be structural, what does that tell us about where the wrong really lives?

What I need is a memo, not a survey: a recommendation we could vote on. Whether to admit lay observations as their own class of evidence in review, kept separate from clinical judgment and recorded verbatim with timestamps; whether to add a mandatory field to the incident system for "concern raised by family or visitor"; what a pilot should look like and for how long. Then the strongest version of the losing side, which is our clinical risk lead's: that this isn't about credibility at all, it's about diagnostic thresholds and hindsight bias, that family recall after a death is reconstructed and unreliable, and that upgrading lay worry to evidence just nudges everyone toward defensive medicine. Take that seriously and don't strawman it, then tell me why we do it anyway, or why we don't. And say one plain thing about repair — if we put Marisol down in the record as having been right, is that actually a repair to her, or just institutional bookkeeping after the fact?

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no sign-off, with the bottom line first.
