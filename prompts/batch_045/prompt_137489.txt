My supervisor kills every objection to our models with the same move: a model is like a map — a map as big as the territory would be useless, so leaving stuff out isn't a flaw, it's the point, and anyone who complains that an assumption is unrealistic (perfect rationality, zero transaction costs, a single representative agent) just doesn't get what models are for. It's what makes the as-if / F-twist defense feel obvious instead of desperate.

I want to audit it. Give me the correspondence table — territory, scale, legend, projection, map-reader — and then where it breaks. The bit I keep circling: I think "unrealistic assumption" covers two different things and the map only fits one of them. Some you can de-idealize: put a bit of friction back in, take a limit, and the result survives as a special case — the omission is like a map's scale, more detail available on demand. Others the model's result depends on for its life: no knob to turn, no limit to take, nothing to zoom into. Does the analogy quietly only cover the first kind?

Two more disanalogies I can't tell are load-bearing: a map doesn't change the territory, and there's exactly one territory it's a map of, checkable by walking around with a better instrument — but publishing a model can feed back into the system it models, and for plenty of the quantities we predict there's nothing independent to check against. Which of these actually matter for the work the analogy is doing (licensing false assumptions, dismissing the critic), and which are just pedantry? And if maps are the wrong picture for the second kind of idealization, what is — a caricature, a wind-tunnel model, something else?
