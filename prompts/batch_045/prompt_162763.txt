I'm a senior in high school, and the math club I help run has been doing a short series on probabilistic reasoning in number theory, which is how I ended up stuck on Fermat numbers for about two months. The setup is the standard one: F_n = 2^(2^n) + 1, the first five are prime, Euler refuted Fermat's conjecture by factoring F_5 = 4294967297 = 641 × 6700417, and every prime divisor of F_n for n ≥ 2 has to have the form k·2^(n+2) + 1. The heuristic we came up with is to pretend each F_n is just a random integer of its size, so by the prime number theorem it is prime with probability about 1/ln(F_n), which is about 1/(2^n ln 2); adding that up from n = 5 onward gives roughly 0.09, and one of my friends therefore says there is something like a nine percent chance of a sixth Fermat prime and considers the question basically settled. What bothers me is that almost all of that 0.09 is sitting on n = 5 through n = 32, and all of those Fermat numbers have already been shown to be composite, so once you condition on what is actually known you have to sum from n = 33 instead, which gives something like 3 × 10^-10 — several hundred million times smaller than my friend's number, even though we started from the same heuristic.

But now I am not sure the naive model is even the right way to formalize the independence assumption, and this is where I would really like help. The analogy I tried is Mersenne numbers: there, the same random-integer-of-its-size heuristic is apparently wrong in the other direction, since the standard conjecture I read about (I cannot reconstruct its derivation) predicts on the order of e^γ log₂ x Mersenne primes with exponent below x, which is far more than the naive estimate would give, and the reason is supposed to be that prime divisors of 2^p − 1 are forced into the classes ≡ 1 mod 2p and ±1 mod 8, so the object being tested is not a random integer but a random integer from a much thinner set. If I back out what that implies per exponent, the corrected probability exceeds 1/ln(2^p − 1) by a factor of about e^γ ln p; transferring that to Fermat numbers by treating the k·2^(n+2) + 1 constraint as the analogue gives a boost of about e^γ n ln 2, so a per-n probability of roughly e^γ n/2^n and a tail from n = 33 of about 1.4 × 10^-8 — still tiny, but around forty times the naive tail. A third thing I cannot reconcile is that I found a published analysis online, far past my level, claiming the chance of another Fermat prime is below one in a billion; that lands near both of my numbers but seems to get there a different way, and I cannot tell whether it is meant to override my analogy or is just the naive computation with sharper constants.
