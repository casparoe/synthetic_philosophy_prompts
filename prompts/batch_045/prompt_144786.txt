I'm in the Lower Sixth, and I'm writing my Extended Project on whether it makes any sense to say that history, taken as a whole, is going somewhere. What got me started was a school assembly for Human Rights Day, where a short film about the American civil rights movement ended on Martin Luther King Jr.'s line from the end of the Selma march: "How long? Not long, because the arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice." Since then I seem to hear some version of it every week. In a debating society debate last term on the motion that campaigning is always worth the effort, two speakers used it in opposite directions: one said that since justice is where history is headed anyway, our side will prevail whether or not any of us lifts a finger, so campaigning is really about personal character rather than outcomes; the other said the opposite, that we have an obligation to place ourselves on the right side of history, as if history kept a register. My history teacher, who I raised this with, more or less snorted and said it was Whig history with a gospel tune attached; my religious studies teacher said it was a secularised version of providence and that without God it is hard to see what could hold the arc up.

I have tried to sort this out myself and I keep going in circles. Sometimes it looks like a straightforward empirical forecast about a measurable trend, in which case I want to know what would count as measuring it — whose welfare is in the sample, over what window, and whether a century containing two world wars and the collapse of the project is a counterexample or just a local wobble on a very long curve. Sometimes it looks like it is not really a prediction at all but a way of declaring resolve, more like a battle cry than a barometer. Sometimes it looks worryingly close to a tautology, because we tend to anoint whatever wins with the name of justice after the fact, and then the line could not possibly be false. Sometimes it looks like a self-fulfilling prophecy, true only because enough people believe it and push — but then I cannot work out why the free rider is not simply being sensible, or what the claim's truth value is supposed to be if it depends on its own being accepted. And I am stuck on whether it needs a purposive agent behind it. My religious studies teacher's suggestion that this is providence in disguise worries me: if the bending is guaranteed by something like God, or by the nature of reason working itself out in time, then it is not up to us; but if it is guaranteed by nothing at all and merely happens when literacy, a free press and constitutional settlement happen to be in place, then it is a conditional claim about institutions rather than a claim about the direction of history, and it is not clear it survives the next century.

So I would like some help getting precise. Could you set out, as a numbered menu I can work through in the project, the distinct theses this sentence could be expressing — from the most anodyne to the most ambitious — and for each one say whether it is more or less trivial, whether it is a substantive claim someone could defend with real arguments, whether it is unfalsifiable, and whether it is probably false? The thing I most need is the last step: for each reading, tell me whether it would actually do the work people wanted it to do in that debate, that is, whether it supports the complacent speaker who thought victory was assured regardless of effort, or the one who thought it imposed a duty to be on the winning side, or neither. If it turns out that the complacent reading is the one that cannot survive precisification, I would like to be told that plainly, and to be told where the argument against it bites. I would rather have the menu with verdicts than an essay on progress in general, and I would rather you kept it to the claims themselves than to who said what about them.
