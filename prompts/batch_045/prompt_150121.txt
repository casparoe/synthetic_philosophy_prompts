I have been asked to present a lifetime achievement award to a retiring newspaper editor — call her M. — at our city's press club dinner in three weeks, and I have agreed. I'd like you to write the presentation speech.

About the evening. Roughly two hundred people: reporters and editors from three newsrooms, two First Amendment litigators, the paper's owners, M.'s children and grandchildren, and a handful of current and former city officials, at least two of whom were the subject of her reporting in the 1990s and are there out of obligation. Before the speech the room will be shown, without narration, about a dozen front pages from her forty-year career; the speech has to sit alongside those images rather than explain them. M. has asked for two things: no recitation of her "greatest hits" (her word for it is gloating), and no claim that her work "changed the city," which she says she does not believe and would be embarrassed to hear in public.

On the substance. I want the speech shaped by Hannah Arendt's account of political lying, as she gives it in the essay written in the wake of the Pentagon Papers and collected in *Crises of the Republic* (1972): factual truth as contingent and perishable, surviving only where people and institutions actively hold it and never asserting itself; the liar as the one who has the initiative while the teller of fact has none; and the modern lie as less a denial of inconvenient facts than an absorption of them into a coherent image or solution, so that nothing falsifiable is being asserted at all and the deceiver may have begun by deceiving himself. I want a speech that honors a career spent getting things onto the record while conceding none of the usual consolations: no "the facts spoke for themselves," no narrative of vindication, no diagnosis of anyone in the room — and still landing, for the non-journalists present, as warmth rather than as a seminar. Arendt should not be named in the speech; her distinctions should do their work in its structure.

Two ideas of my own that I want the speech to make real use of:

1. The frame-stripped fact. A statement that remains literally true but has been detached from whatever once made it bite — an old correction still in the archive but no longer anywhere near the story it corrected. Nothing false is asserted; the fact is intact and inert. It seems to me that Arendt's categories (lie, image-making, self-deception) don't quite cover this, because there is no falsehood anywhere, only an arrangement.

2. The context ledger. For every correction she ran, M. also recorded what it corrected, who asked for it, and where in the paper it appeared — a record about the record. My thought is that on Arendt's view this second-order practice, and not any individual scoop, is what gives facts any chance of surviving.

Please write six to seven minutes of spoken prose, and beneath it a note of about 250 words listing where the speech leans on a contested reading of Arendt, where it takes liberties with her ideas, and which of the claims above you take to be genuinely hers as against my paraphrase or invention. Flag anything you are unsure about rather than smoothing it over, and don't cite anything or suggest further reading — I have no use for a bibliography here. If the framing I've imposed is itself confused — if a speech built on refusing vindication can only collapse into false modesty or into a lecture, or if what I call the frame-stripped fact is just her image-maker under a new name — say that plainly instead of writing me something that flatters the premise.
