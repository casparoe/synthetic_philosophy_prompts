Suppose we try to get side-effect avoidance for free — without giving a system anything like a full specification of value — by declining to rank outcomes at all and penalizing instead changes in what other agents can do: for each other agent in the environment, compare the set of outcomes it could reach, and what reaching them would cost it, before and after our system acts, and penalize the difference. Call this the option-preservation measure, and call the hope behind it the thin-theory hope: that we can identify other agents and their option sets without ever saying which outcomes are better.

Please argue that the thin-theory hope fails, in three steps. First: the measure must decide whether an option is individuated by the outcome it makes reachable or by its causal-historical role, and neither answer is neutral — individuating by outcome requires ranking outcomes after all, while individuating by history permits option laundering, in which the agent destroys a capacity, installs a formally equivalent replacement, and logs zero net impact. Second: per-step penalization is not enough, since a long series of individually trivial erosions of other agents' options can jointly amount to their destruction, so the penalty has to be applied at the level of whole policies over an indefinite future — which is a consequentialist comparison of complete trajectories. Third: what it would cost an agent to reach an outcome must be denominated in some unit — effort, time, risk, resources — and choosing one unit across heterogeneous agents is already the interpersonal comparison the measure was meant to avoid.

Then construct the strongest reply you can on the measure's behalf: that an imperfect, non-canonical penalty is far better than none, because the alternative is attempting to write down all of value. Say whether that reply meets the argument above or only changes the subject, and close by naming which of your three steps you find weakest.
