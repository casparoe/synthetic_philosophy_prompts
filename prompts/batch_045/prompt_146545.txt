I'm writing a short position paper for a reading group on prediction, determinism, and responsibility, and before I commit any of it to paper I want someone to audit the package, because I have the sense that a couple of the things I believe are pulling against each other in ways I can't locate. Here is where I think I stand. (1) Determinism holds at the scale of human action: whatever the correct fundamental physics turns out to be, nothing indeterministic survives aggregation up to the level of neurons, let alone decisions, so for questions about agency the world can be treated as deterministic. (2) Determinism is no threat to moral responsibility; what responsibility requires is that the agent would have acted differently had her reasons been different, not that the laws be indeterministic. (3) Many deterministic systems — the weather, arguably the brain — display sensitive dependence on initial conditions, so no predictor physically realized inside the world can forecast them beyond a finite horizon. (4) Call this epistemic openness: what actually sustains our practices of deliberating and blaming one another is not indeterminism but the sheer fact that we cannot predict each other, so unpredictability rather than modal openness is doing the normative work. (5) Call this shadowing optimism: for a chaotic system whose true dynamics you have in hand, a trajectory computed from slightly wrong initial data is still tracked for a considerable time by a genuine trajectory of that same system from slightly different initial data, and I take this to vindicate approximate prediction — the forecast is at least true of a nearby history even if not of this one. (6) In practically every forecasting domain I know anything about, the dominant source of error is not initial-condition error but the fact that we do not have the true equations; the model is wrong before the measurement is. (7) Two case verdicts that I hold together and that my co-organizer says I can't have both of: an oracle with the true dynamics and exact initial data who announces today what I will do next year does not thereby make me less responsible, since foreknowledge is not influence; but a scanner that reliably flags my decision a beat before I am aware of having made it would unsettle my responsibility, and I don't think that reaction is mere superstition. (8) A methodological preference, since it may matter for what you recommend: I would much rather revise a methodological or explanatory commitment than give up one of my verdicts about a case.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a survey. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, ranking them by severity rather than listing them as equally serious; for each real tension, say exactly what would have to give and what that revision would cost me downstream; separately, identify any pair that merely looks inconsistent and explain why it isn't, since I suspect at least one of the objections I've been getting is of that kind. Two things I'd flag as constraints on a good answer: I don't think the appeal to sensitive dependence in (3) can be doing all the work I implicitly ask of it once (6) is on the table, and I don't think (7) is as tidy as I've made it sound, but I can't say why in either case. Finally, given my preference in (8), tell me which single revision — conceivably one that isn't on my list at all, but a refinement of something that is — would clean up the most, and say honestly if the answer is that the cheap fix isn't available to someone who wants to keep (7) as it stands.
