Hi — quick one before office hours. I'm working on a paper on populism and I sketched what I thought was a nice clean reductio of the whole "the will of the people" business. A referee then pointed out that if it works, it works against a lot more than populism, and I think she's right, but I can't locate the damage. Here it is more or less as I wrote it, numbered so you can hit a particular step:

(1) The shared core of populism — left versions and right versions alike — is a claim about legitimacy: a law or policy is legitimate, or at any rate answerable to the right standard, only if it gives effect to the will of the people.
(2) To give effect to a will, there has to be a will to give effect to. If there's no fact of the matter about what the people wills on some question, then on that question nothing can count as giving effect to it.
(3) The people's will isn't a free-floating entity. It has to be determined by the attitudes of the people who make up the people. Otherwise "the will of the people" is just a name for whatever the leader does.
(4) And the only candidate for the determining relation is aggregation: what the people wills on X is a function of what its members want with respect to X — and of nothing else. Who sets the agenda, what order things get voted in, what the default is: those are procedural accidents, not facts about what anybody wants.
(5) No such function exists. Take the familiar impossibility result: there's no way of getting from profiles of individual orderings to a social ordering that satisfies unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives and non-dictatorship together. Relax it and you get majority rule, which cycles, and then what comes out is settled by the agenda.
(6) So for a great many real political questions there is no fact of the matter about what the people wills; the outcome is an artifact of procedure wearing a costume.
(7) So the core populist claim is unsatisfiable in practice. No actual policy could be legitimate by populism's own standard, and populist legitimation is always a mask for something else.

Two things I bolted on. First, a seperate worry I've started calling the constitutive circle: the "people" whose will is supposed to be sovereign isn't given in advance. On most nationalist-populist versions, the people is partly constituted by who acknowledges the leader as their voice, so the demos is in a sense fixed by the very claim that purports to be answerable to it. Second, a repair I tried: read "the people wills that p" as "p is what the people would settle on under any fair agenda" — the robust-will reading. It looks like it escapes (5), except that on cyclic profiles the robust will comes out empty on everything, which seems worse than mere agenda-dependence.

And here's the referee's objection, which is where I'm stuck:

(8) Run (2)–(6) on an individual person across time. Her will isn't free-floating either; it's determined by her occurrent attitudes; those are famously context-, framing- and order-dependent, and they cycle too, so it's not clear there's a fact of the matter about what she wills either, and she can never act for her own reasons. Same for a court, a faculty meeting, a union local. So the argument doesn't show populism is incoherent; it shows deliberation and agency are, which nobody should accept.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep the thought in (1) that legitimacy has something to do with the governed than keep the conclusion that populism is incoherent, and I'd rather keep the (5) bit than the general skepticism about agency in (8) — but tell me if I've got that exactly backwards. For each candidate step, say why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives, including whether any of the standard repairs actually buy their way out or just relocate the problem: restricting the domain of profiles, moving the base from preferences to reasons or to higher-order attitudes, or going constructivist and saying the will of the people just is whatever a legitimate procedure turns out. Plain prose, no citations or reading lists — I've read the literature, I want the diagnosis. And please write it in American English.
