I sit on the emerging-risk panel for a national research funder, and we have to rule on one proposal before the funding round closes in about three weeks, so I need a short written opinion I can circulate to the panel.

The proposal (call it RECUR) is to build a minimal self-replicating system from scratch: an information-carrying strand plus the enzymes it encodes, set up so that the strand encodes its own copying machinery instead of borrowing the host cell's replication kit. Containment would be by engineered dependence on a synthetic nutrient that only we supply.

Two reviewers say this is ordinary synthetic biology with fancy framing — the interesting part is chemistry, and we already have rules for this. Two others say a threshold is being crossed, and one of them keeps invoking typogenetics (the made-up scheme where the strands end up encoding the very enzymes that manipulate them) plus the analogy with self-reference in formal systems: once a replicator contains a description of its own reader, the usual static containment argument is supposed to stop working. I find the analogy suggestive but cant tell if its doing any real work.

The thing I actually want the memo to adjudicate is what I'll call the fidelity ratchet: once the copying machinery is encoded by the thing being copied, mutations can improve copying fidelity, and past some fidelity threshold heredity becomes open-ended — so our nutrient dependence isn't just a leaky barrier, it's one the system could be selected to do without. Should the panel adopt something like that as a formal review trigger for this class of work, or is it a philosophers worry with no teeth?

Give me a bottom line — fund as proposed, reject, or fund with conditions — with a timeline, and with any conditions worded so the applicants could actually meet them. And please give the two "its just chemistry" reviewers the strongest version of their objection rather than a strawman; a couple of us are still half convinced by them.
