ok so I just had a three-hour argument with a friend about Moore's paradox (the "it's raining but I don't believe it" thing) and I need help turning the wreckage into something usable. Her line was that the whole thing is boring and shallow: there's a norm of assertion, asserting the conjunction commits you to believing each conjunct, the second conjunct says you don't believe the first, so of course it's weird to say out loud — and nothing follows about belief, self-knowledge, or the mind, because the weirdness completely evaporates once you stop asserting and just embed the sentence somewhere or think it silently. I pushed back, mostly by insisting that the oddity does survive in silent thought and in embeddings like "suppose it's raining and I don't believe it is," but honestly by hour three I'd lost track of which claim I was even defending. We kept sliding between "you can't felicitously assert it," "you can't coherently think it," "you can't take it as a live premise when deliberating," and "you can't believe both conjuncts at once," and those seem like three or four different claims that the stuff I've read tends to run together.

So here's the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal. What if you test the paradox by taking away the normal route to self-knowledge? Normally you settle "do I believe p?" by just settling whether p, straight through to the world. But imagine agents who don't do that: someone reading a highly reliable neural readout of their own doxastic states; someone with overwhelming behavioral evidence about themselves; someone with strong reason to think they're self-deceived or that their own avowals are unreliable; someone whose sub-personal states clearly register p while they sincerely and reflectively avow not-p. For such an agent, is "p and I don't believe p" still absurd? If it stops being absurd the moment the second conjunct is supported observationally, that looks like big evidence that the paradox is really a fact about the ordinary method of self-ascription and not about the state itself. If the absurdity survives impeccable third-personal self-knowledge, then something deeper is going on — something about the functional role of belief, or about what goes wrong when a state that's supposed to guide action gets disowned by the very system that has it.

The part I actually think is new, and which I'm not sure belongs in the same project: run the same test on systems that have belief-ish states but differ in whether they can read their own outputs — a bare forecaster with no self-model, one with a read-only monitoring channel, one with a writable self-model. Does Moore-paradoxicality need a first-person concept at all, or only a certain architecture? I worry this is either the most interesting bit or a completely unrelated second project stapled on.

What I need: a proposal for a one-semester supervised project, call it 6000-8000 words, on the question above. Give me the question stated tightly enough that it could come out one way or the other; say why the two standard families of answer (the pragmatic/assertion-norm kind, and the transparency or constitutive self-knowledge kind, plus the "it's just a special case of believing something incoherent" kind) don't already settle it; lay out the method and why it beats the obvious alternatives (more armchair intuition-mongering about example sentences? just picking a side on whether the paradox is pragmatic or cognitive?); say what the possible findings are and what would actually decide between them, including the possibility that the observational-self-knowledge cases are underdescribed and the answer is "it depends"; and tell me honestly what would count as the project failing, and whether the systems/architecture bit is a final section or an entire separate thesis that I should cut. Don't give me a bibliography or reading list, I'll fill in references myself once the shape is clear.
