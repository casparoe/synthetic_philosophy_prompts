I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of history, and I am drafting the last question of the final paper: one essay, 45 minutes, a 900-word ceiling, marked out of 25. The topic is presentism in historiography — whether historians can escape the standards of their own time — and I need a question that cannot be passed by reciting the standard anti-presentist canon.

The failure modes I want to design against, all of which I have seen in previous years: (i) a summary of the polemic against Whig history, plus the observation that the past should be understood on its own terms; (ii) a summary of the intentionalist case against anachronism in the history of ideas, offered as if it settled moral appraisal too; (iii) the quick argument that since all history is written from the present, presentism is unavoidable and therefore not a fault; (iv) the bland compromise that we may condemn the practice while excusing the agent, asserted without any account of what makes the two come apart. A question that only invites any of these is useless to me.

Here is what the question has to force, and I would rather it force these through a concrete demand than through a list of sub-questions:

1. A separation of at least four things that get called presentism: moral-evaluative presentism (appraising past agents by our norms), epistemic presentism (appraising them by what we now know and they could not), explanatory or teleological presentism (explaining a past episode by where it ended up), and agenda presentism (choosing what to study because of present concerns). The student must say which of the usual objections actually bites against which, and must not assume a single verdict transfers across all four.

2. The gap between judging an act and blaming an agent, and whether the standard escape hatch survives being applied to itself. The claim that agents who could not have known better are not blameworthy is itself a normative claim we hold now and project onto the past; I want students either to show why that self-application is benign or to give up the escape hatch.

3. A principle to test rather than to apply. Please build the question around a principle stated in the paper, to be assessed rather than assumed: the *Retrospective Parity Principle* — apply to past agents only those standards you would be willing to have a better-informed historian of the twenty-second century apply to you. Students should be made to consider whether this is a genuine constraint, whether the strong reading of it forbids condemning anything at all (since we cannot foresee the downstream consequences of what we now regard as innocuous), whether the weak reading of it turns out to permit exactly the moral condemnations it was meant to block, and whether the principle is therefore unstable rather than a tidy middle way.

4. The projection of later vocabulary. Ask whether describing an earlier practice with a concept or term that did not then exist — the vocabulary of structural racism, or genocide, for instance — is objectionable presentism or merely a description of something that existed avant la lettre, and what the answer depends on.

Two cases should be stipulated in the question, with all needed facts given, so that nothing has to be looked up and nothing turns on contested history: (A) an elected legislator in a slaveholding society who, in the late 1850s, votes to extend chattel slavery and defends it in explicitly religious language, at a time when abolitionist arguments are in wide circulation; (B) a public-health official who licenses a sedative for use by pregnant women on the evidence available at the time, where the causal link to severe birth defects is not established until years later. The question should require the student to say, for each case, which kind of presentism is in play and whether it is objectionable there, and why the two cases come out differently or the same.

The question should also be self-contained: tell candidates explicitly that no credit is given for naming authors or texts, and warn them not to trade on the other sense of presentism, the thesis in the metaphysics of time that only present entities exist, since it has no bearing here.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. The question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including the two cases, the statement of the Retrospective Parity Principle, and the rubric line about time, word ceiling, and marks.

2. A model answer of roughly 800 words that takes a determinate position rather than balancing considerations, and that ends with one sentence stating what, if anything, the honest historian is left entitled to do.

3. A marking scheme built on five must-hit points whose marks sum to 25, delivered as a table with these columns: Point | Marks | What an A answer does | What a B answer does instead | The error this point is designed to catch. Below the table, give two short paragraphs: one on how marks are withheld (in particular, nothing for defining presentism, nothing for the unavoidability argument unless its inference from situatedness to incurability is itself criticised), and one giving a one-line characterisation each of a first-class, an upper-second, and a third-class script.

4. A JSON object summarising the item, with exactly these fields: "topic" (string), "level" (string), "time_minutes" (number), "word_limit" (number), "total_marks" (number), "crux" (one sentence naming the single move that separates a first from a 2:1), "senses_distinguished" (array of objects with "label", "one_line_gloss", "verdict" from legitimate/contested/illegitimate, and "why"), "must_hit_points" (array of objects with "point", "marks", "a_answer", "b_answer", "error_caught"), "rpp_readings" (array of objects with "reading", "consequence", "stable" as boolean), "disqualifying_answers" (array of strings, each naming a move that earns no credit and why).
