I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a European newspaper, and I am closing a piece on the therapy/enhancement distinction — the line that bioethicists and regulators keep drawing, erasing and redrawing — because a source at a reimbursement committee has told me that within a year it will have to rule on several new interventions that fit neatly into neither box, and I would like to be able to say something more useful than that the line is contested. Two people I interviewed gave me competing tests, and I want to see how far they actually get you. A philosopher I spoke to insisted on what she called the restoration window: the only clean question is whether this patient once occupied the higher level of functioning herself, so that the intervention hands back something that was hers. A health economist preferred what he called reimbursement symmetry: would the same payer fund the identical benefit for someone who is already at that level, and if the honest answer is no, you are doing something other than treating. I have also had the malady gate (no identifiable dysfunction, no therapy), the peer baseline (bring the person inside the statistical range for her age and sex), the offset principle (compensating for a disadvantage the person did not choose and cannot control can be treatment even with no disease present), and the substitution test (if the same gain is available through sleep, training, education or a change of diet, that is a clue) thrown at me.

So, three things. First, sort each of the following interventions into one of four tiers — clearly therapy, arguably therapy, arguably enhancement, clearly enhancement — and give each a score from 0, meaning it purely restores what this person lost or was owed, to 100, meaning it purely adds what was never there, with one sentence of justification apiece: growth hormone for a child predicted to be extremely short but with no hormone deficiency and no disease; a cochlear implant for an infant born deaf to deaf parents who sign and regard deafness as an identity rather than a defect; a wakefulness drug for a night-shift worker with no sleep disorder, restoring the alertness he had when he worked days; a beta-blocker for an orchestral violinist with a healthy heart whose hands shake only on stage; a one-time gene edit that permanently lowers cholesterol in someone whose levels are already normal and who declines to change how he eats; a drug that returns the processing speed and word-finding of a woman with persistent brain fog after a viral infection to what she was like before, and the same drug given to her healthy colleague who simply wants to think faster; a drug that brings the intensity of grief in the first months after bereavement down to the level typical of people who have not been bereaved; a memory-dampening drug given after a traumatic assault to prevent intrusive flashbacks, where only some recipients would have developed them anyway; an implant that gives a fully sighted person a genuinely new sense, such as continuous directional information about magnetic fields, with no deficit being corrected; and a daily pill that brings the aerobic capacity and endurance of a healthy seventy-year-old to the level of a healthy thirty-year-old.

Second, rank the six tests above by how much weight you would actually give them when drawing the line, most to least, saying in a sentence what each one gets right and where it breaks; then tell me which pair of them comes into conflict most often across the scores you just gave, and which single case does the most damage to the distinction overall. Third, the number I want to quote in the piece: of the interventions that people in rich countries currently receive from a doctor for something other than an acute injury or infection, what fraction would you class as enhancement rather than therapy? Give me a figure between 0 and 1 and defend it in two or three sentences. If your case-by-case scores and that fraction sit badly with each other, say so explicitly and tell me which of the two you would revise.
