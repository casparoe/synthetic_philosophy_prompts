I've ended up holding a position on inheritance tax that I don't see anyone else quite holding, and I want to know what would knock me off it.

Here it is. The best argument for letting parents give their kids advantages — the "family values" style one, where the parent-child relationship is a distinctive good the state can't replicate, so partiality inside it is not merely permitted but valuable — is an argument about a relationship between living people. It has nothing to say about a cheque that lands on a 48-year-old whose parents died last year. That isn't partiality within a relationship, that's the dead hand. So: what we should tax steeply is posthumous transfers to reciepients past a certain age (or past a certain age gap), while transfers inside what I'll call the relationship window — parent to dependent kid, transfers you can still see as part of an ongoing shared life — get treated much more leniently. And this isn't really about redistribution or revenue; it's about equal opportunity. Dynastic wealth is basically the one channel of advantage transmission the state can interrupt cleanly, and past the window there's no countervailing good left to weigh against that.

So what would count against it? A few things I want specifically:

- Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, not a flat list of "some people say".
- Split them into the ones a committed holder of my view should concede count against it, versus the ones I can reasonably bat away.
- Do the empirical side as well as the conceptual side. Two I already worry about: (a) if the window is generous, rich parents shift into inter vivos trusts and the base evaporates; (b) an age cliff makes the tax burden depend on how long your parents happened to live, which is brute luck, and I'm supposed to be the guy who objects to brute luck. Also — I have a vague memory that a couple of Nordic countries junked inheritance taxes entirely and nobody thinks they've become rentier aristocracies; if that's roughly right, say what it does to my view, and if I've misremembered, say so.
- Tell me what the view predicts that its rivals don't. I think one is an asymmetry in ordinary moral intuition: people should be noticeably more bothered by taxing a bequest to a small child than to a middle-aged heir, which you could test with a vignette survey. If that asymmetry didn't show up, how much should that move me?
- Be honest about whether I've qualified this into unfalsifiability. My worry: if the residual inequality I can't touch is the non-financial stuff families transmit — socialisation, networks, neighbourhoods, the whole soft side — then I can always point at that, and the financial tax becomes unfalsifiable as an opportunity-equaliser. If that's where I've ended up, say whether it's actually a problem for a view like mine or just how normative views work.

What would move me most is magnitudes, I dunno why exactly: if the real mobility and revenue effects are a rounding error, I should stop caring and admit the whole thing is symbolic.

No bibliography and no citations please — argue it out. Keep it under 700 words or so.
