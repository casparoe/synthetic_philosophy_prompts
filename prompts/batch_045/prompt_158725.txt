You're a professional philosopher who works in formal epistemology — Dutch books, scoring rules, updating, bayesian everything. Please think carefully: sketch the dialectical landscape to yourself first, steelman Reflection before you attack it, and don't just announce which step is broken without pricing the alternatives.

My friend walked me into this argument and I can't find the exit. Roughly staded:

(1) Reflection: rationality requires that my current credence in A, conditional on my later credence in A being x, is x itself — P(A | P_t(A) = x) = x — where P_t is my own future credence function.
(2) The selling point (what makes van Fraassen's principle do work that plain conditionalization doesn't) is that it's a requirement I can acknowledge from where I stand right now: my future self has authority over me without my needing to know what she knows.
(3) Credence laundering. I know I'll be slipped a pill tonight that drives credence in A to 0.9 whatever A's truth value — I've read the trial data, it's pure noise, zero information about A. Since I'm certain that P_t(A) = 0.9, (1) says my credence in A should already be 0.9 now. So Reflection makes belief-manufacturing — arranging for your future self to be confident — into a legitimate route to justified confidence. That's unacceptible, so unrestricted (1) is false.
(4) Everyone's repair: index the principle to C — I won't be drugged, won't forget anything, won't be hypnotised, will retain all my current evidence and possibly gain more.
(5) But now it only bites in cases where I'm certain of C.
(6) And being certain of C seems to require that I can already anticipate the space of ways my evidence might go — some partition I'm certain I'll learn which cell of. Once I have that partition, conditionalization alone fixes what my credence should be in each cell, and Reflection falls out for free. So the repaired principle constrains nothing conditionalization didn't already constrain.
(7) So Reflection is either false (3) or redundant (6) — and the corollary is that there's no distinctively diachronic norm in the neighbourhood at all: everything leaning on Reflection specifically is leaning on conditionalization in a costume.

I don't accept (7). Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (6), which feels right to me, and I'd sooner give up (2)'s picture of future-self authority than give up the thought that an arbitrary updating policy is irrational. So: what's left of Reflection if (6) survives, what work can it still do, and what would its defenders themselves concede as the real exposure? Also — is (6) an actual equivalence, or a slide from "certain I won't be corrupted" to "know which ways the evidence could go"? Argue it, don't cite it.
