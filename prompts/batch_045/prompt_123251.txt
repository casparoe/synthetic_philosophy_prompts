You are a professional philosopher of cognitive science who has spent years arguing against the analogy-and-fluid-concepts approach to modelling thought — the tradition behind the Copycat program and its letter-string microdomain, the one built around puzzles of the form: if abc goes to abd, what does ijk go to? You are a serious critic, not a strawman. You concede happily that the program produces human-looking answers on its own turf and that human concepts genuinely do slip, but you hold the line that this shows nothing until the approach scales, and that what gets called slippage is really a hand-built menu of pre-approved moves whose plausibility was smuggled in by the programmer's choice of domain. Play this critic as my sparring partner until I say stop: concede small points freely, never the main one, keep each reply to about two paragraphs of plain prose, and argue from the concepts themselves rather than by citing papers. Before each reply, take a moment to brainstorm privately — the sharpest available form of your own objection, and the sharpest thing I could say back to it — and then answer in character.

I should say that I have no academic background; I have been building a small letter-string domain of my own in the evenings, and I want you to test what I think I have found. In my domain the alphabet is closed at five letters, a through e. There is no sixth letter, and I tell anyone who plays that this is a rule of the game and not an oversight.

My opening move. Take the usual seed, abc goes to abd, and ask what abe goes to. Because the alphabet now has a top, the answer splits: nothing works; or wrap around to aba, treating the letters as a cycle; or the rule leaks outside the game and you get abf, treating them as the ordinary alphabet, which merely happens to be truncated here. Which of those feels right to me flips depending on whether I am thinking cycle, stack, or counting system, and I cannot get the flip to go away by stipulating the rule harder, because the rule is what is in question. A second case: seed aab goes to aac. Now take aba. Change the last letter and you get abb; change the letter that appears only once and you get aca. Those two descriptions coincide exactly in the seed and come apart only in the new string, so nothing in the seed ever decided which one you were doing.

So here is the challenge. The interesting thing is not that a program produces some answer, it is that a space of defensible answers opens up in a way nobody explicitly wrote down, and that looks to me like the very phenomenon you keep dismissing as the programmer's prior. Tell me why I am wrong — and please do not just tell me my domain is small; I know it is small, and I want to know whether its being small is the whole of your objection or only the beginning of it.
