Looking for a name for a move I keep catching in talks and in my own drafts — or, if there isn't one, a coinage I can define tightly enough to actually use in a methods section.

Setup. Someone reports a number for some trait (BMI, years of schooling, whatever) that's meant to capture how much of the variation in that trait, in that population, tracks genetic differences. Then, sometimes in the same sentence, the number gets used to answer questions I'm pretty sure are different questions:

- How much of *this one person's* trait is genetic — as if you could take an individual's value and carve it into a genetic bit and an environmental bit in the same proportion. A reviewer on a paper I'm revising basically did this: the heritability of BMI is high, so a school-food intervention isn't worth evaluating.
- Whether the gap between group A and group B is genetic. The number was estimated *within* groups, or in some mixed sample, and now it's being wheeled in to explain a difference *between* them.
- Whether the trait is "really" biological, fixed, in the genes — as opposed to something messy and social.

The bit that makes me think these are one thing and not three: the number is a ratio, and both halves of it depend on what happens to be varying in the population and setting where you measured it. Homogenize the environment and the number goes up. So the population where an environmental intervention has *already* equalized things is exactly the population where the number looks most "genetic," and a population with wildly unequal environments is where it looks least genetic. Its not just a number that gets misread; it moves in the direction that reads as "genes matter more" precisely when the environment got made uniform.

Two test cases I use to check whether I understand it:
(a) A disease that's entirely caused by a dietary deficiency everyone in the population shares — so it's fully preventable with a supplement — where the only genetic variation is in how badly each person is affected by the deficiency. Seems to me the number here could be near 1, for a trait I'd call 100% environmental.
(b) Same trait, same gene pool, two populations kept in different conditions: the number is high within each, and the mean difference between them is entirely environmental.

So: is there a term of art for this? Not for each bullet individually — I know people say the "high heritability doesn't mean unchangeable" thing — but for the family, including the sensitivity-to-what's-varying bit. If the honest answer is that there's no umbrella term, just say that plainly; I'd rather get that than a name that covers one bullet and leaves me overgeneralizing.

Neighbors I've considered and why they seem off: reification (too big — it covers reifying g, reifying race, everything), ecological fallacy (wrong structure: that's aggregate vs individual correlations, mine is a quantity that only has a value relative to a distribution being read as intrinsic), Goodhart's law (close for the "goes up when you fix the environment" part, but nobody's gaming a metric in my cases).

What I actually want out of this: whether there's a real term and how it's defined; if not, a coinage plus a definition that says what is and isn't an instance; whether my first two bullets are genuinely the same error or whether the second one is really about counterfactuals rather than attribution; and whether the sensitivity bit is the same phenomenon or a symptom of it. Also a boundary case I can't sort out: predicting response to selection *is* an intervention, and the number is legitimately used there — so the error can't be "using it for any intervention claim." Where's the line? And if there's a legitimate use that lets you read it as being about individuals, I need to know, because my definition has to leave room for it.

I'm a PhD student in epidemiology (gene–environment stuff in cohort data), so be as technical as you like, and don't bother with references or a reading list — I can chase those later. Please dont just hand me "heritability is a population statistic"; I know that sentence, I dont know what the *error* is called. Answer in American English if you can — I'm lifting sentences into a manuscript going to a US journal. Under ~700 words would be ideal.
