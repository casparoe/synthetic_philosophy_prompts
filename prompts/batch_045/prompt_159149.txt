I teach a second-year seminar on philosophical method — 16 students, one 90-minute slot, a seminar room with a projector and a table that's too small for all of us. We've just finished a week on whether intuitions are evidence, and the reading left everyone with that shrug-y conclusion of "well, intuitions vary, so maybe we should be careful". I want them to feel something sharper than that. Here's the thing I keep circling round, which I've started calling *calibration drift*: the verdicts we get from a hypothetical case feel delivered to us, obvious and reason-backed, but they're produced by something tunable, and you can install a confident new one in twenty minutes with nothing but feedback on a handful of unrelated cases — after which it feels exactly like an original one. If that's right, then the interesting thing about cross-cultural variation in intuitions isn't the first-order differences themselves but the question of which calibration any given population got, and whether "obvious to me" carries any information about that at all. So: design me a runnable in-class activity — a game, simulation or staged task with real choices and small stakes — that makes 16 undergraduates run into this themselves before anyone names it. The conceit I've been toying with is a fake "corpus annotation project": they're hired as annotators coding how people use "knows" and how names get used in a big dataset, they're split (by something arbitrary they don't notice — favourite colour of the week, whatever) into two groups, and each group is trained on one of two rival coding manuals for about fifteen minutes using short cases with an immediate answer key. They must not know there are two manuals. Then everyone comes back, re-answers two target vignettes they answered cold at the start — I've roughed these out and you can use them or replace them: (1) Priya checks the station app, which says "on time"; unknown to her, the app defaults to "on time" unless someone manually overrides it, and today nobody has, and the train does in fact arrive exactly on schedule — did Priya know the train would arrive at 18:04? (2) A town museum acquires a bronze bust the local paper attributes to the sculptor Elin Vago; it's actually a forgery by Halvard Bryn, but everyone in the town learns the name "Vago" from the paper and the plaque and uses it to talk about the bust; years later a scholar says "Elin Vago didn't make the bust after all — Halvard Bryn did" — true, or merely misleading? The two manuals need to agree on easy cases and diverge on both of those, and crucially they must *not* just be two named theories in fancy dress (no "you're the reliabilists, you're the descriptivists"): I want each manual to be reliability-flavoured on knowledge but causal-flavoured on reference, and the other the reverse, so nobody can work out which manual they're on and start reciting a position from the reading. No lecture up front, and I'd rather they didn't hear the words intuition, cross-cultural, or universal until the last twenty minutes.

What I need from you is the actual runnable kit: minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the materials (slips, two one-page manuals, coding sheets, whatever else), the full training sets with answer keys, the rules and payoff scheme — points, sweets, a small prize, no real money — and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific claims. Please build in three mechanics I think the whole thing hinges on. First, a paraphrase check: at some point each student writes down what they took the vignette to be saying, so I can hold up two matching paraphrases from opposite verdicts and kill the "they're reading a different case" move before it eats the session. Second, a small number of secret controls — two or three students quietly pulled out of the training for an unrelated filler task — because I suspect the untutored responses will be all over the place, and if so that's the most useful thing in the room: the "neutral baseline" turns out to be noise, not bedrock, and that's a point I want them to walk into rather than be told. Third, after the reveal, a forced second-order choice: the project has to adopt one manual, and they must pick a selection procedure from three on offer — majority vote of the room, the verdicts of the two highest scorers on a comprehension quiz, or the verdicts of the untrained controls — with pairs wagering points on which procedure will win and on which manual will survive it. Debrief should then show that each of those three procedures is itself a contestable judgement about whose verdicts count as data, and that this is the level at which variation actually bites: even if you've established a difference, you still need a non-question-begging way to say which difference matters. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce — I assume it's something like this: my variation is installed in twenty minutes by an explicit answer key, whereas enculturation takes years and nobody hands you a key, so the analogy to real cross-population differences may be thinner than the students will want it to be — tell me where exactly it breaks and what I'll have to supply in words.

Two more things. One: I'm a bit uneasy about the deception, and I'm sure at least one student will be too. Give me a variant that gets the same effect without lying to them, even if it's slightly less punchy, and tell me what you'd say if someone challenges it afterwards — and while you're at it, how you'd run this async or online if I ever have to. Two: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My guesses: the training won't take because the cases are too long and people drift; someone spots the family resemblance to a famous case and derails everyone into theory; the pair deliberations collapse into "fine, whatever, let's just say yes"; and the debrief gets stuck on "so are intuitions just arbitrary then", which isn't the conclusion I want. What should I do in the moment in each of those cases, and what goes in the handout or on the board to steer them off it? Ideally by the end they should be able to say, in their own words, why the felt obviousness of a verdict is not evidence that the verdict wasn't installed, and why "let's just ask more people" doesn't settle anything.
