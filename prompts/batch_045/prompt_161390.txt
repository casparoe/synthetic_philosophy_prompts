Two people in my reading group got into an argument about Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain story and asked me to settle it, which I couldn't really do. Nadia's opening move was that the argument is a bit of a sleight of hand: it's supposed to show that any patterned principle (take equality) can be held in place only by forbidding harmless consensual acts, but she said it only get there because Nozick smuggles in the whole libertarian package first — that Chamberlain owns his own body and labour, that whoever owns the stadium owns it legitimately, that the fans' quarters are really theirs to give. Grant all that and of course liberty upsets patterns; the argument just restates its own premises at somebody who never accepted them.

Sam said that misdescribes the target. The argument isn't meant to convert someone who rejects self-ownership from scratch; it's meant to show that patterned principles have a structural feature — keeping a pattern requires ongoing interference with what consenting adults do with what they've got — and that feature is a real cost whatever you think about self-ownership. How big a cost is a further question, and Nozick only needs it to be big enough to be worth noticing.

Nadia came back with: "big enough" is exactly where it collapses, because you can't count liberty without a theory of what counts as a liberty-loss. Taxing Chamberlain at some rate to fund things those same fans want isn't obviously a bigger net restriction on freedom than banning a milion 25-cent purchases; you can't just ring up one interference per forbidden act.

Then someone floated a variant, and they went at each other again. Suppose the fans don't each decide seperately — a million people sign conditional pledges (each pays a quarter only if at least 250,000 others sign too), and they say out loud that the whole point is to break the pattern, because they think equality is bad. Nadia said this changes the case: now it isn't an invisible-hand byproduct of innocuous individual choices but a coordinated political project, and banning coordinated pattern-breaking is much harder to dress up as tyranny. Sam said it makes Nozick's case stronger, not weaker, since each transfer is still consensual and the coordination is only speech plus conditional promises — so the state would have to police motives and pledges, not merely acts, which is a far uglier interference than taxing one ball game. Nadia's last word was that the state needn't police anything: it can just tax the resulting wealth, motive-blind.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
