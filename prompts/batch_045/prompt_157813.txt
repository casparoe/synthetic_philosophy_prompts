THE QUESTION (20 marks)

In Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," published in Mind, Achilles tries to get the Tortoise to accept, on logical grounds alone, the conclusion (Z) of a two-premise Euclidean argument: (A) things that are equal to the same are equal to each other, (B) the two sides of this triangle are things that are equal to the same, so (Z) the two sides of this triangle are equal to each other. The Tortoise says he accepts A and B but not the hypothetical (C) "If A and B are true, Z must be true," and asks Achilles to force him, logically, to accept Z. Achilles adds C to the notebook as a further entry; the Tortoise grants it but then asks for (D) "If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true," and so on. When Achilles protests that Logic "would take you by the throat, and force you to do it," the Tortoise replies that "Whatever Logic is good enough to tell me is worth writing down," and dictates a further hypothetical (E).

Call the character in the dialogue the withholding Tortoise: he is happy to accept every step Achilles offers as a further entry in the notebook, and his refusal is always located in a hypothetical he has not yet granted. Now consider a different character, the compliant Tortoise: he accepts A and B, and he will accept C, D, E and every further hypothetical Achilles cares to write down, never once asking for a new one. He nevertheless declines to accept Z, and when pressed he says: granting items for the list is one thing, being bound to move from them to Z is another — nothing I have granted puts me under any requirement to grant anything else.

(a) Explain, in a few sentences, the mechanism of the regress and why extending the list of entries can never satisfy the withholding Tortoise's demand. Be explicit about whether C is a rule of inference, a proposition, or something else, and about what work, if any, adding it can do.
(b) Does Carroll's regress have any purchase at all against the compliant Tortoise? If not, say what problem, if any, remains for him, and whether it is the same problem as the withholding Tortoise's.
(c) Does any of this show that deduction cannot be given a non-circular justification? Distinguish a constitutive reading of the moral (a claim about what drawing an inference consists in) from an epistemic reading (a claim about what a reasoner must be justified in believing in order to infer), and say which, if either, the dialogue actually supports.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) Achilles wants to move the Tortoise from A and B to Z. The Tortoise points out that accepting A and B is one thing and accepting the hypothetical C — "if A and B are true, Z must be true" — is another, and he demands that Achilles force him to accept Z using logic alone. Achilles's response is simply to add C to the notebook as a further premise. This cannot work. C is, logically speaking, just another proposition: it is written in the same notebook, as one more item in the same list, and it is the sort of thing that can itself be accepted or withheld. It is not a rule of inference, and only a rule of inference could do the work that Achilles needs done, because a rule licenses the transition from premises to conclusion whereas a premise merely sits in the list awaiting such a licence. So the Tortoise can grant A, B and C and then demand a further hypothetical D licensing the move from A, B and C to Z, and then E, and so on without end. Achilles's threat that Logic would take him by the throat is only a metaphor. The dialogue therefore shows that no list of propositions, however long, can by itself constitute or compel the drawing of a conclusion: the compulsion would have to come from outside the list. Validity is one thing, being moved by an argument another.

(b) The compliant Tortoise is in exactly the same position. He grants C, D and E and every further hypothetical offered, yet he still refuses to accept Z; so there must still be something he is withholding, namely the further hypothetical to the effect that if he accepts A, B, C, D and so on, then he must accept Z. Achilles can enter that as F, and the compliant Tortoise will then withhold on G, and the regress simply restarts one level up. The shape of the difficulty is unchanged: a reasoner who declines to make the transition can always locate his refusal in some hypothetical that is missing from the list, and the list can therefore never be closed.

(c) The upshot is that deduction cannot be justified by argument. Any argument for a rule of inference would have to proceed from premises to a conclusion and would therefore have to employ some rule of inference. If it employs the very rule in question it is circular; if it employs a different rule, that rule stands in need of justification in turn, and we begin again. What Carroll shows is that the reasoner must already accept the relevant rule before any inference can get going: one cannot infer by means of a rule one does not take to be valid. But no argument can produce that acceptance without circularity, so the acceptance must be non-inferential — a basic, a priori item of knowledge. This is the sense in which the dialogue supports foundationalism about logical knowledge: the rules are the foundations. Coherentism, which allows a belief to be justified purely by its relations to other beliefs, cannot accommodate this, since it would leave the Tortoise waiting forever for one more premise.
