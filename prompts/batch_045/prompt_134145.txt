I'm a retired physical chemist — forty years on phase equilibria, so I'm used to quantities that look like they should be smoothly ordered and aren't — and I've been hanging around the philosphy department's work-in-progress seminer for a year. They've let me give a 45-minute talk next month with questions after, and I want to be ready.

The thesis I'm defending has two halves. First: the usual ways of shrugging off spectrum arguments against the transitivity of 'better than' don't touch the version I build. Everybody says either that some step in the chain is a borderline case, or that 'better' quietly changes its meaning as you walk along the chain. So I take two dimensions — total welfare and equality, say — set it up so that any gain in one, however tiny, outweighs any loss in the other, and then move the outcome continuously around a *closed loop* in that two-dimensional space, back to exactly where it started. Every step is an improvement by the stated rule, there's no first or last item, and no step is vaguer than any other. I've been calling it the titration case. Second half: even if that works, I don't think anything practical follows, and I want to say why the money-pump line doesn't scare me. An agent that only ever moves locally downhill and then stops is like a system sitting in a local minimum — being willing to take one step and refusing to be walked all the way round the loop isn't irrational, it's just not globally optimizing, and I don't see why rationality should require global optimization.

The room will be maybe fifteen people. A decision theorist who will go straight for cyclic choice and exploitation. A population ethicist who has spent her career on continuum arguments of the repugnant-conclusion family and is fairly sure she's heard this before. A metaethicist who will ask what I think 'better than' even is, if it isn't an ordering. Two grad students who are on my side but will keep asking why this isn't vagueness again after all. And a visiting economist who will want to know what my choice function looks like if there's no ordering behind it.

What I'd like from you is a prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from these people, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it rather than the way a generic philosopher would, with a two-minute reply and a frank note on where the reply is weakest. Also tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes — I'd rather give up something real early than get cornered with it later — what I have to hold or the talk is dead, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Don't bother with a reading list or citations, I'll fill those in myself if anyone asks for them. At the end, say how confident you are in the sheet overall, and single out the one thing in it you're least sure about.
