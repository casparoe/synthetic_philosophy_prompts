I hold a version of counterpart theory that I have started calling the no-relation reading, and I want to know what would count against it before I commit to writing it up.

The view, in four theses. First: a de re modal claim — of the shape this lectern could have been made of ice, or Aristotle could have died in childhood — is evaluated relative to a counterpart relation that functions as a parameter of the context of utterance, in the way that a domain of quantification or a resolution for a demonstrative does. Second: that relation is not a real cross-world relation, and does not track one; it is a device of interpretation, selected by conversational context from among the respects of similarity that the conversation has made salient. Third: the view is officially neutral about what worlds are. They can be concrete Lewisian worlds or actualist ersatz surrogates; the thesis is only about the form of the truth conditions, and it is compatible with either. Fourth: the standard Kripkean objection — that in saying Nixon might have lost I am speaking about Nixon himself and not about some man who resembles him — is answered by the observation that context can supply a relation as demanding as one likes, including one that encodes origin, matter, and constitution, so that every judgment the identity theorist makes can be reproduced; what looked like a metaphysical disagreement then turns out to be a disagreement about which relation context in fact supplies. Consequently the view is not offering to reduce or analyse modality. It is a semantics, and it claims only that neither transworld identity nor real counterpart relations are needed in order to state truth conditions for de re modal discourse.

I already know the three objections I would classify as background rather than as news: the problem of how many counterparts an object has and what fixes the number, the difficulties counterpart semantics faces with iterated modality and with anaphora across modal operators, and the circularity charge that any similarity ordering over worlds already presupposes the modal notions it was meant to explain. Treat those as on the table and tell me where they rank rather than explaining them at length.

Two things I want you to work on specifically.

The first is a family of cases I have been calling the indifference cases: de re modal judgments in which the conversational context supplies no candidate respect of similarity, and in which the judgment nonetheless strikes us as determinately true or false. Consider my saying, of a man in an unidentified nineteenth-century photograph, that he could have been a sailor. Nobody in the conversation has any beliefs about his origin, his social role, or what matters to him; there is no respect of comparison that anyone has made salient, and there is no relation that the context can plausibly be described as selecting. If the view's answer is that the truth value of my claim is indeterminate until some relation is supplied, then it seems to me to get the wrong result about a perfectly ordinary piece of modal talk, and it gets it wrong in a way that the identity theorist does not. I want to know whether that is right, whether the class is genuinely populated or whether there is always some default relation the context can be said to supply, and whether a defender should simply bite the bullet and embrace a new kind of indeterminacy.

The second is a collapse argument I have been sketching, which I cannot tell is fatal or trivial. Suppose that in order to validate the modal inferences we actually make — symmetry for the characteristic B axiom, transitivity for S4, and the intersubstitutivity of identicals inside modal contexts — the contextually supplied counterpart relation has to be constrained to be an equivalence relation whose cells match each object with at most one counterpart per world. Under those constraints, it looks to me, the apparatus is intertranslatable with transworld identity plus a Kripke-style semantics, and the distinctiveness of counterpart theory evaporates. Which of those constraints is genuinely required, which is imposed only by the desire to reproduce classical modal logic, and what happens to the view if it declines to impose them? And if the argument goes through, is collapsing into the rival a defeat — a demonstration that the view never had its own subject matter — or only a change of notation, on the grounds that the two theories were always saying the same thing in different vocabularies?

Beyond those, give me the full map. Rank the defeaters from fatal through merely awkward, and be explicit about the ranking criterion you are using. Say which of them a committed defender of the view would concede as counting against it, as opposed to which they would dismiss as resting on a confusion or as question-begging against them — I want the defender's own concessions, not only what critics allege. Say what the view predicts or explains that its rivals do not, or conclude honestly that it predicts nothing extra. And give me an insulation check: once the relation is a context parameter and the view has disclaimed any reductive ambition, is there anything left in it that could be false? If there is not, say whether that is a defect in a thesis of this kind or whether a semantic thesis is allowed to be insulated in a way that a metaphysical one is not.

For what it is worth about my own priors: I would be moved most by a case in which the view yields indeterminacy where our modal practice has a determinate answer, and by a formal result showing that the view collapses into its rival under weak and independently motivated constraints. I would be moved least by appeals to what we intuitively or pre-theoretically refer to, since I take the contextualist machinery to be precisely a story about why those intuitions vary.
