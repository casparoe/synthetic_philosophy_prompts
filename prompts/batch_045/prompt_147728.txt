I've been slowly working through Hofstadter's ant-colony sections in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* — the conceit that Aunt Hillary, the colony, is the one who does the thinking, while the individual ants are something like her neurons — and I've ended up holding a version of that view for real, though in a deliberately deflated form. I am not claiming that a colony is conscious. My claim is the modest one: the states that play the belief and desire roles in the best explanation of what a colony does are states of the colony as a whole; they are not states of any single ant, nor straightforward aggregates of ant states; they survive the complete turnover of every individual; and a colony-level intentional story predicts and explains foraging, recruitment, nest-site choice, and collective defense in a way that no ant-level story does. That, I have been arguing to anyone who will listen, is enough to make the colony the subject.

What I want from you is the case against, mapped as exposure rather than as a catalogue of objections. Begin with what a sympathetic defender of this position should herself concede as counting against it, and only afterward with what critics allege and defenders wave away, and rank the defeaters from the fatal ones down to the merely awkward. I'm particularly interested in three families. First, redundancy: is there any principled reason to think that once you've specified the ants, their local chemical environment, and the physical layout of the nest, the colony-level story adds nothing — not merely in practice but in principle? I want that argued rather than asserted, since "it's just a convenient shorthand" is both the objection I hear most often and the one I find least convincing, given that we say the same thing about people's beliefs. Second, content: what, exactly, does Aunt Hillary believe? Trails and recruitment plausibly carry information, but there may be no fact of the matter as to whether she believes that there is food to the north as opposed to merely sending foragers northward, and a distributed homeostatic feedback loop can look functionally identical to a belief from the outside. Tell me how much of the view's appeal rests on that indeterminacy going unnoticed, and whether such a result would kill the view or only force it into a gentler shape. Third, I'd like you to take seriously four cases I invented while arguing with a friend, because they seemed to me to pull in different directions: a colony whose queen dies and whose workers go on foraging, defending, and re-nesting for months; two unrelated colonies gradually fused over several weeks until they share a single foraging network and a single colony odor; one colony cleanly bisected, each half retaining the full range of castes and continuing on its own; and — the one I found hardest — a pheromonal overlay, in which the complete trail network of colony B is artificially imposed on colony A, so that A's own workforce ends up acting out B's history while A's ants and nest remain in place. For each case, say which verdict the view is committed to, whether that verdict is one its defenders should find embarrassing or welcome, and whether the case really shows something about colonies or only about our grip on the concept of a single mind.

Finally, the meta-question, which is the one I actually care about: how insulated is this view? If it amounts to little more than the instruction to take the intentional stance toward any system with the right kind of distributed functional organization, then it is hard to see what could refute it, and I want your honest assessment of whether I am making a substantive empirical claim about social insects or restating a general functionalist thesis that happens to use ants as its favorite illustration — and, if the latter, whether that is a defect or simply what the view always was. I would personally be most moved by something showing that colony-level ascription is either provably redundant with an ant-plus-environment story or provably indeterminate in content; I would be least moved by intuition-mongering about what it is like to be a colony. Please don't spend your answer on what various authors have argued or on where I should go read about this — I want the arguments themselves, not the sources.
