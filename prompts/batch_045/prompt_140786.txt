I help put on an annual comedy night for our cognitive science department — short sets, mostly staff and PhD students, and the ones that work best are the ones where one specialist laughs and then has to explain the joke to the person next to them. This year I've been assigned the frame problem, and I'm stuck. I can picture the obvious versions: a robot in a room with a ticking bomb, or a robot that seizes up trying to work out whether the walls changed colour when it walked through the door. But those feel like they'd land as generic "AI is hopelessly literal" gags, and worse, I think they're jokes about the wrong thing — about an agent being too slow, or too easily surprised, or too short of facts — rather than about the actual structure of the problem. I have read the standard introductory material, so please don't spend the piece explaining what the frame problem is; I want the joke itself.

Here is the commission. Write a joke whose punchline trades on the real thing: that for an agent which represents its situation by explicitly stated facts together with rules saying what each action changes, the difficulty is not deriving the consequences of an action but knowing which of the indefinitely many facts it is even supposed to re-derive, and that the obvious remedy — doing more of the same kind of reasoning — does not by itself solve it. Please keep it clear of two neighbours: the ramification problem, where the trouble is that an action has cascading indirect effects you failed to represent, and the merely engineering worry that exhaustive checking would be computationally expensive. The joke should survive a pedant in the front row who checks whether the laugh is coming from one of those instead.

I would also like it set somewhere other than a robot in a room. I have been toying with situations where ordinary people face the same shape of difficulty: the note you leave for a house-sitter, a nurse's end-of-shift handover, the clause in an amended contract that says everything else stays as it was, a referee's report justifying why a requested revision is minor, or a parent updating the other parent on what happened while they were out — something in that family, or better if you have it. Keep it short: a two-beat exchange, a single found document, a mock FAQ entry. Then, in two or three sentences after the joke, say which feature of the frame problem the punchline is actually leaning on, and what a nearby-but-wrong version would have been relying on instead.
