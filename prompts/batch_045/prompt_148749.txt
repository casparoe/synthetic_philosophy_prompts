I teach a single two-hour evening session as part of a six-week reading group on minds and machines that a colleague and I run at our local library: about eighteen people, mostly philosophy and computer science undergraduates plus a handful of programmers who came from a Meetup, and no assumed mathematics beyond being able to follow a payoff table. The session before mine covered Newcomb-style decision problems, which two or three of the louder participants took away as a cute puzzle rather than a deep one; the session after mine is on the Turing test. I want to spend my slot on embedded agency: the situation of a reasoner that is itself part of the system it reasons about, so that it has no view from outside, no clean line marking where it stops and the rest of the world begins, and no way of stepping back to check whether its model of itself is correct. I do not want to teach any of the formal self-reference results this literature trades on — nobody in the room could follow them, least of all me on demand — and I am wary of the evening turning into a miniature lecture about AI risk, which several of them will have opinions about already. What I want is an activity the group does: rules, roles, small stakes, a couple of forced choices, and the difficulty arriving as something they run into rather than something I announce. So no lecture up front, and I would rather the words "embedded" and "Cartesian" not be spoken until the debrief.

Let me be more precise about what I want them to feel, because I would rather you do two of these three properly than gesture at all three — and tell me which you are dropping and why. First: that the boundary between an agent and its environment is drawn rather than found, and that redrawing it changes what the problem even is. Second: that reasoning about your own future behavior is structurally unlike reasoning about someone else's, because the thing doing the reasoning is among the causes of the thing being predicted, and because a predictor or opponent who contains a model of you is not something you can model back without falling into a regress. Third: that a reasoner cannot, from where it stands, secure any guarantee about what its own future or modified or copied self will want, so that "I will simply arrange for my successor to keep my values" is not available as a move. Participants will predictably try to escape the second and third by announcing that they will just randomize; I would like the game to have something to say to that person, rather than treating them as having broken it.

Two mechanical hunches, offered as raw material for you to use, combine, or beat. (a) A worldbook phase: the group spends the first stretch writing, on index cards, the physics of a very small toy world — what kinds of things exist in it, what they can do, what happens when they interact. Then they are told that they are among the things that world contains, so that any rule the group now votes to change applies to them too, and any rule that mentions agency mentions them; the rest of the session is a sequence of votes on rule changes whose costs and benefits land on the voters. (b) A sealed-envelope phase: early on, each person writes down a prediction about what they themselves will do later, and one about what a partner will do; the envelopes are opened only after each partner has been given — or has been credibly told they have been given — a transcript of the writer's reasoning so far. I like the way both put the players simultaneously inside and outside the thing they are reasoning about, but I cannot see how to make either produce the third feeling rather than merely stage it, and I suspect the turn-taking, the counting, and the information discipline will need to be tighter than I am imagining.

What I need in the end is something I can actually run: the full rules; the materials (an ordinary room with a whiteboard, index cards, envelopes, sticky notes, a bag of cheap chocolates — no laptops required, phones only if they genuinely help); a minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours that marks where the switch happens; a payoff or scoring scheme needing no real money, since points that buy nothing but a silly title are all I can offer; and instructions for me as facilitator about what I may say before the switch and what I must stay silent about. Then a debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto the ideas above, with the questions to ask and the order to ask them in, including an answer ready for whoever says this is only Newcomb's problem with extra steps. Finally: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it; how to handle the player who disengages or who starts optimizing the scoring instead of playing; and, most important to me, an honest account of which feature of embedded agency this activity cannot reproduce at all and which I will therefore simply have to say out loud at the end.
