I sit on the board of a mid-sized regional transit authority. Since 2023 we have run twelve low-speed automated shuttles on a fixed three-mile downtown loop. Each vehicle carries an onboard attendant — a contract worker earning $19 an hour with nine days of training — whose only controls are an emergency-stop button and a door override; there is no steering wheel and no accelerator. Our public materials describe the attendant's role as intervening in rare edge cases, and our own Policy 4.2 requires a qualified human operator aboard every revenue vehicle.

Ten weeks ago a shuttle struck a pedestrian in a marked crosswalk at eleven miles per hour. She survived with a fractured pelvis and tibia. Telemetry shows the perception system classified her as static street furniture for 4.1 seconds before impact, and that the emergency stop was pressed 1.6 seconds after the collision. Nine days earlier the manufacturer had pushed an over-the-air update raising the confidence threshold for pedestrian classification near fixed objects; it was validated against fleet-level statistics, where it reduced false-positive emergency stops by a few percent, and was not validated against individual edge cases. Attendants were not informed, and our union agreement bars new duties without bargaining. Eye-tracking logs — which attendants know we keep — show that in the thirty seconds before impact the attendant had looked at the mandatory fleet tablet for nineteen of them; that tablet displays telemetry for all twelve shuttles and requires an acknowledgement tap every thirty seconds.

Two things I want you to engage with directly. First, our safety officer has argued in writing that the attendant role suffers from what she calls attentional bankruptcy: the monitoring architecture spends the operator's budget of responsible attention, so the human is nominally in the loop but has nothing left to be responsible with, and her presence then absorbs blame without adding control. Second, note the naming asymmetry: the demand that the authority identify, by name, the person responsible has grown louder in exact proportion to how little any individual could have done to prevent this, and the injured pedestrian's family has formally requested that we name someone.

We must put a recommendation to the city council before its renewal vote in three weeks. Please draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a timeline — retaining the attendant role as configured, redesigning it so that the human has genuine control, moving to remote oversight, or removing the human and adopting an explicit enterprise-liability and no-fault compensation posture with a stated refusal to blame individuals — and it should do the following: engage seriously with the strongest argument for the option you reject rather than the weakest; address what the authority should say publicly about who is to blame and whether the attendant should be named; and account for the fact, which our counsel has flagged, that keeping humans aboard may worsen our litigation position while removing them may worsen real safety.
