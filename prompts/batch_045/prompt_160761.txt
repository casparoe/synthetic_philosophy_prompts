I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and in three weeks I'm giving a work-in-progress talk in my department's philosophy of mathematics series: twenty minutes of presentation, then about twenty-five minutes of questions, with replies that have to land in roughly ninety seconds. What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view.

The thesis I'm defending is that the choice between classical and intuitionistic logic is not where the disagreement between realists and anti-realists in mathematics lives. Constructive mathematics, on my reading, is classical mathematics carried out inside a language with an implicit verifiability operator: the negative translation shows that classical arithmetic theorems become intuitionistic theorems about double negation, and the embedding of intuitionistic propositional logic into S4 shows that intuitionistic reasoning is classical reasoning conducted under a modal operator. On that picture the two systems are intertranslatable variants of one another rather than rival metaphysics, and whatever real disagreement survives is disagreement about ontology, not about logic.

My argument has three steps. First, a criterion I introduce and call the clearance condition: two logical systems make a genuine, non-verbal disagreement about a domain only if they disagree about some sentence in that domain whose truth, once established, could in principle be certified by a finite computation that both parties could perform and inspect — the "clearable" sentences. For first-order arithmetic I place the clearance class at the level of sentences of the form "for every x there is a y such that R(x,y)" with R decidable. Second, the conservativity result for such sentences obtained via the Friedman translation: classical Peano arithmetic and Heyting arithmetic prove the same ones, so within the clearance class there is no disagreement at all. Third, a stability requirement I impose on myself: a verdict of "merely verbal" ought to survive the passage to richer languages, and mine does not, since once we move to full second-order mathematics classical logic proves existence claims that intuitionistic logic does not. So I want to conclude something narrower than I advertised: the dispute is locally verbal at the first-order computable level and genuinely ontological higher up, which means constructivism as a philosophy of logic was never the interesting thesis and the realism question has migrated to higher-order ontology.

The room matters a great deal here. Roughly twelve people. Most are philosophers of mathematics of a broadly realist stripe — neo-Fregeans and ante rem structuralists, a couple of whom think the access problem for mathematical objects is still the crux and who will suspect that any "verbal dispute" verdict is a way of changing the subject. Two are philosophical logicians who work on the metatheory of arithmetic and know the negative translations and the constructive subsystems cold; one of them works on the point that constructive mathematics itself fragments into incompatible schools, differing over principles like Markov's rule and Church's thesis, and will press on whether "constructivism" is even one position for my verdict to attach to. One is a computer scientist from a neighbouring group who works on dependent type theory and proof assistants, and for whom a proof is a program: she will object in the vocabulary of computational content and extraction rather than the vocabulary of truth. And nobody in the room is a card-carrying intuitionist, so I cannot rely on anyone sharing the constructive starting intuition — every objection will come from a realist, a logician, or a type theorist.

Please give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, with a ninety-second reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft.
