I'm leading one session of my 4th-year epistemology seminar next week and I need an actual activity, not a discussion plan. Setup: 16 students, 80 minutes, ordinary seminar room with a projector and a laptop, and I can bring index cards, a deck of cards and a bag of poker chips. No apps, no phones if I can help it. My profs one condition was that the class does something rather than listening to me present.

The session is on that Williamson argument that no non-trivial condition is luminous — no condition such that, in every case where it holds, you're in a position to know that it holds — and then on the move from that to the failure of KK: if knowing p doesn't in general put you in a position to know that you know p, the principle is just false. As I understand it the argument runs off three things: a long series of cases where adjacent ones are imperceptibly different, the requirement that knowledge needs a margin for error (you'd have to be right, not just actually right, in the neighbouring cases too), and the luminosity claim itself. Those three can't all hold.

I want them to run into that before anyone names it. So: I don't say "luminous" or "KK" until the debrief, and the result should come out of the rules and the incentives rather than out of me asking leading questions.

Roughly what I have in mind:

- A first phase that's a real game with real if tiny stakes — chips, an explicit payoff scheme — built around a series of cases that differ imperceptibly, with catch trials mixed in, arranged so that playing well requires claiming knowledge at each step while staying safe requires leaving a margin, and those two pull apart in their hands. I was thinking something perceptual (a tone ramping up from below threshold? two weights? two nearly identical greys on the projector?) but pick whatever is actually runnable in a seminar room and say why.
- A second phase where they get to repair the condition and try to save luminosity — coarsen it, build their own judgment into it, or redefine it into something trivially true — and then find out what each repair costs them. Someone in seminar already tried "fine, then the luminous condition is: I'm in pain and I believe I'm in pain", and nobody had a good reply on whether that's cheating.
- A short third phase where the same structure gets turned on their own knowing, so the KK consequence lands without me announcing it.
- A wrinkle I'd like handled: someone said the argument only seems to go through if you hold the basis or method fixed across the neighbouring cases, since luminosity only says you're in a position to know — maybe by some other route in the next case — so nothing forces you to be unreliable there. Is that a real gap, and can the game be built so that escape isn't available while they're playing but does get opened up in the debrief?

Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific premises, says what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guesses: people watching my hands, counting trials, adopting a fixed threshold policy, or refusing to claim anything and making the game unplayable — tell me how to handle each), and is honest about which part of the argument the game is rigging rather than showing. I'd rather they notice for themselves that the payoff asymmetry is doing the work of the margin-for-error premise than feel tricked about it later. And tell me which bit I just have to say out loud at the end because no game can produce it.
