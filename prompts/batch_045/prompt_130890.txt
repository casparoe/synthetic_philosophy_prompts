I put together a small dossier of fictional corporate documents for a reading group I run on blame, responsibility, and the ways institutions quietly engineer responsibility out of their own paperwork, and the next piece I need is a parody of a multinational's internal compliance-and-conduct policy — the solemn HR document that circulates after a scandal and that nobody reads — for an invented logistics-and-software conglomerate (call it the Arnswald Group) whose board has adopted Leibniz's moral psychology and his theodicy as its official ethics framework. I'd like roughly 700–800 words: a short preamble in the voice of the Group's Chief Ethics Officer, then eight to ten numbered clauses, then an appendix of three short sample determinations applying the code to named cases. My colleague thinks any such document becomes incoherent within a clause; I think the interesting version is one that stays coherent all the way down and is worse for it, so keep the reasoning tight and the tone deadpan and administrative throughout.

Here is the machinery the document should run on, stated in my own words so you don't have to guess how committed I am to it. First, no agent ever acts without the perception of some good, and the will always follows the greatest apparent good, so that what presents itself as malice, perversity, or weakness of will is really a confused or partial perception of something genuinely good. Second, every bad thing is permitted only because it is bound up with a greater good that could not have been got without it, and once the connection is traced the bad thing is not merely excused but revealed as a condition of something better. Third, goods conflict: some goods cannot be realized alongside others, and having one may exclude having another. Fourth, any legitimate response to wrongdoing is measured by whether it improves the wrongdoer or protects or instructs others, never by whether it pays back a debt. Two hard constraints: the name Leibniz may not appear anywhere, and neither may Candide, Pangloss, the familiar slogan about the best of all possible worlds, or the word for the branch of theology that defends providence — the comedy has to live entirely in how the policy reasons, not in name-dropping.

The jokes should come from that machinery working exactly as designed, and they should be fair: someone who has actually read Leibniz on these matters should recognize each clause as an exaggeration of something he really held rather than a straw man. In particular the code may never use the vocabulary of blame, desert, guilt, or retribution — I want the drafters visibly straining to say something actionable about misconduct using only the vocabulary of clarification, amendment, and example — and the only sanction available should be further clarification, which means the sanctions section has to face up squarely to what happens when clarification fails, and then when it fails again. I'd also like an office of compossibility review that must sign off on any proposed reform and that finds, correctly and on principle, that the reform's benefits are not jointly realizable with something the Group already does, so that the Group cannot change anything and must explain why this is rigor rather than inertia; and a certification form on which a manager declares that a bad outcome was a necessary condition of a greater one, with the consequence that the person who brought it about is neither sanctioned nor thanked. For the appendix: a regional director who diverted client funds for two years and whose diversion accidentally exposed a fraud four times larger; a whistleblower whose disclosure was accurate, well motivated, and destroyed a quarter of the Group's market value; and an employee who credibly insists that she perceived no good whatsoever in what she did and cannot be brought to see one. Close it straight-faced, with no editorializing, no footnotes, no citations, and no final paragraph telling the reader what to think: by the end, someone who has followed the reasoning should be able to feel, without being told, that a view like this has quietly made blame unusable, and should be left genuinely unsure whether that is a defect in the view or a discovery about blame.
