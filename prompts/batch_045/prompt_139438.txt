I am in the lower sixth and I help run a small philosophy society at my school, and next term we are doing a short series on whether anyone's suffering can really be deserved. I have been given the session on karma understood as a theory of moral desert rather than as a piece of religious imagery, and I would like your help building the main instrument for it: a decision tree, set out in sentences rather than as a diagram, that begins from the single commitment that some person's suffering is in some way a repayment for what he has done, and sorts that commitment into the distinct positions it can become. I would like eight branch questions and between ten and fourteen leaves. Please build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of views: give me first the questions and the answer-patterns they permit, then place the actual positions into the space that results, and tell me plainly which combinations no one occupies and why you think each is empty, whether because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely untried.

Every branch question must be a concrete case or claim that someone in the sixth form can answer without knowing any labels, since the point of the session is that people discover they disagree rather than pick a name they like. Four of the questions must turn on cases I have drafted. The first, which I call the unremembered heir, is of a girl born with a painful and shortening condition whom we are to imagine, just for the case, to have been cruel in an earlier life she cannot remember and that no one can verify: is the suffering hers to deserve, or is it simply suffering? The second, the wrong currency, is of a man who spent his life making other people feel small and who dies painlessly, honoured and rich: has the debt been paid, or must repayment be in kind? The third, the flat tyre, is of two people who form the same murderous intention, one of whom carries it out while the other is prevented by a breakdown on the motorway: is the desert the same? The fourth, the inherited bill, is of a village that grows rich on money extracted by the grandparents and whose grandchildren, who disapprove of the whole business, lose everything in a flood. Please also find room somewhere in the tree for whether the ledger can run a credit, so that someone who has suffered far more than he ever inflicted is owed something, and for whether anyone other than the person concerned could in principle read the balance, since I suspect that is where the view becomes impossible to test.

Among the leaves I need, named in your own words, are a retributive one on which the suffering is genuinely deserved; a corrective one on which it is not deserved at all but is inflicted for the sake of the person's reform; a constitutive one on which the consequence of a bad act just is the becoming of a certain sort of person, so that nothing is imposed from outside; and two exits from the whole idea, one deflationary (there is only causal balance in the world, no desert) and one sceptical (karmic language cannot be checked even in principle and had better be dropped). If any pair of these turns out to sit in the same cell, say so rather than inventing a distinction to prise them apart, and flag any leaves your questions cannot separate.

Two constraints. Please do not lean on scriptures, quotations, or named authorities, and please avoid Sanskrit and Pali vocabulary: I cannot check any of it, and neither can my audience, so I want the distinctions earned from the cases. And please commit: choose one ordering of the branch questions and defend it, give one diagnosis for each empty cell, and tell me which leaf you would stand on yourself and why, instead of staying neutral between the options. I would be grateful if you wrote the whole thing as continuous prose, a bit like a long email, with no headings, bullet points, bold type, tables or diagrams, since I want to be able to read it aloud to the group.
