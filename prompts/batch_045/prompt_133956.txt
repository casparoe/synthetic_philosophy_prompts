Hey — I'm a science journalist and I'm finishing a piece on a new twin study, and the whole nut graf rests on an inference I'm not sure about.

The study (big national registry, a couple thousand twin pairs, same measures run in two countries) puts the heritability of years of schooling at around 0.5. Two things jump out of the tables: the number is higher in the Nordic sample than in the US one, and inside both samples it's higher for kids whose parents finished college than for kids whose parents didn't finish high school. My read of that: it's what you'd expect if a generous welfare state and well-off parents flatten out the environmental differences — food, tutoring, neighbourhoods, whether anyone in the house has ever dealt with a university. Once the environment stops varying much, whatever variation is left over has to be genetic. So the Nordic number is bigger *because* the place is more equal.

Here's where I loose my footing. If that's right, then it looks like the more equal you make a society, the more of what's left is genetic — which is to say, the less there is left for schools or policy to do. Equality kind of uses up the environmental part, and the genetic part is the floor you hit when you're done. I want to run that as the closing idea: heritability as a measure of how much room there is to improve an outcome. (Related: in an earlier draft I wrote that a heritability of 0.5 means half of the gap in test scores between rich and poor kids is genetic. A quant psych person I ran it past flagged it, I cut the line, but I dont really understand why it was wrong either — so if you can explain that too, please do.)

So: is the closing idea right, half-right, or backwards? I need to know before I file, and I need a version of the point I could actually defend to a behavioural geneticist who will definitely email me about it. What can a heritability number tell you about whether an outcome can be changed, and what can't it tell you — and where exactly does my chain of reasoning break?
