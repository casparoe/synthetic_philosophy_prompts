so I'm a backend engineer, basically zero philosophy background unless you count arguing in code reviews, and I work at a company that deploys government-facing infrastructure in a couple of countries in West Africa and Southeast Asia. I keep running into the same class of bug at work and I've realised I don't actually know what my own position is on it, so I want to hear two smart people go at it.

The setup: our field teams keep getting asked for money or gifts in situations that feel morally completely different from each other, but our compliance handbook and basically every international index I've seen lumps them into one number. I've started privately sorting them into three buckets and I want the dialogue to use my buckets (or tell me they're wrong):

- "latency bribes": the thing you are legally entitled to is just... not happening, or happening at 1% speed, and paying makes the system run at the speed it's supposed to. Customs sitting on a rack of servers for three weeks, $150 makes it move.
- "routing bribes": the payment changes which rule applies to you. Skipping an environmental review, getting a competitor's licence yanked.
- "obligation gifts": not a transaction at all — you pay a hospital bill for an official's mother, or sponsor a digital-literacy programme run by a minister's family foundation. Nothing is bought right now. You've just made a durable relationship where someone now owes you, and one day someone will call it in.

Here's what I want. Write me a dialogue between two people who are both sharp and both acting in good faith, somewhere between 1000 and 1500 words, no narrator interjections.

Person A works at our HQ, in the global integrity / compliance world. She thinks the anti-corruption standard really is universal: a public office is a trust, the whole point of a state is that it treats you as a case of a rule rather than as a person it likes, and the moment an official's judgment is bought the institution stops being able to do the thing it exists to do. I halfway remember that US anti-bribery law actually carves out small "facilitation" payments, which already feels like an admission that the clean rule doesn't survive contact with reality — but I want her to make the strongest version, not a "rules are rules" version. In particular I want her to go after my first bucket: if clerks throttle on purpose because they're paid to un-throttle, then paying is feeding the ratchet, and my "it's just latency" excuse is helping build the latency.

Person B is from one of the places we operate and is tired of being lectured. He thinks the impersonal-bureaucracy ideal is a pretty recent local export that most of the exporting countries don't even meet, that refusing a gift can be the genuinely insulting and relationship-destroying move, and that the real question isn't "was it impersonal" but "who is this person actually accountable to, and can the people they serve punish them." I don't want him to get away with "it's our culture" either — I want him to have to answer for the fact that clientelist systems reliably deliver worse roads and worse clinics, and that the "it's our culture" move is usually made by elites who are doing great out of it.

Things I specifically want them to fight over rather than talk past each other:

1. My latency bucket. Is it even stable? If the queue is slow because the office has two working computers and no budget — not because anyone is extorting me — then is the payment a bribe or just me self-funding a service the state failed to deliver? And if the two cases are genuinely different, how would anyone in the field ever tell them apart?
2. Hiring a minister's cousin who is, in fact, the best candidate. My company hires people's friends' referrals all the time and calls it networking. Is the difference just that one is called nepotism, or is there something real there?
3. The thing that makes me most uncomfortable: our own HQ does strategic philanthropy, maintains "relationships" with regulators, and there's an entire legal industry of influence-buying in Washington. Person B gets to use this. Person A then has to explain why that isn't just the same practice with better lawyers and more digits — and if her answer is "it's legal," I want to see whether she can say why legality is the load-bearing part.
4. Where the hell do my obligation gifts go? They seem worse than latency bribes to me and I can't say why, and "it creates a conflict of interest" feels circular.

I'd also like them, by the end, to sort out how much of their disagreement is actually about morality versus about empirical claims nobody in the room can settle (does impersonal administration actually produce better outcomes in a low-capacity state? do anti-corruption campaigns get enforced selectively?) versus just verbal. And if one of my three buckets turns out to be doing no real work, have one of them collapse it and say so.

Finally: if they land anywhere at all, tell me what the two or three lines in our team handbook should actually say. I have to be able to hand it to a 24-year-old engineer with a laptop and a deadline, and it can't be "use your judgement."
