I'm putting together a problem set for a grad seminar on induction and I want one exercise that isn't just "state the new riddle of induction and say why grue is unprojectible". What I need is the opposite: a case where the grue-style predicate is the one a competent reasoner should actually project. I've been going back and forth on whether that's even doable without cheating, so I want the case built to specifications and then checked against them.

What I need:

- A realistic setting, one paragraph. A mine, a factory QC line, a hospital lab running a screening programme, a field survey, an archive where only some boxes got catalogued — your pick. No sci-fi, no demons, no perfect predictors, no one being tricked by an evil neuroscientist.
- Two predicates that have exactly the same extension over every observation available in the case and that disagree on the unobserved instances. One is the plain, natural-looking one; the other is positional, indexed either to a fixed cutoff time t or to the order in which observations become available. Say which one you're indexing to and why that choice matters.
- The positional predicate has to be the one that wins.
- But you can't get there by the cheap routes. No stipulating that the objects change at t. No hidden mechanism that switches on at t (no pigment that degrades, no supplier that swaps, no instrument that gets recalibrated at t unless you can make that fall out of the setup rather than being put in by hand). The positional predicate can't be syntactically longer or more gerrymandered than the natural one — they should be interdefinable, roughly the way green and blue come back out of grue and bleen. And you can't lean on green having been projected more often in the past: in your case the observation vocabulary should be new enough that the entrenchment of the two predicates themselves is a tie.
- The reason the positional side wins should be structural, about how the evidence was gathered rather than about the world changing underneath you — something about the sampling, the selection rule, what it takes for an item to become observable at all. If you can build it so that Goodman's own overhypothesis move, the bit about all the emeralds coming from the same source and the sample being representative, ends up favouring the positional predicate too, so much the better.
- The positional hypothesis should be unreachable by any observation the agent can make before t: nothing available in the case may count against it.

After the scenario I want: the two predicates written out explicitly; a line-by-line check against each of the constraints above; which single feature of the case is doing the work; what happens to the verdict if you remove that feature; and how someone who thinks entrenchment is what settles projectibility would push back on your case. Last thing: tell me honestly if one of my constraints is doing something illegitimate — in particular if the "it's about the sampling" move is really just a mechanism at t smuggled in under another name, I'd rather know than have a clean-looking case that collapses when a student pokes it. Keep the whole thing under a page.
