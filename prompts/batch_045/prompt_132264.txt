I am working on a paper about whether anything survives of Keynes's early logical conception of probability once it is translated into the vocabulary of imprecise probability, and I need a number rather than a survey of the options available.

The proposition I want your credence on, call it K: there can be two propositions h1 and h2 such that, relative to a fixed body of evidence e, there is no fact of the matter whether h1 is more probable on e than h2 — and this absence of a fact is not merely our ignorance of a determinate number that a set-of-priors treatment would capture, but a genuine failure of the comparative probability relation to be total.

Give me your credence in K, and decompose it. The sub-questions I would like numbers on, with one line of justification each, then the combination:

1. That some non-psychological conception of probability — evidential or logical, rather than degree-of-belief — is defensible at all, i.e. that the familiar Ramsey-style critique does not force every probability statement to be read as a report about an agent's confidences.
2. That any pattern of comparative confidence containing gaps can be faithfully represented by a convex set of numerically sharp probability functions, so that "imprecision" already says everything Keynes was gesturing at and K is either false or trivially reducible to it.

Then the consideration I actually care about, which I will call the ledger argument. Its premises: an agent must act; any pattern of choice satisfying only weak structural conditions (transitivity plus a dominance condition) can be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to some set of priors; therefore any comparative-probability gap either vanishes under set-representation or leaves no trace in choice; a thesis with no trace in choice is decision-theoretically inert. Conclusion: Keynes's indeterminacy thesis is either false or empty. Assign a probability to the representation premise, to the premise about inertness, and to K conditional on the argument's being sound, and tell me which of those three carries the weight.

3. Whether Keynes required the strong reading, on which the gap persists even when e is deductively closed and complete, or whether the weaker reading — indeterminacy as a fact about bounded evidence, which would dissolve for an ideal reasoner — suffices for everything he wanted; and how much your number on K would drop if only the weaker reading were available.
4. Whether his distinction between the probability of a hypothesis and the weight or amount of evidence bearing on it does any work that no set-of-priors framework can replicate.

Report the total, state explicitly whether you are reading K as a claim about evidential relations or about the attitudes of a rational agent, and say how much your number would shift under the other reading. Then tell me which single term your total is most sensitive to, and what kind of consideration — a formal result, a counterexample, a conceptual argument — would move it most.

For calibration: I sit at roughly 0.55 on K, a colleague at roughly 0.15. On the decomposition above, which sub-question is our disagreement most likely living in, and what would each of us have to concede to move toward the other?
