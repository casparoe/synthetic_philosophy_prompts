I am in the final year of an undergraduate philosophy degree, and I am writing the assessed essay for my module on the philosophy of perception. I want to write about Shakespeare's Hamlet, and in particular about the scenes in which the Ghost appears: the opening scene on the battlements, where several people see it and one of them is converted from scepticism; the scene in which the Ghost tells Hamlet of the murder and is apparently present to him in a way it is not to the others; and, above all, the closet scene in Act 3 in which Hamlet sees the Ghost and Gertrude sees nothing, and accounts for his experience by saying that his brain has coined it.

The seminar has spent the term on the argument from illusion. Roughly: since a hallucination can be, from the inside, indistinguishable from a genuine perception, seeing and hallucinating must share a common experiential core, and what we are directly aware of in either case is some intermediate item rather than the object itself. Disjunctivism denies the inference: it holds that seeing is a relational state in which the object itself is a constituent, and that a hallucination is not a state of that kind at all, so there is no common factor for the argument to isolate and nothing for it to generalise over.

What I want help with is a wrinkle I have been trying to work out on my own. Call a state object-involving if the subject's experience is partly constituted by a mind-independent particular that is genuinely there before them, and non-misleading if that experience presents the particular as it actually is. The two plainly come apart. And the play seems to me to be a machine for producing cases where they do. Hamlet's own hypothesis, later on, is that the thing he saw may have been a devil who assumed his father's shape: if that hypothesis is the true one in the fiction, then his state was object-involving and yet radically misleading about what he was confronted with. So: would a disjunctivist have to count that as a case of genuine perception, or as a case it must classify alongside hallucination? Does the possibility of experience that is object-involving but misleading show that the metaphysical claim about kinds does no epistemic work by itself, so that a disjunctivist who also wants to say that perception puts the subject in a position that no hallucination can is really making two separable claims that can pull apart? And does the play bear this out, given that Hamlet goes on to stage the Mousetrap as a further test rather than trusting what he saw?

I would like a reading, not a survey. I do not need the literature adjudicated or anyone's views attributed; I can get that from the handout. What I want is an account of where the disjunctivist reading genuinely illuminates those scenes and where it strains: ghosts are not the ordinary middle-sized objects the debate is staged over, the play's metaphysics of spirits is not ours, and there is a further fact the play keeps in view — that whether Hamlet is perceiving or merely seeming to is settled, for everyone else in the play, by who has the authority to say so, and Gertrude has it. Is there something in the disjunctivist position that speaks to that social fact, or is that the point at which the reading runs out? Does the play, in the end, dramatise something the arguments leave out — and if so, what, and which scenes would you put at the centre of the essay to make the case?

Please write in British English, and there is no need to quote the play: I know the scenes, so describe them in your own words.
