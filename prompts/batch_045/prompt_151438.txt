Question: In Lewis Carroll's short dialogue about Achilles and the Tortoise, Achilles tries to get the Tortoise to accept a conclusion from two premises:

A: things that are equal to the same thing are equal to one another
B: the two sides of this triangle are things that are equal to the same thing
Z: the two sides of this triangle are equal to each other

The Tortoise grants A and B but refuses to accept Z, and asks Achilles to add a further premise to the list:

C: if A and B are true, Z is true

Achilles writes C down. The Tortoise grants C as well, then asks for one more:

D: if A and B and C are true, Z is true

and so on without end.

(a) Say precisely what the Tortoise is demanding, and explain why the demand regenerates at every step. Your answer should state the standard diagnosis of the story in terms of the distinction between a premise and a rule of inference, and should say whether the story shows anything about the justification of deduction as opposed to about what it takes to get someone to infer.

(b) Suppose that, instead of C, Achilles offers a looping premise — a premise that mentions itself:

P: if A and B and this very premise are true, Z is true

Assess this proposal. Does adding P to the premise set satisfy the Tortoise's demand once and for all, so that the regress of premises stops after finitely many steps? And is the argument for Z that results a good one — does it give the Tortoise a non-question-begging reason, and does it leave him with nothing further to reasonably ask for? Show your work. Here's the answer I want marked:

(a) The Tortoise grants A and B but won't take the step to Z. What he asks for is a further premise that records the link between them: C, which says that A and B entail Z. Once Achilles writes C down, the Tortoise accepts that too, but now wants D: if A, B and C all hold, Z holds. And so on forever. The standard diagnosis is that the Tortoise is confusing a premise with a rule. A is a premise, B is a premise, but the move from A and B to Z is licensed by a rule, and no amount of writing that rule down as one more line of the argument can do the rule's work, because a premise — even one that formulates a rule — is inert unless you already have some rule for using it. So each new conditional just re-poses the same question one level up: granted all of this, why should I take the step to Z? The regress isn't a chain of reasons that grows longer; it's the same refusal re-applied at each level. Note also that the Tortoise never challenges whether A, B or C is true; he grants every premise he's handed, so the problem isn't about where premises get their support. What the story shows is that you can't force someone to infer by adding premises, since inferring is something you do, not something you can put on a list. It doesn't show that deduction is unjustifiable or that it rests on a circular foundation, because validity is a matter of the premises guaranteeing the conclusion, and nothing in the dialogue touches that.

(b) The looping premise is a clever fix and I think it works. The Tortoise's standing demand is: give me a premise of the form if everything I currently accept is true, then Z is true. Once P is on the list the list is just A, B and P, and P says exactly that if A, B and P are all true then Z is true. So his demand is already met by a premise he has already accepted, and the regress terminates at a fixed point instead of running forever. This isn't circular in the bad sense. Circular would be a premise that asserts its own truth, or an argument that uses Z in order to establish Z; P does neither, since it only mentions itself inside its own antecedent, and mentioning a sentence isn't asserting it. So both the circular horn and the endless horn are avoided. And because every demand the Tortoise has actually made is now satisfied, he isn't in a position to ask for another premise — anything further he asked for would just repeat P. That means Carroll's moral has a limited scope: it applies to premise sets that don't contain their own linking conditional, but not to looping ones. We then get a short argument: A, B, P, one application of the conditional, done. As for whether P might be paradoxical, I don't think so: its antecedent is just a conjunction of premises, one of which happens to be P itself, so it's an ordinary material conditional describing what would follow if the whole set held, and there's nothing self-undermining about that.

(c) Yes — the Tortoise's regress is a version of the regress of justification, specifically the third horn of the trilemma: every justification either stops at an unsupported assertion, goes in a circle, or goes on forever, and Carroll is showing that logic is stuck with the third. The Tortoise's point is that each new conditional premise is itself an unsupported claim standing in need of support: C says that A and B entail Z, but what entitles us to C? Another conditional, D — and what entitles us to D? Another still. Each premise inherits the defect of the one before it, so the chain never terminates and we never arrive at Z. The only alternatives are the other two horns: either we dogmatically assert a rule of inference and refuse to give any reason for it, which is the first horn, or we justify a rule by using that very rule, which is the second. So the dialogue shows that any attempt to give deduction a justification ends up on one of the three unhappy horns, and the best we can actually do is stop somewhere and dig in, which is the first horn in disguise.
