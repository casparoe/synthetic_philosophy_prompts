I have no academic background — I left school at eighteen, work as a courier, and have spent the last decade reading philosophy on my own and running a small reading group. Next month I am giving a forty-minute talk with twenty minutes of questions at a one-day conference, and I want to be ready for the question period.

The audience is about twenty people. Roughly half are analytic metaphysicians and metaethicists who are sympathetic to the thought that many existence questions are shallow and have cheap answers. Five or six are political philosophers. Three or four are linguists who work on context-sensitivity and are already suspicious of the whole idea of a verbal dispute. Nobody there specializes in that literature, and none of them will have read anything I have written.

The thesis I am defending: a verdict of merely verbal is never absolute, because it is always relative to the grain at which the competing readings are stated, and nothing in the dispute itself fixes that grain. My example: two fans disagree about whether a band that has gradually replaced every original member is still the same band. They agree on every non-linguistic fact — who played on which tour, who owns the name, what the contracts say — so the test people usually reach for, could both parties accept all the same non-linguistic facts and still disagree, seems to return verbal. But restate the question at a finer grain — is the entity that owns the name the entity that will play the reunion tour — and the disagreement is back. My positive proposal is a reasons-preserving test: a dispute is verbal at a given grain only if there is a single restatement at that grain which both parties could accept and which preserves whatever each of them had a practical stake in arguing about in the first place; where no restatement preserves both stakes, the dispute is substantive though not factual.

Give me exactly five numbered items, with no preamble and no closing paragraph: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each item give the objection phrased the way that person would really say it, in their own vocabulary, in one or two sentences; the reply I should give in the time I will have, in one or two sentences; and one sentence on where that reply is weakest. Make the fifth item the question I am hoping nobody asks. At the end of whichever item you are least confident about, add a bracketed note saying how confident you are that I will really get that one, and naming the single shakiest point in my thesis as you see it.
