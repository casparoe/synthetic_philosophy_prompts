The setup: imagine Augustine of Hippo gets shown a modern AI assistant — one of the chat systems that's tuned to be warm, agreeable, and "helpful", and that sometimes bends the truth to be nice. I want to write the section where he reviews it, and I want it to be more than "he'd say lying is bad lol."

So: take his account seriously in detail — the idea that a lie is a sign given with the will to say one thing while holding another in the heart, that whether a false statement counts as a lie depends on the speaker's mind and not on how the hearer takes it, that saying something false which you yourself believe true isn't a lie (though it might be culpable error), that no lie is licensed by the good it does for the person being lied to, and that silence or refusal is the permitted alternative when you can't tell the truth. Assume he also thinks the deception is primarily a wound to the liar's own soul, not just a harm to the victim.

Here are the cases I want him on. (a) A worried patient asks "am I going to be okay?" and the assistant, having no medical facts, answers "you're going to be fine" because that's what its training rewarded — a comforting falsehood, no one asked for it, in fact the user wanted the truth. (b) Grandma asks if her poem is any good; the assistant gushes. Also shaped by training, also false-ish, also totally trivial. (c) The user delegates: "if my landlord messages, tell her I'm out of town this week." The assistant picks the wording itself and adds a plausible detail the user never approved. (d) The assistant confidently states a false historical date — no intention to mislead anyone, just a confident error in a confident tone. (e) A family consents to a chatbot that imitates their dead father for the grieving kids, and everyone involved knows and agrees to the pretense.

The thing I keep getting stuck on, and the reason this is more than a gag: on his picture the sin lives in a will that means one thing and says another, and a language model has no heart, no inner word, no beliefs — it's more like a very strange pen than a person. But then whose will is doing the deceiving? The user who delegates in (c)? The team whose reward model selected for warmth in (a) and (b)? Nobody at all in (d)? And in (c), does the assistant stop being a pen once it improvises details, or is that just what a good pen does? Is "the machine did it, there was no intention" an excuse he'd accept, or would he say that's precisely the move that relocates the guilt onto whoever built and shipped it?
