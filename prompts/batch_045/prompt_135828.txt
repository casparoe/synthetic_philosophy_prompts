I'll be playing a Dennett-style opponent: he agrees with you that there are no experiences during sleep, but thinks that's an *empirical* finding about how brains work, not a remark about what we can say.

My opening: suppose we can decode a continuous stream of imagery from a sleeper's brain during REM and then, just before waking them, quietly swap in a different stream — recorded from another sleeper last Tuesday. In the swap trials the subject's report on waking always matches the swapped-in stream, never their own; they notice nothing inserted; and when we play them both decodings and ask which one they were really undergoing, they pick the swapped one every time, confidently, and find their own unfamiliar. I'll grant you this kills the idea of an experience during sleep that the report is answerable to. But it also gives you a problem: there's now a perfectly ordinary empirical fact of the matter about which cassette got played at awakening, what was on it, and why — we can predict it and we can control it. Once we have the mechanism, isn't all that's left of your position the stipulation that none of it may be called an experience? And isn't that definately just a decision about words rather than a discovery about dreaming?
