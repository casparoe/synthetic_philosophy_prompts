I am writing a long-form essay on suffering-focused ethics, and I want to attach to it a self-scoring diagnostic: an instrument that sorts readers by what they are willing to say about concrete cases rather than by which label they would apply to themselves. I need you to design that instrument, and I need it to be built from the logical space of the debate rather than from a list of familiar names.

Start from this working definition, which I supply so that nothing hangs on how the view is characterised elsewhere. Call a view a lexical-threshold negative-leaning view (LTN) if it holds: (i) hedonic states admit of a severity ordering; (ii) there is a severity threshold T; (iii) no finite quantity of pleasure, however large and however distributed across persons, compensates for a single episode of suffering at or above T; and (iv) below T, pleasures and sufferings trade off in something like the ordinary way. I want the tree to begin from this definition, not from any literature, and I do not want any citations, reading lists, or attributions anywhere in your answer.

The splitting claims I want the branch nodes drawn from are these, in whatever order you judge best:

1. Whether pleasure and suffering are magnitudes on a single scale at all, or only lexically ordered.
2. What bears the threshold: a momentary sensory episode, a temporally extended experience, a whole life, or an entire world.
3. Whether many sub-threshold sufferings can compose into something that clears T, and if so whether composition is additive or takes the maximum.
4. Whether T is fixed or indexed to the subject's own attainable range.
5. How to count a suffering that is partly constitutive of a valued composite experience.
6. Which reading of "outweighs" is in play: that the outcome is not better, that it must not be chosen, or that it is not thereby justified.
7. Whether the lexical priority extends to risks of threshold suffering under ignorance, and whether it extends to creating as well as preventing.
8. Whether the threshold drifts with habituation, and whether any such drift is morally relevant or merely predictive.

Six cases must appear among the questions. The Drip: a very large number of people each endure a brief irritation at the very bottom of noticeable unpleasantness, set against one person enduring one minute just above T. The Chili: a burn that is unpleasant in itself but is the point of an experience the subject values and would repeat. The Smear: two episodes at 0.99T and 1.01T, where the subject cannot tell which is which. The Capped Subject: someone whose attainable range of suffering tops out below T, whether by constitution or by an analgesic. The Bargain: a life of eighty years of intense flourishing containing a single hour above T, offered to someone who does not yet exist. The Ignorant Chooser: a policy set for a population by someone who does not know whether she will be among those above T, or how sensitive she herself is.

The deliverable: nine questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options phrased in ordinary language, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns rather than individual answers onto five to seven leaves. No question may use the words negative utilitarian, threshold, commensurable, lexical, or outweigh, and no option may name a position. Each branch node should be labelled by the claim that splits it; each leaf should carry a name where a name is warranted, or be marked unoccupied, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Include a table showing, for each question, which pair of leaves it separates, and flag any question that separates nothing or merely repeats another. Then give a short section on leaf pairs your instrument cannot distinguish and why, a section on unoccupied combinations in the logical space with a one-line diagnosis of each (incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored), and one calibration item that detects when a respondent has misread a case rather than taken a position, with advice on what to do about it.

The audience is mixed: readers of a semi-popular ethics blog who will grade themselves in about ten minutes, and in a second life an upper-level seminar of about twelve graduate students, so add a brief note on how the same sheet works as a discussion handout. Close with one paragraph on what the shape of the instrument you were able to build itself shows about the debate: specifically, how much of the work is being done by the threshold rather than by the negative-leaning part of the view, and whether the commensurability question turns out to be downstream of the question about what bears the threshold.
