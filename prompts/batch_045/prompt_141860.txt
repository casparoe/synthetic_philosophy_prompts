I work in operations for a regional freight company — I build schedules and balance capacity, and I'm good at it — which is probably why I picked up Hannah Arendt's book about the Eichmann trial after hearing it mentioned on a podcast, and why I have not been able to stop arguing about it with two friends ever since. I've read no philosophy since one elective in high school, so please don't hand me a reading list or point me at the scholarly back-and-forth: I can't get to a university library and I'd rather reason about the cases directly. What I want is the principle behind my own verdicts, if there is one. Here they are. (1) The scheduler: arranges rail capacity, never met a victim, never raised his voice, quietly proud of a well-built timetable, and would have refused to shoot anyone with his own hands. Banal, obviously. (2) The enthusiast: a guard who volunteers for the work, enjoys it, seeks it out. Not banal — one friend says it is, on the grounds that he's as ordinary as anyone, and I think that's exactly the wrong answer. (3) The true believer: well read, articulate, acts from genuine conviction, would keep going without orders. I say not banal, even though his reasons are entirely inherited slogans — which bothers me, because if banality is a matter of stock phrases and second-hand language, he should come out banal. (4) The careerist: no conviction at all, entirely aware of the killing, thinking about it constantly — risk, promotion, appearances — and excellent at his job; he'd switch sides next week if the wind changed. I say banal; my other friend says no, because he is thinking all the time, and banality is supposed to be about a failure to think. (5) The tormented one: keeps a diary, reads poetry, examines himself, feels real guilt, and goes on doing it. I don't want to call this banal, but I can't say why, and it's the case that makes me doubt that banality has anything to do with having "no depths". (6) The dutiful one: acts from an explicit principle, that the lawful order of a lawful state just is the moral law, and he checked the paperwork was countersigned — I have a half-memory that Eichmann himself said something like this at his trial, invoking some famous rule about duty, though I may be mixing up my sources. I say banal, but by what looks like the opposite route: too much rule and too little judgment. (7) The wilfully blind one: stamps the forms, never asks what happens at the other end, would be horrified if told — but the not-asking is something he has practised and got good at. This looks to me like the purest case, and yet "thoughtlessness" again seems the wrong word, because the ignorance is maintained rather than merely suffered. (8) My own candidate, which I've started calling the competence trap: genuine and publicly praised mastery of the narrow sub-task is itself the mechanism — the better he is at the piece he owns, the less he ever has to look at the whole, and the more of his self-respect is invested in not looking. I think this is banal and I think it's the centre of the thing, but I invented it, so test it hard.

The rough principle I keep coming back to is: an evil is banal to the extent that the agent's part in it is fully explained by motives and competencies that are in themselves unremarkable — wanting to do one's job well, to get on, not to be the odd one out — together with a failure to take up the standpoint of anyone outside the task. My worry is that the two halves keep coming apart: the careerist seems to lack the second standpoint but has something like a vice, while the tormented diarist seems to have the second standpoint and still does it, which would make the first half decorative and the second half everything; and the dutiful one fails the second test by having too rigid a standpoint rather than none. There's also a case I go back and forth on: a data person at an insurance firm who builds a model that quietly strips benefits from thousands of people, never meets one of them, experiences it as an optimisation problem, and is kind to his colleagues. I want to say banal, my friend says the analogy is an evasion because the scale and the intent are nothing alike, and I can't settle it.

So: can you give me a principle, or an honest small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself, with the case that motivates each clause? Tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and would force me to overturn, and where it leaves the competence trap. Then tell me what it predicts about three cases I haven't put on the list: a rescuer who saves people from unadmirable motives and never thinks about it morally at all — is there such a thing as banal goodness, and does your answer about it strengthen or weaken your account of banal evil; an agent who performs ordinariness in the dock, playing the dull functionary because it is his best defence, where the banality is staged rather than real; and an agent with no bad motive and no wilful blindness, who simply never had the question occur to him because nothing in his role supplied it. One last thing: one friend insists that the ordinary-motive cases are better described by the grander phrase Arendt apparently used earlier, in a book about totalitarianism, before she switched — as I understand it, something like "radical" evil — on the grounds that ordinary motives, systematically organised, produce destruction on a scale that no personal vice could. Is the right contrast for banality that one, or the demonic/sadistic one, and can I hold both without fudging? If the honest answer is that "banality" bundles two or three separable conditions and my verdicts are tracking different ones in different cases, say so and tell me which condition is doing the real work.
