I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether trained neural networks should be said to have representations, and we stopped without converging — not, I think, because either of us ran out of arguments, but because by midnight I was no longer confident I knew what the disagreement was about. He is finishing a PhD in machine learning and works on interpretability; he is a fairly committed realist about features. I work in philosophy of mind and philosophy of science, and I had been defending a more deflationary line. I would rather not send him another message until I can say what the argument is actually over.

The dispute, stated carefully: feature-level interpretability talks about units that are not individual neurons — directions in activation space, the features that sparse autoencoders recover, whatever a linear probe latches onto — and then says things like "this model represents grammatical number here" or "there is a feature for deception." The question is whether such claims describe representational structure the network really has, or whether they are a parameterization we impose from outside that happens to predict, and sometimes let us control, the network's behaviour.

We agreed on a surprising amount, which is part of why I am suspicious that the residual disagreement is verbal. We agreed that individual neurons are not generally the natural units and that something like superposition is real. We agreed that the recovered features are often humanly interpretable and that intervening along them sometimes changes behaviour in the direction the feature story predicts. We agreed that a probe can recover a direction carrying information about some property even when the network never uses that property, so predictive success cannot by itself settle anything. And we agreed that nobody currently has a criterion that picks out a unique decomposition of activation space into units.

My argument was that the phrase "the network represents X" is doing at least three different jobs at once, and that the realist claim only has teeth at the second and third: (a) *indication* — the direction reliably carries information about X, so that an outside observer can read X off it; (b) *control* — intervening on the direction changes behaviour in the way the X-story predicts, including off the training distribution; (c) *mechanistic use* — the network's own downstream computation is sensitive to that direction *as* X, so that citing the feature explains how the circuit works and why it was learned. I said that nearly all the evidence we actually have supports (a), some of it supports (b), and that (c) is the claim worth arguing about, and is largely unsupported. I also floated a distinction between an *indexical* reading of representation (a direction represents X iff it indicates X for some interpreter) and a *mechanistic* reading (a direction represents X iff the system's own subsequent processing treats it as X), and said the realist needs the second.

He replied that I had moved the goalposts; that (a) is itself a nontrivial discovery and not something you get for free; that (c) is a demand for a kind of explanation nobody can meet for any messy learned system, including brains, so that making it the standard of representation would be a sceptical argument in general rather than a point about neural networks; and that the optimization pressure on the network gives us a prior that the useful, compressible features are really there. At one point he compared the situation to the older philosophy-of-biology argument about whether molecular genetics has a single gene concept or several incompatible but equally legitimate ones — his point being that a concept can be real and useful while admitting no unique carving.

On the whiteboard we then sketched three tests, and I could not tell whether any of them is question-begging:

1. **The transplant test.** Train two models on the same distribution with different seeds and initializations; learn a linear or near-linear alignment between their activation spaces; ask whether features from one transfer to the other, both for prediction and for intervention. If they do, training converges on the same units and the units look like something discovered rather than imposed. My worry: the transfer might be explained by shared data statistics rather than by shared internal machinery.
2. **The rival-basis test.** Deliberately construct a competing decomposition of the same activation space — different units, equally good reconstruction, comparable interventional profile, disjoint feature descriptions — and ask whether the realist has any non-question-begging reason to prefer the original. My worry: the adversary may only be reachable by a construction that no training process would ever find, in which case its existence proves little.
3. **The downstream-readout test.** Ask whether later layers specifically read out the candidate direction, in a way that is not equally well explained by their reading out some scrambled or rotated version of it. My worry: without an independent grip on "specifically," this collapses back into (a).

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as something I need confirmed: this is not a verbal dispute, and it is not going to be settled by running more experiments either. I think the load-bearing question is whether there is a *privileged* decomposition of a trained network into units — whether training dynamics and the loss landscape pick out one carving of activation space as the one the model itself uses — and that this is partly a mathematical question about what training converges to, and partly a question about what standards a claim about units is answerable to. If so, then the realist and I are not disagreeing about facts at all, or about the word "representation," but about which counterfactual-supporting structure claims about representation owe their truth to; and that would explain why we kept talking past each other and why each of us thought the other had changed the subject.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on who won. Argue against my hypothesis where it is wrong; I would much rather be told it is a verbal dispute than have my hypothesis humoured.
