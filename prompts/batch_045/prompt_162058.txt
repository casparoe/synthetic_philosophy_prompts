I copy-edit grant proposals and review articles for a molecular biology institute, which means I read a lot of prose about DNA that is technically careful and yet, I've started to think, quietly doing something. The word "code" is the centre of it. As far as I can tell, the people using it are drawing on at least four different models without noticing that they are different: a cipher, where an arbitrary mapping is fixed by a convention and has to be broken; a computer program, which is executed by machinery that would do nothing without it; a legal code, which is a set of norms that can be complied with or violated; and a body of writing, which is read, punctuated, misspelled, annotated, and can contain junk. Each of these licences a different kind of inference. A cipher suggests someone wrote it and meant something. A program suggests the cell is a machine that would sit inert without instructions, and that finding the instructions is the whole job. A legal code suggests norms, and so talk of errors, violations, and correction. A text suggests that every stretch of sequence is meaningful until proven otherwise. My impression is that the tremendous aptness of the word in the narrow case — there really is an arbitrary, historically frozen mapping from triplets to amino acids — is what quietly pays for its use in the wide cases, where the arbitrariness and the fixing process are absent.

I don't think this is any of the things I'd first reach for, and I'd like to know why it isn't, or which of them it really is a variant of. It isn't plain equivocation, as far as I can see, because the slide never happens inside a single argument: no one sentence, or even one paper, uses the term in two senses in the same inference. Each passage is individually clean; the borrowing is spread across a literature, and across years, and nobody seems to hold the bill. It isn't motte-and-bailey either, because there's no adversarial exchange and no one retreats to a defensible core when challenged — there is no challenge. It isn't quite a dead metaphor, since the term is still doing live work in generating hypotheses, and it isn't quite reification, since nobody quite asserts that the genome is literally a book. Two further details make me think it's a thing of its own. First, the borrowing is patched together from mutually incompatible sources: a text is read by an agent outside it, a program is executed by machinery inside the system, and a blueprint is scaled up rather than decoded — yet the inconsistency never registers, because the terms are used one at a time and never in the same sentence. Second, the aptness seems to attach only where it does: nobody calls the metabolic network a code or a language, even though enzymes map substrates to products just as specifically as the translation machinery maps codons to amino acids. So whatever makes the borrowing acceptable is not mere specificity of mapping.

So: is there an established concept for this — not for "is DNA literally a code", which I know has its own large literature about signalling, convention, and proper function, and which I do not want to reopen — but for the migration of a term's credibility from the case where it fits to the cases where it doesn't, spread across a discourse rather than located in an argument? I have been calling it "authority transfer across senses" in my notes, and my colleague, less charitably, calls it the thesaurus effect; if either is already taken for something else, say so. What I actually want is the carving rather than the label. How does the phenomenon differ from each of the nearby ones I named, and from the general philosophy-of-science story about metaphors and analogies being constitutive rather than decorative? Does it need a sender, an interpreter, or a convention to get going, or would it work equally well with any mechanism that happens to be described in intentional language? Can it be benign — a term migrating into a new domain and carrying fruitful methods with it, as I suspect "selection" and "information" both did — and if so, what distinguishes the fruitful migrations from the merely misleading ones? And finally, give me a diagnostic: a test I could apply to a single paragraph of real molecular-biology prose to tell whether the code talk is doing explanatory work, licensing an inference that would fail without it, or only ornamenting one that stands on its own. If nothing established fits, coin and define a term, and give me two clear cases of it and two near-misses that are not instances.
