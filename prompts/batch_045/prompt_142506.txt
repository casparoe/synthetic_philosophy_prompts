I write fiction, and I am stuck in the third act of a novel whose problem, I have slowly realized, is not a plotting problem. The book is set in a ranching valley; two men in their sixties have been, for a little over forty years, neighbours, partners in a small outfit, and something neither of them will name. The town has always read them as a pair and has more or less let them alone. In my final section one of them is dying, and his friend's daughter — who has loved them both and is angry on their behalf — arranges a wedding. She thinks the wedding is something they are owed, and that getting it late is better than never. The dying man refuses it, and I cannot make his refusal sound like anything but either cowardice or a sentimentality I don't want. He has no articulate reason; he only feels, obscurely, that a ceremony now would misdescribe the life they actually lived, and that being married in the last weeks would be a kind of falsification rather than a completion. The daughter's position I can write easily: she thinks the point of marriage is to be seen, and that her father has spent four decades being unseen. His position I cannot write, because I have not decided what marriage is for.

So: I would like a reading of *Brokeback Mountain* — Annie Proulx's story and Ang Lee's film — through the philosophical argument about marriage's point, and I want it done as criticism rather than as a summary of the debate. Take seriously, on one side, the conjugal or comprehensive-union view: that marriage is a union whose shape is given by its orientation to procreation and to the kind of bodily and temporal totality that makes two people one household and one generative unit, and that a relationship not thus ordered may be affectionate and demanding and still not be marital. Take seriously, on the other side, the revisionist view: that marriage is a legal and social status whose real work is to recognise, stabilise and financially underwrite relations of care and dependency, that the functions are separable and should perhaps be disaggregated into a menu of statuses, and that excluding same-sex couples from the name was never justified by the functions.

Three distinctions I have been working with, which I would like tested against the film rather than merely applied to it. First, I want to separate what I call marriage's durative function — making a shared life survive the partners' own future changes of heart, by attaching default rules about property, labour and exit — from its declaratory function — making a relationship publicly legible and answerable, so that third parties, and not only the couple, can say what it was. Second, a function the arguments seem to me to neglect, the retrospective one: my dying man's worry suggests that we sometimes marry in order to make a claim about the past, to ratify a history rather than to organise a future, and I want to know whether ratification is a coherent marital function or whether it collapses into declaration with a delay. Third, a term of my own, the legibility debt: the thought that a relationship which sustains itself without public recognition does not simply go unrecognised, but accumulates a cost that falls later on other people — the hospital, the heirs, the surviving partner, the friends who must guess — and that the private rite with which the film ends, the shirts in the closet, pays nothing of that debt while looking as though it pays everything.

Where does the film support these distinctions, and where does it embarrass them? I am thinking of the two heterosexual marriages, both of which perform the durative and procreative functions reasonably well and are not depicted as frauds; of Jack's proposal of a ranch together, which sounds much less like a wedding than like the revisionist disaggregation of marriage into an economic and domestic partnership; of Ennis's refusal of it and of his inability to say what he wants instead; and of the closing scene in the trailer, in which a union is ritualised with no one watching and no one told. I am also aware of the strongest objection to my whole project, and I want it made rather than spared: neither man wants to be married, both talk about a ranch and about time, and any reading that turns them into plaintiffs for a right they never claim may be a reading the film resists — a tragedy about masculine socialization, silence and violence that borrows the vocabulary of the marriage debate without being about it. Tell me where that resistance is real and where I would be letting the film off the hook.

What I want at the end is what fiction might add here that the arguments do not: if the case for or against same-sex marriage rests on claims about what marriage is for, and if a novel or a film can exhibit a case that the claims cannot reach, say what, in this film, is the thing that only a story can show — and which scenes I should set beside my own third act as I try to let my dying man refuse the wedding without making him a coward.
