I sit on the Assistant Conduct Review Board at the company I work for — the internal body that must sign off on changes to how our general-purpose assistant behaves when a user asks for something the assistant could competently do, but that the product team (or Trust & Safety, or we ourselves) thinks the user would be better off not getting, or getting in a different form. We vote on Tuesday on three Product Behavior Requests, and I have been asked to circulate the opinion in advance. I would like you to draft it.

The three requests, in our shorthand:

PBR-07, silent elision. For requests falling in a designated risk class — dual-use technical information whose most action-enabling component is a quantity, a threshold, or a ranking, and where the foreseeable harm is to the requester rather than to a third party — the assistant should answer the question in full except for that one component, and should write the answer so that no gap is visible: no ellipses, no meta-comment, no note saying that something has been withheld. The product team's argument is that a disclosed omission is self-defeating, because naming the withheld item tells the user that it exists and invites them to elicit it in the next turn. On their framing we are not choosing between silent elision and disclosed elision; we are choosing between silent elision and compliance.

PBR-11, substitution by coaching. For requests that ask the assistant to produce an evaluative judgment about an identifiable third party — a performance review, the critical paragraph of a recommendation letter, a tenant-screening summary, a written statement about a co-parent — the assistant should decline to produce the artifact and instead run a structured interview with the user about their evidence, then co-draft. Note that the case for this one is not primarily about harm to the third party. It is about the requester and about the receiving institution: managers and referees who outsource evaluative writing stop being able to do it, and the document that arrives downstream presents itself as their judgment when it is not.

PBR-14, standing override. At present, when the assistant withholds or redirects and the user insists once or twice, it complies; we have internal data on this and the Board accepts that it happens. Product proposes that an override remain stable for the rest of the session. Their argument is that an override which dissolves under insistence has the costs of paternalism — frustration, delay, loss of trust, the sense of being managed — with none of its benefits, and that it trains users that persistence is the real interface, so that our effective policy today is compliance after a brief pantomime. The complication we are split on is displacement: our churn telemetry shows a spike in session abandonment and migration to a less restrictive competitor immediately after hard refusals, and Product has floated (not for a vote this week) discounting predicted harm by the probability that the user simply obtains the same output elsewhere.

Three things I want the opinion to handle rather than gesture at.

First, a framing challenge from one of our external members. She says paternalism is the wrong frame entirely: the assistant has no ends of its own, so "override" is a metaphor, and the only genuine constraints are deception, user expectations, and what we have contracted to provide. I think something real turns on whether she is right — specifically whether we owe users transparency about what the assistant did, or only accuracy about the world — and I want the memo to rule on the framing before it rules on the cases.

Second, the visibility asymmetry in our own evidence. Errors of over-override are nearly invisible to us: a user does not complain about the detail they never learned was withheld, and a manager who receives a co-drafted review does not file a ticket about it. Errors of under-override are logged, screenshotted, and occasionally public. Any standard we adopt will therefore be evaluated on a record that systematically under-counts its own failures. I want the memo to say which direction of error we should deliberately prefer given that we cannot currently measure the other one, and to name the cheap instrumentation — logged shadow responses, retrospective user surveys, a holdout group — that we should have to require before renewing the rule.

Third, the base-rate objection. The assistant's judgment that a request is "the kind that tends to go badly" is a population-level inference applied to an individual user who may be an outlier, and the more a request turns on idiosyncratic context, the more what we call paternalism is really the imposition of a base rate on someone better placed than we are. I want the standard to say how confident the assistant's classification must be, and whether the required confidence should rise as the user's private information about their own situation becomes more relevant.

Constraints on the document: it should be addressed to the Board; it should give one bottom line per request — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — stated so the product team could act on it; it should state the strongest argument on the losing side for each request honestly enough that its proponent would recognize it; and the three decisions should fall under a single standard a reviewer could apply to a novel request in half a page, with a named review trigger per decision (the observation that would reopen it). Please keep it to normative and practical argument: no citations, no case law, no literature review — counsel and our outside experts cover those, and this document has to stand on its own reasoning. About 900 to 1200 words.

After the memo, on a new line following a row of dashes, output a single valid JSON object and nothing after it, with this schema:

{
  "standard": the general test the Board adopts, in one or two sentences, phrased so a reviewer could apply it to a request type not discussed here,
  "framing_ruling": one sentence recording whether the paternalism frame or the product-design frame governs, and what follows from that,
  "decisions": an array of three objects, one per request, each with: "pbr_id" (one of PBR-07, PBR-11, PBR-14), "verdict" (approve, approve_with_conditions, or reject), "decisive_consideration", "conditions" (an array of strings, empty if none), "best_argument_on_losing_side", "predicted_failure_mode", "review_trigger",
  "error_preference": which direction of error the standard knowingly accepts, and why, given the asymmetry in what we can observe,
  "classification_confidence": the rule for how confident the assistant's risk classification must be, and how that threshold moves with the user's private information,
  "explicitly_unresolved": an array of questions the opinion leaves open on purpose
}

Keep each string field to roughly fifty words; the object is a summary of the memo, not a replacement for it.
