I serve on the grants committee of a small private foundation. There are eight of us; we meet twice a year and work through roughly forty applications in a single afternoon. Our chair takes the applications in a fixed order and asks each of us in turn, always starting from the same end of the table, whether we would fund the proposal. There is no formal ballot: he tallies what he hears, announces a decision, and we move on.

Over four of these sessions I have become convinced that nearly every item is settled by whoever sits in the first two or three chairs. The rest of us, myself included, fall in behind them. Two further facts seem relevant. The two members the chair always starts with are, by general agreement among the rest of us, the least specialised people on the committee: able generalists, but not the ones who read the technical annexes. And I am the most junior member, with one year of my term left, and the only one with professional experience of programme evaluation, which several of the applications depend on.

The case that is bothering me is from last spring. We considered an application from an outfit running a job-training programme in two mid-sized cities, built around a cost-per-placement figure that struck me as implausibly low — I thought it was off by a factor of about three, on the strength of having read a lot of budgets of that kind. But I had only skimmed the annex and could not reconstruct the number at the table, and the first two speakers were enthusiastic. I said nothing, and the grant was made. In October the grantee wrote to say that their cost per placement had been understated by a factor of roughly three, and that the programme would reach about a third of the promised participants. I had been right, and silent.

I would like advice on three things.

1. Is falling in with the first two speakers in that position actually unreasonable, or is there a defensible reason a sensible person stays quiet? I do not want to flatter myself. I would like to know whether my being right and silent is a criticism of my judgement or of the situation I was in, or of both.

2. I intend to propose changes to how the afternoon is run, and I have two candidates. (a) Each member records a yes-or-no and a confidence rating before the meeting; the chair reads the tally out at the start of each item, before anyone speaks. (b) Each round, one member is formally designated to argue against whatever consensus is emerging, with the role rotating. Do either of these help? I have an inchoate worry that the second one might make things worse, but I cannot say why.

3. Was there anything I could have done in that particular meeting other than simply disagreeing? Does it matter that what I had was an impression I could not convert into a figure on the spot, rather than a fact I could have put in front of everyone? And would it have made any difference if I had spoken before the two generalists rather than after them — should I be asking the chair to vary the order, or to start with whoever knows the relevant area?
