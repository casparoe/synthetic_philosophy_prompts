I'm 71 and retired — most of my working life was in enzyme kinetics and later protein chemistry, mostly tRNA synthetases, funnily enough — and since last autumn I've been sitting in on a graduate reading group in philosophy of mind at the university where I used to teach. We got stuck on the business of original versus derived intentionality, and I think part of the problem is that I simply don't have a nose for when a philosophical distinction is doing real work versus when it is just a way of restating an intuition in more syllables. So I want to try an exercise: translate the whole dispute into a vocabulary where I do have a nose for that sort of thing.

Here's the setup as I understand it, in case I've got it wrong. Searle's side, roughly: there is a difference between the way my thoughts are *about* something and the way the words on this page, or the needle on a fuel gauge, are about something. Words and gauges have their aboutness borrowed, from us — it's observer-relative, it depends on someone interpreting them — whereas ours is supposed to be intrinsic, underived, not a matter of anyone's interpretation, and that bedrock is what makes genuine misrepresentation possible. Dennett's answer, as I remember it, built around his two-bitser example — a gadget whose whole job is telling quarters from slugs — is that this bedrock doesn't exist: the little machine's aboutness is borrowed from its designers and users, yes, but so is ours, just by a longer and messier route, and there is no stopping point at which aboutness becomes intrinsic. There is no such thing as original intentionality, on his telling; it's derived all the way down, and that's not a paradox. Both sides also lean on the idea that error is the tell: a device can only be *wrong* about quarters if something outside it sets the standard.

So: restate both sides in the vocabulary I spent forty years in, and be honest about where the translation strains. I mean things like sequence and specificity, codons and anticodons, the codon-to-amino-acid mapping and its degeneracy, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and their binding pockets, stereochemical complementarity versus arbitrary assignment, editing and proofreading sites, mischarging and misincorporation, promiscuity and kinetic versus thermodynamic discrimination, orthogonal tRNA/synthetase pairs and engineered noncanonical codes, and "information" and "code" in the loose sense molecular biologists actually use them. Assume I'm completely fluent in all of that and don't explain it back to me; assume I know nothing technical about philosophy. As you go, flag explicitly whenever you find yourself importing an assumption of biology rather than translating — e.g. if you quietly cash out "function" as selected-effect function, or "error" as reduced fitness, say so rather than presenting it as the faithful rendering. Two cases I keep chewing on and would like you to take up directly. First, the permuted-code organism: imagine a lineage engineered so that the entire codon-to-amino-acid mapping is systematically permuted, with the tRNAs and synthetases rewired to match; its proteome and its fitness are unchanged, and it is reproductively isolated from everything else. Which side of the argument does that case actually help, and does it help them because it shows content is derived all the way down, or is it a dud because everyone already agrees a code is a convention? Second, what I think of as the stereochemical floor: a synthetase pocket that discriminates its amino acid by shape and volume and charge is doing something that is intrinsic, perfectly non-mysterious, and certainly not derived from any interpreter — nobody assigned that fit. Is that a candidate for what Searle wants, offered in a deflationary chemical key? Would he be able to accept it, or does his notion of intrinsicness need something more than differential causal responsiveness? And does Dennett's derived-all-the-way-down story have any room for it, or does it have to treat it as just more causation? Note also that in real organisms each layer of specificity is implemented by molecules that were themselves built under that same code — the code is read by machinery the code specifies — so "derived from what?" seems to run in a circle rather than down to a foundation, and I'd like to know whether that circle is evidence for Dennett or just a place where biology has no word for what Searle is after.
