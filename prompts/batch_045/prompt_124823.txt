Setting: I convene a small reading group of non-specialists who work through one philosophical argument per session. Last week we spent the session on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the impossibility of moral responsibility. Two members — call them M. and R. — carried the argument on afterwards over email. I have reproduced their exchange below, lightly tidied for length but not strengthened in either direction. Both are sharp, and neither looked to me like they were losing. They have asked me to referee and I genuinely cannot tell who is right about what, so I am passing it to you.

The argument as both of them understood it (my paraphrase):

P1. What you do is a function of how you are, mentally speaking — your character, values, desires, deliberative dispositions.
P2. To be truly morally responsible for what you do, you must be truly responsible for how you are, at least in the mental respects that explain the action.
P3. But you cannot be truly responsible for how you are: to be, you would have to have deliberately made yourself the way you are, and that requires that you already had a nature in light of which you made yourself that way, and so on without end. Nothing can be the source of itself in the required sense; you cannot get something from nothing.
C. Therefore no one is ever truly morally responsible for anything — and, as Strawson also insists, this holds whether or not determinism is true, since indeterminism cannot help: mere chance or probabilistic divergence is no more self-determination than causal necessity is.

The exchange:

(1) R.: As stated, the argument establishes only that we are not self-creators. That is not in dispute and it is not what responsibility requires. The natural compatibilist view is that responsibility requires the action to issue from the agent's own reasons-responsive mechanism: had the relevant reasons been different in the right way, the agent would have responded differently. Nothing about a regress of self-formation touches that condition. You might as well argue that a thermostat does not control the temperature because it did not build itself.

(2) M.: What you call the natural compatibilist view is precisely Strawson's target, not his opponent. Reasons-responsiveness can be fully present in a being whose entire structure of sensitivities is the product of heredity, upbringing and circumstance. Satisfying the condition just means you are a well-functioning channel of influences you did not choose. On Strawson's picture responsibility is a *tracing* notion: you inherit responsibility for the output only if you have it for the input, and the chain has to terminate in something you authored. If every link is inherited, there is nothing at the far end for responsibility to trace back to. Independent test: imagine you discover someone was covertly engineered to be perfectly reasons-responsive. You would not think the responsiveness earned them anything.

(3) R.: Then P2 together with the tracing requirement is doing all the work, and I think it rests on a bad model of how a regress can be stopped. Regresses of the every-link-has-a-prior-link shape are harmless — chains do not need first links. I can be responsible for my character at thirty partly in virtue of what I did at twenty, and for that partly in virtue of what I did at fifteen, with no first member anywhere. Responsibility can be accumulated or *bootstrapped* rather than created from nothing, and it plausibly comes in degrees. Nobody, compatibilist or otherwise, ever claimed the total authorship Strawson says is required.

(4) M.: Strawson considers exactly that reply. Any process of shaping yourself has to begin somewhere, and it begins with a nature you did not choose; every later stage is a consequence of that unchosen starting point together with further unchosen influences. Bootstrapping cannot manufacture the missing ingredient, because the ingredient is ultimate authorship, and ultimate authorship is not the sort of thing that comes in degrees which sum: an endless series of zeros is still zero. If the first link is not authored, the last one is not either. And if you prefer the version with no starting point at all — an infinite series in both directions — that is worse for you, since then there is nothing in the series that is you in the relevant sense.

(5) R.: If that is right, the argument proves far too much to be an argument against compatibilism in particular: it also destroys the libertarian picture on which agents are substance-causes that initiate events without being caused to do so, since the agent-cause has a character too and did not select it. Strawson seems happy to agree, given how hard he insists on neutrality about determinism. But then the real conclusion is not that we lack the property of being morally responsible. It is that the desert-entailing concept of responsibility is unsatisfiable in principle — an incoherent ideal rather than a status we are unlucky enough to miss. And once you put it that way, the live question is what to do with the concept: revise it, replace it with something thinner and forward-looking, or retain it as a useful fiction.

(6) M.: I accept the first half — Strawson does think the argument is neutral on determinism and does think it sinks those views as well, and he treats that as a virtue, since it explains why the free-will debate circles forever. But you cannot infer that the concept is incoherent. A concept can be perfectly clear and unsatisfiable: we understand well enough what it would be to deserve blame in the ultimate sense, and we simply cannot do it. Nor can we quietly revise the concept, because our actual practices — resentment, blame, guilt, praise, punishment, holding oneself to account — are saturated with desert-entailing commitments and are not the sort of thing you can keep running on a stipulation. So the honest options are the hard ones: give up the desert-entailing practices, or keep them while conceding they are unjustified.

(7) R.: That last move looks unstable to me. If the concept is unsatisfiable but we cannot stop deploying it, the problem has quietly become a practical one, and then whether to go on blaming and resenting depends on what blaming does — which is just the revisionist, forward-looking option you ruled out a sentence earlier. Second, your claim that the ordinary concept is robustly desert-entailing is not something you can simply assert. There is, as I recall, work on folk intuitions suggesting that people's responsibility judgments in concrete cases are largely compatibilist and only swing toward incompatibilism when the question is put abstractly, in terms of a universe in which every event is determined. You cannot have it both ways: if the folk concept really were desert-entailing at the core, determinism would be front and center in ordinary judgments rather than an artifact of framing.

(8) M.: One concession: the argument may refute only the strongest grade of responsibility — the grade that grounds desert — and not the weaker grades, such as being answerable for what you did in the sense that it manifests your judgments and commitments and entitles others to demand an account, or such as being assessable as a person in light of it. But I deny that the concession helps you. Those weaker grades are forms of assessment, not the moral responsibility we were arguing about, and the question that mattered — whether anyone ever deserves blame, praise, punishment or reward in the basic sense — is untouched by them and, on my reading, answered negatively.
