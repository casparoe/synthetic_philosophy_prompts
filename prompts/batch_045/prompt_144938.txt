I'm presenting a paper at a normative ethics workshop next week, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view.

The thesis I'm defending: the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism is best reconstructed as a claim about *complaint units* — a loss to one person is not automatically outweighed by a larger gain to another, because the two do not fall within a single standpoint from which they could be traded off against each other — and the boundary of a complaint unit is not the boundary of a human organism but the reach of a unified deliberative standpoint. Two consequences I argue for: (1) aggregation can fail *within* a life when that life lacks deliberative unity (I use a case of dense, permanent amnesia that destroys psychological continuity between successive stretches of one person's life, and a hypothetical case of "serial selves" who share a body and a memory store but never deliberate together); (2) aggregation can succeed *across* persons when agency is genuinely pooled through joint intention and shared deliberation, so that the standard contractualist reading over-intellectualizes the objection by tying it to the distinctness of persons as such.

The room is about twelve people. Four or five prioritarians and person-affecting theorists of the "competing claims" stripe: sympathetic to the diagnosis, hostile to my first extension, since their view licenses free aggregation within a life and needs that. Three act-utilitarians of the no-independent-constraints sort, who think the whole objection is question-begging and that my "complaint unit" is a name for the intuition I am supposed to be arguing for. Two Kantian constructivists who think duties are not derived from any trade-off or aggregation picture at all, and that "the separateness of persons" is a slogan rather than an argument. A couple of agnostics who will follow whichever objection sounds strongest in the room.

Draft the prep sheet as a table with columns: objection (phrased as that camp would phrase it, so I recognize the vocabulary when I hear it) | who raises it | which of my two claims it targets | two-minute reply | where the reply is soft. Order the rows by how likely and how damaging each objection is, not by the room's seating. Be explicit about epistemic status throughout: mark which of my claims are near-orthodox among people who work on these topics, which are genuinely contested, and where my own reconstruction is sufficiently novel that I should present it as a proposal rather than as something the literature supports.
