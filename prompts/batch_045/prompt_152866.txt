I'm a high school student who got into this through an online article on possible worlds, and I've ended up convinced of something that most people I mention it to take to be obviously false. The view: I could not have come from a different sperm and egg. If my parents had conceived a child an hour later, or if the particular gametes that produced me had been replaced by any others — even by ones differing from my actual ones by a single nucleotide — the resulting child would not have been me but someone else, and in those scenarios I would simply never have existed. I take this to be the same kind of claim as the familiar point about a wooden table not being the same table if it had been made from a completely different block of wood: at least some things have their origins essentially.

I'd like to know what would count against this. Not the quick dismissals people reach for, but the objections a defender should take seriously — and I'd like them ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an explicit note on which ones someone holding the view would concede as genuine costs and which they'd be right to reject as resting on a misunderstanding.

Some things I especially want addressed. First, the small-change cases, which worry me more than the dramatic ones: a sperm whose DNA differs from my actual one by one base pair; an egg whose cytoplasm and mitochondria come from a donor while the nuclear genome is my mother's (which I gather is an actual medical procedure); an embryo from the same two parents but produced by a different meiotic event — my actual sibling. If my view is right, does it give sharp verdicts about whether I exist in each of these? Where does it locate a cutoff, and is that cutoff defensible or arbitrary? Second, the cases where one organism seems to have more than one origin: if the zygote that became me had split, I'd have had an identical twin — would I exist there, and as which of the two? And since two fertilized eggs occasionally fuse into a single organism, what does my view say about a person with two origin stories — two essential origins, one, or none? Third, the asymmetry that bothers me most: correcting one letter of my genome in the sperm before fertilization versus correcting that same letter in the zygote an hour after. The resulting child looks the same in every other respect, yet the first intervention looks like a change of origin and the second like mere modification. Can my view explain that asymmetry, or is it an artifact of an arbitrary choice about what counts as the origin — the gametes, the zygote, the fertilization event, implantation?

Beyond cases, I want the structural exposure. What does the view predict that its rivals do not — the rival that says I'd still be me with different gametes, or the one that says I'd exist but as a numerically different person? What else would have to be true for it to be false: something about the necessity of identity, about whether it even makes sense to suppose vague or borderline answers to questions about whether I would exist, about whether the word I picks out a biological organism or something more like a person, about whether origins have to be essential for artifacts and ordinary objects as well? Could anything empirical put pressure on it — a discovery that chimerism or very early genetic correction is far commoner than we assume, or that gametes aren't the stable particulars I'm implicitly treating them as — or is the question misplaced because the claim is purely a priori?

Finally, an honest assessment. A friend says the position is insulated, because any apparent counterexample can be met with the reply that in that scenario I simply don't exist, and that costs me nothing I could ever observe. Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? If so, is that a defect for a claim of this kind, or just what metaphysical claims about identity look like? And if you had to name the single consideration most likely to move me, what would it be?
