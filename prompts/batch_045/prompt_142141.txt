I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and the vocabulary I am most fluent in is that of measurement: operationalization, construct and criterion validity, gold standards, sensitivity and specificity, rater effects, and what goes wrong when a proxy becomes a target.

Please restate, as faithfully as you can, the Turing test and the broader claim that behavioral criteria are the only workable criteria we have for attributing mind, in that vocabulary. Concretely: treat the imitation game as a diagnostic instrument, with interrogators as raters, their questions as items of differing difficulty, the machine's performance as a score on a latent capacity, and passing as a cut-point somewhere on that scale. Say what the instrument's sensitivity and specificity would be, what plays the role of the gold standard, and whether what is being measured is the machine or the machine-interrogator pair.

Then the residue report: what translated cleanly, what translated only with distortion, and what could not be expressed at all. Three cases I care about. First, whether the absence of any gold standard independent of the test itself makes the test a stipulation of what mind is rather than a measurement of it, and whether that is a defect or a feature. Second, whether a system that passed by brute-force enumeration of replies, passing without anything in it we would recognize as thinking, is a false positive your model can price in, or something it has no room to represent. Third, whether the normative stake, what we would owe a system that passes and what a failure would license, survives the translation at all, given that a mistaken diagnostic verdict carries consequences in a way a mistaken verdict about a machine need not.

For each piece of residue, say whether it counts against the measurement framework or against the determinacy of the original philosophical claim.
