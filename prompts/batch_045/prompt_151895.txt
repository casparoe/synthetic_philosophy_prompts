My mate and I spent about two hours arguing about the Ellsberg thing last night and got nowhere, and I think it's because we were arguing about different things.

Setup so we're on the same page: one urn with 50 red / 50 black, one urn with 100 balls in some unknown mix of the two colours. You name a colour, then a ball gets drawn from whichever urn you picked. Most people will pay something to draw from the known urn, whichever colour they're betting on.

My claim was that "ambiguity aversion" isn't really one thing, and that the standard setup muddles together at least three: (a) not knowing which chance is the true one; (b) not collapsing a two-stage lottery — the unknown urn is really "first the mix, then the draw" — into a single-stage one; (c) half-suspecting that someone chose the mix and might have chosen it knowing what you'd bet, i.e. that you're playing against a person rather than against nature.

Can you explain how you'd go about separating these? I made up three variants and can't work out what each would actually show: (1) make the known urn compound too, so its 50/50 is itself produced by drawing from a big bin and neither option is single-stage; (2) the mix is fixed by a machine with no stake in your bet versus fixed by someone who saw your bet first; (3) the mix was drawn uniformly at random and announced to the room, but you arrived late and missed it, so you know the generating process exactly and are only ignorant of its outcome.

And the bit that really got us going: there's that old argument that someone who prefers the known urn for both colours is inconsistent — they'd have to be treating the ambiguous urn as worse than 50/50 for red and worse than 50/50 for black at once, and in a sequence of choices they can be steered into a combination of bets that's plainly worse than one they rejected. I can't tell whether that shows ambiguity aversion is irrational or whether it just assumes you always have one sharp subjective probability to begin with, which is the thing being denied. Is it question-begging?

Write it in British English please, and keep it conceptual — I don't want the experimental literature, I want to know what the disagreement is really about and which of (a), (b), (c) is doing the work.
