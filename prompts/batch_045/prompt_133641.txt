I've somehow ended up on the committee at my hospital that signs off on the tool deciding which discharged patients get put into our six-week remote monitoring program (daily check-in texts, and a nurse calls if you miss two). It's intrusive and it eats a big chunk of nursing time, so getting flagged when you didn't need it is a real cost, not a technicality. Meetings start next week and I keep going in cirlces with one colleague — call him Raj — who keeps saying: "the score is calibrated within each group and it catches the same share of genuinely at-risk patients in both, so we're done, there's nothing left to argue about." I think he's wrong, but I can't make the point in the abstract. I need one worked example I can put on a slide.

So build me one. Definitions first so we don't talk past each other:

- Y=1 means the patient has an unplanned acute care event (ED visit or readmission) within 60 days. It's a fixed fact about the patient; we're not intervening on it.
- The score is a number in [0,1] meant as that patient's probability of Y=1, taking only a handful of distinct values.
- Calibration within group g: among group-g patients assigned score s, exactly fraction s have Y=1.
- TPR = share of that group's Y=1 patients who get flagged; FPR = share of that group's Y=0 patients who get flagged; PPV = share of flagged patients in that group who have Y=1.
- AUC = probability that a randomly chosen Y=1 patient in that group scores above a randomly chosen Y=0 patient, ties counting half.

What I need:

1. Two groups of patients, A and B, with different base rates of Y=1. Keep both somewhere a clinician wouldn't laugh at (say between 0.08 and 0.35) and make them differ by at least a factor of two. Give me integer headcounts: for each group, a small table of score value / number of patients at that value / how many of those have Y=1. Pick per-group totals that make the fractions come out exact (1000 per group is fine, nobody on the committee will count). Calibration within each group must be exact — the realized positive fraction at each score value equals the score itself. No rounding slop and no "approximately."

2. A single threshold, the same number for both groups (0.5 is fine). I don't want to be accused of tuning the cut separately per group.

3. TPR equal across groups — exactly if your integers allow it, otherwise within half a percentage point, and show both fractions.

4. The punchline: FPR strictly higher in B, the group with the LOWER base rate, and higher by at least 1.5x, so it's visible on a slide.

5. And not by quietly building a garbage score for B:
 (a) both scores have to be genuinely informative, AUC well above 0.5, and ideally the two AUCs within about 0.05 of each other. Report both numbers; if you can't get them that close, say what you had to give up.
 (b) being flagged has to be informative in each group: PPV has to beat that group's own base rate.
 (c) you can't just flag most of B.

6. Then check every one of those conditions against your own numbers, in order, arithmetic shown, and give me the headcount version of the harm: how many patients in each group get pulled into monitoring who didn't need it.

7. Which feature of the case is doing the work? I have a vague guess — something about where on the risk scale each group ends up getting cut — but I want it stated properly. In particular: for a group whose flagged patients all sit at one score value s with exact calibration, derive the relation between FPR, TPR, s, and the base rate. Then point at the term that's carrying my example.

8. How fragile is it? Show that if I forced both groups to be cut at the same score value — same threshold and the same realized score in the flagged band — the FPR gap flips back to the intuitive direction, higher FPR for the higher-base-rate group. Then say plainly what that means for whether Raj should be convinced.

9. Last: give me the strongest version of Raj's objection. He's definately going to say something like "you just built a worse score for one group," so give me that objection at its best, plus the honest reply, including the part where he's partly right.

Keep the whole thing to about one slide: two small tables and short bullets.
