longtime lw reader here, now i do evals / "safety infra" at a mid-size lab, so i sit in these meetings constantly and i need help taking apart a line that keeps ending them.

yesterday we were discussing how to supervise a coding agent that has real tool access (can edit our repo, hit internal apis, the usual). the proposal on the table was basically: route every action through a monitor that is *the same model* as the agent, but a fresh copy with a different system prompt, no conversation history, no access to the original task spec beyond the action itself, and we only wake a human up when the monitor flags. as a bonus hack someone proposed cross-grading: spin up two copies, have each grade the other's actions, escalate disagreements to humans, on the theory that they can't communicate so they can't coordinate. a third person wanted to paraphrase the action into "neutral" language before grading it, to defeat steganographic stuff in the chain of thought.

our most senior safety person killed the whole thing with: "look, you can never let the model grade its own homework." everyone nodded. i think the slogan is doing way more work than it can bear, but i also can't tell which of the things it might mean are the ones worth keeping.

so: give me a menu of the precise claims "don't let the model grade its own homework" could be making in this setting, from platitude to radical. as best i can tell the candidates are things like — don't trust a system's self-report about its own safety; don't train on labels the system generated (reward-model/goodhart-ish worries); don't let the grader share the graded system's optimization pressure or blind spots; don't let the grader see the graded system's hidden reasoning; the thing that matters isn't identity but *correlated* failure, in which case cross-grading with identical weights is worthless and a different-family model is fine; and the maximal version, that no ai output can appear anywhere in a safety case without a human independently checking it. there are probably readings i'm missing (something decision-theoretic about two copies of the same weights being correlated even with no channel between them? is that a real concern or a tdt fever dream?).

for each reading: is it trivial, substantively defensible, or false/dangerous, and does it actually rule out what was on the table? i specifically want to know which readings would forbid (a) same-weights monitoring with a different prompt, (b) cross-grading between copies, (c) paraphrase-then-grade, and (d) monitoring with a weaker *trusted* model — because (d) is the thing we'd do instead and i have a nagging feeling it's the one that's actually broken in the high-capability regime, since the weaker model may just be unable to notice subtle sabotage. i.e. i want to know whether the slogan is being used to block a scheme that's better than the alternative.
