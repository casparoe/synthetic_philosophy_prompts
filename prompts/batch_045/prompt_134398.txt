I am the rules editor at a small publisher of abstract strategy games. Every month I get a stack of submitted games, and one of the first calls I have to make — before anyone spends money on art or playtesting — is whether a title is structurally broken: whether the first player has a proved winning strategy via the strategy-stealing argument, or whether the argument at least rules out a win for the second player. I have an undergraduate math background but no combinatorial game theory, I have about half an hour per game, and the output has to be a one-line verdict the owner, who does not want to see any math, can act on: publish as is, add a swap rule, or reject.

What I need is a decision procedure, not a proof sketch. Turn the conditions the strategy-stealing argument actually relies on — role symmetry, including symmetry under an involution of the board rather than literal identical positions; the requirement that an extra stone of your own color can never hurt you; the need for a first move you can waste or an extra stone you can safely pretend is not there; what changes when draws are possible; what determinacy has to be assumed; and what breaks if a game has chance or hidden information — into ordered questions, each with what counts as a sufficient yes, where a no means the argument simply does not apply, and what I should do in that case (playtest, add a swap rule, or accept that I have no certificate). Say which conditions are necessary and which are only sufficient, and mark the places where the question is really a judgment call I have to make myself. Be plain about what the procedure cannot deliver: it proves existence, not strategy, so it cannot tell me how large the advantage is or how to fix it.

Then run it on three games on my desk right now.

Cairn: players alternate placing one stone of their color on an empty cell of a 7x7 grid; Red wins the instant she has an orthogonally connected chain of her stones touching the top and bottom edges, Blue the instant he has one touching the left and right edges; if the grid fills with neither, the game is a draw.

Thicket: the same board and goals, except that a player who ever completes a 2x2 block of their own stones loses immediately, and a player must move.

Chomp, which I am told the argument covers even though the two players are not in symmetric roles: a rectangular bar with the bottom-left square poisoned; a move consists of choosing a remaining square and eating it together with everything above and to the right of it; whoever eats the poisoned square loses.

I would rather be told honestly that the procedure returns no verdict than be handed a proof that does not go through, so include any case where the answer is one I will not like.
