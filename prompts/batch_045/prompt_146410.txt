Hey — I'm the non-voting secretary of my city's franchise advisory board, and we've got a proposal on the agenda next week that cuts across three things we've already ruled on separately. I can't work out which way being consistant with ourselves actually pushes, so I'd find it useful to hear someone reason it through. No citations or reading recommendations needed please, our board is mostly retired accountants, I just want the argument.

Our track record:

- 2017: voted down compulsory turnout for national elections (fine for abstaining). Minutes say voting is a right and can't be converted into a legal duty, and that staying home is itself a political statement.
- 2019: voted for a legal duty to get yourself onto the electoral roll once you're eligible, with a small administrative penalty if you don't. The distinction the board drew: enrolment is paperwork, voting is expression.
- 2021: voted to let 16 and 17 year olds vote in municipal elections. Reason: they pay tax on part-time jobs, they use the buses and libraries, the city's bylaws bind them like anyone else.
- 2021, same meeting: voted against letting non-citizen permanent residents vote in national elections even after twenty years here. City-level is one thing, national self-determination is another.
- 2022: voted for municipal votes for non-citizen permanent residents after three years' residence.
- 2023: voted against lowering the national voting age to 16. This one was 4-3 and the minutes are thin, and it sits a bit oddly next to 2021.
- 2024: approved automatic enrolment off the municipal roll, which now sweeps in citizens 18+, citizens aged 16-17, and non-citizen permanent residents all together.
- 2024: funded weekend voting and two weeks of early voting, on the grounds that if the city is going to ask anything of people it has to make compliance easy.

The new proposal: compulsory participation in municipal elections only. Everyone on the municipal roll has to attend a polling place or return a ballot, or pay a $25 fee. But there's a check-box on the form, "I attended and decline to cast a vote", and ticking it counts as complying. Turnout in our municipal elections is around 28% and skews heavily toward older homeowners, and the two newest groups on the roll are expected to turn out lowest of all.

What I'd like: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own precedents, and separately, does the check-box make this really the 2019 enrolment case rather than the 2017 compulsion case? Should the duty fall on all three groups on the roll equally, or only some of them — say adult citizens only, on the theory that rights follow from being governed but duties follow from membership? Which of our precedents is the closest analog and why? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would we be adopting with each option? And what single further proposal (a tweak to this one, or a new case entirely) would actually tell the competing principles apart, as opposed to just re-running the same disagreement?
