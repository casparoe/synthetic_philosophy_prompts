So here's my situation. I'm retired — thirty-odd years as a solid-state / physical chemist, mostly X-ray crystallography, which means I spent a career thinking about lattices and defects and about the fact that swapping two identical atoms in an otherwise perfect crystal is not an operation anyone can perform, detect, or mean anything by. About a year and a half ago I started sitting in on the metaphysics reading group at the university in town (my wife teaches there, that's how I got in the door), and next month they've given me a slot of my own. I want to use it on Black's two-spheres argument against the identity of indiscernibles. My thesis, in short: that world doesn't refute the principle, because the intuition that there *could* be two qualitatively identical spheres just is the contested haecceitist intuition dressed up as a modal claim, and once you look at the closest real physical analogue — a structure with a nontrivial symmetry group, where permuting the "two" things is a relabeling rather than a difference — the count of two is a fact about the structure, not about two individuals. I want to replace the principle with what I've been calling the lattice test: things get individuated by their role in the total structure, and a perfectly symmetric structure doesn't contain two indiscernible objects, it contains one type with multiplicity two. I also want to spring two cases I havent seen anyone discuss, both straight out of my old trade. First, the vacancy world: an otherwise perfect infinite crystal with exactly two vacancies, i.e. two missing atoms, two lattice spacings apart. The vacancies are qualitatively identical and related only symmetrically, and here's the part I don't have an answer to — they aren't objects at all, they're absences, and yet we count them and we want to say they could have been three spacings apart. My structural story feels like it owes the same answer about the holes as about the spheres, and I'm not sure it gives one. Second, the continuity worry: put a single speck of dust off at some distance from one of the two spheres, so the symmetry is broken by an arbitrarily tiny amount. If the spheres were indiscernible before and discernible now, did they become two things all at once or gradually? I definately don't want distinctness to come in degrees, and I can't see how my view stops it from doing so.

The room will be about a dozen people and I know roughly who they are: a Leibniz scholar who will want to know what any of this has to do with the principle of sufficient reason and whether I've quietly stopped doing metaphysics and started making an epistemic point; a modal metaphysician who is a card-carrying haecceitist and will say of course there's a primitive fact about which sphere is which, and will ask how a structure-based view handles pairs of possible worlds that differ only by a permutation; somebody in philosophy of physics who follows the literature on whether indistinguishable quantum particles are only "weakly discernible," and who will want to know whether that literature is helping me or sinking me (I suspect some of each, and I'm not looking forward to it); a younger person who works on grounding and will ask what my talk of roles in a total structure is supposed to be grounded in; and several generalists — other retirees, grad students' partners — who will just say come on, there's obviously two spheres, and who I think will land the objection I find hardest to shrug off.

What I'd like from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — I want to hear the Leibniz person's vocabulary and the physicist's vocabulary, not one generic philosopher voice — with a two-minute answer for each and a note on where my answer is weakest or where I'm bluffing. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes rather than have dragged out of me later; I'd rather give something up early than be cornered into it. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. My guess is that it's the one about whether the lattice test is just the old bundle theory with a new name, but you may see something worse.
