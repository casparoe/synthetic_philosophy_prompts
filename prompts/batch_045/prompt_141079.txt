I'm a junior at a public high school, and I help edit the school literary magazine. For the spring issue we're running a short section in which students write something that came out of a class in a different department, and I volunteered to contribute for my philosophy elective.

The unit I want to write about is origin essentialism — the view my teacher presented under Kripke's name, that a person could not have originated from gametes numerically different from the ones they in fact came from, so that if some other sperm had reached that egg first, what would have existed would not have been me living a different life but someone else entirely. I'd like a pantoum: four or five quatrains, rhymed ABAB (slant rhyme is fine where a perfect rhyme would force a clunker), in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza reappear as the first and third lines of the next, and in which the poem's last line is its first line again.

The reason I want that particular form is that the repeated lines should carry the origin claim itself — something in the neighborhood of "this sperm, this egg, and no other" — so that each time it returns it is standing in a different situation. The reader should feel the same sentence come back looking self-evident in one setting and useless in another. The situations I want it to pass through:

- the replacement case: a different sperm wins the same race. It feels arbitrary which one did, and yet on this view there is no scenario in which I exist and it was a different one.
- the multiplication case: a single fertilized egg splitting into identical twins. Same sperm, same egg, two people — which seems to show that the origin is at best necessary and not what picks me out.
- the fusion case: two very early embryos, from two sperm-and-egg pairs, merging into one child, so that one person has two origins. I gather this happens rarely in humans and is usually discovered by accident, but I don't want the poem to lean on the biology being common — only possible.
- the upstream case: if I couldn't have come from different gametes, and those gametes came from particular cell divisions in particular bodies, then my beginning seems to start reaching back before my conception, and I'd rather the poem open that regress than close it.

One further thing I'd like somewhere in it: the gametes should figure as the particular cells they are, not as packages of DNA. No two sperm are genetically identical, so "same genome, different gamete" isn't even available as a case, and I'd like at least one line to register that the claim is about those two cells rather than about the information in them.

Please keep it a poem first: no footnotes, no words like "thesis," "objection," or "modal" in the text, no puns on "essential." Someone who has never opened a philosophy book should be able to read it as a poem about where a person begins, while my philosophy teacher should be able to point at each stanza and say which case it is working through. End on the returned first line and don't resolve anything — the fact that the poem closes by repeating where it opened should be the only answer it offers.
