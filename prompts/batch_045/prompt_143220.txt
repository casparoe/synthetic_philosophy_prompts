Me and my mate had a massive row in the pub last night about the little city record office where she sits on the acquisitions panel. I said archives basically just keep the paperwork of whoever was already in charge and let everyone else quietly disappear; she said no, they're deliberately collecting the people the record left out, and that half of what gets called history is just whatever happened to survive. She's got this phrase she keeps using, "the second silence" — the hole you make when you accession one thing and everything around it gets pulped, so future readers can't even tell there was a hole. Then we went through what they've actually taken and refused over the last couple of years and I couldn't work out what she's really going by. We're meeting again Sunday and I want to be able to tell her what rule she's actually running. Her verdicts, as near as I can remember:

- took the parish vestry minute books, 1838–1929 — outdoor relief, removal orders, bastardy cases, paupers named in them
- took a shoebox of notebooks and stapled poetry pamphlets by a woman who worked forty years on the fish dock
- took the records of a branch of the transport workers' union, 1960s to 80s — minutes, strike committee papers, the membership list, all of it
- turned down the diaries of a mill owner, 1890s to 1930s, full of stuff about his workforce, offered by his grandson. She said they'd already got the company wage books and the local paper on microfilm
- turned down a retired head teacher's personal diaries, 1965–2010, pupils and staff named, most of them still alive
- turned down forty lever-arch files of press cuttings, parish newsletters and photos that one man had spent thirty years collecting about every planning application he'd ever objected to, which he offered as the true history of the town
- took a set of oral history recordings a community group made with elders from a Caribbean congregation about arriving in the city and working in the hospitals and on the buses — signed consent forms, deposited jointly with the group rather than just handed over

What's bugging me is that the same set of decisions could of come out of three quite different things she might believe: that she's filling holes in what's already held and doesn't want duplicates; that she only wants material made from the receiving end of power, or in resistance to it, and won't take the papers of people who already wrote their own history unless those papers show them actually doing the thing; or that what's really driving it is whether the people in the documents would of wanted to be in the record at all. All three seem to fit every case above, and there's probably a fourth answer where she's just worried they can't look after anything born digital.

Two offers came in that she'll have to rule on next month and I want to call them right.

First: the admin of a tenants' campaign group that fought their housing association's regeneration plans for four years wants to donate a full export of the group's private WhatsApp — tens of thousands of messages, photos from meetings, a fair bit of gossip about neighbours, pictures with kids in them, and screenshots of a leaked internal spreadsheet from the housing association about who was getting moved where. Nobody in the group knew it was being kept for an archive and none of them are going to sign anything now. He reckons it's the real story of how the estate saved itself.

Second: someone inside the same housing association has quietly offered them the association's own consultation files and internal emails about that estate, which name residents and include some pretty blunt comments by officers about them. Also without anyone's consent, and the association would go mad if they knew.

So: does she take the WhatsApp thing, does she take the emails, and does she open either of them or shut them for fifty years? What am I missing that would tell those rules apart — and if you were me, what's the one extra case you'd put to her on Sunday that would force her to say which one she's actually using?
