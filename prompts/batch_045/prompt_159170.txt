I'm finishing a dissertation in historical geography and in six weeks I'm giving, to a comparative historical social science workshop, the paper I intend to turn into my job-market talk. The thesis I'm defending is aggressive: the longue durée should not be understood as a claim about a timescale at all. What Braudel's three-tier scheme (event, conjuncture, structure) is really tracking, I want to argue, is an asymmetry of counterfactual dependence — a slow structure is a variable that is insulated from the fast variables of a system, in that shocks to the fast ones leave it more or less where it was, while it itself sets the range within which those fast variables can move. Duration, on this reading, is a symptom of insulation rather than its content. I then propose to operationalize insulation as something I've been calling causal viscosity: for a candidate structural variable and a set of fast variables, compare the displacement the structural variable suffers under observed shocks in the fast ones with the displacement the fast ones suffer under observed shocks in the structural variable, each normalized by the variable's own typical variability; a variable counts as structural when that ratio stays well below one. The payoff I'm claiming is that this converts the stale structure-versus-agency quarrel into an empirical question about a measurable asymmetry, and gives comparative-historical work something to test rather than something to declare.

The audience is why I'm nervous. About twenty people will be there, and the ones who will actually talk are: two cliometricians who will ask what identifies anything here and who will suspect I have reinvented Granger causality under a worse name; a historical geographer who will raise the modifiable areal unit problem and spatial autocorrelation immediately, since my variables are regional aggregates reconstructed from patchy sources; a comparative-historical sociologist, sympathetic to large slow causes, who will want to know what mechanism carries the insulation and whether my asymmetry is just path dependence relabeled; a cultural historian of the event who will insist that shocks are not theory-neutral, because what counts as a shock depends on what it meant to the people living through it; a Braudel scholar who will want to know whether I am reconstructing his practice or quietly replacing it; and a paleoclimate person who will press on whether climate counts as a structure on my account at all, given that it varies on every timescale at once and is exogenous to the social system in a way that trade networks are not. My running example is from my own material — the reorganization of a river-delta settlement hierarchy as overland caravan routes gave way to coastal shipping, where I want to say the route network was structural and the annual harvest fluctuations were not. My supervisor thinks the whole thing collapses into a quarrel about scale; a friend in the economics department, after a single explanation, said it sounded like impulse-response functions with extra steps, and all I have against that so far is the claim that the intervention reading matters. I have already decided to concede up front that the partition of a system into fast and slow variables is not given by the sources, and that viscosity is defined only relative to a chosen scale and a chosen set of shocks.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do to the argument rather than by how likely they are, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — I want the cliometrician's vocabulary of identification and standard errors, and the cultural historian's vocabulary of meaning and contingency, not a generic list of worries about causation in history. For each objection: what I should concede at once, what I should hold onto, a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes with a clock on the wall, and one line on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which single reply has to land if the paper is to survive, and flag the two objections I should actively welcome because answering them lets me make the view look better than it does on the page. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks. My own guess is that it is the one about whether interpreting events is prior to counting them, which would make my measure theory-laden all the way down, but I would like to see whether you think the more dangerous question lies somewhere else. Assume I know the standard literature well and do not need it summarized back to me; I do not want citations or reading suggestions, since this is a methodological argument and I will supply whatever references I need myself.
