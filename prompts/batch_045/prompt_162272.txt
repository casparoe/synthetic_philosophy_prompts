I am a PhD student in distributed and multi-agent systems, working mainly on coordination and symmetry breaking in anonymous networks. The vocabulary I actually think in is that of identical processes running at interchangeable nodes, input-indexed deterministic algorithms, the standard impossibility results for anonymous systems (deterministic leader election in an anonymous ring, consensus without failure detectors or randomization), randomized symmetry breaking, common knowledge in the Halpern–Moses sense, correlated equilibrium, and — as my bridge into the game-theoretic side — program equilibrium in Tennenholtz's sense: players submit programs that are run with each other's source as input, CliqueBot and its fragility, FairBot and the Löb-style argument, the folk theorem that any feasible and individually rational payoff profile can be realized. My familiarity with philosophical decision theory is that of a careful reader, nothing more.

I would like you to translate Hofstadter's superrationality — the notion introduced in his 1983 Scientific American column and reprinted in Metamagical Themas, on which a superrational player in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma cooperates because she takes it as given that every superrational player facing the same problem arrives at the same strategy, and fixes that shared strategy before maximizing — into that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report.

Please work through four cases rather than only the headline one.

First, the two-player prisoner's dilemma, where I expect the translation to be clean: the superrational profile is not a Nash equilibrium of the base game but is straightforwardly a program equilibrium, and the argument that all superrational players land on the same answer looks like a fixed-point or self-reference condition.

Second, the mixed-strategy extension: take an n-player one-shot lottery in which each player independently decides whether to send a message; if exactly one player sends, that player receives the prize, and if zero or more than one send, everyone receives nothing. In the symmetric version the superrational answer is understood to be a randomized one, each player sending with probability 1/n. Translate this, and tell me whether what I am looking at is a one-round randomized leader-election attempt in an anonymous network, with all the convergence properties and limitations that implies, or whether something in the original argument is doing work that my reading of it as symmetry breaking cannot capture.

Third, and this is the case I care most about: keep the lottery, but give the prize a player-dependent value, so that if player i is the sole sender she receives something worth v_i, with the v_i distinct, positive, and known to everyone. Every player still runs the same decision procedure, now taking its own index and the full vector of values as input. Here the move on which superrationality rests — I choose as though my choice fixes everyone's — seems to lose its footing, because the players are no longer in a position to make the same choice, and a canonicalization has to be supplied from somewhere: a welfare functional over the vector of expected prizes, a bargaining solution, a maximin rule. Tell me what the target vocabulary has to say about which canonicalization is available, and be explicit about whether program equilibrium can even select among them, given that its folk theorem is permissive rather than selective.

Fourth, two perturbations of that asymmetric case. In one, each player is privately handed a distinct label drawn from a known set and assigned adversarially, where the labels carry no payoff information at all; the standard move in my field is to break symmetry on exactly such arbitrary identifiers, and I want to know whether superrationality can avail itself of them without ceasing to be superrational. In the other, only a known fraction q of the players are superrational, the remainder being ordinary expected-utility maximizers who know q and know that the rest are superrational but not which players those are; this looks to me like a Byzantine population with a known fault bound and unknown fault locations, and I want to know whether the analogy is exact or whether it breaks at a specific place.

For each case, give me three things: what translates cleanly, what translates only if I import an assumption I would not otherwise have made, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Please do not let the translation quietly supply the missing selection principle and then present the result as a translation; flag every place where you are choosing on Hofstadter's behalf.

At the end, give me your verdict on the aggregate residue. My suspicion is that superrationality is a coordination-and-selection principle presented as a constraint of rationality, and that once the symmetry of roles is broken its substantive content is revealed to live entirely in whatever canonicalization or source of salience one adds. But I want you to argue the other side as well: whether the residue instead marks a genuine gap in a framework that has precise tools for what agents can jointly implement and nothing at all to say about what a single agent ought to conclude, and whether that missing normative dimension is something my vocabulary should want.
