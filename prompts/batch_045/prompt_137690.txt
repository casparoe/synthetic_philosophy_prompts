A podcast I've been listening to did an episode on Goodman's new riddle of induction, and the host sums up the puzzle in one breath like this:

"Goodman wasn't worried about whether induction works at all — that's Hume's problem, and Goodman was happy to grant that past regularities give us some reason to expect future ones. His question is which regularities we're entitled to project. Here's the puzzle. Call a thing 'grue' if it is first examined before some future time t and is green, or is not first examined before t and is blue. Every emerald examined so far has been green, and so every emerald examined so far has also been grue; the hypotheses 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' therefore fit the evidence exactly equally well, and they contradict each other about the next emerald we examine — one predicts green, the other blue. Since the evidence can't break the tie, the deciding factor can't be anything about the world; it has to come from the predicates themselves. Goodman's answer is that 'green' is projectible and 'grue' isn't, because 'green' is entrenched: speakers have projected it, and used it, vastly more often than 'grue', which somebody invented yesterday. So whether a generalization licenses a prediction is really just a matter of our linguistic habits — there's no deep fact about which predicate is the natural one. A community that had grown up with 'grue' rather than 'green' in its language would be perfectly rational to expect emeralds examined after t to be blue, and nothing we could say would show them to be wrong."

Later in the episode he applies it:

"That is exactly the position of a modern machine-learning system.
