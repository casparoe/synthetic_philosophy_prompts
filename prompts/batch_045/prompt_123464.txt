I've been circling a question for a couple of years and I'd like help finding the shape of what I actually think about it. I have no academic background — I read a lot of history and watch too many movies. The question is what we owe the real dead when we put them into stories: biopics, historical novels, plays, films with a note at the front saying the events have been dramatized.

Start with what seems obvious to me: the dead can't be harmed. They aren't around to be hurt. But I don't think my verdicts are only about the living audience being misled either, because in several of the cases below the audience knows perfectly well the scene is invented and my verdict doesn't soften. So if something is wrong here, it's wrong somewhere other than where I first looked, and I'd like to know where.

Here are my verdicts, going by gut.

A biopic about a nineteenth-century composer has him say to his wife, during an invented argument, a sentence he really did write — in a letter to his patron. Same words, different occasion, different person addressed. Fine. I actually prefer it to a scene of him writing the letter.

A film about a twentieth-century politician has her deliver, in earnest, a line she really wrote — but she wrote it as a joke, in a private letter mocking precisely the view the film has her defending. This feels worse than outright invention. Considerably worse.

A film about an explorer collapses four real shipmates into one invented sailor. Fine, no hesitation.

A film about a real painter invents a close friend — a whole person, no historical basis — and has the painter confide to this friend the doubts she recorded in her own notebooks. No reputation is damaged, the film is good, and everyone watching knows the friend is made up. Still not fine. It feels like a trick, and I can't say why it differs from the shipmates case.

A film about a civil rights leader who argued publicly for nonviolence his whole life invents a night on which he nearly abandons it. As far as anyone can tell he never wavered. Not fine.

A film about a dictator known for public cruelty invents a scene of him being tender with his dying mother. Fine — more than fine. It feels generous, and it makes him more of a person rather than less. I know this is the same move as the previous case and I don't want to give up either verdict.

A novelist writes about a real woman about whom almost nothing survives — a few letters, a court record — and invents a childhood for her. Fine, and I notice I'm more comfortable here than with inventing for someone documented to the hour. But a friend points out that this is exactly the case where the invention will harden into fact, because there is nothing to check it against. I think she's right and I don't know what to do with that.

A memoirist whose war record was largely fabricated. A film dramatizes the fabrication. Fine — it feels owed.

A self-taught artist who spent fifty years telling a false story about her own origins, and whose work is unintelligible without it. A film sets the record straight and shows the real origins. Not fine. It feels like vandalism. Again, the same move as the memoirist, and again I don't want to give either verdict up.

Two more things I'm fairly sure of. Money and permission look beside the point: a student production staged for sixty people gets the same verdicts from me as a streaming film, a family's blessing doesn't make the civil rights case fine, and a family's outrage doesn't make the dictator case not fine. But a diary in which the painter wrote that she did not want to be written about does move me. That asymmetry feels load-bearing and I can't articulate it. And I'm confident about the joke-line case in a way I'm not about anything else on this list, which seems like a clue about where the real principle lives.

My working rule has been: inventing is permissible when the invented thing is one the real person could have recognized as a fair way to be remembered, and impermissible when it enlists them behind a position they rejected in life. It handles the two cases I'm most confident about, says nothing about the obscure woman or the diary, and I suspect it only survives the dictator case by accident.

Can you give me the principle — or the two or three best candidates — that captures as much of this as possible? I'd like it stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case that forces each clause, and I'd like it to return verdicts on the cases I haven't been able to settle: a studio that licenses a dead actor's likeness from his estate and generates new performances of lines he never spoke, with his children's approval, where the fact that it is his actual face and voice seems to me to matter and I don't know why; a real person who is now mostly a name on a memorial, about whom every dramatization is invention by necessity; and a satire that takes a real dead politician's actual stated positions and pushes them to a grotesque but recognizable conclusion, which I want to protect as parody even though the exaggeration does enlist him.

Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts a defensible principle would force me to drop, and tell me whether this is one duty or two — something we owe the audience and something we owe the subject — and if two, which one explains the joke-line case.
