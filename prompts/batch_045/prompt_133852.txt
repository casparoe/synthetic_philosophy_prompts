I'm writing a novel and there's one scene I can't fake my way through. Two characters are stuck in a cabin during a power cut; one of them is a decision-theory postdoc (slightly insufferable, genuinely trying to be decent) and the other is a criminal defence lawyer — very sharp, no maths since school, argues for a living. He tries to explain updateless decision theory to her at the kitchen table with a candle stub and a pack of cards. I need the scene to be pedagogically right, not just vibe-right: if someone who actually knows this material reads the dialogue I dont want them to wince. So don't write the explanation, plan it.

Specifically: what has to be in place before the word "updateless" can even mean anything to her. She has a lawyer's working notion of precommitment — binding contracts, signing something before you know which way the facts will fall — and I reckon that will both carry her most of the way and then actively block her; tell me where to cash it in and where to break it.

One case has to carry the whole thing. I'd rather not use Newcomb's problem or the smoking lesion: my readers will have met them and they read as puzzles rather than as something a person would say out loud at a kitchen table. Give me the case instead, and say what it has to have and what it has to not have — I'm drawn to something small, human and repeatable rather than a one-off encounter with an all-knowing predictor, but argue me out of it if that instinct costs me something essential.

Then the misconceptions, in the order she'll form them, and the exact point in the sequence to head off each. The ones I can already see: that "updateless" just means refusing to look at the evidence; that it's causal decision theory with a precommitment bolted on; that it's evidential decision theory in a trenchcoat; that it only gets traction if there's a perfect predictor or a real physical duplicate of you. And one a beta reader flagged that I don't know what to do with — apparently some versions of this ask you to optimise over logically impossible versions of yourself, inputs your algorithm provably won't output. Is that a real wrinkle she'd hit, where in the arc does it belong, and does a twenty-minute candlelit version survive it or does he have to openly wave it off? Same question for the "copies" business: is the policy chosen for exact copies of your decision algorithm, or for sufficiently similar ones, and does that distinction have to appear on the table at all?

Finally: one line she could say at the end, in character, that would show me the idea had actually landed — plus what she'd say instead if she'd only half-got it — and what he should pointedly refuse to explain at this level. That last one matters: should he even name UDT as opposed to the newer functional version, or would naming it be a mistake in a scene like this?
