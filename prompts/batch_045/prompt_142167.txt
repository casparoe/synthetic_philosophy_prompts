I'm TAing a first-year seminar next term with a syllabus built around the question of when a legend becomes history — the usual three case studies, Homer's Troy, the Arthur who accretes from chronicle to romance, and the Exodus — and I want to open the first week with Ford's The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance (1962), because it seems to me to be that question in plot form: Ranse Stoddard gets beaten up by Liberty Valance, stands up to him in the street and fires the shot that drops him, builds a whole career on it, and ends up governor, senator, ambassador and a probable vice-presidential nominee; except Tom Doniphon was in the shadows with a rifle and fired at the same moment, knows it, and lets the credit go to Stoddard along with Hallie. At the end the newspaper editor listens to the true version, burns the reporter's notes and tells Stoddard that once the legend has become the fact you print the legend. (Don't recite the line back at me, I've seen it six times.) A student in the section already announced that the film is basically about fake news, which I think is both right and useless, and I'd like something better to say.

So here's the apparatus I've been fiddling with, and what I actually want is a reading that tells me which of these the film supports and which it only looks like it supports. (1) The last-witness condition: a legend becomes history when nobody left alive can contradict it, so the change is a fact about the evidence situation rather than about the past. (2) The uptake condition: it becomes history when institutions start routing decisions through it — a polity founded on it, a law or a land claim that depends on it, a liturgy that reenacts it — so that the legend is a fact in the only sense that has consequences. (3) The anchoring condition: some particular has to be picked out and held fixed even if nearly everything said about it is false, so that later inquiry has something to attach to and correct; a name with no anchor at all never becomes history however much uptake it gets, it just becomes a character. And then the bit I can't get straight, which I've started calling the Doniphon problem in my notes: Doniphon knows the story is false and stands aside, and what he buys with it is genuinely good — statehood, the railroad, the cattle barons losing, Hallie's life — and the film is clearly on his side emotionally while being really unsparing about what it costs him: the half-built house he torches, the coffin nobody comes to, the cactus rose on it. Is the film saying a polity may be founded on a story its founders know to be false and that this is fine, or that it's sometimes worth it but corrupts what it founds, or that founding just is the manufacture of a usable legend so the question doesn't arise? And does the film's answer have the same shape for a founding narrative like the Exodus as for an event nobody was keeping records of like the Trojan War, or for a figure that grows by accretion like Arthur?

Also, and I think this is the part I need most: tell me where the film resists this whole reading. Which scenes are just a Western about a man who steps aside, and where would pressing my three conditions on it be bad criticism rather than good? I don't want to show eighteen-year-olds a two-hour allegory if it isn't one. Please end with a one-sentence bottom line for the seminar, and then a final line of the form \answer{...} naming which of the three conditions the film actually vindicates — or \answer{none} if your honest read is that it defeats the apparatus — followed by a clause on whether the Doniphon problem has the same answer for founding legends as for event legends.
