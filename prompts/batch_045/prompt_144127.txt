Hi — I'm on a small commitee helping rewrite the label text for the ancient-world galleries at a regional museum. I'm the non-specialist on it (the others are a prehistoric archaeologist and a designer), and we keep tripping over the word "collapse", so I want to know what exactly we're signing up for when we use it.

Here's the practice I want you to look at. Our current labels say the Bronze Age world of the eastern Mediterranean collapsed somewhere around 1200 BC, and that the Classic Maya collapsed in the ninth century. But when the evidence looks pretty similar — fewer big buildings, inscriptions stop, long-distance trade dries up, the settlement hierarchy flattens out from four tiers to two, fine pottery gets crude — we reach for different words depending on the case: Egypt "entered an unsettled period" at the end of the Old Kingdom, the western Roman world "gave way to successor kingdoms", and as far as I can tell the same material signature in post-Roman Britain gets described as people "reverting to a simpler way of life". Three things about this bother me. First, we only seem to say collapse when the change is compressed into about two or three generations; the same change spread over three centuries is a "transition", even if the before and after states are identical — so maybe "collapse" is secretly a claim about the rate of change and about what it felt like to live through it, not about where things ended up. Second, we keep switching units mid-sentence: the Classic Maya polity collapsed, but Maya people and Maya languages obviously didn't, and we quietly slide between polity, culture, population, language community, and elite institutions depending on which one gives us the answer we want. Third, we seem to measure a society's fate by what happens to its monuments, its writing system and its tax-collecting elite rather than by what happens to a typical farming household — I have a feeling that even if we found out ordinary people ate better and worked less after the palaces burned, we'd still call it a collapse, which makes me suspect the word is doing evaluative work about complexity rather than just describing a decline.

So: what has to be true about what a society is, and about when the same society persists, for this labeling practice to make sense? Reconstruct the implicit theory of social persistence, and be sharp about what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits comfortably with — where is it philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories of persistence would license the same labels? Is "collapse" a description of a social event at all, or a verdict about a kind of loss, and does the practice need the rate-dependence and the unit-switching to be consistent with each other? Which views does it quietly rule out — for instance a view on which a society just is its population over time and there's no further fact about institutional continuity, or a view on which questions like "did the same society persist?" only have answers once you've fixed a reference class and a comparison baseline? And finally: if we adopted the "transformation" framing consistently across every case, what would our labels lose? Would we become unable to say that anything went badly for anybody, or to attach any responsibility to whoever was running things — and is that loss a reason to keep the collapse language, or a reason to think the language was smuggling in a moral claim? Please also flag it if any of my archaeology is just wrong; I'd rather find out now.
