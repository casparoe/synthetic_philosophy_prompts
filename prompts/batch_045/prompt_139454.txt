You are drafting the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two research programmes on the nature, persistence, and change of social norms. Both programmes are characterised below; treat these characterisations as the only commitments either side has, so that every argument in the document is reconstructible from what is given here plus ordinary conceptual analysis. Do not cite, allude to, or name any published work, author, or experiment; the document must stand on its own without any bibliography or reference list.

Camp A, the equilibrium camp (Lewisian in spirit), holds that the core case of a social norm is a convention: a regularity of behaviour in a recurrent situation that persists because it is a coordination equilibrium, together with the expectations and common knowledge that go with it. Conventions need not be optimal, only self-enforcing; their arbitrariness is shown by the existence of equally serviceable alternatives; their apparent normative force is explained by the preferences and expectations participants have, including preferences about conformity and about sanctioning. Norm change is best modelled as movement between equilibria, driven by changes in the payoff structure and by shifts in salience.

Camp B, the sanction-and-transmission camp, holds that the core case of a social norm is a regularity sustained by empirical expectations about what others do and normative expectations about what others approve of, backed by actual or anticipated sanctioning, and that its content and spread are shaped by population-level cultural transmission: conformist copying, prestige bias, and threshold dynamics. On this view many socially central norms are not solutions to coordination problems at all, predictions about persistence and change require transmission dynamics rather than payoff structure alone, and the equilibrium camp's claim to cover all norms depends on loading sanctions into the preferences, which makes it hard to test.

The collaboration is organised around the following stipulated body of field data, which both camps accept as accurately described. Two hamlets face each other across a river, linked for generations by a single ferry. The long-standing regularity is that the ferry waits ten minutes past the hour for latecomers, and everyone plans arrivals around it; no one has ever been fined, excluded, or formally criticised for the ferry schedule, and there is no authority enforcing it. In year 0 a bridge opens two kilometres upstream: crossing by bridge is faster and cheaper for every resident whatever anyone else does, so continued ferry use is not an equilibrium given residents' actual preferences. In years 1 to 3, 41 per cent of crossings are still made by ferry. Interviewed ferry users overwhelmingly say that people here use the ferry. Seventy-one per cent of all residents assert that most others would think less of someone who abandoned the ferry, while 4 per cent say that they themselves would think less of such a person; no instance of criticism, refusal of service, or exclusion of a bridge user has been recorded. In year 2 researchers post the true survey results, including the absence of any recorded sanctioning, on the notice boards of both hamlets. Ferry use falls to 22 per cent within six weeks and then flattens, remaining at 22 per cent for the following two years. The residual users are disproportionately the two oldest boat owners, the people others describe as the ones who know the river. Separately, within days of the bridge opening and with no agreement and no enforcement, a new regularity appears in which everyone parks on the north side, although the south side is equally accessible; it is first adopted by three households whose heads are the people most often named as those others take their lead from.

Use the term phantom-equilibrium regularity, which the collaborators agree to stipulate, for a behavioural regularity that is stable only because participants hold false beliefs: in the belief-phantom variant, false beliefs about others' preferences; in the sanction-phantom variant, false beliefs about others' willingness to sanction, where in fact no one would sanction.

Produce the joint statement in four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: the propositions both camps can sign about what an account of norms has to explain, about what the hamlet data do and do not show, about which features of the case are non-diagnostic because both accounts predict them, and about how each term is being used. Each claim must be substantive enough that a competent outsider could imagine it being false.

Second, a disagreements table with one row per residual disagreement and columns for the issue, Camp A's formulation of its own position in wording a partisan of Camp A would sign without hedging, Camp B's formulation of its own position in wording a partisan of Camp B would sign without hedging, and an assessment of whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal, with a one-sentence reason. Include at least the following issues: whether the ferry regularity in years 1 to 3 is a convention, a norm of some other kind, or neither; whether the 22 per cent plateau is a phantom-equilibrium regularity, and if so of which variant; whether loading sanctions and conformity into the preferences is legitimate modelling or an immunity manoeuvre; whether the north-side parking regularity is best explained by salience or by prestige-biased transmission; and whether the distinction between conventions and sanction-backed norms is a difference in kind or a difference in the mechanism that holds a regularity in place.

Third, a cruxes section. Identify at least four sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each crux, give the sub-question, a further study or argument in the hamlets that both camps agree would bear on it and that does not require any external literature, Camp A's prediction stated in advance with the magnitude or direction it commits to, Camp B's prediction likewise, and a note of what result would move which side and by how much. Where both camps predict the same observable outcome but for different reasons, say so explicitly and record the mediating variable that would discriminate between them.

Fourth, two short closing subsections: which parts of the original dispute have dissolved into questions about words, and why; and what each camp would count as evidence against its own view, including where each camp expects itself to be wrong.

Constraints on the drafting: define every technical term at the point of use; do not attribute to either camp a claim it would disown for the sake of symmetry; avoid formulations that are true but vacuous; and keep the whole document such that both camps could put their names to it while continuing to disagree.
