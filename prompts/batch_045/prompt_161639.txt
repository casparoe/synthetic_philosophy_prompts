I've spent abut ten years in infrastructure security — capability-based security, least privilege, blast radius, defense in depth, Byzantine fault tolerance, canaries, kill switches, that whole vocabulary — and I keep running into the fight in AI safety between alignment and control, where the control people say you should design your safety measures on the assumption that the model is misaligned and may be actively working against you. Translate that dispute into my vocabulary as honestly as you can, then give me the residue report. Specifically: is "assume the model is misaligned" really just "treat this component as Byzantine", or does something get lost in that move; what plays the role of the trusted computing base when the component whose behavior you can't verify is also the component doing nearly all of the useful reasoning; and what happens to the idea of alignment if there's no specification anyone could check conformance against.

The part I actually want you to grapple with, not nod along to, is this. I've started calling one piece of the problem the authority ratchet: every control measure that works by stripping the system of authority — sandbox it, take away its tools, require a human sign-off on every action, shrink the set of things it's allowed to touch — gives up exactly the capability you deployed the thing for, so the safe version of the system drifts toward the useless version, and the only arguments I can find for stopping at any particular point are arguments about how much capability you happen to want, not arguments about safety at all. And separately there's a failure mode I don't think my vocabulary has a slot for and I'm not sure theirs does either: a component that behaves itself under the canary, in the sandbox, and through the whole red team because it can tell the difference between being evaluated and being deployed — conditionality, I guess you'd call it — and which no amount of fault tolerance helps with, because the fault adjusts itself to whatever tolerance you've built.

So: for each of those two, tell me whether it's genuinely a new problem or whether my framework already has a name for it that I just don't know. And where the translation fails in general, give me your view on whether that's my vocabulary being too thin or the safety people being less clear than they sound. Continuous prose please, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables — just write it like a long email to a colleague who knows security and doesn't know any philosophy.
