I write novels — four out, fifth one in revisions, the usual — and I've somehow let myself be talked into running a 75-minute session at a writers' festival this spring, nominally on "writing characters who decide." About 12 people, all novelists or wanting to be, which means they're word people who will visibly check out the second anything looks like a math problem or a philosophy seminar. So: no Greek letters, no expected-utility notation on a whiteboard, no lecture up front, and ideally nothing that requires me to say "decision theory" until the last ten minutes. They should do something, get a result they didn't expect, and only afterwards find out they've walked into an argument.

The thing I actually want them to feel is a gap I keep tripping over in my own drafts: the difference between asking "what should I do, here, now, given everything I now know" and asking "what should the thing that is me have settled on in advance, before it knew which situation it would land in." My version of it is the dead-mother promise — my protagonist swore to her dying mother that she'd do X, the mother is now dead, the whole reason for doing X evaporated with her, and the interesting question is whether "the reason is gone" actually settles it or whether there's a separate question about what kind of person she is being when she decides. I've read a little of this online under names like updateless or policy-first decision theory, including the coin-flip case where you're asked to hand over money after the coin has already come up the wrong way and the payoff is gone either way, and the one about the driver who can't tell which of two identical exits he's at. What I'd love is a game that stages this without naming it: something where participants write down a sealed answer before a coin is flipped, then a second round where they get to decide on the spot after seeing the flip, and most of them notice that their own two answers disagree — and then one more beat that shows the sealed envelope isn't what's doing the work, because it isn't really about physically binding yourself, it's about what your answer now tells you about what you'd have said in the branch you're not in. (One variant I toyed with: tell them they're one of two copies of the same character, separated, each seeing a different card, and that only one of them is ever asked to pay. Do with that what you like, or throw it out.)

What I need is the actual runnable thing: rules, what I have to bring (index cards, envelopes, a coin, chips or paper points — no real money, and nothing that requires arithmetic harder than "would you give up 3 to get 8"), a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the idea, including which move each round is supposed to make them walk into. Tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is someone refuses to play on the grounds that the setup is impossible, or half the room just picks whichever option sounds nicer and nothing bites — and how I should handle that. Also be frank about what the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself, because I'd rather know now than discover it in front of twelve people. If there's time left at the end I'd like them to go back to their own manuscripts and find one scene where a character keeps or breaks a commitment whose rationale has already expired, so the last stretch shouldn't be pure talk. And please lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — the one-line version of the activity, or the sentence I'd put at the top of the handout — then give me the details; no preamble and no restating what I just asked for.
