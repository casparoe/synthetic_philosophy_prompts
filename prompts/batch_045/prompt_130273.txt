I'm 71, retired, spent thirty years as a hydrologist measuring what actually moves water and what doesn't, and I've drifted into an online reading group on causation, which is turning out to be a good deal less settled than mesureing a river.

Two slogans keep turning up and I cannot tell what anyone means by them.

The first: at the inquest into my sister-in-law's death in a care home, the home's barrister kept saying an omission can't be a cause. The night staff didn't answer her call bell for the better part of an hour, she'd fallen and was bleeding, and he wanted to say that whatever caused her death, it wasn't something nobody did. He was using it to get to: so nothing here is attributable to my client.

The second: my granddaughter came home from a demonstration with a placard saying not acting is acting, which she was applying to people who scroll past a video of someone being beaten. Also sounds like a causal claim, and it was being used to get to roughly the opposite sort of conclusion.

So: lay out for me, as a list, the precise things each of those sentences could be claiming, from the boring to the wild, and say for each whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's just false, and — the part I actually care about — whether that reading would do the work its user wanted it to do. Don't tell me who in the literature said what; I want the menu, and I want to know which items on it would actually get the barrister his conclusion and which would get my granddaughter hers.

Three things I want the menu to handle.

(a) I keep thinking in circuits, which may be a bad habit from instrumentation: there's a difference between a standby pump that was simply never switched on and a pump that had been taken apart for service. In one case the machinery was intact and idle, just not energised; in the other there was nothing there to energise. Which readings of an omission can't be a cause respect that difference, and which quietly collapse it?

(b) My sister-in-law was going to die within weeks anyway. The most anyone claims is that responding to the bell would have bought her another day or two, maybe only hours. So is the failure to respond a cause of her death, or only of her dying when she did rather than later? And whichever way that goes, does it change which reading of the slogan is doing real work for the barrister — because it looks to me like he needs a reading that survives the timing point and I can't see one.

(c) Does omission in these slogans already have a duty baked into it? Is she omitted to check on her just she didn't check on her, or does the word already smuggle in she was supposed to? I suspect whichever way that goes decides whether the slogan can even be a premise in a causal argument or is really a conclusion in a moral one wearing a causal hat, but I can't get it straight.

If the circuit picture is a bad model, say so and hand me a better one. I'm not attached to it, I'd just be greatful for something I can hold in my head.
