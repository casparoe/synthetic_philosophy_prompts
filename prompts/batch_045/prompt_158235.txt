I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in agricultural entomology, mostly integrated pest management, and for the last couple of years I've been sitting in on philosophy lectures at the local university and getting into arguments I'm not equiped for. The current one is about insecticides. I'm on the board of a community greenhouse coalition and we have to write an actual pest-control policy: aphids and whitefly in the glasshouses, and the town next door is asking whether the mosquito abatement district should switch from broad-spectrum adulticide fogging to a Wolbachia release program that suppresses the population by making eggs fail rather than by killing adults. I keep getting two answers from my philosophy friends. One says: if the insects are sentient at all, the numbers are astronomical and nearly any intervention is a moral catastrophe. The other says individuals barely matter, spray away, end of story. Neither helps me write a policy, and I think both are skipping the step I actually want.

So before I evaluate anybody's theory, I want the spec. What does a theory of when it is permissible to kill — or otherwise suppress — insects for crop, comfort or disease reasons have to be able to do, to count as adequate for my purpose? The purpose matters: it has to yield a usable verdict for a person or a board making a real decision next season, on the evidence actually available, under honest uncertainty about invertebrate sentience. Here are the cases I think are load-bearing; tell me which really are: (i) fogging versus Wolbachia, where one option kills maybe hundreds of millions outright and the other kills almost nobody but prevents a whole cohort from existing; (ii) a choice between one knockdown treatment and repeated low-dose treatments, i.e. trading a huge number of very short likely-miserable lives for a small number of longer ones; (iii) biological control using a generalist predator that afterwards becomes the pest itself (cane toads, harlequin ladybirds, that sort of thing) — can a theory that only looks at an intervention's immediate victims say anything at all; (iv) a control method that reliably selects for resistance, so it buys relief now and costs more suffering later when its too late; (v) the replaceability thought — in most pest species nearly all juveniles die anyway, so a spray may mostly shift who dies rather than whether anyone dies, and my total-utilitarian friend concludes from this that spraying is nearly costless, which I think is mad but I can't say why. There is also a distinction nobody seems to police: between what makes killing an individual permissible and what makes a population-level intervention permissible, given that a policy aims at populations and only incidentally at individuals.

Give me exactly five numbered desiderata, each one a sentence or two, no preamble and no closing summary. Each item should name the requirement, name the case or consideration that motivates it, and carry a tag at the front saying [non-negotiable] or [preference of a camp]. Among the five, include: one requirement you think is really just a prejudice of one side of this debate and should be dropped; the pair of requirements, if there is one, that cannot both be satisfied; and the single requirement that does most to disqualify the popular options — the sentience-threshold view, the total view, and the compassionate-conservation do-no-harm line. If one of my five cases is a red herring that no adequate theory needs to handle, say so inside the item where it would otherwise have gone.
