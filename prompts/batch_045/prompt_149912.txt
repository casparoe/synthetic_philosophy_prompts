Before I defected to philosophy I spent about a decade doing data modelling and DBA work (mostly Postgres, a fair bit of warehouse stuff, endless arguments about schemas), and I still think natively in base tables and rows, primary and foreign keys, joins and join predicates, virtual views vs. materialized ones, updatable views and INSTEAD OF triggers, cascading deletes and referential integrity, transactions and commit, normalization and 1NF, NULLs and three-valued logic. That's the vocabulary I want the translation in, and assume I'm fluent — don't explain what a left join is or stop to define 1NF.

The job: take the special composition question — van Inwagen's framing in Material Beings, roughly "for any non-overlapping xs, when is there a y such that the xs compose y?" — and restate the main answers in that vocabulary, one at a time, as faithfully as you can: nihilism (only simples; where it seems there's a chair there are just simples arranged chairwise, and nothing is ever really composed), universalism (any xs at all compose something, including the fusion of the Eiffel Tower and my left ear), van Inwagen's own organicist restriction (composition happens exactly when the xs are caught up in a life — the swarm of bees isn't an object, the bee is), the brutalist answer (there's a substantive necessary condition and it's just a brute fact that that's the condition), and, if there's room, the argument from vagueness that's supposed to shove you toward one of the extreme answers by pointing out that any interesting condition like "fastened together" or "acting jointly" is vague and would make existence itself indeterminate. Some things I want you to specifically graple with: is composition more like defining a view or more like materializing one, and does that distinction do any real work or does it collapse under pressure? Where do gunk and junk land — no atomic level to bottom out at, i.e. no possible base table / no reachable first normal form?
