i restore old houses for a living, mostly 1800s timber-frame stuff in new england, and i got into an argument with a friend who reads a lot of biology blogs. he says biologists basically have two competing accounts of what the function of a body part is: one says the function is whatever that part was selected for way back in the history of the species, the other says the function is just what the part currently contributes to keeping the organism running right now. apparently they argue about things like whether the function of the heart is pumping blood or making a thudding noise, and about a hypothetical organism assembled by sheer luck out of swamp muck that comes out identical to a real frog down to the last heartbeat: its heart pumps, but nothing ever selected for anything, so does the heart have a function, and if not, what is it doing.

the thing is, this feels like a problem i already solve every week. half my job is working out what some beam, board, drain or pipe is for in a house where everyone who knew is dead and the drawings are long gone. my vocabulary for that is pretty specific: original spec, what the guy who built it was trying to do, load path, what is actually bearing weight right now, retrofit, adaptive reuse, grandfathered in, nonconforming use, an orphaned system (pipe that runs to nothing), a part that got repurposed (the coal chute is now the wiring chase), a part that is failing (rotted sill that is nominally still carrying the wall), and the opposite case, something doing a job nobody planned (a stone foundation wall that, ever since the addition went up in the 40s, happens to be the only thing keeping that addition from sliding downhill).

so here is what i want. translate those two biology accounts into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, the way you would explain it to a crew: for each one say what i would be pointing at in a house when i said a thing's function is X, and give me the building version of the heart case and of the lucky-frog case. then give me the residue report, because i think buildings are a bad fit in at least three places and i want to know whether that is my problem or theirs.

one: in a building there is always somebody who meant something. even with the drawings lost i can usually reconstruct intent from tool marks and joinery. in biology there is no architect, nothing selected anything on purpose. so when the history side says that what matters is where the part came from, is my translation quietly sneaking a designer back in, and does that make the history side look more natural than it deserves to?

two: i can't ask the builder either, but i also can't inspect the history directly the way i can read a scribe mark on a beam. seems to me biologists normally work out what something was selected for by looking at what it does now and how well shaped it is for that job, which looks circular if the point was to define function in the first place. does that worry even have an analogue in my world, or is a building the one case where you can genuinely break the circle, because you can go find the permit application or the catalog the house was ordered out of?

three: the broken part. my rotted sill is not holding anything up any more, it is basically compost, and i would still say its function is to carry the wall. that feels like an easy win for the history side and a real problem for the does-it-now side, except my friend says the does-it-now people have a reply about what the part does in a normal or healthy member of the population. can you put that reply in building terms, does it actually work, and is it smuggling the history back in by the back door?

and then there is the accidental-usefulness case, the stone wall propping up an addition it was never meant to carry. function or not? my trade has a defenitely third answer that neither biology side seems to offer, something like: it is carrying load, so treat it as structure regardless of what anyone meant. i want to know whether that third answer is a real position in the biology argument under another name, or whether it is just a practical dodge that works for contractors and says nothing about frogs.

be straight with me about where the analogy breaks down and what the breaking tells us. if some part of the biology disagreement simply cannot be said in my vocabulary, say so, and then tell me whether that is because my trade is missing a concept i should go learn, or because the disagreement is thinner than the biology people think it is.
