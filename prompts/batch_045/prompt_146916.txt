I co-convene a small interdisciplinary reading group — PhD students in political science and epidemiology, plus two people from a conflict-prevention NGO — on whether it makes any sense to say that violence has declined, and we keep talking past each other in a way that reading another book will not fix. What I have realised is that the disagreements are not mainly about the data: people are tracking different quantities (realised harm versus exposure to risk), dividing by different denominators (absolute counts, per head, per person-hour of social contact, share of all deaths), drawing on different domains (interpersonal killing, war, state punishment and policing, slow or structural harm, harms to animals), and measuring against different baselines (a supposed state of nature, prehistoric foraging bands, the mid-twentieth century, the recent past). So instead of another session of readings, I want a classification instrument: a decision tree or flowchart that takes someone from a short series of concrete cases to a named position, built so that I can use it to split the room into pairs who disagree at exactly one node and therefore have something precise to argue about.

Please build it as follows. Start not with measurement but with the meta-question of whether there is a fact of the matter to be had at all, so that someone who thinks the category itself is a political or conventional one exits early to that leaf rather than being forced through measurement questions they regard as rigged; tell me whether you think that exit is premature, and why people who take it often go on to make confident empirical claims anyway. Then run the measurement nodes, and include as explicit branch questions two distinctions my group kept circling and that I have not seen handled anywhere: deterred or counterfactual violence, where a harm does not occur because a credible threat of overwhelming force prevents it (a city with a very low homicide rate because everyone knows a heavily armed force will respond within minutes and occasionally kills in the process — has violence declined there?); and substituted harm, where over two centuries physical punishment and private vengeance are replaced by fines, debt, eviction and administrative exclusion, so that violent deaths fall sharply while deprivation rises (same quantity, or a different one?). Add a node on displacement, where violence is not reduced but outsourced to a periphery or to non-state actors, and a node on statistics: whether what looks like a downward trend in the data we actually have is better read as the quiet stretch of a heavy-tailed process, so that no trend inference is licensed. Give me eight to ten questions, each a concrete case or a forced choice, none of them naming an author, school or book, none of them honestly answerable with it depends, and each phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which position it leads to.

The leaves should number ten to fourteen. For each, give a short descriptive label (with a familiar label in parentheses if one exists — I do not need names, citations or a reading list), one sentence naming that position's most awkward commitment, and one signature prediction: something measurable the position commits you to about the next fifty years. Then flag two things. First, any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, and why — I would rather know that two positions are observationally equivalent than have the tree pretend otherwise. Second, the combinations of answers the logical space allows but that nobody seems to occupy: for instance, is there anyone who accepts per-capita measurement, counts harms to animals inside the domain, and still says things have got worse? For each empty cell, say in one line whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored and available. Finally, mark which nodes do the real work of separating people in a room like mine and which are almost never contested, so I know where to spend the session.
