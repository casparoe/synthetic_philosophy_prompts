I write about medicine and ideas, and I'm currently working on a piece about the places where suffering quietly gets assigned a number: triage protocols that rank patients by pain scores, cost-effectiveness thresholds expressed in quality-adjusted life years, the unwritten rules an intensive care team uses when deciding whether another week of treatment is a rescue or a cruelty. What I keep finding is that everyone involved behaves as though pleasure and pain sit on a single scale, while my own reactions to concrete cases refuse to. I don't want a literature survey or a reading list — I want a principle, argued for, that I could paraphrase in a paragraph without naming a single philosopher. So here are my verdicts, given cold.

(1) Ninety seconds of severe pain during an otherwise routine procedure, followed by forty healthy years: not even a trade, obviously worth it. (2) Two days of hard, unmedicated post-surgical pain in exchange for one weekend of the most intense pleasure I can imagine: I take it, though here for the first time I feel myself actually weighing. (3) One week of unremitting pain at the intensity of a full-blown trigeminal neuralgia attack, in exchange for one week of equally intense pleasure for the same person: I refuse, and not by a margin — the offer simply stops making sense to me. (4) The same week of neuralgia, with the pleasure going instead to a thousand strangers for the rest of their lives: I still refuse, and I am unsettled by how little those extra thousand lives move me. (5) A year of crushing grief after the death of my child, set against never having had the child: I'd have the child, and I don't think I'm confused. (6) The agony of a climber's last push to a summit, which he later counts among the best things in his life: I don't treat it as a cost needing to be offset at all; yet the same intensity and duration of agony in a patient with advanced dementia, who cannot understand what is happening or why, I treat as worse than the climber's, not less bad. (7) Twenty minutes of unmistakable agony during a procedure performed under a drug that blocks memory formation, so that the patient writhes and screams and then remembers nothing: my gut says this barely counts, which sits very badly with (3).

The only formulation I have managed to get myself to endorse is something like this: suffering is commensurable with pleasure only when it is integrable, meaning the subject can place it, at the time or afterwards, inside a project, a relationship, or a self-conception she endorses; suffering that destroys the very capacity for such placement crosses a lexical threshold and is not offset by any quantity of anyone's pleasure. I like that it explains the climber, the grief, and my refusal in (3). I can also see that it makes a mess of the dementia patient in (6), who has little capacity left to integrate anything, and of (7), where nothing is integrated either and yet the pain seems to matter less.

So: give me two or three candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself without your help, with each clause paired to the case that forces it — including at least one candidate that drops my integrability clause and says plainly what dropping it then costs me. Then run whichever principle you favour over cases I haven't listed: the same agony in a non-human animal; a policy that imposes a very small risk of above-threshold suffering on each of ten million people in order to spare one person a year of mild chronic pain; a life that contains one such week at eighty and is otherwise excellent. Tell me which of my seven verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate and which single one you would overturn, whether any of the candidates is really a lexical threshold view wearing a more complicated costume, and whether the whole enterprise collapses into the conclusion that a world with no sentient life in it would be the best one.
