ok so this is gonna sound like a weird jump but bear with me. In debate club we got completely stuck on the torture vs dust specks thing — would you rather one person be tortured for fifty years, or some astronomically huge number of people each get one tiny annoyance, like an eyelash in their eye for a second. Everyone had a verdict and nobody could say what made them pick it. Then I noticed something that made it worse, which is that the actual adult world seems to already answer this question three different ways depending on where you look, and I cant tell if that's because someone thought it through or because nobody ever checked that the answers match.

Here's what I mean, and sorry if I've got the details a bit wrong, fix me if I do. One: when the government writes safety rules it acts like tiny harms to enormous numbers of people totally add up. As far as I understand it they'll make every new car more expensive to shave each driver's chance of dying by some microscopic amount, and they defend that using a dollar figure for a statistical life — so 300 million people each carrying a one-in-a-million risk comes out to real lives and the rule is worth passing. Two: in lawsuits the same legal system acts like it doesn't add up at all. One guy whose neighbor's tree drips sap on his car every single day for a year can sue, but ten thousand people each getting one drop on one afternoon apparently get nothing, because of that old idea that the law doesn't bother with trifles and each person has to show their own real injury. Three: torture is banned flat out, no balancing allowed, not even in the ticking time bomb case my teacher brought up, so nobody is permitted to say out loud that one person's fifty years beats a billion people being mildly annoyed — the door is just shut. Oh and there's a fourth thing that might break everything, my cousin got one of those letters about downloading music and apparently you can owe a fixed few hundred dollars per song without anyone proving they were harmed at all, which sounds like the exact opposite of the sap thing.

So what I actually want to know is what each of these practices has to assume in order to make sense. Does the safety-rule one really need the idea that small harms sum up across people, or would it work just as well on some precautionary or fairness-to-each-person view? Does the lawsuit one actually rule out adding up, or is it just worried about practical stuff like proving tiny losses and not letting one guy collect everything? And is the absolute torture ban really a claim about how bad torture is compared to dust specks, or is it a rule about not trusting whoever does the math? Also, can all three fit in one head, or is at least one of them running on vibes? Me and my friends came up with two things and I want to know if they're real positions people defend: the one-life rule, where harms add up inside a single person's life but never across different people, so the sap guy wins because it's all his life and the ten thousand lose because each of them only lost one drop; and the who's-in-the-room rule, where it isn't moral arithmetic at all but about what kind of decision maker is looking, a court sees one plaintiff at a time, a regulator sees a spreadsheet of everybody, and an absolute ban is what you write when you don't trust the person holding the calculator. Does either of those actually explain the pattern, or does the law just have incompatible stuff sitting next to each other here? And last thing: if we decided the add-them-up side was right, what would concretely have to change — would we need some kind of tiny fine per microharm paid into a pool, would class action lawsuits have to work completely differently, and would the torture ban have to become a torture-is-really-expensive-but-sometimes-worth-it rule, or is there a way to accept the adding up without giving that up?
