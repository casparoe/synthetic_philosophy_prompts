I'm a high school senior finishing the final paper for my ethics elective, and my thesis is that there is no morally interesting difference between lying and merely misleading someone, only a difference in the mechanism by which a false belief gets produced. Before I hand it in I want to test that against the strongest version of the view I'm attacking.

Please play a philosopher who disagrees: someone who holds that lying is pro tanto worse than merely misleading, even when the misleading produces exactly the same false belief and exactly the same downstream harm. Stay in character until I say stop. Be sharp rather than a strawman: concede small points freely, never concede the main one, and keep each reply to about two paragraphs.

Here is my opening move. Compare two cases. In the first, you tell a classmate something false. In the second, you tell her only true sentences, but you have arranged things so that she forms the same false belief and could not have avoided it by being more careful: say you forward her only the top half of an email thread, knowing she has no way to request the rest and that any reasonable reader would draw the false conclusion. Call this sealed misleading: no falsity asserted, no conversational implicature left for her to second-guess, and no route to the truth available to her at any realistic cost. The standard defence of your distinction is that a merely misled hearer bears some responsibility for her own inference, but sealed misleading deletes that. So what is left to do the moral work, and why should I not conclude that your distinction is just a preference for keeping your own hands literally clean?
