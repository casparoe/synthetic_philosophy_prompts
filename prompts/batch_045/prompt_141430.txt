I write novels — four so far, none of which anyone in a philosophy department has heard of — and I've been ask to run a ninety-minute workshop at a writers' festival next month, one of those "steal from other disciplines" sessions. Mine is on Dennett's old essay "Where Am I?" (the late-seventies one where his brain gets taken out and kept alive in a lab while his own body is driven around by radio link, and he keeps trying to say where he now is). About eighteen people, novelists and short-story writers, mostly literary with a few SF types, zero philosophy between them. Half won't have read it and I've decided not to make them, so I've got maybe twenty-five minutes to get the idea across before I can use it for anything.

Why they're there is my own book. My narrator — call her Marta — has had her brain moved from a hospital in Lisbon to a clinic in Toronto, and it's hooked up by radio to her body, which is still in Lisbon, still walking around, still being Marta to everyone who meets it. I'm intercutting chapters: the Toronto chapters in first person present inside the vat, the Lisbon chapters in close third on the body. Every single beta reader so far has come back with the same question in the margin — "but where is she really?" — and I've realised I don't know what would count as an answer to that, and, more usefully, that they don't either.

So: don't explain the puzzle to me, I've read the essay. Plan how I explain it to them.

A few things I've already worked out and want you to build around rather than replace. I've been using three little tests on people, my own names for them, nothing to look up: the passport question (if a border official or a court had to record her location, which one goes in the box), the visit question (her daughter can get on a plane to exactly one city — which flight is she visiting her mother on), and the harm question (a stranger does something vicious to the body in Lisbon versus somebody sabotages the vat in Toronto — which of those is a crime against Marta, and does it matter that readers will answer the visit question and the harm question differently). Then there's what I think of as the camera problem: a scene has to have a vantage point, a pronoun, a room, and prose can only be in one of them at a time, so the form I'm writing in seems to force a determinate answer that the philosophy doesn't obviously give me — and my worry is the reverse too, that my students will think the form has *settled* it, that deciding to narrate from the vat is the same as deciding where she is.

What I want from you: the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what, since I don't think you can just open with the split case and expect anything to land; the single example or scenario that should carry the whole twenty-five minutes, including what it has to have and what it has to lack (I'm fairly sure it has to keep the body alive and doing things, so the body's side stays in play, and that it must not explain how the radio link works, because the moment it's engineering everyone says "oh, so it's just a machine problem"); the misconceptions this particular crowd will bring or form, roughly in the order they'll form them, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off — I can predict three or four of them myself, the materialist who says she's obviously where her brain is and that's that, the person who says it's all semantics so the answer is "both", the Star Trek teleporter reflex, and the one who wants to dissolve the whole thing into an emotion, she's where she loves or where she's needed, but I'd bet there are two or three more I'll get ambushed by; and one exercise at the end that shows me they've understood the *question* rather than just followed my plot.

Also tell me what to refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing. I'm assuming that means personal identity over time, the hard problem of consciousness, split-brain cases — and I'm genuinely torn about whether to mention that Dennett later wrote about the self as a kind of centre of narrative gravity, which sounds made for a room full of novelists but might be a distraction, or worse, might let them feel they've resolved something. You decide that one for me.
