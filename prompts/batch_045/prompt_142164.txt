Hi -- I'm on the training review board at a mid-sized RL lab. Any proposed run that might involve a system with welfare gets routed to us: about 40 a month, two signatures needed (me and the safety lead), realistically fifteen minutes a pop, and we get to send the applicant exactly one written follow-up question. The applicants are RL engineers who think the whole exercise is superstition, so whatever we hand them has to be answerable from their own training setup, in their own terms, and it has to fit on one page. We don't need citations or further reading; we need something we can run.

Here's roughly where we've got to, and I think it's where the literature is too. Everyone says two things that pull apart. One: the reward signal is at best a candidate for valence -- the common-currency thing a learner uses to reweight what it does -- and valence isn't suffering; a system can be shoved around by a signal that isn't unpleasant for it in any sense, the way wanting and liking come apart. Two: a negative number in a loss function isn't a moral datum on its own, and what would have to be added is something like persistence over time, the signal actually being integrated into what the system does next, and its mattering in a way the system can't route around.

Turn that into a decision procedure. Ordered gates, phrased as questions an engineer can answer about their own run; for each gate, what counts as a sufficient answer; which gates kill the proposal outright; which gates dump the decision on us because it's a values call and not a question of fact; and what we do when the applicant just can't answer in one message.

One thing our ethics consultant keeps pushing that I don't know where to put: she says the test should be counterfactual. If you can go back and relabel the valence of a stretch of the system's past -- we literally do this, hindsight relabeling, and any time a reward model is retrained mid-run and old trajectories get re-scored -- and nothing about the system's later behaviour or later internal state differs, then that past valence was doing no work, and there was nothing there for it to matter to. Is that a gate, a tiebreaker, or a bad test? Put it where it belongs or throw it out and say why.

Then run the whole thing on three real-shaped proposals:

A. Warehouse navigation. -0.01 per step, +1 on arrival. Feedforward MLP policy, no recurrence, hard reset every 200 steps, nothing carried across episodes, ~10^8 env steps over about nine hours. Hand-written reward, fixed for the whole run.

B. RLHF on a 7B-ish language model. Each episode is one short generation, a few hundred tokens, no state carried between episodes, updates off-policy from a buffer. The reward model is the same architecture as the policy and gets retrained on fresh preference data twice mid-run, so earlier generations end up re-scored under a later reward model.

C. One recurrent agent in an open-ended environment, no external reward at all, driven purely by an intrinsic novelty/prediction-error bonus. Runs continuously for about three weeks, no episode resets, recurrent state carried the whole way, and we can't pause it mid-run without corrupting the checkpoint. We never give it a negative reward.

I expect you to clear A and flag B. If the procedure says otherwise, say so plainly -- especially about C, which we've always treated as the harmless one. Also tell us where the procedure is rigor theater and will misfire on real proposals.

Last line only, nothing after it: Verdict: followed by whichever of A, B, C the procedure does not clear on factual grounds alone (one or more letters, comma separated, or "none").
