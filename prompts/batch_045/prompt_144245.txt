hey, i'm slowly picking my way through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759, the sympathy book, not the wealth one) and I've got properly stuck on Part III, chapter one, "Of the Principle of Self-approbation and of Self-disapprobation". Here's the passage:

"We can never survey our own sentiments and motives, we can never form any judgment concerning them; unless we remove ourselves, as it were, from our own natural station, and endeavour to view them as at a certain distance from us. But we can do this in no other way than by endeavouring to view them with the eyes of other people, or as other people are likely to view them. Whatever judgment we can form concerning them, accordingly, must always bear some secret reference, either to what are, or to what, upon a certain condition, would be, or to what, we imagine, ought to be the judgment of others. We endeavour to examine our own conduct as we imagine any other fair and impartial spectator would examine it. If, upon placing ourselves in his situation, we thoroughly enter into all the passions and motives which influenced it, we approve of it, by sympathy with the approbation of this supposed equitable judge. If otherwise, we enter into his disapprobation, and condemn it.

Were it possible that a human creature could grow up to manhood in some solitary place, without any communication with his own species, he could no more think of his own character, of the propriety or demerit of his own sentiments and conduct, of the beauty or deformity of his own mind, than of the beauty or deformity of his own face. All these are objects which he cannot easily see, which naturally he does not look at, and with regard to which he is provided with no mirror which can present them to his view. Bring him into society, and he is immediately provided with the mirror which he wanted before. It is placed in the countenance and behaviour of those he lives with, which always mark when they enter into, and when they disapprove of his sentiments; and it is here that he first views the propriety and impropriety of his own passions, the beauty and deformity of his own mind. [...] The passions themselves, the desires or aversions, the joys or sorrows, which those objects excited, though of all things the most immediately present to him, could scarce ever be the objects of his thoughts. The idea of them could never interest him so much as to call upon his attentive consideration. The consideration of his joy could in him excite no new joy, nor that of his sorrow any new sorrow, though the consideration of the causes of those passions might often excite both. Bring him into society, and all his own passions will immediately become the causes of new passions. [...] they will now, therefore, interest him deeply, and often call upon his most attentive consideration."

three things I can't get straight. Please just reason off the passage and off how the sympathetic mechanism is supposed to work in the rest of the book — I don't want a reading list or references, and I can't get to a library right now anyway.

1) The three-way thing. "either to what are, or to what, upon a certain condition, would be, or to what, we imagine, ought to be the judgment of others". A guy in my reading group says this proves Smith is just an internalisation theorist: the "fair and impartial spectator" is nothing but a deposit of what actually drew approval and disapproval in the environment you grew up in, so a kid raised by three people who all thought tormenting animals was hilarious would, on Smith's own account, end up with a conscience that approved of it — and there'd be no remaining sense in which that conscience is wrong. I said no, becuase the third limb ("what we imagine ought to be the judgment of others") lets Smith say the actual onlookers are themselves off, so the spectator functions as a critic of your environment rather than a recording of it. Settle it: which limb is actually carrying the weight here, and is the "ought to be" limb a genuinely independent, normative standard, or is it just the second limb in a costume — i.e. "what would be the judgment of others, provided they were fully informed and weren't a party to the case"? If it's the latter, does my friend basically win?

2) Mirror vs. motive. In the solitary-man bit Smith looks like he's giving two different explanations of what society does for you: first it hands you a mirror (you can't see your own face without one), then it hands you a reason to care (your own passions "could scarce ever be the objects of his thoughts" until other people start reacting to them, at which point they "become the causes of new passions"). Are those two claims, or one claim said twice? Here's a test case I made up and can't find anything about: two people who grow up in total isolation and only later meet other humans. Person A spent those years obsessively journalling and inventing imaginary companions who react to what she does; person B just watched clouds. Does Smith's machinery predict any difference between them, and does the answer differ for the mirror-function and the attention-function? Same question in reverse: take someone socialised normally who then ends up the last human alive. The mirror is gone but the habit is there. Does Smith's account say her conscience decays, freezes, or keeps developing via purely imagined spectators — and is there anything in the wording here that tells you which?

3) The last sentence of the first paragraph bugs me. We "approve of it, by sympathy with the approbation of this supposed equitable judge" — but that judge is me imagining someone else, and I can only get at his approval using the very same imagination-plus-sympathy operation that got me into this in the first place. So is there a regress (a spectator to check my spectator, and so on), or does the operation hit a natural stopping point? Someone floated a distinction at me that I only half-remember, and I want to know which horn Smith is on: either the spectator's verdict is just one more sentiment for me to sympathise with, in which case nothing seems to pin down which spectator is the right one; or the change of place produces a distinctive feeling of its own — something like the sentiment of propriety — that isn't itself up for further assessment, so the regress stops because there's literally nothing left for a second-order check to do. Which fits how Smith describes the mechanism, and what would each version predict about why people raised in the same village still end up with noticeably different consciences?
