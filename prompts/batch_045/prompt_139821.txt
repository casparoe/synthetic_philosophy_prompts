I'm in a master's-level seminar on rational choice, and last week we spent a session on Good's theorem on the value of information: the result that for an agent who maximises expected utility, the ex ante expected utility of the best signal-contingent plan is never less than that of the best unconditional act, so that cost-free information can never have negative value. My sense is that the proof itself is a two-line argument and that everything worth arguing about lies elsewhere — in whether the antecedent is ever really satisfied, and in what we should say about agents who are not expected-utility maximisers. So I don't want your credence in the theorem; I want your credence in the substantive thesis people take it to support.

The thesis, call it T:

It is never rational to decline cost-free, decision-relevant information. Every apparently rational case of declining such information is really a case in which at least one of the following holds: (a) the information is not genuinely cost-free, because acquiring, storing, or processing it carries a cost; (b) it is causally efficacious in some way besides guiding the choice — its receipt is observable to others, or the act of obtaining it has consequences of its own; (c) the agent's preferences are not fixed but are reshaped by what is learned; (d) the agent cannot in fact execute the signal-contingent plan the theorem evaluates, having no commitment device and facing a later self who will re-decide; or (e) the agent is not rational, in the sense of violating the expected-utility axioms.

Please give me the following, in this order.

2. The decomposition, as a table with three columns — sub-question, your probability, one-sentence justification — covering at least these terms, plus any you think I have omitted:

Include your view on whether the proof requires full Bayesian conditionalisation or only the weaker requirement that the agent's post-signal evaluations average back to their pre-signal evaluations, i.e. the law of iterated expectations.
- P2: causally inert, act-independent signals are common enough in real human decisions for the theorem to have real content. Consider what I've been calling the observable-receipt worry: a genetic test that is free to take, whose result you can be compelled to disclose to an insurer, so that the realisation of the signal rather than only the state it tracks affects your payoffs. Here the signal is not a pure evidence variable, and the proof has nothing to say.
- P3: preferences are typically stable under learning; and where they are not, the standard rescue — redescribing outcomes so that the agent's belief state is itself part of the outcome, thereby restoring fixed preferences over an enriched outcome space — is a genuine defence rather than a manoeuvre that makes the thesis unfalsifiable.
