I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a chapter on Hume's *Of the Standard of Taste*, and I've hit a point where I need help thinking through what Hume himself — working only with the resources of that essay — would most likely have said about a case he could not have anticipated. I want the answer argued from the essay's own machinery rather than from the secondary literature, so there's no need for citations, bibliographies, or pointers to further reading; I'm after the argument, not the sources.

My chapter defends a distinction I'll call reliability versus constitution. On the reliability reading, the joint verdict of qualified critics matters because it is the best available estimate of what any suitably situated judge would converge on; the standard is a measuring instrument. On the constitutive reading, that verdict is partly what makes the work beautiful, and the qualifications on a critic are conditions on whose sentiment can speak for beauty at all. I think the essay pulls toward reliability, and I want to test that by running a case through it.

Here is the case. Suppose a system is trained on the complete run of a national newspaper's architecture criticism from 1955 to 2024 together with the buildings those critics reviewed. Given a new building, it returns a rating and a prose justification in the house style. In a blind trial, a panel of practising architects finds that the system's verdicts discriminate more finely among buildings than any individual panellist does, are more internally consistent across repeated presentation, and predict the panel's own majority better than individual panellists predict one another. Call it the Archive.

Four questions, roughly in order of how much they matter to the chapter:

First, on the five qualifications Hume sets out — delicacy of taste, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, good sense — which would the Archive satisfy and where would it fail, and would Hume's verdict shift if the standard is read as reliability rather than as constitution? I suspect delicacy is the hinge: I want to separate fine-grained discrimination from being moved appropriately, and to ask whether Hume's talk of delicacy (and his analogy with a trained palate) commits him to the second and not merely the first. If it does, the reliability reading may be in trouble, since a mechanism can discriminate without undergoing anything.

Second, take freedom from prejudice seriously. Hume's disqualifying conditions look structured around interests, personal relations, and parochial upbringing. Now suppose the training corpus systematically under-represents, say, Brutalist buildings and buildings from outside Europe and North America, so that the Archive's verdicts inherit that skew. Is that prejudice in Hume's sense, or does his notion essentially involve a subject with concerns that can be set aside? And notice the oddity: Hume treats good sense as the faculty that detects and corrects prejudice in a judge. Can that corrective operate where there is no sentiment being distorted, or does the remedy misfire when applied to something that has no sentiments at all?

Third, if I am right that Hume's case for there being any standard at all leans on the observation that certain works sustain admiration across very different audiences and long periods, then a device that aggregates across vastly more encounters than any human critic has had looks like it should be closer to the standard, not further from it — and the joint verdict of many critics begins to look like a merely practical substitute for a single well-calibrated instrument. Would Hume accept that the standard, properly pursued, collapses into one judge rather than a jury? If he would resist it, what in the essay does the resisting?

Finally, a wrinkle I can't place. Suppose a related system can also generate buildings that the panel reliably admires, though no panel member can say why. Does the provenance of an object do any work in Hume's framework, or is the standard of taste entirely a doctrine about judges and silent about makers? My sense is that it is silent, but that makes me suspicious that I am importing a later worry about mechanical production that the essay gives me no right to attribute to him.

So: what would Hume most plausibly say about the Archive, and where is my own framing — the reliability/constitution distinction especially — doing violence to what he actually argues? I'd rather be told that a question is anachronistic or ill-formed for his purposes than be given an answer to it.
