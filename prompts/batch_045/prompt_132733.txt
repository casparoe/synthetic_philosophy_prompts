The question I set was:

Explain Bertrand's paradox and say what it shows about the principle of indifference. Does the paradox refute the principle, or is there a reading on which the principle survives it?

And here's the answer I got:

The principle of indifference says that if there are n mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive possibilities and we have no reason to prefer any of them to the others, we should assign each of them probability 1/n. Bertrand's paradox, from his 1889 Calcul des probabilités, is meant to show that this principle gives inconsistent results. Take a circle of radius R and inscribe an equilateral triangle in it; the side of the triangle is R√3. What is the probability that a chord chosen "at random" is longer than that side? A chord is longer than R√3 iff its midpoint lies within R/2 of the centre, so the question is just how likely the midpoint is to land in that inner region.

That depends on how you randomise. (1) Pick two points independently and uniformly on the circumference. Fix the first point and rotate the triangle so that one vertex coincides with it; the second point must then fall on the arc of 120° opposite that vertex, which is a third of the circumference, so the probability is 1/3. (2) Pick a radius at random and then a point uniformly along it, and draw the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point. The side of the triangle bisects the radius, so the probability is 1/2. (3) Pick the midpoint uniformly from the interior of the circle. The midpoint has to fall inside the concentric circle of radius R/2, which has half the radius and therefore half the area of the original circle, so the probability is again 1/2.

So there is no real paradox. The three procedures are three different experiments — picking two points on the rim, spinning a radius and choosing a point on it, throwing a dart at the disk — and each answer is perfectly correct for its own experiment. Once you say which chance set-up you mean, the principle of indifference applies fine: it just tells you to be uniform over the outcome space of your experiment. All Bertrand showed is that "choose a chord at random" is an incomplete specification of an experiment, not that there is anything wrong with the principle itself.

And in any case the matter has been settled. In his 1973 paper "The Well-Posed Problem", E. T. Jaynes applied the principle of indifference to the symmetries of the problem — rotational, translational and scale invariance — and derived 1/2 as the unique solution. So 1/2 is the objectively correct answer and the paradox is resolved.
