Hey — I co-run a little film night at the public library (we alternate "serious classics" with stuff that's just fun) and I'm also TAing a first-year historiography seminar this term, and the two have collided in a way I didn't plan. We're screening Rashomon in a couple of weeks and I want to use it to get at hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, which is honestly the part of the seminar I find confusing rather than the part I can teach. Personal reason it's got under my skin: I recently went back and read old messages from the end of my last relationship, and I'd been carrying around this very tidy story about why it ended — she got distant, so I did the thing, so it fell apart — and the messages from the time show I had basically no idea what was happening week to week. I'd built the arc backwards out of the ending. So I know the phenomenon from the inside; I just don't have good words for it.

The vocabulary problem is where I want help. Seminar's covered Fischhoff's mid-70s experiments on the "I knew it all along" effect, and then the narrative-fallacy complaint about imposing explanatory coherence on stuff that was just contingency (Taleb's version, and Kahneman borrowing it). I've been sketching a distinction of my own that I suspect the film could sharpen: (1) classic hindsight bias, where knowing the outcome makes it feel like it had to happen; (2) the narrative fallacy proper, where a run of events gets a causal shape it didn't have; and (3) something I've started calling "genre-retrojection" — not "this caused that" but "this was the beginning of a decline / a tragedy / a false dawn," where the endpoint decides what kind of story it was rather than what caused it. My hunch is that (3) does most of the actual work in historical writing, that it's a different failure from (1) and (2), and that nobody's named it — but I may just be reinventing something, and I'd rather find that out before I stand up in front of eighteen-year-olds.

So: read the film through this. Which of the four accounts in the grove — the bandit's, the wife's, the dead husband's through the medium, the woodcutter's — is genuinely a case of retrospective reconstruction rather than just self-serving lying, and is the difference between those two even stable? There's an obvious worry here, which is that Kurosawa isn't showing us people seduced by the shape of the past at all, but people protecting their self-image in the present, which is motive rather than memory, and that reading it as a fable about narrative is a category mistake people make because the film has an epistemic reputation. I want you to press on that honestly rather than just making the reading work. Then the other half: does the framing at the gatehouse and that last business with the abandoned baby say anything about whether getting out of narrativizing is possible or even desirable, or is the film's real claim that we'd rather have a bad story than no story? Which moments would you actually pause the DVD on, and — the bit I care most about — what does a film like this show about hindsight and narrative that the psychology papers and the philosophy of historiography don't?
