So I had a proper argument with a friend last night and I think I lost it, or at least I could not say clearly what I actually believe. He's a physicist, does climate modelling adjacent stuff. His position was: the atmosphere is deterministic apart from a bit of quantum noise, so in principle if you knew the state exactly you could forecast as far out as you like, and anything we call chaos is really just an excuse for having too few observations and not enough compute — run a big enough ensemble and the uncertainty collapses. I kept trying to say that determinism and predictability are two different things, and that there's a difference between "we don't know the starting state well enough yet" and "no amount of knowing the starting state would help past a certain point", and I could of told while I was talking that I was mush-mouthing it. Then he threw in that quantum indeterminacy settles the whole thing, which seemed to me to be a totally separate point that neither of us was in a state to argue about at 11pm.

I want this in a form I can actually use, not a philosophy seminar. I'm on the duty desk at a regional flood forecasting centre. Through the season, three or four of us sit down every morning and have about twenty minutes to decide what goes in the public bulletin and whether to recommend to the county that they warn a neighbourhood, close a low-lying road, or pre-position pumps. Anything that costs money or moves people has to be signed off by the duty manager, and anything we write has to be defensible a year later if there's an inquiry. Nobody on the desk has done nonlinear dynamics properly — undergrad physics and geography, mostly — so the procedure has to be runnable by someone who knows what a spread is but couldn't derive anything. We have: an ensemble from our own model, a second model from a different centre, yesterday's runs compared against what actually happened, and occasionally time to nudge an input and rerun.

What I want is a decision procedure, in the order I should ask the questions, that takes a specific forecast-and-decision and sorts the uncertainty into buckets I can act on. The distinctions I think matter, and that my friend was steamrollering: uncertainty that would shrink if we had better observations versus uncertainty that just regrows however good the analysis is because of sensitive dependence; uncertainty that isn't really about the starting state at all but about the model being the wrong model; and — this is the one I care about most — cases where the spread is genuinely wide but every outcome in it recommends the same action, so the unpredictability is decision-irrelevant and hiding behind "the models don't agree" is the wrong move. For each bucket: what observable signature tells me I'm in it, using only products we already have and arithmetic I can do on the back of an envelope. Does the spread grow with lead time in the characteristic way? Do the two models disagree with each other more than each disagrees with itself? Is yesterday's error bigger than yesterday's spread? Does the recommended action flip if I perturb today's input by about the observation error — I've started calling that the nudge test, tell me if it's a bad idea. Where should the procedure just stop with a no (don't issue it, don't escalate), and where should it hand the decision to a human because it's a values question, like how much weight to put on a small chance of a bad outcome?

Then run it on three real-shaped cases. One: 48 hours out, both models agree on the rainfall but the ensemble splits roughly in half on whether a levee overtops upstream of a small town. Two: the three-month "wetter than average" outlook we put out every quarter, which farmers and insurers have started to actually act on — I wrote the current template, and I suspect your procedure is going to tell me to stop issuing it in that form or stop calling it a forecast, and I want to see the argument done properly before I decide whether to fight it. Three: a six-hour closure of a road that floods maybe four times a year, where the spread is wide and the action is the same across all of it. And then tell me plainly what the procedure can't do and where it will misfire — a levee breach, a catchment that's changed because of new development, anything where the past error statistics aren't a guide — and how I should word the bulletin so I'm not overclaiming or underclaiming in each bucket.
