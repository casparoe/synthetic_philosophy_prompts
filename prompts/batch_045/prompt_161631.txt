I'm in this interdisciplinary reading group — half economic historians, half people who do social theory — and we've spent two sessions on Weber's Protestant ethic thesis and gotten absolutely nowhere, mostly I think because we keep answering different questions. So I want to nail down one proposition and get a number on it.

The proposition: at least one culturally transmitted ethos (the spirit of capitalism being the candidate) made a non-redundant causal contribution to the emergence of sustained capital accumulation in early modern Europe — where non-redundant means it isn't just a downstream expression of property rights, legal institutions, literacy, urbanization or access to trade routes, and isn't just a story people later invented to dignify behaviour they were already stuck with. I'm at about 0.45. My econometrics friend is at 0.75, but I suspect she's answering a weaker question, something like do regional cultural differences predict economic outcomes at all, which I'd put at 0.85 myself. What I want from you is your own number on the strong version, plus the decomposition: whether the causal claim is even well-posed given that the spirit is an ideal type rather than something you could put on a spreadsheet (and what resolution would even consist of here — what evidence would count as vindicating or refuting it?); whether the mechanism is meant to run through consciously held doctrine or through dispositions that outlive it; how much of any observed confessional gap survives once literacy and human capital are controlled for; and how badly the direction of causation is underdetermined by the evidence we actually have, given that commercial cities probably self-selected into reformed confessions rather than the other way round.

Here's the bit I keep getting stuck on and I want you to engage with it directly. Call it the zombie-ethos test: I want to say a value-cause earns its keep only if it keeps moving behaviour after the beliefs that produced it have lapsed. A thoroughly secularized population that still works and saves like its Calvinist great-great-grandparents is evidence for Weber; a population whose work discipline collapses the moment the doctrine fades is evidence that the doctrine was doing the work all along and the ethos was definately just a transmission belt. Concretely — imagine a decent natural experiment, two adjacent regions matched on soil, urbanization, legal institutions and distance to trade routes in 1550, one going Reformed and one not, and suppose that 250 years later the Reformed region has measurably higher capital per head but no detectable difference in religious belief, practice, or stated attitudes toward work. Does that result raise your credence or lower it? I've heard it read both ways in the group and genuinely can't tell which reading is fair. Then give me the combination, tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and name the one piece of evidence — real or merely possible — that would move it the most, and in which direction.
