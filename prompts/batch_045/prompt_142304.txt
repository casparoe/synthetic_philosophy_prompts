My friend and I spent last night arguing about whether things like germs, climates and volcanoes can properly be called historical actors, and we discovered that we agreed on nearly every case while disagreeing about why. I want to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. They are:

— The cholera bacterium in a nineteenth-century port city whose council had declined to filter the water supply: an actor, plainly. I do not withdraw that when I learn about the council; the bacterium did the killing, and it had to be tracked on its own terms.

— The eruption of Tambora and the cold, starved year that followed: an actor only barely. I would sooner say trigger, and I think the parlor game of tracing a novel and a bicycle to it is precisely what makes my friend distrust the whole idea.

— The boll weevil reaching the cotton South: a large actor — though everything it did ran through credit, sharecropping and tenancy, and with different arrangements the same insect would have done far less. I still call it an actor.

— The tsetse fly and the cattle it killed, offered as an explanation for the shape of precolonial African states and economies: I balk, even though the mechanism is as clean as the cholera one.

— The germs that devastated the Americas after 1492, in the grand story where the animals Eurasians happened to live beside supplied the diseases: I balk in the same way, and yet I do not want to deny that the germs did most of the killing, or to let the conquistadors off.

— The Little Ice Age, invoked for the upheavals of the seventeenth century: a backdrop, not an actor — compatible with too many different outcomes to explain any of them. My friend's reply, which I could not answer, is that "the weather got colder" is no vaguer than "the bacterium arrived."

— "Britain had coal, and that is why the Industrial Revolution happened there": I call this the bad kind as well, although unlike the last one it is perfectly specific.

The case I kept coming back to is the blight in Ireland. I want both verdicts at once: the organism was an actor, and the famine was made by policy, since food went on being exported and relief was made conditional. My friend says the second verdict demotes the organism to an occasion, or worse, to an excuse for the people who shipped the grain.

So: give me one principle, not a menu, with each clause tied to the case that forces it. Say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and which one I should abandon and why. Apply it to the two cases we never got to — the mosquitoes that defeated the French canal attempt and were then controlled by the Americans, and the drought of the Dust Bowl years. And tell me whether "actor" is doing any work beyond "cause": does it matter that germs evolve in response to what we do while volcanoes do not?

Please commit. Take a definite position, tell me which of my verdicts has to go, and say where your own principle is weakest and what would change your mind. If you think the way I have set the whole question up is wrong, say so and give me the replacement.
