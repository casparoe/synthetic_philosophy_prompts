I have spent the better part of a decade teaching and writing around van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the requirement, roughly, that your present credence in a proposition, conditional on the supposition that your later credence in that same proposition will be some value r, ought to equal r — and I have reached the point where I can predict every move in the exchange in advance. Nothing anybody publishes now seems to move anybody. I would like a structural diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I have a hypothesis I want tested rather than flattered.

The dialectic as it currently sits, as I read it. On one side, defenders run the diachronic Dutch book argument: an agent who violates Reflection can be induced to accept a sequence of bets each of which looks fair to her at the moment she accepts it and which jointly guarantee a loss, so Reflection is supposed to stand to diachronic rationality as probabilism stands to synchronic rationality. On the other side, critics produce agents who know they are heading into worse epistemic conditions — they will drink, or be drugged, or simply forget the evidence they now possess — and Reflection appears to instruct such agents to move toward credences they know to be less well-grounded than the ones they already have. The standard defensive move is a proviso: the principle was only ever meant to apply when the agent does not expect to forget, to be systematically misled, or to have her judgment distorted, and when she takes her later self to be evidentially no worse placed than she is now. Critics reply that the proviso is now doing all of the real work, and that the principle is either trivial — little more than conditionalization plus the assumption that one will update by conditionalizing — or false. A third front has opened around self-location: where the agent's later uncertainty concerns where or when she is rather than how the world is, Reflection appears to fail even with the proviso satisfied, and more general principles have been proposed that collapse back into Reflection only in the special case. And there is a fourth position, which I think is under-discussed: that there are no genuinely diachronic requirements of rationality at all, that rationality is a matter of how things stand with an agent at a time, and every apparently diachronic norm is really a synchronic norm about one's present attitudes toward one's own future selves.

My hypothesis is that the Reflection debate is a proxy war. The real dispute, I suspect, is about whether rationality has any genuinely diachronic dimension, and the Dutch book argument is being asked to settle a question about the kind of norm Reflection is supposed to be — a coherence constraint, a substantive epistemic requirement, or merely advice about self-management — that no money-pump argument can settle on its own. A second, quieter disagreement about scope runs underneath: whether forgetting cases and self-locating cases are counterexamples to Reflection or are simply outside its intended domain, which means the two sides are not even counting the same cases as data.

Two cases I have been using in my seminar push on exactly this, and I want to see whether they support the hypothesis or break it. The first: a researcher knows that at noon she will be given a drug that erases the outcome of a certain experiment from her memory, and that at six in the evening she will be handed back the notes she wrote this morning. She knows now that her credence in H will be 0.3 at noon and 0.7 at six. Reflection appears to demand that her present credence be 0.3 and that it be 0.7. Of course the reply is that she should defer to her epistemically best-informed successor — but note what that costs: the principle now needs a clause picking out a successor, and in a life of repeated forgetting with no restoration, or in an indefinitely long future in which information arrives and is then permanently lost, there may be no such successor, or no determinate one. A fission variant makes it worse: if she will have several successors with different credences, deferring to the mean is one option among many and the arithmetic does not select itself. The second case: a scientist who will be taught a new theory next year whose central terms she cannot now formulate, and whose future confidence will attach to propositions she cannot now express. There is then, it seems, no proposition for Reflection to range over at present, and the principle goes silent precisely where one would have wanted guidance — in episodes of radical theory change. That makes me wonder whether Reflection quietly presupposes a fixed space of propositions and a shared conceptual scheme, and is therefore a conservatism masquerading as a coherence requirement.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of this impasse, argued from what each of these positions actually commits itself to: is the disagreement verbal or substantive; is it a disagreement about which cases count as data, about what a Dutch book or accuracy argument can establish, about whether coherence and epistemic requirement come apart, or about a starting intuition concerning the unity of the agent over time that neither side can argue for; is a hidden formal question doing the work; or is the question as posed simply ill-formed? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a theorem about what the Dutch book argument does and does not show, a distinction that separates the no-forgetting condition from the no-misleading-evidence condition, a more general principle, a change of subject — or tell me honestly why no progress should be expected. Please do not survey the literature or adjudicate the principle on its merits; I know the literature. I want to know what kind of disagreement this is.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the question back to me.
