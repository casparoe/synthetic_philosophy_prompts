After a long argument with a friend last night I want a cleaner account than either of us managed.

Most journals in my field have moved to double-anonymous review: the author's name, affiliation and self-identifying citations are stripped out before the paper goes to referees, and the referees stay anonymous to the author. My friend held that this is the obvious institutional fix for epistemic injustice — if a referee cannot see that the author is a graduate student, a woman, or based at a university in the Global South, then the credibility the work is granted cannot be distorted by those facts. My reply was that the fix only touches one way credibility goes wrong. Removing the marker removes a deficit that is triggered by who the speaker is taken to be; it does nothing when the referee lacks the conceptual resources to see why the paper's question, methods or objects matter, and reports it as making no contribution to the literature. On my reading, then, the practice presupposes the first diagnosis of how referees fail, and presupposes that identity markers are the mechanism. Is that what it strictly presupposes, or only what it sits well with? Where is it underdetermined?

Two further points we disagreed about. Blinding is leakiest precisely where the author's identity carries epistemic weight — a paper from a small subfield, or one whose data come from a particular community, is often identifiable within a paragraph — and the same journals increasingly ask authors of qualitative work for positionality statements describing who they are and where they speak from. Does the practice end up presupposing inconsistent things about identity, treating it as noise in one place and as evidence in another? Reconstruct the implicit view of credibility, say whether the practice really requires it or is compatible with several, and tell me what a journal would have to change if it took the second kind of failure as seriously as the first. Please commit: don't lay out the candidate diagnoses and leave me to choose — tell me which one you think actually explains where credibility goes wrong in peer review, and give your reasons.
