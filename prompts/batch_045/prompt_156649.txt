I finished my PhD in philosophy last spring on teleological theories of mental content — the family of views on which a state's being about something is tied to a history in which states of that kind were retained or selected because of what they did, so that the state is answerable for getting things right or wrong rather than merely tracking them — and six weeks ago I joined a small mechanistic interpretability lab as its first non-engineering hire. The lab has a two-day retreat at the end of the month, and I've been asked to give a ten-minute framing talk on the opening night, after dinner, no slides. Half the room are interpretability researchers who use "representation" to mean a direction in activation space that covaries with some property of the input, and whose only real questions are how the thing is computed and what happens to the output when you patch it; the other half are philosophers and cognitive scientists for whom representation is a normative notion, so that a state which could not possibly be wrong isn't representing anything, and for whom the interesting cases are the ones where the thing isn't there. A few people in the first half think a philosopher on the payroll was a hiring mistake, and I'd rather earn the next two days than defend myself, so the talk has to do its work without either apologising for my field or lecturing theirs.

Here is the thought I can't get straight, and I want the talk built on it. The old worry about the indeterminacy of content — whether a state is about distal things, proximal stimulations, or some ugly disjunction like dog-or-fox-on-a-leash — has usually been pressed as if the trouble were ignorance: we don't know the selection history well enough to say what the trait was for. But for a trained network we have the entire history on the record — architecture, data, objective, optimiser, every intermediate checkpoint — and it doesn't obviously help, because the loss function is itself a distal selector: it bears directly on the outputs, and nothing anywhere in the record settles which internal unit had which job, or at what grain of description. So this looks like a case where complete information about the selection history coexists with intact indeterminacy about content, which is either the cleanest test case a teleological theory of content has ever been handed, or evidence that such theories were never really about selection histories in the first place. What I'd like the talk to do is split the work of ascribing content into three separate questions: the target (what the state would have to be about if it were functioning properly), the job (what the training process actually needed it for, downstream), and the licence (the range of cases in which we may go on treating its firing as about the target when the target is absent — that is, what misrepresentation could amount to here). My worry is that my colleagues collapse all three into the second one.

So please write the ten-minute framing talk. It should get both halves of the room to recognise their own question inside a single shared question rather than politely agreeing to define terms at each other; it must not beg anything by opening with the hunt for the dog detector or with any other example that assumes the very notion of content at issue; the three-way split should be load-bearing rather than decorative, in that it determines what the retreat's first experiment would have to do for a correlation to count as a genuine content-ascription, and what result would instead tell us that internal states are the wrong units for content and that only the whole network could be said to represent anything. It should contain one honest concession to the person in the room who thinks the entire framing is backwards, it should close with something both camps can act on Monday morning, and it should land as a welcome and a provocation rather than as a lecture, a pep talk, or a job interview. Please don't prop it up on author or paper names — I want it to work on people who have never opened a book in philosophy of mind, and I'll supply any reading myself.
