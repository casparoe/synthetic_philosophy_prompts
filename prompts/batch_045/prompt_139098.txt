I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a hospital in a jurisdiction that recently extended assisted dying to competent adults whose suffering is unbearable and without reasonable prospect of improvement, explicitly including suffering that is psychiatric rather than terminal. Our last meeting broke down over a single case: a forty-four-year-old patient they referred to only as M., eleven years into severe treatment-resistant depression, no significant physical illness, who has reiterated the same request at every assessment for eighteen months, has declined the remaining options on offer, and whose clinicians agree she meets every capacity requirement. Two colleagues — I'll call them Nadia and Owen — went at it for the better part of an hour, and I was asked to write up where things landed; I honestly could not tell who had the better of it. Their exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it: (1) Nadia said the justification for assisted dying is respect for the person's own verdict on her own life, and that excluding psychiatric suffering on the grounds that the illness might be distorting that verdict just is substituting our judgment for hers; it treats depressed patients as less than full agents precisely when they are asking to be treated as one. (2) Owen replied that the wish to die is here a symptom rather than an exercise of agency — that the judgment that one's life is not worth continuing is exactly what this illness produces — so honouring it is not respecting agency but collaborating with the disease. (3) Nadia countered that every preference has a causal history, including the preference to go on living, and that if causal etiology discredits a wish it discredits that one too; so the asymmetry is arbitrary. (4) Owen said there is a difference between causal influence and distorting influence: in depression we have independent grounds for thinking the evaluation is off — a systematically pessimistic assessment of one's own future that, by hypothesis, is not tracking anything — whereas nothing parallel makes the wish to live a distortion, which is why the asymmetry is grounded rather than arbitrary. (5) Nadia answered that even granting the distortion there is no correction available: the treatment has failed, the condition is permanent, there is no later standpoint from which the wish would be revised, so "wait until you are better" is not on the table, and any reaffirmation next year can be dismissed as still distorted — a loop in which the patient can never qualify. (6) Owen said the relevant test is not a future standpoint but continuity with the patient's longer-standing values — what she cared about and said before she became ill — which is checkable, so the loop is not real. (7) Nadia replied that this makes permissibility depend on an accident of biography: a patient whose depression began in adolescence and never lifted has no pre-illness self to appeal to and is excluded for a fact about timing, and it turns the assessment into, in her words, "an archaeology of the authentic self" that clinicians have no training or warrant to perform. (8) Owen's closing move was that the dispute is not really about agency at all but about which error a practice should tolerate — refusing a suffering person the death she wants, versus giving death to someone who, a year later, would have been glad to be alive — and that the second error is worse and, as he put it, unauditable: the one person who could tell us we got it wrong is the person we killed, so medicine's ordinary feedback loop cannot operate here. He called this the missing-feedback problem. (9) Nadia said the same is true of any decision made now for a later self — we honour advance refusals of life-sustaining treatment by people who will never report back, and that isn't treated as decisive — and that "we cannot learn from our mistakes" is really an argument against ever acting in irreversible cases, which would rule out a great deal more than assisted dying.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the nine steps, I'd like a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification, and where a move goes wrong I'd like you to say exactly what it assumes or smuggles in — for instance whether the step from "all preferences have causes" to "no preference is distorted" is available to Nadia, whether Owen's causal-versus-distorting distinction is doing the work he needs or just relabels the problem, and whether his missing-feedback point is genuinely specific to assisted dying or applies equally to the irreversible interventions Nadia points to. Then say who is more right overall, and — the part I found hardest — what both of them are missing: whether there is a distinction between the permissibility of a single act and the design of a practice with real error rates that neither of them draws, whether the authenticity test they are fighting over is even the right kind of test, and whether there is a third option neither considers. Please engage with the arguments as actually made rather than restating the standard positions on this topic; I can read those for myself, and the committee needs something that speaks to this exchange specifically. A page or so would be ideal.
