Hey — I'm taking a seminar this term that's cross-listed between classics and philosophy, something like "lives and how to learn from them", and I've ended up defending a position I didn't expect to defend, which now makes me suspicious of how exposed it actually is.

The position is that reading lives — real biographies of real people, with Plutarch's Parallel Lives as the paradigm case — can be a genuine route to moral knowledge, not just inspiration or a mnemonic, and that Plutarch's own methodological remark is what saves the practice from the obvious objection. (The remark I mean is the one near the start of the Life of Alexander, I think it's the Alexander — my TA mentioned it in section — where he says he's writing lives rather than histories, and that a stray phrase or a small joke will often reveal someone's character better than a set-piece battle with thousands of dead. I'm paraphrasing from memory, don't hold me to the wording.) The obvious objection is that biography is systematically distorted in the subject's favour, and that this gets much worse in hagiography, in the lives of saints, where whole episodes seem built to display a virtue. My version of the reply, which I've been developing with a friend after class, reframes the whole thing: moral learning from a life isn't like deriving a rule from a principle, it's like calibrating a perceptual capacity on labelled examples. The life is the training data; the verdicts — noble, shabby, cruel, generous — are the labels. On that picture hagiography is label noise, and label noise isn't automatically fatal, for two reasons: noise that is uncorrelated with the features you're training on averages out over enough cases, and the features Plutarch actually tells you to attend to (the offhand remark, the small expenditure, how someone treats a servant or takes a defeat) are exactly the ones a panegyrist has least incentive to invent or polish, so they are closer to honest labels than the famous deeds are. That's why, on my view, the small details are doing real epistemic work rather than just being vivid.

What I want from you is the map of how exposed this is. Tell me what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which defeaters would a committed defender of my position have to concede as really counting against them, and which would they be entitled to dismiss as resting on a picture they've already rejected. I'm especially interested in where the argument stands or falls with other things — is it hostage to the historical reliability of the anecdotes, or does the calibration story survive even if the anecdotes are mostly invented? Does it stand or fall with moral perception being trainable at all, rather than just being the application of principles you already have? I can see two things that worry me most and I'd like you to push hard on both: first, if the labels are in fact supplied by the reader's own antecedent moral knowledge, then my account seems to collapse back into the principles-first picture it was supposed to replace, and the biography becomes decorative; second, if what's going on is really pattern-matching on examples, then the view seems committed to saying that a well-made *novel* would teach you just as much as a real life, which I suspect is a consequence I should either embrace or be embarrassed by, since Plutarch's whole practice seems to depend on these people having actually lived.
