I teach a second-year Philosophy of Mind seminar: 16 students, mixed philosophy, cognitive science, and computer science majors, one 75-minute session. It is the first meeting on the Chinese Room argument, and I want them to run into it before they read it — so the argument's name, its author's name, and the vocabulary of syntax, semantics, and "strong AI" must not appear anywhere in the session until the debrief is finished. What I want them to come away having felt, not been told, is the standoff between the view from inside the room (I am moving shapes around according to a book and nothing here is understood) and the view from outside it (there is a fluent speaker in there), and the fact that each side's confidence is insulated from the other side's evidence.

Design a runnable activity for this. Things I want built in:

1. Something actually has to be done. Not a described case discussed from the outside. Students should execute a lookup procedure in an unfamiliar script and successfully answer questions posed in it — small groups, paper materials, no screens, learnable in minutes by someone with no linguistics background — so that some students get the inside view in their own case while others only ever see the outputs. Assign roles and information restrictions so that roughly half the room knows only what an outside observer could know and half only what the operator knows, and keep them apart until a fixed moment.

2. A third round that I have not seen staged anywhere, and which is the main reason I am asking. Put the room itself on the stand. Feed the operator, through the same procedure, questions about what it was like to answer the earlier questions — which ones it understood, which struck it as ambiguous, what it was unsure about — and let the procedure emit a fluent, convincing first-person report. Students are told the report came out of the same mechanism. The question for the room is whether such a report is evidence of anything, and of what: it seems mad to ignore it, and it seems mad to treat it as data. I want both pulls felt before either is resolved.

3. A stipulation that quietly removes the easy escapes. In my version the rulebook was not written by anyone who understands the language. It was produced by an automated procedure that reverse-engineered the rules from a large body of usage, and the causal chain runs back through that corpus to people who did understand it, but no stage in the chain understood the language at the time it acted. So the "the programmers understand" and "the designers put the understanding in" replies are blocked, while the system reply and its relatives stay open. I would like students to generate the escape attempts themselves and discover which ones the setup has closed off, with the facilitator holding the blocking stipulations back until someone proposes the move they block — but tell me if you think that is too cute to work in practice.

4. Small stakes, so that confidence is revealed rather than asserted. A prediction market, chips, a pooled prize, or something similar: students commit to how the room will perform or to what they think is going on, and the commitments get checked against what actually happens. No real money.

Give me the rules and materials in enough detail to run tomorrow, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, the exact framing I read out at the start (containing no theory), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the argument — including which classical reply each student escape attempt corresponds to, once the names are allowed.

Two more things. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time: I expect at least one student who has already heard of the argument, at least one who refuses the premise that the procedure yields no understanding, and a script task that will be either too hard or too easy. Tell me how to handle each. Second, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief.

And I want you to be self-critical about one objection in particular: the inside view is vivid here only because we staged a procedure that is transparently mechanical, whereas the case that matters is one where the procedure is not transparently mechanical. If you think the activity manufactures the very intuition it then asks students to trust, say so, say what would change your mind about that, and describe what you would run instead.
