**Request: a diagnostic instrument for the luck-egalitarian family (decision tree + sorting quiz + scoring key)**

I teach a ten-session graduate seminar on distributive justice to a mixed room of philosophy MA students and public-policy professionals. Most of them arrive able to use phrases like brute luck, option luck, and expensive tastes, but unable to say which version of the view they actually hold, because the versions come apart only in cases they have not been asked about. I want a text-only diagnostic instrument that sorts them by their verdicts on cases rather than by the label they attach to themselves. Assume a closed society with a functioning tax-and-transfer apparatus and no interesting facts about real political feasibility: every question is about what the apparatus should do, not about what is enactable. Please do not include any bibliography, reading list, citation, author dates, or verbatim quotations; label positions doctrinally (equality of resources, equality of opportunity for welfare, democratic equality, and so on), and where you are unsure whether some combination is actually defended in the literature, mark it unoccupied rather than attributing it. Define any term of art you use on first appearance, since half the room has not read the literature.

**Deliverable.** Three parts.

1. A decision tree, written as nested numbered nodes rather than as a diagram, that starts from a commitment nearly everyone in the room already accepts — that an inequality traceable to factors a person had no hand in is pro tanto unjust — and terminates in between eight and twelve leaves. Each branch node must be a single sentence stating the claim that separates the views below it, phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing where it leads. Each leaf carries: the doctrinal name of the position; one sentence stating its most awkward commitment; and a note on whether anyone in fact holds it.
2. A ten-item sorting quiz. Each item is a concrete case or claim with two or three answer options, and each option describes what the apparatus should do (compensate in full, compensate partially, compensate not at all, pool the risk in advance, and so on) rather than which principle the reader endorses. No item may ask the reader to self-classify.
3. A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the leaves, plus a short diagnostic appendix.

**The angle I want the instrument built around.** Do not lead with the currency question (resources versus welfare versus capabilities). Put the *timing* question near the top: does the principle regulate the structure of chances people face before the outcome is known — a hypothetical insurance or pooling device fixed ex ante — or does it regulate outcomes ex post, after the lottery has resolved? I want the tree to group together views that differ on currency but agree on timing, because that is the axis my students are most confused about; the currency question may appear, but only below the timing split. Below that, in whatever order you think does the most work, the tree should contain at least the following separating questions.

- The *underwriter test*, which I want introduced as a new device. Say that a risk counts as genuinely the agent's own only if, at the moment of action, some competitive insurer would have been willing to underwrite it at a finite premium; where no such insurer exists — because the risk is unmeasurable, because exposure to it is not avoidable by any available action, or because the agent lacks the standing or wealth to buy cover — treat the resulting loss as brute luck however voluntarily it was incurred. Give the branch three options: accept the test; accept it only in a weakened counterfactual form (an insurer would have written the policy had markets been complete and the agent solvent); or reject it as irrelevant to what responsibility requires. Tell me which of your leaves each option feeds, and whether the intersection of ex ante pooling with the unweakened test is occupied.
- The stopping point for the regress of causes. Choices are themselves shaped by circumstances; ask where, if anywhere, the tracing back halts, with options at least including reasons-responsiveness being sufficient, the agent's identification with or endorsement of the relevant desire being required, and there being no principled stopping point at all. The last option must be allowed to terminate in a leaf that abandons responsibility-sensitivity entirely, and that leaf should be named.
- What is owed to the imprudent who end up destitute: nothing beyond whatever a floor independent of the principle provides; something, but at a level that leaves them worse off than the prudent unlucky; or a floor that makes the imprudence costless to them. This is where the standard harshness objection bites, and I want it to be a live fork rather than a footnote.
- The leveling-down probe: a case in which the only available way to remove a brute-luck advantage makes the advantaged person worse off and benefits no one. Require each leaf to say whether it is committed to doing this, permitted to decline, or required to decline — and note any leaf whose answer is unstable across variants of the case.
- Expensive tastes and self-cultivated needs, including at least one variant where the person deliberately trained the taste and one where they did not choose it but could now attenuate it at modest cost.

**Cases the quiz must contain.** At least three items should form a dose–response gradient over a single activity: same action, same expected value, but varying ex ante probability and severity of the bad outcome, so that the reader has to locate, if anywhere, the point at which their verdict flips from personal responsibility to collective sharing. Also required: one item in which the bad outcome lands on a third party — a dependent, a bystander, a child born as a result — rather than on the agent who took the risk; one item where the underwriter test fails; one item where the agent was culpably ignorant of a risk they could have discovered at negligible cost; and one leveling-down item. Otherwise invent the cases.

**What the appendix must contain.** First, at least three unoccupied regions of the logical space your questions define, each with a one-sentence diagnosis of why nobody is there: incoherent, unattractive, unexplored, or merely unlabeled. Second, at least two pairs of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate by case verdicts — where the views differ only in justification, or coincide whenever some background condition holds — flagged as such rather than forced apart by stipulation; the hypothetical-insurance view and the type-based partition are likely candidates, and I would like you to say what extra case, if any, would pry them apart. Third, which items in the quiz are pivot items that do most of the sorting and which are merely confirmatory. Fourth, a note on which positions your starting commitment excludes by stipulation rather than by argument, so my students can see what the instrument is not testing.
