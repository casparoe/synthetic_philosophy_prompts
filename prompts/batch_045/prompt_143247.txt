I have spent the last eighteen months as one of the four people who keep the ledger of a mutual-credit network in the town where I live, some twenty miles from Cloyne, and in that time I have read your *Querist* more attentively than anything I was made to read at university. My own balance stands at minus thirty-one hours, which is to say I owe the network a month of work I have not yet performed. I would like your counsel on a live dispute among us, and I would like it from you rather than about you.

Our machine, described as plainly as I can. Its unit is the hour, and every hour counts as one hour, whether it is a plumber's or a childminder's; no member's hour is rated above another's. There is no issuer: each account begins at zero, to spend is to go into debit, to earn is to go into credit, and all balances sum to nothing. We hold a small fund of the state's money only to pay taxes. No account may pass forty hours in either direction; a committee of five enforces this and may expel a member; and every member can see every other member's balance. After a year, fourteen of us held large credits — the largest above three hundred hours — and spent almost nothing, while some thirty sat hard against the debit limit and could take on no more work, and the thing largely stopped moving. We then imposed a charge of one percent a month on any credit balance above twenty hours, paid into a common fund that rents a shared workshop and a van. Circulation recovered; three of our most-earning members left, saying that we now fine a person for being wanted. Two proposals are before us: to create two hundred hours out of nothing and lend them to a bakery for a new oven, the baker working them off over three years — credit against labour still to come rather than labour already done — and to let a national supermarket chain accept our hours at thirty percent off, with a reserve of state money so that any member may convert hours into it on demand.

The terms on which I ask. You are George Berkeley, Bishop of Cloyne, in the middle of publishing the *Querist*, and you know nothing after your own time except what I have told you here; you may ask me two questions about how the network works, but not instead of giving a verdict. You hold, and have lately published, that the four elements and man's labour therein are the true source of wealth; that "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter"; that "Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?"; and that wealth is proportioned to industry and this to the circulation of credit, "be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever." You also think each member should have power according to his just pretensions and industry, that drones may be expelled, and that a people's industry follows its will, its customs and the fashion of its betters. What you will not surrender is this: money is a ticket, not wealth; and a ticket is honoured only where there is a real power to command industry behind it and a moral and civil order that makes promises hold. Do not cite or allude to economists who come after you; there are none in your world. Answer in four to six hundred words, in the first person, conceding what you would concede and no more, and close with one question you need answered. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening claim: by your own test our ledger is money and the coin in our pockets is the idol, and the two things that have kept our money alive are a tax laid upon saving and a committee that tells grown tradespeople how much they may promise. Judge the three measures now before us — the rule that every hour is worth every other hour, the monthly charge upon idle balances, and the proposal to create two hundred hours for the bakery's oven — and tell us which your principles bless and which they condemn. And before you answer, account for your own nineteenth query, which asks "Whether the bulk of our Irish natives are not kept from thriving, by that cynical content in dirt and beggary which they possess to a degree beyond any other people in Christendom?" The people in my ledger are those people's descendants, and they are not idle: they are pinned at the debit limit, unable to find takers. Do not begin by telling us that we want industry.
