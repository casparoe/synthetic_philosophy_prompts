I sit on the privacy review board of a large hospital system, and for several years I have been noticing a family of complaints and incidents that our intake categories handle badly. Our forms assume a subject who has data, a holder who holds it, and a flow between them that is either authorised or not. The cases that trouble me do not fit that shape, and I want to know whether there is an established concept for what they have in common, or for the differences between them.

(a) A man sends a consumer ancestry kit to a testing company. His results come back and disclose to his sister — who submitted nothing, is not a customer, and has no relationship with the company of any kind — a fact about her own parentage that she did not want and had not sought. Nothing about her entered the company's systems. The fact was derived from a record that was, in its own terms, entirely about someone else.

(b) Four of a woman's friends install an app that uploads contact lists alongside coarse time-stamped location. From the pattern of co-presence alone the firm can assign a home address, a workplace, and a weekly routine to that woman, and it does. She has never used the app, has no account, has passed through no consent flow, and has no setting to change.

(c) A fitness platform publishes route heat-maps that it describes as aggregate. In a village of roughly two hundred residents the aggregate makes visible the fact that one identifiable person leaves the house before dawn each day and walks a fixed loop. No individual record was disclosed; the visibility is a property of how thin the population is.

(d) A household's grocery and pharmacy purchases feed a model that flags the household as likely to contain a pregnancy, and the flag triggers marketing and a call from a care-management programme. The woman in question is not pregnant. If privacy is a matter of facts about a person getting out, this is not a privacy matter at all — nothing true was learned about anyone — yet the experience of the flagged person and the shape of the downstream effects are indistinguishable from the cases where the inference happens to be correct.

There is one further feature that recurs across all four and that I suspect is the heart of it. In each case, the person affected cannot protect herself by her own restraint. Her not uploading her own genome stops protecting her once enough relatives have uploaded; her not installing the app does not help if enough of her friends do; her caution about her route is undone by how few of her neighbours use the platform. The protective value of her own restraint is a function of other people's choices, and it erodes as theirs are made.

The terms I have run across seem near but not right. Group privacy, as I have seen it used, concerns a collective that holds a right of its own against outsiders, not one individual whose exposure is a by-product of another's. Contextual integrity concerns the appropriateness of flows of *my* information given the norms of a context, but in (b) the flow is perfectly appropriate with respect to everyone who did share, and the person harmed is not a party to it at all. And the mosaic or aggregation worry is about many scraps concerning *one* person accruing to a single holder, which is not what happens in (a) or (b).

So I am asking for more than a label:

1. Is there an established concept or literature for this, and if several concepts are nearby, which fits and why do the others fall short?

2. Is it one phenomenon or several? I would like a taxonomy with reasons: I suspect (a) and (b) differ in kind — a fact jointly held versus a fact inferred statistically — and I suspect (d) may not be a privacy case at all. Tell me if those suspicions are wrong.

3. Which accounts of privacy's value predict a wrong here, and which go silent? Control-based accounts seem to have nothing to say, since the affected person never had control to lose; access and accessibility accounts seem to do better in (a) and worse in (c); contextual accounts need a story about who counts as a party to the context. Is this best described as a new kind of privacy harm, an old harm reached by a new route, or a harm of some other kind altogether? Please argue the alternatives rather than assert one.

4. Where are the edges? Must the derived fact be true? Must there be a pre-existing relationship, or is bare statistical proximity enough? Does the concept extend to firms or states as affected parties? Does it require identifiability, or does membership in a flagged class suffice? And when two people have incompatible interests in one record — my sibling wants to upload, I do not — what is the structure of the conflict: a veto in either party, a majority among the exposed, a balancing, or something else?

5. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it precisely, and specify a case that lies just outside it, so I can tell whether I have drawn the boundary in the right place.

I am working from a library copy of an anthology with no internet access, so I cannot chase references; please make the case for the carving directly instead of pointing me at papers I cannot retrieve.
