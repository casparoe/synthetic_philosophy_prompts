I am rewriting a problem set for a second-year philosophy of language course, and I want a case that does not resolve itself in the first ten seconds. Last year I asked whether a promise the promisee never hears is really a promise, and every student came back with the same answer — nothing was received, so nothing happened — which left me with nothing worth grading. So I want a scenario where the untutored verdict genuinely splits, and where the split can be traced to one structural feature of the case rather than to the reader's temperament.

What I need is this: one paragraph, set somewhere ordinary — a hospital ward, a clinic's on-call rota, a university department, a shared workshop — in which a person addresses an explicit commissive to a named colleague, something like I promise I'll cover your Saturday clinic, and in which the utterance is recorded and acted on by an institutional channel while the addressee themselves never receives it. A shift-swap board, a ticketing system, an auto-responder, a shared log, a pager gateway: nothing more exotic than that. The speaker is sincere, has the standing to make the promise, is under no duress, is not joking, quoting, or writing fiction, and the message is unambiguous and in a language the addressee reads. Nothing may turn on the addressee failing to understand it, on the speaker lacking authority, on the message never having left the outbox, or on the speaker's later change of heart. Ideally there is some mild practical upshot as well — someone is short-staffed, or out of pocket, or aggrieved — so that whether an obligation exists is not a purely academic question.

Then do the check, framework by framework rather than as free-ranging discussion:

1. Austin's felicity framework. I take Austin to require that uptake be secured for an illocutionary act to be happily performed, though I am not confident where that requirement sits in his taxonomy — say whether this is a misfire or something else, place it in one of his categories with a reason, and say explicitly why it is not an abuse, given that the speaker is sincere. Verdict: was a promise made, yes or no?
2. Searle's constitutive-rule analysis. Walk through the propositional content, preparatory, sincerity, and essential conditions one at a time, say which are met, and give the verdict. I read Searle as holding that uptake, or what he calls the illocutionary effect, is needed for communication to succeed but not for the act itself to have been performed; if you think I have misread him, say so and say what follows for the case.
3. A Gricean, intention-recognition account of the kind on which a speaker performs an illocutionary act only if the audience recognizes the speaker's reflexive intention in producing it. Distinguish expressing an intention from communicating it, say which happened here, give the verdict, and say what normative residue, if any, is left behind when the act fails.
4. A practice-based or scorekeeping view on which obligations are what the community's practice of attributing commitments makes them, so that what matters is the public standing of the utterance within the practice rather than any individual's psychology or any single hearer's uptake. Give the verdict and say what has to be true of the practice for you to reach it.

Close with three things. First, name the single structural feature of the case that is doing all the work — my suspicion is that it is the gap between being registered by a practice and being received by a person, but argue for whatever it actually is — and identify the one sentence in your scenario that the whole case turns on. Second, run two ablation tests: remove the institutional channel entirely, so that the message sits unread in an inbox and nothing else changes; then put the channel back and have the addressee read the message the next morning. Say in each case which frameworks change their verdict and which hold steady, and whether the case collapses back into a familiar and uninteresting dispute about unheard utterances. Third, for each framework that comes out looking wrong or unhelpful here, give its defender's strongest line of attack on your scenario — the best thing an Austinian or a Searlean could say to make the case look rigged, rather than a dismissal. If you think some combination of the verdicts I have asked for cannot be jointly satisfied in a realistic case, say which one and why instead of forcing it.
