Take the night the Titanic's lifeboats were loaded in 1912 and read it through Garrett Hardin's lifeboat ethics. His setup, as I remember it: a boat built for 60, fifty aboard, a hundred swimmers, three options -- take everyone aboard and swamp it, take ten and spend the safety factor, take nobody and then guard against boarding parties -- with the two questions he thinks sink the middle option: which ten, and what happens when the other ninety try to climb in. What I want is a careful mapping. Who exactly is the lifeboat here, and who are the fifty -- because the people in the seats are not the people who decided how many seats there were, and the swimmers had paid for the same ticket, so Hardin's inside/outside split doesn't obviously land on anything. What were the officers' actual options that night: seats, time, davits, whether loading to the rating was even safe, what a full boat does in cold water. Then the part I keep snagging on: no lifeboat sank from being overloaded. They rowed away with empty seats, so the real failure mode is one Hardin has no name for -- call it the half-empty-boat problem, capacity that existed and went unused. His own safety-factor point, the one about never loading to the rated number, seems to predict under-filling once you're on a real boat whose gear you don't trust, which is a use of it he nevber intended. Where is the theory silent? On allocation among the people already aboard, which is most of what was actually being decided that night, given women-and-children-first and the steep class and sex gradient in who got off. And the bit I care about most: does the case leave lifeboat ethics standing as a metaphor about rich nations and strangers in the water, or does taking it literally expose that the move doing all the work is positing a settled manifest plus outsiders, so that a distributive question gets to look like self-defence? Numbers as I remember them: about 2,200 aboard, seats for roughly half, around 700 picked up, survival tracked class and sex hard -- say if any of that's wrong.
