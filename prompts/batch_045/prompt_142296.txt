I am putting together a small interdisciplinary research programme on the social construction of kinds — race, gender, money, and the classificatory categories used in medicine and official statistics — and I have to decide what to fund. The remit is roughly four funded projects over three years, each pairing a philosopher with one empirical collaborator: a social scientist, a clinician, or someone who actually builds and maintains classification systems. So I do not want a survey of the territory. I want a defended argument for which four lines of work deserve those slots, in what order, and — this matters as much — which candidate questions are overrated or rest on a confusion and should be struck from the list.

Let me set out how I currently carve up the space, so that you can tell me which of my distinctions are doing real work and which are merely relabelling.

First, the split between *anchoring* questions — what, if anything, fixes the extension and the persistence conditions of a kind term — and *consequence* questions — what follows, normatively and institutionally, once a kind is up and running. I suspect much of the heat in these debates comes from people answering one while being read as answering the other, but I am not sure the split survives scrutiny for kinds like money, where the kind's existence seems to consist in its being treated as existing.

Second, what I think of as the *dual-role* problem. Nearly every kind on my list has to serve two masters: it must be usable as an explanatory or evidential category (in epidemiology, in economics, in clinical prediction) and it must be usable as a practical category for law, policy, and self-description. Stable, legible, demographically comparable categories seem to be what the second role demands; fine-grained, causally responsive categories seem to be what the first demands. Is there a principled way of trading these off, or is the tension itself the interesting result?

Third, *classificatory feedback*. Applications of a kind to a population typically change the population: some categories are self-confirming under use (creditworthiness as computed by a scoring model), some are self-eroding (diagnostic categories that widen as they are applied), and some are straightforwardly self-defeating (a category whose application destroys the phenomenon classified, as with a run on a bank). Is there a general account of when feedback discredits a kind, when it merely complicates it, and when it is exactly what the kind is *for*? And does the answer differ between race and gender on the one hand and money on the other?

Fourth, the *transplant* problem: what happens when a kind built inside one institutional setting is exported into another — clinical categories of gender into legal gender recognition, census categories devised in one country into the statistics of another.

Fifth, *deference design*: who should hold authority to revise a kind's application conditions, and how the claims of affected populations, of technical experts, and of institutional stability are to be weighed.

Give me your ranking of what to fund, and the reasoning. State the criteria you are using — how much other work depends on the answer, whether a philosopher working with one empirical collaborator can realistically move it in three years, how much of it is already being done under some other description, what difference the answer would make to anyone outside the seminar room — and be explicit about the trade-offs between them. Tell me which of my five you would drop, and what I have failed to include. Finally, tell me what would count as evidence, over the three years, that you had ranked something too high or too low. Assume no access to a library or to current work in progress: argue from the structure of the problems rather than from who has said what about them.
