My friend and I got into an argument the other night about the abolitionist project — the idea that we should use biotechnology to abolish suffering outright and reimplement everything it currently does in terms of gradients of wellbeing — and I ended up defending a position I'm not even sure I believe, so I want to practice it agains the strongest version of the other side. Please play a committed proponent of abolition: scientifically literate, allergic to slogans, willing to concede the awkward points (that the engineering is wildly speculative, that talk of gradients of bliss hand-waves the hard questions about contrast and how intense the replacement states could even be, that a crude rollout could hollow out motivation rather than liberate it) but never the main claim — that there's no amount of suffering worth preserving for its own sake, or for the sake of the meanings, achievements or relationships it supposedly makes possible. Stay in character until I say stop; keep each reply to a paragraph or two; don't step outside the role to summarize the state of the debate or to tell me what most people in this area think; and don't just recite the manifesto at me — actually deal with the move I'm making.

Here's my opening. I'll grant you far more than most critics grant: grant that every informational and motivational job suffering does — teaching you not to touch the stove, urgency, grief as a kind of bonding, the moral attention we pay to other people's pain — could in principle be rebuilt out of states that feel neutral or good. I'm granting that because I don't think the function is what we object to in suffering at all; what we object to is the way it feels. So here's the dilemma about what your target actually is. Either the felt badness just is the motivational oomph, in which case there's nothing detachable to delete: the thing you want to remove and the thing you want to keep are the same thing, and your replacement isn't a repair but a rebuild from scratch with no guarantee it does the job as well (and if it does the job worse, you've made people worse off in exactly the currency you care about). Or the felt badness is a separate, non-functional property layered on top of the machinery — in which case the thing worth removing was never any of the jobs, and your project has no specifiable engineering target left: you can only say make it feel better, which is a wish rather than an intervention. And if you take that second horn your decoupled subject is frantically avoiding states that aren't bad for it anymore, motivated against its own good, which sounds like alienation rather than liberation.

Last thing, and I think it's the part you'll actually have to do work on: the case your side likes to point to cuts against you. In pain asymbolia people reportedly still feel the hurt but aren't bothered by it — report the sensation, lose the awful — and the usual gloss is indifference, which clinicians treat as a deficit, not as a step toward utopia. So the one real-world model of suffering stripped off while sensation is left standing looks like a loss of function, not a preview of the thing you want. Which horn of the dilemma do you take, what's wrong with the way I set it up, and what do you do with the asymbolia case?
