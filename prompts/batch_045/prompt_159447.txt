My study group did the lottery paradox and the preface paradox in the same session last week and I went home convinced they are the same problem in two costumes, then managed to argue myself into a conclusion I obviously don't accept and can't find the exit from. Here's how I got there. The two standard versions look disanalogous: in the lottery you *know* some ticket wins (the rules guarantee it, probability 1), while in the preface the author only has a statistic — people in her field get things wrong sometimes — for thinking one of her claims is false. But I invented a bridge case to close the gap: a "leaky lottery" of a million tickets run by a machine that has a 4.9% chance of malfunctioning and selecting nobody. Then for each ticket, P(it loses) is about 0.999999, P(somebody wins) is 0.951, and P(nobody wins) is 0.049 — which are basically the preface's numbers, since 300 claims you're each 0.99 confident in give P(all true) ≈ 0.049 and P(at least one false) ≈ 0.951. On that way of seeing it the pure lottery is just the far end of a continuum: the preface claim goes to probability 1 and the errors become perfectly anti-correlated (exactly one winner instead of scattered ones). I'm attached to this bridge case because I made it up, but tell me if it's smuggling something.

The argument:

(1) The two paradoxes are one phenomenon. For each member of a set S you're justified in believing it (your credence clears whatever threshold outright belief requires), and you're also justified in believing that not all of them are true. Number of claims, error rate, and how the errors are correlated are just parameter settings, so one fix has to cover both cases.

(2) The fix I like: outright belief is credence above a threshold, thresholds don't survive conjoining, so justified belief isn't closed under conjunction — from "justified in believing p" and "justified in believing q" you may not infer "justified in believing p and q." Call this No-Aggregation.

(3) No-Aggregation can't be a special rule for big sets, because nothing in the reason for it turns on how many conjuncts there are: P(p ∧ q) can sit below the threshold while P(p) and P(q) both sit above it. And you can build miniature versions: three claims you're 0.7 confident in, threshold 0.6, independent errors — you believe each (0.7), believe at least one is false (0.657), and don't believe all three (0.343). (I worked out the general condition on the back of an envelope: with n claims, equal credences and independent errors you need t^n < 1 − t, so a two-claim version exists for any threshold under about 0.618 and a three-claim one under about 0.68; if you allow the errors to be anti-correlated you can push it as far as t < n/(n+1). Tell me if I've dropped a decimal somewhere.) So aggregation fails for two beliefs as easily as for three hundred.

(4) But if aggregation fails for two beliefs, no deductive argument with two or more premises can ever give me a justified belief in its conclusion. Reasoning deductively just is moving from the premises taken together; if I'm not justified in taking them together, there's nothing for the inference to transmit. And nearly everything I think I know rests on more than one premise — any historical claim, any experimental result, my plan to leave for the airport at six. So: either almost none of my beliefs are justified, or (2) is false.

(5) The other exits don't look better. Keep aggregation and deny the member beliefs instead, and you get the skeptical result that ordinary strong-but-inconclusive evidence never justifies belief — the author's 300 checked claims and my million unchecked lottery tickets both fail, which is absurd unless you think justification requires certainty. (Though note: that bullet looks cheap in the pure lottery and expensive in the preface, which is exactly why I want the uniform treatment in (1).) Or bite the bullet and say the author is simply permitted to have an inconsistent belief set, since nothing requires global consistency — but then run the same permission on a variant where a referee she trusts tells her specifically that claim 184 is false: by that logic she may keep believing claim 184, because her own evidence for it is exactly as good as her evidence for any other claim, and surely she may not. Yet I can't say what the difference is supposed to be between the spread-out inconsistency and the targeted one, if believing is just a matter of clearing a threshold.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd most like to keep (2), and I'd like to keep the uniform treatment in (1), since those are the bits that got me excited — but if the thing I have to give up is my bridge case, say so plainly. Also: is the conclusion of (4) actually absurd, or is it a bullet that some people bite, and what do they pay for it? Please don't give me a reading list or citations, and don't assume I know what the literature calls any of these positions — plain descriptions only. I want to know why the step I'm rejecting looked fine in the first place, not just that it's bad.
