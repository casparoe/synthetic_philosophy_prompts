I'm doing a decision theory course this term and I've talked myself into a position for my essay that I can't seem to lose, which makes me suspicious of it. The usual money pump story is meant to show that cyclic or intransitive preferences are irrational because they let someone drain your money. My claim is that most versions of that argument cheat: the trader normally needs an informational edge (they know your ordering, they can predict your next move, they get to watch what you do), and being exploitable by someone who knows more than you isn't a defect in your rationality, that's just being out-played. So I've tried to isolate an honest version: a *blind* pump that's a standing public offer anyone can take or leave, say name the option you're currently holding and any other option you'd rather have, and I'll swap them for a penny, offered by someone with no information about you who learns nothing useful from your choices. My thesis is that intransitivity is irrational exactly when it makes you exploitable by a mechanism like that, and that pumps which need a knowledgable adversary, or need to wipe your memory between choices, or only work if you're myopic about your own future choices, show nothing at all.

What I want is what would count against this, ranked fatal to merely awkward. Concretely: does the blind pump really avoid smuggling in information, given that the agent has to consult its own preferences to use it, and is self-knowledge the kind of edge I said shouldn't count? Does the reply that you can see the cycle coming, refuse the third swap, or just prefer the whole sequence rather than each step, kill the argument, or does that reply prove too much? Does exploitability track rationality at all rather than just prudence? What does my view say about the dial case (a thousand settings, each step up is a tiny improvement on the one below it, the top one is agony) and about cyclic preferences that come from regret or from comparing things pairwise on similarity: do I have to call those irrational, and if I'd rather not, what does that cost the thesis? Please also say which of these a defender of my view would have to concede rather than explain away. And be honest about whether I've insulated it: if every apparent counterexample gets blocked by adding that it has to be a mechanism that isn't trading on any failure of yours other than the cycle, is there anything left it could be wrong about, and is that a problem for a view of this kind? Answer in British English please.
