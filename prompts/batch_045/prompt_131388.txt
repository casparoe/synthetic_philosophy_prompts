I'm in a small reading group that has spent about two months on the inefficacy objection — the argument that since my individual flight, my purchase of factory-farmed meat, or my vote makes no difference to any outcome that matters, I have no reason grounded in outcomes to do otherwise — and we have stopped moving in a way I'd like to understand rather than just push past. One member treats the expected-difference reply as decisive and thinks the objection was refuted decades ago: even if my act almost certainly changes nothing, its expected value is the tiny probability of mattering times a very large harm, and that is all the arithmetic requires. A second member thinks that reply is beside the point even when it works: for her, the reason not to buy the meat is that one would then be participating in, profiting from, or failing to do one's share in a collectively imposed harm, and she is unmoved by the observation that no chicken's life goes better if she orders the beans. A third keeps insisting the whole thing is empirical and that we are speculating about supply-chain responses, electoral margins, and emissions trajectories that none of us has measured. Nobody has changed anybody's mind, and I notice that each of them reaches for whichever of the three cases — voting, meat, emissions — best fits their response, and quietly switches cases when pressure is applied.

To get a grip on this I've started sorting inefficacy into three failure modes that I find it useful to keep apart. (i) Imperceptibility: my contribution really does add to the total, but the increment is below any detection or response threshold, so nothing observable differs. (ii) Redundancy or overdetermination: the outcome would have occurred anyway, at the same time and to the same degree, because enough other contributions were already in place — the landslide, the threshold already crossed. (iii) Displacement or substitution: my act changes something real in the short run, but the system reabsorbs it — the unsold unit is discounted and someone else buys it, the supplier adjusts the next order, my emissions are offset by a price change — so the long-run total is unchanged. My suspicion is that a good deal of the stall is a mismatch: the expected-difference reasoning looks compelling only for (i) and (ii), looks question-begging for (iii) because it presupposes the very counterfactual at issue, and is applied to whichever case happens to be on the page, while the participation and share-of-the-burden responses are really addressed to (iii) and get evaluated as if they were competing answers to (i). If that is right, at least part of this is a disagreement under a single name rather than a dispute, and there may also be a further slippage between asking which act has better consequences, asking what can be a reason for an agent to act, and asking what we may blame or praise someone for.

So here is what I'd like: a diagnosis of this impasse rather than a verdict on the inefficacy objection, and a diagnosis that engages the distinction I've drawn — tell me where that three-way taxonomy clarifies things and where it distorts them, whether it collapses on inspection, and whether one of the three modes is doing all the real work in the literature's favorite examples. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or a matter of different questions being answered under one heading; is a hidden empirical question (about just how substitutable individual consumption actually is, or how sharp the thresholds are) carrying more weight than anyone admits; and do the non-consequentialist moves — complicity, fairness in the distribution of a collective burden, the expressive or symbolic quality of one's action — change the subject or answer the original one? Finally, given whatever you diagnose, say honestly what progress would even look like here: a distinction that dissolves one strand, a formal result about aggregation or expected value, an empirical measurement, a reframing away from act-tokens toward roles or policies — or an argument that this is bedrock disagreement about what counts as a moral reason and that no progress of any of those kinds should be expected. Please argue for the diagnosis; I'm not looking for a survey of the positions or for references to go read.
