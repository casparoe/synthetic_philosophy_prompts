I am a novelist, and the pivot of a chapter in my current book is a document rather than a scene: the joint memorandum that the two surviving members of a regional crop-protection board are ordered to co-sign after a season of deadlock. One, a welfare consequentialist, holds that a pest-control regime is to be judged almost entirely by the valenced experience of the individual insects it brings about; the other, an ecological holist, takes the functioning of the agro-ecosystem and the viability of the smallholders who depend on it to be what matters, and treats insect deaths, if at all, as evidence about the state of the system. Their immediate subject is a proposed protocol under which a grower abandons calendar spraying with a broad-spectrum insecticide in favour of two measures: bait stations that attract the pest with a species-specific pheromone and sucrose and deliver a fast-acting neurotoxin, and, later in the season, a mass release of sterilised males of the same species.

Draft the memorandum both of them could sign. Numbered first: the claims they accept together — about what the protocol does to pest numbers, about the counterfactual fate of the insects it kills, about what neither of them is in a position to know. Then a table of the disagreements that survive, each row stated twice, once in the wording the consequentialist would accept for her own view and once in the wording the holist would accept for his, with a flag on any row that turns out to be a dispute about words rather than about the world. Then the cruxes, the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one of them: whether the suffering a control method causes is dominated by the insects it kills outright or by the size of the pest population in later seasons; whether a swifter death can count as a benefit to the animal killed and so partly justify causing it, given how that animal would most likely have died anyway; and how much weight either should give to laboratory measures of time to immobility when both distrust them as a guide to the field. For each crux, give the concrete case, measurement, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each predicts it will show; where they would expect the same result but read its significance differently, say so rather than smoothing it over.
