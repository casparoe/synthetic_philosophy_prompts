Is there a name for this? I keep noticing a pattern in how much credit a story gets, and I'm not satisfied with the couple of words people have offered me for it.

Here's the shape of it. Two people have exactly the same pile of facts in front of them. All of it is already known to both of them — nothing new gets discovered, no new measurement gets taken. Each of them produces a story that fits the facts. The fits are, as far as anyone can tell, equally snug: same number of loose ends, same amount of detail accounted for, same awkward bits left over. But one person built their story by looking at those facts and adjusting it until it fit, and the other built their story for reasons that had nothing to do with those facts and only afterwards noticed that it fit. Everyone involved — including me, including the person who built the tuned story — treats the second one as being in much better shape. And when I try to say out loud why, I end up flailing.

Three cases that I think are the same thing, or two of one and one of another:

1. At work, two analysts are given the same internal spreadsheet of last year's customer cancellations. Both have had access to it for months. One builds a model by fitting curves to that spreadsheet. The other spends her time on support-call transcripts, forms a story about why people cancel, and then checks it against the spreadsheet; it lands about as well as the fitted one. Nobody thinks these two are in the same epistemic position, and I agree — but the data is identical and equally old in both cases.

2. A historian friend has a theory about crop rotation that predicts a very specific up-and-down pattern in tithe records. The records are printed, public, and have been sitting in a county archive for a century; nobody ever tabulated them. She spends three weeks tabulating. The pattern is there — but it could easily not have been, and if it hadn't been, her theory would be dead. This feels like a real test to me even though not one new fact about the world came into it. Is it the same phenomenon as case 1, or a different one?

3. The one that breaks my rule of thumb. Sometimes a story has essentially no dials to twiddle — nothing to adjust — and it was built with the fact in full view, but it lands on some extremely specific detail that nobody had thought to connect to anything. My rule of thumb says: built while staring at the fact, so no credit. My gut says: credit, obviously. One of those is wrong.

What I've been handed so far doesn't fit. Someone told me it's "ad hoc," but as far as I can tell that's about extras bolted onto a story after it gets into trouble, whereas my thing is about how the story was originally built. Someone else pointed me at something called the problem of old evidence, which I gather is about how a fact you already knew can't move your probabilities. That sounds like it's about the facts being old, not about the route by which the story was arrived at — and it seems to apply equally to both of my analysts, so it can't be what's generating the difference in credit between them. That's exactly what I care about: the difference comes from a fact about the history of the person's thinking, not from anything about the world, and that makes me suspicious that it isn't a real epistemic difference at all.

So: is there an established concept for this? If the nearest established concepts are the two I mentioned, I want to know precisely how each of them cuts the territory and which of my three cases each one does and doesn't cover — and what's actually doing the work when the tuned story gets discounted. Also: how finely can you draw the line, given that nobody ever builds a story in a vacuum and everyone has background facts in view the whole time? Is "built by staring at the data" a difference in kind or a difference in degree, and if degree, what's the scale? If there's no established concept, I'd rather have a coined term with a real definition and clear boundary cases than a name. A label on its own won't tell me anything.
