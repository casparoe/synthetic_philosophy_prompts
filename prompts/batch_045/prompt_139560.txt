hey — I run a fortnightly reading group at my uni, mostly final-year philosophy undergrads plus a couple of psych and econ people who drifted in, about 16 of us on a good night, and I've got one 80-minute slot. I want an activity that makes people *feel* the problem with transformative experience — the thing where you have to choose something partly on the basis of what it will be like, and you can't know what it will be like until you've done it, and by then the you that's judging isn't the you that chose. Please don't open with the canonical examples (the becoming-a-vampire one, the first-baby one, the mosquitoes one) — I want them to run into the structure cold and only get the name and the standard cases in the last five minutes.

Constraints on the design: index cards, envelopes, a whiteboard, maybe a couple of smell-vials or a phone for one sound clip. No apps, no real money, max ~2 hours of prep for me. No lecture up front — the idea should arrive in what happens to them, not in what I say.

Here's roughly what I have in mind, but please sharpen the mechanics, especially the incentives, because that's where I think it'll collapse:

- Everyone fills a short "now self" card: rank four goods (something like novelty / comfort / mastery / devotion) and write down one thing they'd never trade away.
- Then the offer: keep the known modest payoff, or take a sealed envelope whose payoff and whose *ranking of those four goods* will both change. Crucially they decide without opening it, and the only third-party info available is that nearly all past transferees report it was worth it, while also reporting that they could not have predicted they'd say that.
- The bit I most want you to build out, and the bit I think is genuinely new: make the testimony from past transferees unusable in a way that isn't just "you had to be there." Have the reports be sincere and content-bearing but written in a vocabulary whose evaluative terms have drifted — value-words swapped onto sensory or social properties, or the reverse — so participants can see there's information there and still can't decode whether it's praise or complaint. I want them to argue about whether the failure is phenomenal, semantic, or something else, and I want at least one pair of reports that is consistent with both a great and a terrible outcome.
- Before committing, they can buy an escape hatch (revert later, at a stiff points cost). Some will buy it, some will refuse on the grounds that buying it makes the choice not real — that refusal is a moment I want preserved and named in the debrief, so set the costs so both responses look reasonable.
- On reveal: each person who transferred gets a new card with a new ranking, a scored payoff, and a short first-person note from their transformed self in ordinary language. Then two verdicts, taken separately and written down: was this a good choice judged from here-with-the-new-ranking, and was it a good choice judged by the person who was deciding earlier. Those two numbers should come apart for most people, by design.

Scoring: I don't want a single winner, I think that ruins it. I was imagining two scoreboards posted side by side from the start — total experienced value, and number of people who'd choose again knowing only what they knew at the time — so that EV reasoning is visibly answering only one of them and someone can top one and bomb the other. Tell me if that's the right call and how to keep people from just ignoring the second board.

What I need from you: full rules and a timeline with minute marks; the exact wording for the offer and for the drifted reports (a few sample cards I can copy); the payoff numbers with a note on how to tune them; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the problem — the un-decodable reports, the escape-hatch refusals, the two verdicts, the fact that "everyone says it's worth it" was never usable evidence for the person deciding.

Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (people treating it as a plain lottery, someone guessing the trick and meta-gaming, the report phase turning into giggles, the reveal running long, someone getting quietly weirded out because the cards brush up against real life changes they're facing), and how I handle each. And be straight with me about what the activity can't do: which parts of the problem it only gestures at and I'll have to put on the board myself afterward — irreversibility, the timescale and stakes of real cases, the transformation of what you can attend to or know as opposed to what you want, and whatever the formal decision-theoretic difficulty actually is supposed to be, since a game can show the conflict between the two verdicts but not why modelling it is hard.
