i mod a pretty big rationalist-adjacent forum — think the ACX/LW comment section with its own house culture, 200–400 comments a day, five of us on rotation, all volunteers, and every removal has to be justified in the thread itself because people will definately litigate it. our rule right now is "cite or hedge" and it's worthless as written: half the board satisfies it by typing "i think" and then being maximally confident, and the other half gets deleted for saying true things they happen to have no public source for.

what i want is an actual decision procedure — checklist, ordered questions, where it stops with a no, where it hands off to a person — grounded in whatever the philosophy of assertion actually knows about this. please don't give me a reading list or point me at papers, i'm not going to chase citations. lay the terrain out in your own words. the way i understand it there are three candidate norms: assert only what you know, assert only what's true, and assert only what you're reasonably/warrantedly confident in; plus a separate fight about whether the bar moves with what's at stake for the people hearing it; plus that wierd lottery-style case where something is 99%+ probable on base rates alone and nobody "knows" it.

constraints: 30–60 seconds per call, run by a mod who usually knows nothing about the topic; output is one of leave-alone / require-a-hedge / require-a-link / tag-unverified / remove, plus a one-sentence public justification that can't out anybody's private info; and it has to be consistent across five mods, so include a calibration set of ~8 one-line test comments with the intended output for each.

the bits i specifically need handled:

- step zero, is this even an assertion: rhetorical questions, "suppose p" inside an argument, jokes and sarcasm, quoting someone in order to disagree, posting a link with a one-word caption, "everyone knows", "my understanding is", "reportedly", "go look it up yourself". and separately: does reach matter? i've been applying what i call the nesting discount — a claim buried nine levels deep gets a lower bar than a top-level comment — and i can't tell if that's principled or just laziness.
- hedges: what does "i think p" / "probably p" / "i'd put 70% on p" actually commit you to under each of the three norms? if the knowledge norm governs bare p, what governs "i think p" — knowledge of your own credence? calibration? and is a hedge a real reduction in assertoric load or just a social move? i've started calling the fake kind warrant laundering ("i'm no expert, but [extremely confident claim]", "studies show [thing i can't produce]", "people in the field say...") and i want to know whether that's one coherent category or three different things i'm collapsing.
- warrant a mod can actually see: a public checkable source vs. a domain-local track record (i want a real "assertoric license" rule — earned by a public record of checkable claims in that one topic, expires, doesn't transfer across topics, doesn't cover insider info) vs. nothing visible at all.
- stakes and reach: pick a side on whether the bar moves, and say what picking that side costs you. include the statistical-certainty cases — "the flight will land fine", "the approval gets delayed again", "the build ships late" — where the probability is high and the knowledge is thin.

and the thing i keep tripping over: the norm governs the *speaker*, but moderation runs on *my* evidence. someone can fully satisfy it and still look unsourced to me; someone can violate it and look fine. be explicit about which of the two your procedure is really tracking, because i suspect our current rule is confused about exactly this.

then run it on three: (1) a three-week-old account, top level, no track record: "scaling keeps working, i'd put 85% on capabilities improving sharply through 2027" — which half the board believes anyway. (2) a nested reply where A asks for a source and B says "this is well documented, X has been shown repeatedly" with no link, and B is basically right but the specific number is wrong. (3) the one where i won't like the answer: a verified ML researcher with a long record of correct inside-baseball posts writes "the next model drops in march, i know people there" — no citation possible, no names, i believe him and want it left up. what does your procedure do to him, and is that actually licensed by the norm or is it just my evidence problem leaking into a rule?

close with what the procedure can't do — the failure modes i should expect (e.g. that it will systematically hit true-but-ahead-of-the-evidence claims, and let confidently wrong but well-licensed users through).
