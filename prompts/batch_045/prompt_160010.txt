I'm on a small risk team at a payments company, and we've been asked to respond to a regulator's consultation on "run-resistant" deposit account design — the idea being that maybe you can change the plumbing of a retail deposit account so that a run is harder to start. Two colleagues and I keep argueing in circles about it, so I wanted an outside read. The case I keep coming back to is Northern Rock in the UK in September 2007: the bank had funded long-term mortgages with short-term wholesale borrowing, credit markets seized up, it asked the Bank of England for emergency liquidity support on 14 September, the news got out, and within about a day there were the famous photos of people queing around the block to pull their savings. It ran for several days, the government ended up guaranteeing the deposits, and the bank was nationalized in February 2008 — reportedly the first British bank in something like 150 years to fail by way of a bank run. (I'm fairly confident about those facts, but if any specific detail is load-bearing for your analysis, say so rather than quietly building on it.)

What I want is a two-way analysis. First, take the standard rational-herding / information-cascade picture — agents acting in sequence, each holding a small private signal about some fixed underlying state, each able to observe what earlier agents did, and the possibility that after some point the public record swamps private information and everyone just copies — and map it carefully onto Northern Rock. Who exactly are the players (branch depositors? online depositors? wholesale counterparties? the media?), what counts as the private signal versus the public one, and what is the state they're uncertain about? Is the physical queue itself the signal later depositors were reading, and does it matter for the model that queueing is a costly and highly visible action in a way that moving money in a banking app is not? Which features of the episode does the cascade model explain beautifully — the suddenness, the self-feeding quality, the fact that it started before anyone had hard news about the balance sheet — and where does it start to misdescribe what happened? My own hunch, which I'd like you to pressure-test, is that the model is in real trouble here: in a bank run the variable you're unsure about isn't a fixed state of the world, because whether the bank actually fails partly depends on how many other people run. That looks much closer to a coordination problem with strategic complementarities (the Diamond–Dybvig style story) than to a pure inference problem, which would mean Northern Rock isn't really a confirmation of the cascade model at all but a case it was never designed for. Tell me if that's right, and if it is, what the cascade framework still contributes once you've admitted that.

The part I actually care about, though, is using the episode to adjudicate three design ideas we've been kicking around — and, more importantly, to figure out whether the cascade lens is even the right one for judging them. (1) Lag the disclosure: don't publish real-time withdrawal data; publish yesterday's aggregate flows, so nobody can watch the queue form while it's forming. (2) Pro-rata clearing inside a short window rather than first-come-first-served, so the early runners don't get out in full while the late ones eat the loss. (3) A "stay bonus": the deposit insurer pre-commits to paying something like 2% on balances to depositors who do not withdraw during a declared stress window, conditional on the bank surviving. For each one, tell me what the model says it does to the incentives, what it does to the information structure, and what Northern Rock specifically suggests about whether it would have worked. And be honest about where the theory goes silent — for instance, a lot of the private signal in a real run isn't about solvency at all, it's about whether you need rent money, and I want to know whether any of these tweaks do anything for that kind of depositor or whether they just make the run slower and nastier without breaking it.
