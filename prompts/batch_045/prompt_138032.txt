I serve on the Standards Committee of a regional intergovernmental organization whose member states have undertaken, in the organization's founding instrument, to maintain free and fair elections and the conditions under which governments can be changed by vote. The instrument's Standards Clause empowers the Committee, on a finding of democratic backsliding in a member state, to suspend that state's eligibility for a discretionary fund and to refer the matter to the plenary Assembly. The Committee has never made a finding and has never defined backsliding; it has worked instead with an unwritten three-part formula that successive secretariats have applied inconsistently. I have to draft the opinion that goes to the Committee for a vote in three weeks, and it has to do two things at once: recommend a finding in four pending files, and tell us whether the formula survives contact with them.

The formula, in the terms our internal notes use:

1. The formal-legality condition. The measures were adopted through formally valid legal procedures, by a government that came to power in a genuinely contested election. This is what separates backsliding from a coup, and it is why the formula is supposed to be about constitutional retrogression rather than about dictatorship.

2. The ratchet condition. The measures, taken together rather than one at a time, predictably shift the expected distribution of future power toward the incumbents, and the shifts reinforce instead of offsetting one another — control of public media plus redrawn districts plus reconstituted courts is more than the sum of its parts.

3. The lock-in condition. The package cannot be unwound by a bare legislative majority after a single lost election: it raises the cost of alternation above the cost of winning a majority, so that losing an election no longer means leaving office on the same terms you entered it.

Our Chair wants to add a fourth, the intent condition: that the government acted with the purpose of entrenching itself. Several members resist it as mind-reading; the Chair says that without it the formula is an accidents test that will convict reformers.

The four files. Please treat the following as stipulated: the Committee is not being asked to re-adjudicate the record, though the memo should flag where a finding turns on a single load-bearing fact.

Hungary. Fidesz–KDNP won the 2010 election with roughly 53% of the party-list vote and just over two-thirds of the seats; it then adopted a new constitution in 2011, entrenched a class of cardinal laws requiring a two-thirds majority, reorganized the media regulator, redrew single-member districts, expanded and repopulated the Constitutional Court, and cut the number of seats; it has been re-elected under the new rules in 2014, 2018 and 2022.

Poland. Law and Justice took under 38% of the vote in 2015 and an outright majority of Sejm seats; a dispute over the appointment of Constitutional Tribunal judges followed within weeks, then a public-media law converting the broadcasters into state-supervised national media, then 2017 legislation on the National Council of the Judiciary, the Supreme Court's retirement age, a disciplinary regime for judges, and an extraordinary appeal mechanism; the EU opened infringement proceedings and its own rule-of-law procedure against Warsaw in the same period.

Turkey. A failed coup in July 2016 was followed by a state of emergency, mass dismissals of civil servants, academics and journalists, and the prosecution of an opposition party's leadership; in April 2017 a constitutional referendum, held under the emergency and passed by roughly two points, replaced the parliamentary system with an executive presidency holding wide decree and appointment powers, and the incumbent has since been re-elected under it.

United States. After the 2020 presidential election the losing incumbent pressed claims of fraud on state officials and on the Vice President, and the certification of the result in the capital was disrupted by violence; several states then enacted new election-administration statutes tightening mail and early voting, restricting drop boxes, adding identification requirements, and shifting control over local election boards, while other jurisdictions expanded access; a 2023 Supreme Court decision rejected the strongest version of the claim that state legislatures are constitutionally unconstrained in setting federal election rules. Note that the founding instrument binds member states, and nearly all of these measures are acts of subnational governments.

What I want in the memo, beyond the per-file recommendation:

- Apply the formula prong by prong in each file and say where a file passes or fails; where a file turns entirely on one prong, say which.

- Run a mirror test. Suppose a member state elects a government on a platform of constitutional reform, and that government abolishes an unelected upper chamber, introduces automatic voter registration, and bans a party it calls extremist. Does the formula flag that package as well as it flags ours? If it does, say what that shows about whether we are measuring entrenchment or merely unpopularity with this Committee. If it does not, say honestly what that shows about the charge that backsliding is a label we apply only to the other side.

- Fix the baseline for the ratchet condition: is the comparison the status quo immediately before the measures, or some independent standard of electoral fairness? Note what this does to a member state whose defects long predate the episode (think of malapportioned upper chambers and indirect electoral colleges that antedate every measure in our files).

- Say what the formula cannot see, and be disciplined about it rather than forcing it to speak: erosion driven by private actors, violence or intimidation rather than legislation; subnational retrogression in a federal member state; slow drift that no single package triggers; and the background entrenchment above.

- Say who decides, operationally: who bears the burden, what standard the Committee should require, whether validation by a member state's own constitutional court counts as evidence against a finding, whether a subsequent election won by the incumbents under the challenged rules defeats it, and whether findings should carry a mandatory review date.

- Give the strongest argument on the losing side for at least two files, in a form its proponents would recognize, and say whether it is answered or merely outweighed.

Do not survey the democracy-literature, and do not propose indicators or composite indices: we have those. This should read as a document the Committee can vote on — recommendation first, reasons after, no citations or references, under 1,600 words.
