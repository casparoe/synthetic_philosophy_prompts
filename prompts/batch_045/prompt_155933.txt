The situation is this. In the rationalist-adjacent reading group I have been part of for years, we have been going round on what attitude to take towards durable, apparently intractable disagreements — the sort where both sides have heard the other's case and neither has moved. Two members of the group keep reaching for the parable of the blind men and the elephant as their working picture of the Jain doctrine of many-sidedness, and they are using it to carry a quite specific conclusion: that in any such disagreement each party has hold of something genuinely true, that a flat claim that the other side is simply wrong is therefore never in order, and that the correct response is always to steelman and to treat the whole truth as the union of the partial reports. It is doing real work for us. Steelmanning is being sold not as etiquette or as a heuristic for good Bayes, but as metaphysically mandated by the structure of the world. My suspicion is that the parable, rather than the doctrine, is carrying that conclusion, and that if you swap the parable out the conclusion floats free.

So I want an audit of the analogy as a piece of argument. Concretely:

1. Set out the correspondence table explicitly: what is supposed to play the role of the elephant, of the men, of their blindness, of the parts each one touches, of the sighted onlooker who sees the whole animal, of the quarrel, and of the moral the story is usually taken to have. Then say for each row whether the mapping survives contact with the doctrine as you understand it.

2. The crux, as I see it: in the parable as normally told, are the men each partly right about the whole, or is each of them simply wrong — a man who takes a leg for a pillar has made an error, not a partial discovery? Work out what that implies. An unqualified assertion of one standpoint's claim is supposed to be false on the Jain account, while the same claim qualified by its standpoint is true; can the parable represent that difference at all, or does its structure force every claim to be straightforwardly mistaken?

3. Structural question: the parable partitions its object into spatial parts, and its natural reading is that the truth is the mereological sum of the reports. But many-sidedness is standardly presented as a thesis about one and the same locus being described from different standpoints — the substance-perspective versus the mode-perspective, for instance — rather than about different chunks of it. Does anything in the doctrine correspond to the composite description the parable arrives at? And does the sighted onlooker model omniscience, or does it import something the doctrine rejects — a single privileged human-level standpoint, or the idea that the omniscient cognition is just the conjunction of the partial ones rather than a grasp of the thing in its infinity of aspects?

4. Try to model the conditional predication itself. The interesting cases, it seems to me, are the joint mode where a thing is and is not in different respects, and the mode that is supposed to be inexpressible. If the parable cannot represent these, say exactly what that costs, and whether the cost is to the parable or to the claim that many-sidedness is a distinctive thesis rather than a mild empirical caution about limited evidence.

5. Two variants I have tried, which I would like you to adjudicate. In the first, all the men are touching the same leg and differ only in the vocabulary they use to describe it — one reports a pillar, one a load-bearing column, one a limb — so the disagreement is about conceptual scheme rather than about access. This worries me, because if the doctrine reduces to that then it collapses into something like framework relativism, where every disagreement is verbal and nothing substantive is ever at stake. In the second variant there is no elephant at all: six men are holding six different objects, and the word elephant names nothing but a social rule against contradicting one another. That worry is the opposite one — that the whole thing is a norm of assertion, and the metaphysics has quietly evaporated. Which variant is closer to the doctrine, and what does each one smuggle in?

6. Finally, the verdict. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the use my group is making from the ones that are merely cosmetic, and tell me whether the conclusion — always steelman, nobody is flatly wrong, truth is the union of reports — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Note in particular the awkward possibility that the parable taken at face value licenses something closer to deference to whoever claims unmediated access to the whole, which is not the conclusion anyone in my group wants. And if there is a better analogy for what anekāntavāda is actually claiming, give it to me, together with what it gets right and what it still loses; if there is not, say why no analogy can do this work.
