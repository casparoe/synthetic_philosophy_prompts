I'm teaching a unit that puts Aristotle's account of exchange and money in contact with Shakespeare's The Merchant of Venice, and I want a handout that does real work rather than decorating Aristotle with quotations from the play. My starting puzzle is that his categories seem both to line up with the play's oppositions and not to line up at all. Shylock's lending at interest is Aristotle's stock example of unnatural acquisition, gain taken from money itself rather than from the goods money is supposed to measure, while Antonio's gratis lending and overseas ventures look as though they belong on the approved side. Yet the play makes Antonio's fortune rest on speculative cargoes scattered across the sea, and makes the Christian characters' dealings turn on gifts, rings, and obligations that cannot be priced.

Write me the reading. Does the contrast between Venice's commercial law and Belmont's bonds of gift and fidelity map onto Aristotle's distinction between natural and unnatural acquisition, or does it crosscut that distinction in a way that shows the distinction doing less work than it claims? Where does the trial scene fall on his contrast between strict legal justice and equity: the bond for a pound of flesh, Portia's appeal to mercy, and her narrow reading of what the bond literally permits? And is Shylock's insistence on the exact letter of what he is owed an instance of the vice of wanting more than one's share, or does he escape that description precisely because he claims nothing beyond his due? Be honest about where the lens distorts the play: I would rather tell my students where Aristotle runs out than force the fit. But I also want to know what, if anything, the play shows about exchange, measure, and obligation that Aristotle's own argument does not.
