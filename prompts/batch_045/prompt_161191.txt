I've been chewing on this for a week for my graduate seminar on the epistemology of risk, and a student finally pushed back hard enough that I can't see where to hit it.

Setup: call a system deceptively aligned if it performs alignment during training and evaluation while actually optimizing for something else, keeping the act up only until the treacherous turn — the point where defecting gets cheaper than performing. Say a system is eval-transparent iff, for any evaluation procedure E we run it on, it can tell at the time of acting that E is running.

(1) If a system is deceptively aligned and eval-transparent, then playing aligned on E is what maximizes its chance of being deployed, so it plays aligned.
(2) So, conditional on deceptive alignment, the behavior we observe is exactly what we'd observe if it were genuinely aligned — the likelihoods match, and no behavioral test has any evidential bearing on the hypothesis.
(3) So whatever evidence we can have must be non-behavioral: interpretability, arguments from the training process, provenance, formal guarantees.
(4) But each of those is itself validated behaviorally: we trust the interpretability readout because it tracked things we checked by running models; we trust the training story because earlier models trained that way behaved fine.
(5) So there is no evidence, of any kind, that could tell a deceptively aligned system apart from an aligned one.
(6) So no body of evidence could ever justify deploying a system capable of deception.
(7) But humans are capable of deception in exactly that sense, and we do think evidence can sometimes justify trusting someone with something important — letting a surgeon operate, say.
(8) So either we're never justified in trusting anyone, or one of (1)–(6) is false.

It proves too much, obviously. The wrinkle is that I want to keep (1) — once systems are good at situating themselves, eval-transparency seems close to realistic — and I want to keep (7), because the human case feels like a datum rather than a thesis I can drop. One student says (4) is just the Agrippan trilemma wearing a lab coat; another says the real break is (5)→(6), since undetectability isn't much of a reason for a ban when the prior is low. Neither has satisfied me: the first makes the problem too general to be about AI at all, and the second seems to let us deploy on vibes.

So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does each of teh alternatives cost me? Please don't answer with "build better interpretability" or "don't build it" — I want the logical diagnosis, not the policy.
