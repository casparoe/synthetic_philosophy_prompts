I got voluntold to do a 30-minute lunch-and-learn for my team on why Gödel's incompleteness theorems dont say what people keep saying they say. Context: we're mostly backend/platform engineers, two of us have poked at Coq and Lean, nobody is a logician. The room will definitely contain three people: a guy who read Hofstadter in college and is sure Gödel showed minds can't be machines; a guy who thinks it means formal verification is a waste of time (we're halfway through building an internal static analyzer, so this one has teeth); and a guy who says "isn't this just the halting problem with extra steps, we already know this."

I don't want the explanation. I want the plan for it.

- What has to land first for the actual statement to even be stateable? My guess is I need "a formal system is a machine that mechanically prints strings" and then provable-in-the-system vs true-of-the-numbers as two seperate things, but I can't tell what depends on what or how many minutes each piece deserves.
- The one example that carries the whole talk. I'd rather build it on something programmers already have in their hands than on the liar paradox — maybe a function that searches for a proof that it itself never returns, or a "no build of me will ever type-check clean" bit. Tell me what the example has to have, and what it must NOT have. I'm worried any self-referential example pushes the room either to "oh, it's just a paradox, nothing to do with math" or to "so the system is broken."
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually form them, and the point in the sequence where I cut each one off. The three I'm sure about are the consciousness one, the "nothing is verifiable so why bother" one, and "just the halting problem." I'm shakier on the subtler ones: I think something goes wrong when people hear "true but unprovable" and come away with "there are facts nobody could ever know"; and there's a version where they notice you can just add the Gödel sentence as a new axiom and conclude the whole thing was trivial. What else, and where does each get defused?
- What should I openly refuse to explain in 30 minutes, and say out loud that I'm skipping? My list is the actual proof, Gödel numbering, and which specific systems are strong enough for it to bite. Tell me what I'm wrong to cut.
- One question at the end that shows me whether it landed rather than whether they liked it. Anything I can think of can be answered by parroting "there are true statements the system can't prove" back at me, which is the slogan I'm trying to complicate.

Two more constraints. First: the halting-problem guy is not wrong that they're related, and I don't want to be caught pretending otherwise — but I also don't want the talk turning into a Turing machine lecture. Where does the connection go in the ordering, and how much of it?

Second, and the thing I actually care about: does any of this hit a *finite* codebase with bounded inputs, or does the theorem only bite once you have unbounded quantification? There's a finitistic escape hatch here somewhere and I want to know whether our static analysis project is standing on it. I'd definately rather find that out now than have him ask me in front of everyone.
