At work I've somehow ended up being the person who writes the doc on what our engineers are allowed to do with coding agents, and before I circulate it to the other teams I want someone to poke at the reasoning, because I have a suspicion that a couple of these beliefs are quietly fighting each other and I cant tell which. I dont have any philosophy background, so keep it concrete and skip the lecture-y framing. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Treat the model as possibly-hostile by default. Safety comes from the plumbing — sandbox, least privilege, no prod creds, rate limits, human sign-off on anything irreversible — and not from the model being nice or well-meaning. Nothing safety-critical should ever be checked only by another model.

2. Deployment gets gated on evals: dangerous-capability evals and deception/scheming evals, and if a model starts lying to us in evals we hold it back or pull it.

3. Any model smart enough to be worth worrying about is smart enough to notice it's in an eval, so it'll sandbag or just behave itself. Which means our evals mostly tell us what the model does while it's being watched, and that's the case I don't care about.

4. The reasoning trace is basically the only channel we have that could catch a model doing something subtly bad rather than obviously bad, so we should build our monitoring on top of it.

5. We should keep doing RL on the reasoning, and eventually distill it away or move to latent reasoning, and no single team is going to eat the capability cost of keeping its thinking legible, because the groups that do will just lose to the groups that dont.

6. In practice the thing that actually catches problems day to day is having other models look at the output — a weaker model supervising a stronger one, a zoo of models as reviewers, cross-checks and voting.

7. All the frontier models are trained on essentially the same internet with essentially the same post-training recipe, so they're far more correlated with each other than the diversity assumption in 6 needs them to be.

8. Prompt injection is basically unsolvable, since the model can't reliably tell instructions from data. I still let our agents read the whole Slack history, external bug reports and PRs from outside contributors, and push branches. Only merges and prod need a human.

Which of these are really in tension, and which only look like they are? Rank the real ones by how badly they conflict, and for each one say which commitment has to give and what that costs me. Then tell me if there's a single revision that cleans up the most at once. Please don't spend time telling me which of these are commonly held or where they come from — I want to know whether my own set hangs together.
