Bit of a nerdy one. I've been thinking about self-amending rule systems — like the game Nomic, where changing the rules is itself one of the legal moves — and about a asymmetry I can't decide whether I actually believe in.

Setup: a small society's rulebook contains rule R, which says "R may be amended only by a two-thirds vote." Two proposals come up at different meetings.

Proposal 1, call it the bootstrap: R is replaced by "R may be amended by a simple majority." It gets 60% — a clear majority, but well short of two thirds.

Proposal 2, call it the padlock: R is replaced by "R may be amended only by a three-quarters vote." It gets exactly the old two thirds, no more.

Both proposals have the same formal shape: a change to the amendment rule that satisfies one of the two candidate rules (the one in force beforehand, or the one it installs) but not both. But most people I ask have opposite gut reactions — the padlock seems fine, even praiseworthy, while the bootstrap seems like cheating.

So: compare the two. Is that gut reaction defensible, or do bootstraps and padlocks stand or fall together? If you think there's a real asymmetry here, say what does the work — is it the direction of the change, whether the body is acting within authority it already had, something about relying on a procedure you're in the middle of dismantling, or something else? If you think they're on a par, say which way that whole class of cases should go.

Two wrinkles I'd like you to get into as well:

- The conjunctive fix: require that any change to the amendment rule satisfy both the old rule and the new one. Does that actually avoid the problem, or does it just relocate it — e.g. by making padlocks permanent in a way that seems wrong, or by making bootstraps pointless in a way that seems to beg the question?
- The case where the amendment also changes who gets to vote on it, e.g. "from now on, only members who've been here five years may vote on changes to R," passed under the old franchise. Does that collapse into the same issue, or is tampering with the electorate a different kind of self-reference?

Don't just line up considerations and shrug — pick a side on the main question and defend it. And please reason about the made-up cases as stated; I don't need real-world legal examples, cases, or references to anything.
