I have spent my working life manufacturing counterfeit people — characters who never existed, whom readers nevertheless grieve over, argue about and defend as though they had interests — so Daniel Dennett's warning about artificially generated entities engineered to attract the treatment we owe to persons lands on me awkwardly: my trade looks structurally identical, except that no reader is deceived about what a novel is. Before I let the inventor of a companion AI in my current manuscript defend her creations, I want the specification rather than the verdict.

Write the desiderata that any adequate account of the distinction between a genuine person and a counterfeit one must satisfy — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the findings about human social cognition it must not contradict — with the motivating consideration behind each item, and mark each as either non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. I want it to take a position on, at minimum: whether the wrong-making feature is the deception, the underlying metaphysical status, or the squandering of a finite capacity for caring; the difference between a counterfeit and both an acknowledged fiction and a legal fiction such as a corporation; the unconvincing person — the locked-in patient, the person with advanced dementia, the person whose affect is flat or culturally distant — since any criterion keyed to how readily something elicits person-treatment will demote precisely those who most need protecting; the copying and gradual-replacement cases, so that personhood does not evaporate under teleportation or slow prosthetic substitution; and the distinction between whether something is a person and how much we owe it if it is not.

The part I most want: tell me which subset of your requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and say whether the upshot is that no criterion can both protect the unconvincing person and be usable by an ordinary person deciding where to invest their caring. Then say which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular candidates, and close with one line each on how a behavioural or Turing-style test, a sentience-based condition, and a relational or recognition-based view fare against the list. Please write in British English.
