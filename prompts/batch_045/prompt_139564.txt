Ok so my flatmate is doing a PhD in behavioural ecology and we've been arguing about the handicap principle for like three weeks, and I think I've built an argument that refutes it, which is exactly why I dont trust it. Here it is cleaned up:

(1) The minimal claim. When sender and receiver interests partly conflict -- I want to be chosen whatever my quality is, you want to choose only high quality -- a signal can be stably honest only if signalling, or signalling at a higher level, is differentially worse for a low-quality sender than for a high-quality one. Something has to make faking not worth it.

(2) If that differential cost is what buys honesty, then a more costly display is a more reliable one. Crank up the cost and you crank up how hard the signal is to fake.

(3) Reliable signals are good for receivers, and being reliably chosen is good for senders, so both sides push in the same direction, which is up. Nothing in (1) or (2) says where this stops. So the theory predicts a ratchet: displays should escalate until the cost eats essentially everything a sender has to spend.

(4) What we actually see is moderate. Displays are costly but not crippling, and a lot of honest communication is free. Some signals are honest just because they're mechanically tied to the trait -- a bigger resonating chamber produces a lower call, so the deep call can't be faked by a small animal even though producing it costs nothing extra. And animals and people in ongoing relationships tell each other true things for free all the time.

(5) She also threw two made-up cases at me. Case one: a bird where the male's display is carrying a heavy stone to the female, and she then uses the stone as a nest anchor. The cost is a transfer -- nothing is wasted, no resources are destroyed -- but only strong males can carry big stones, so the signal is perfectly honest. Case two: a species where nobody pays anything in equilibrium, but any male who displays above his station gets beaten up by the others. The cost exists only in the counterfactual.

(6) So either the theory predicts runaway waste we don't observe, in which case it's false. Or you patch it by saying "cost" just means whatever keeps the signal honest -- in which case both of her cases count, the cost actually paid can be zero, and the theory retrodicts every honest signal there is and would be refuted by none.

(7) Either way it's not something I should believe.

I'd most like to keep (1) -- it feels like it ought to be close to a theorem for the conflict-of-interest case -- and I'd rather give up almost anything else. But I can't tell whether (2) and (3) really follow from it or whether I've smuggled something in, and I have no idea how to answer the vacuity version of the charge in (6), which is the one that actually bothers me. I don't want to be holding a view that can't lose.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? If you reject the move from (1) to (3), what does the theory actually predict about how big a display should be, and what would have to be true for it to be enormous? Do her two cases refute (1), or do they quietly satisfy it -- and if they satisfy it, what's left of the claim that signals have to be wasteful? And how would I tell whether the patched version in (6) is vacuous or just more general than the original Zahavi-style story about waste?
