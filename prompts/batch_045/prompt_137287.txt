I'm writing a short position paper for a graduate reading group on whether death is bad for the person who dies, and before I commit anything to paper I'd like an audit of the package I've ended up with. I've been arguing about this with a friend who is a thoroughgoing Epicurean: I keep conceding his premises one at a time and then refusing his conclusion, which makes me suspect I'm holding something jointly untenable without being able to see where. Here is where I think I stand, numbered so you can refer back: (1) I accept the Epicurean argument — a dead person is not a subject of anything, being dead is not a condition one is in, so there is nothing bad for one in the state of being dead, and the ordinary horror of one's own corpse is misdirected. (2) Death can nonetheless be bad for the person who dies, and its badness consists entirely in deprivation, in the goods they would otherwise have had. (3) I hold an existence requirement: nothing can be bad for someone unless that someone exists at some time; so merely possible people are not harmed by never being created, and I do not want to say that anyone is harmed by not having been brought into existence. (4) I accept temporal neutrality: where in a life a good or a bad falls makes no difference to how good or bad it is for the person, and prudential bias toward the future is irrational. (5) I reject the symmetry argument: the time before I was born is not bad for me, even though the time after I die is. (6) The harm of death is independent of the person's attitudes toward their own future — someone who has stopped caring about it, who is tired of life, who has decided they have had enough, or who never formed long-term projects in the first place, is still harmed, and can be gravely harmed, by dying young. (7) I hold a comparative view of magnitude: how bad a death is for someone is fixed by the difference between how well their life actually goes and how well it would have gone had they not died when they did; and if the counterfactual continuation would on balance have been bad, death is no harm to them at all. (8) Two case verdicts I am attached to: (8a) thirty years of suspended animation with no experiences whatsoever — put in the box at forty-five, woken at seventy-five — is not, I want to say, a misfortune for me in the way that being dead from forty-five onward is, and a single night of dreamless sleep certainly is not one either; (8b) when an eighty-year-old with two weeks to live is murdered, something terrible has happened to him, and I do not want to say it is a small thing merely because the deprivation is small.

What I want is an audit rather than a tutorial. Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like it — in particular, tell me whether (1) and (2) are really compatible or whether I am equivocating between two different requirements a harm has to meet, whether (3) and (2) actually pull against each other or whether the existence requirement does its blocking work somewhere else, and whether either of my verdicts in (8) is one my own principle in (7) cannot deliver. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set — I am especially interested in whether the moves I vaguely remember being available here (locating the asymmetry in something about identity and origins rather than in our attitudes, biting the bullet on dreamless sleep, distinguishing what is bad for a person from what is wrongfully done to them) are actually open to someone who also holds (4) and (6), since those two seem to close off the usual routes. Then tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most, and what it would commit me to that I have not signed up for. I have no idea which item to drop and would rather not be told which one I secretly like least.

Two more things. First, be explicit about confidence: separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and tell me when you are going beyond anything that has been established rather than presenting a minority view as the consensus. Second, if one of the commitments above happens to be a position with a standard label in this literature, name the label so I can recognise it in discussion — but do not give me citations, bibliographies, or reading suggestions, since I have no library access where I am writing this.
