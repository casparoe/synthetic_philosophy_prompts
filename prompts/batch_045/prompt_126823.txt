I convene a small reading group — three graduate students and one faculty member — that has spent two terms on the metaphysics of persistence, and I am writing up the discussion as a literature-review chapter. What strikes me most is how little movement there is.

The endurantist side says that an object is wholly present at every time at which it exists, so that persistence is literally identity across times: change is handled by relativizing property possession to times, or by treating ordinary properties as relations to times, and fission cases are handled either by denying that both successors are the original or by allowing a single object to be wholly present in each of two overlapping regions. The perdurantist side says that persisting objects have temporal parts, so that persistence is a matter of being parts of a single, usually continuous, temporally extended whole: change becomes a matter of one part's having a property another part lacks, and fission becomes a single object that branches. Every argument seems to be on the table and none of them seems to decide anything — the problem of temporary intrinsics, the overdetermination worries about coinciding objects and about too many thinkers, the vagueness puzzles, and the claim that relativistic physics is naturally read as quantifying over a four-dimensional manifold. Each side has replies that its opponents describe as question-begging and its advocates describe as decisive, and nobody in the room has moved in two terms.

My hypothesis is that this is not a dispute about what persistence is like, but about what a theory of persistence is supposed to be a theory of: an analysis of ordinary 'same F' discourse, a claim about what there fundamentally is, or a claim about which facts ground which. On that reading the two camps are answering different questions under a single label, and the standoff is an artifact of the label.

Three things make me doubt the hypothesis, and I want them taken into account. First, the case of an entity that exists, goes out of existence, and exists again — say a research institute dissolved and reconstituted a decade later under the same charter, in the same building, with half the same staff. Whatever one says about it, the perdurantist seems forced to say whether the relevant whole can have gaps, and the endurantist seems forced to say what carries identity across the gap; that looks like a genuine, if small, substantive question. Second, the fork-and-merge case: a project that splits into two and the two later re-amalgamates into one. It is not obvious to me that either side has a stable answer, and both answers seem to turn on something the official statements of the views leave open. Third, suppose someone were to prove a clean model-theoretic equivalence result: for every model of endurantism there is a model of perdurantism making true exactly the same sentences of some neutral language, and conversely. Half my group thinks that would end the debate by showing it is verbal; the other half thinks it would leave everything where it was, because the real difference would then have to be located elsewhere — in which structure is fundamental, or in which vocabulary carves nature at the joints.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, substantive, disguised as a difference about standards of metaphysical significance, a cluster of different questions under one heading, or an ill-posed question that no amount of argument could settle? I want a diagnosis argued from what each side actually says, not a survey of the literature — I have read the literature. And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result of the kind sketched above, a change of question, or nothing at all. Please write in American English, in continuous prose; no citations or bibliography are needed or useful to me.
