I'm hunting for a name, if there is one, for something I keep running into at work and cant get straight in my head.

Background: I write software (ML infra mostly) and I read philosophy of mind on the side, badly. I've come across the idea that there are three rough ways to predict what a system will do: grind through the physics, reason from what it was built to do, or treat it as if it wants things and believes things and predict on that basis. Call them the physical, the design and the intentional way if that helps. Fine as far as it goes.

The thing I have no word for is when the third one gets *more accurate because people use it* — and not just in the boring sense that they get more practice at it. Cases:

- A chatbot is tuned to be helpful, and a lot of making it helpful consists in making it behave like something that grasps what you're asking and wants to answer it. Once everyone is talking to it that way, the "it wants to be helpful" description really does predict better than it used to. It stops being merely convenient. But nothing at the level of the weights got more literally goal-directed in a way that would show up if you went at it the first way.
- Two learning agents sharing an environment. Agent A models B as having a goal; B is learning too, so B partly becomes the thing A is modeling. Model and modeled co-evolve and the intentional description of B gets sharper over time.
- My dog. I'm fairly confident treating her as having intentions makes her easier to predict, and that this isn't purely in my head.

Contrasts where I think it doesn't happen: the weather (talking about what winter "wants" changes nothing), and a slot machine (I can treat it as teasing me as long as I like).

So: is there an established concept for predictive descriptions whose accuracy is partly produced by their own adoption? Is this just a special case of the claim that patterns can be real without being physically "there" — observer-relative, compressible, whatever the right phrasing is — or is something else going on? Is it the same thing as a self-fulfilling prophecy? My sense is no: in a self-fulfilling prophecy the belief is what's doing the work and the prediction would still be a bad prediction for someone who didn't share it, whereas here the description can become the genuinely best available description even for a newcomer who knows the whole history. But I'm not sure. And is there a distinction worth drawing between the case where it becomes accurate for everybody and the case where it stays accurate only for people already inside the practice?

A friend said this sounds like "reflexivity" in social science, but as I understand that it's about theories changing the things they're theories of, which isn't quite the same as a prediction getting better.

I don't just want a label — I want to know what the thing is, what it isn't, and where its edges are, and if there's no name, I want a definition I could actually use to tell the real cases from the slot machine.
