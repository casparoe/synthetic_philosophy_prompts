I'm in a reading group on the Vienna Circle and I got into an argument after the session where we did Carnap's distinction between questions asked from inside a linguistic framework and questions about which framework to adopt — the bit where he says accepting a framework isn't a matter of truth but of decision, and that the decision is a practical one guided by how efficient or fruitful the framework is as an instrument. My friend reads that together with the Circle's general optimism about planning (Neurath and his schemes for an economy calculated in kind, the manifesto's closing gestures about rational principles reshaping economic and social life, that whole 1920s mood) and gets the following picture: real political disagreement is an engineering problem plus a one-time administrative decision about the framework, and once the decision is made everything downstream is empirical and technical, so politics dissolves into administration. I think that's too quick, and I want a case to settle it for a seminar paper I'm trying to write.

So here's what I need: a thought experiment, built to order, about a planning dispute. Constraints. Set it in an actual planning body — a city housing authority, a regional hospital network, a transit agency — realistic, mid-century-to-now bureaucratic, one paragraph, no sf furniture and no philosophical props that wouldn't be in the room (no demons, no perfect predictors, no trolleys). Both sides share everything empirical: the same data, the same forecasts, the same causal model, the same budget, and neither is corrupt, stupid or posturing, so the disagreement can't be explained away as an information gap or a difference about the facts. Then: (a) on Carnap's own criteria, the disagreement has to come out as external — a choice between two conceptual frameworks (a unit of account, a way of individuating what the plan is about, a metric of success, a counting convention) rather than a theoretical question, and I want that verdict generated by applying the criteria to the case, not stipulated by having the characters announce that their difference is conventional, because a case that stipulates the verdicts is useless to me. But (b) it also can't be an 'anything goes' case: Carnap's instrumental criterion has to give a determinate verdict about which framework to adopt here, or the case just shows relativism and my friend can happily agree with it. And here's the wrinkle I've been chewing on, which I'd like the case to actually bite on: build it so the two frameworks disagree about what would count as the plan succeeding, so that there's no neutral yardstick for fruitfulness — the fruitfulness of a framework gets measured against purposes the framework itself partly specifies, and I've been calling that the fruitfulness regress. So there's no neutral measure, and yet the case still has to yield a recommendation; if you think that combination can't be built, say so and say which constraint you'd drop.

After the scenario, please do the following: say what the case looks like on each of three readings — Carnap-as-technocrat (this is an internal, empirical, soluble question), Carnap-as-decisionist (this is external, a decision, tolerance applies, and the residue is exactly where the political was all along), and the reading I'm drawn to, that the internal/external line can't be drawn in the place my friend needs it drawn — then tell me which reading the case actually supports and why, not just which one I like. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens if I remove it: what if the two sides also disagreed about purposes rather than sharing one, what if one framework were strictly more fruitful, what if the framework choice were made explicitly revisable at the next planning cycle instead of once-and-for-all. And give me the strongest attack a defender of whichever reading comes out worst can make on the case — I'd rather hear the objection now than from my reading group. For context on what counts as cheating: the case is for an argument that Carnap's distinction relocates the political rather than eliminating it, so anything that just declares the political question conventional, or sneaks in a factual disagreement and calls it conceptual, defeats the purpose.
