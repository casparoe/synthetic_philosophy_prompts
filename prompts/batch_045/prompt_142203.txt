A friend and I have been arguing about anthropic probabilities for months, and he has now produced an argument whose conclusion neither of us accepts but whose steps I cannot fault individually. I would be grateful for a diagnosis: which step is doing the illegitimate work, why does it look sound, and what does giving it up cost?

The set-up. Let H1 and H2 be two hypotheses that, by stipulation, are exactly equally favoured by all of our astronomical evidence before any anthropic reasoning is applied.

H1: the universe contains 10^11 observers, all of them embodied organisms living in galaxies.

H2: the universe contains those same 10^11 organisms plus 10^30 echo minds: minds that arise very rarely in causally sealed regions, whose complete subjective histories are qualitatively identical to the life of an ordinary organism — same apparent memories, same apparent evidence, same apparent ongoing coherence with a world. Nothing you could ever observe, do, or discover would tell you whether you are one of them.

(1) Self-indication: the fact that you exist is evidence for hypotheses on which there are more observers, in proportion to how many observers there are.

(2) But you also have your total evidence E, and conditionalizing on E means the boost in (1) is a boost by the number of observers who have E, not by the number of observers simpliciter.

(3) The echo minds have E by construction; that is precisely what makes them unavailable to the usual reply of screening extra observers off by saying their experiences differ from yours. So H2 is favoured over H1 by a factor of about 10^19, and no possible observation can ever defeat this.

(4) Nothing in (3) depends on the mechanism. Any hypothesis can be given an arbitrarily large boost by stipulating that it also contains arbitrarily many subjectively identical duplicates of you somewhere inaccessible; neither (1) nor (2) caps the number, and the only counterweight is the complexity cost of the extra stipulation.

(5) So what you should believe about the universe is settled by a race between prior simplicity and how many undetectable duplicates of you a hypothesis can be written to contain. And this seems to dominate everything else: for any conclusion C that you now hold on the strength of evidence, there is a gerrymandered H* containing enough duplicates of you to outweigh that evidence no matter how strong it was.

(6) A rule of inference that can make the entire content of your experience irrelevant to what you should believe is not a rule of inference. So (1) is false, and self-indication goes.

Two things I want. First: name the step you reject, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives — in particular, what becomes of the cases self-indication was introduced to handle, such as the 1/3 answer in Sleeping Beauty and the flip-flopping in the Adam-and-Eve set-up. Second: does the self-sampling assumption escape? My friend says no, because (4) can be rerun in terms of the reference class: whether echo minds are inside it or outside it decides the case, and nothing in self-sampling tells you which. I have been attached to self-indication for years, so I would rather keep (1) and (2) than accept (6) — but tell me honestly what keeping them costs me.
