I cover science and ideas for a national newspaper, and for the past two years I've sat, as one of two public members, on my country's research council's Transformative Research panel — twelve people, the other lay member a retired appellate judge, the chair a condensed-matter physicist who wants a decision on Tuesday and goes visibly glassy-eyed whenever anyone says the word incommensurable. The application in front of us has split the scientists five to five, and I have to circulate something before the meeting. I'll describe the field anonymously, since the material is confidential: for about thirty years it has measured its central quantity using a calibration convention adopted when the current consensus framework was put together, and it has accumulated a small set of stubborn anomalies — a sign reversal under conditions nobody can fully reproduce, an unexplained drift in the oldest datasets — that the field has treated as instrument noise. A consortium of eleven researchers at four labs, led by a senior figure who helped build the consensus framework in the first place, is asking for five years to develop a rival one: mostly to build instruments, but also, in their words, to develop a vocabulary adequate to what they think they are seeing. They argue that the consensus quantity is an artifact of the calibration convention, and they decline to state their central predictions in the field's existing terminology, on the grounds that any such restatement would misdescribe them. They cite Kuhn — the familiar claim, in paraphrase, that the proponents of competing paradigms work in different worlds and that there is no common measure between them — and add that the later Kuhn narrowed this to a local, lexical point about the terms that actually change meaning. Our own rules make this harder: funded projects must lodge pre-registered predictions stated in the field's standard terms, and our draft reviewer guidance tells panels to score proposals against the current standards of the field, which seems to me to beg the whole question. Our statistician puts the objection most sharply: if the applicants cannot say in advance what result would make them abandon the project, then whatever this is, it isn't science.

What I need is the memo. Approve, reject, or approve with conditions; a concrete course of action with a time frame; and a fair statement of whichever position you're rejecting — I'd guess the strongest version of rejection is that a public funder has no epistemic warrant to bankroll a framework that by its own account cannot be checked in terms anyone else can audit, that the base rate for self-declared paradigm-topplers is dismal, and that "you can't judge us by your standards" is exactly what cranks say; the strongest version of approval is that paradigm change is real, that panels have no neutral ground in any case, and that one should therefore fund the people rather than the programme. I've been toying with a few devices and would rather you adopt, discard or rewrite them than that you humour me: requiring a bridge lexicon, a table mapping their terms onto the incumbent ones with explicit flags where the mapping fails; requiring a give-up condition — some observation, stated in whatever vocabulary we share, that would lead them to stop; funding a matched investigation of the same anomaly by an incumbent lab; and ring-fencing money to keep the old calibration chain alive in case the anomalies turn out to be real.

Please write it as a document the panel could act on and the applicants could answer: lead with the recommendation in a single sentence, then give the reasons, including an honest account of where the Kuhnian machinery actually leaves us — whether the incommensurability claim is about meaning or about evidence, whether it is supposed to be global or only local, and whether any of it tells a funding body anything it can use. Assume I may write about this afterwards, anonymised, so the reasoning has to be defensible in public and not just in committee. No more than 700 words.
