My metaontology reading group re-runs the exact same fight every few months and nobody moves an inch: do ordinary objects like tables really exist? Side A says no — strictly speaking there are only simples arranged tablewise (living organisms maybe excepted), and "there is a table" is true only in some loose, derived way. Side B says of course there are tables, that A is using "there are" in an invented philosophers' sense, and that the whole thing is verbal: B will happily say "there are no tables" in that narrow sense and then insist nothing was ever substantively at stake. Both sides know each other's moves cold, nobody is confused, and it still goes nowhere.

My guess is the impasse isn't about objects at all but about what ontology is even for: A treats it as inventory-taking, finding out what the world's furniture is, while B treats it as bookkeeping — a choice about which quantifier to speak, judged by convenience, a bit like chosing a coordinate system. I also keep pushing a test and can't tell if it begs the question: imagine we met speakers whose "there are" only covers things that last more than a day, so they'd deny the sandcastle and accept the table, and imagine we could translate them perfectly. If the disagreement evaporates under translation, it's verbal; if each side still wants to call the other wrong, it's real.

So what kind of stalemate is this, actually — verbal, a substantive fight about which meaning of "exists" is the natural or joint-carving one, two different questions going under one label, or just bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Is my translation test useful or circular in a way I'm missing? And given whatever diagnosis you give, what would count as progress here, or should we expect none and why?
