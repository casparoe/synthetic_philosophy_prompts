I'm running a discussion section for intro ethics next week and I've paired Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" with the torture-vs-dust-specks thing — one person tortured for fifty years versus an astronomically large number of people each getting a barely-noticeable speck of dust in the eye for a second or two. Most of my students flatly refuse to aggregate the specks, and I want to push them on why, because "the number is big" isn't an answer they'll accept back.

A friend of mine, also a phil person, invented a distinction I keep chewing on and I want to test it against the story. Call a harm narratable if you could pick it out in a life and tell a story about it — this happened to me, here, and it was bad — and sub-narratable if it's too small or too diffuse ever to be picked out: nobody can ever narrate their own speck. His claim is that the anti-aggregation intuition isn't really tracking intensity at all, it's tracking narratability, and the specks case just smuggles in a heap of harms that aren't narratable. His test: re-run the torture case as one person whose fifty years consist entirely of sub-narratable micro-pangs, none of them ever quite enough to notice individually. If narratability is doing the work, this has become a trivial harm, which is absurd. So either narratability has to piggyback on intensity, or it proves too much.

So read the story through that. Omelas is close to the mirror image of the specks case — one intense, maximally narratable suffering bought with the happiness of a whole city — and Le Guin obviously wants us on the side of the ones who walk away. But is that a stance about aggregation, or about something else entirely: that the child is being used as a means, or that the suffering is deliberately produced and kept where nobody has to look? Here's the bit I can't settle. The ones who walk away don't help the child at all; the kid stays in that locked cellar either way. If they're anti-aggregation purists, isn't leaving just an expressive gesture — and is that a problem for my reading, or does it show the story's moral was never consequentialist to begin with?

Where I think the story might resist my friend: my own worry is that Le Guin's whole rhetorical set-up — you never see the city's happiness in the aggregate, you see one neglected child in a basement — is exactly the narratability bias he's describing, just weaponised. If the price of Omelas were that every single citizen felt one faint pinch a day, would there even be a story? I want to know whether that's a critique of Le Guin or a thing the story already knows about itself.

Which scenes would you put in front of eighteen-year-olds for this — the visit to the cellar, the moment where the ones who leave seem to know where they're going, whatever else you'd pick — and where does the story push back on the framework instead of illustrating it? Don't give me quotes from the text, I've got it right here.
