I am putting together a position paper on moral progress, and before I commit to the view in print I want the view itself audited. Below are the things I take myself to believe. I cannot locate the strain myself — three readers have pointed at three different places, and I trust none of them — so please do not assume I know which item is the weak one, and do not assume I have told you which I would be willing to surrender. I have not decided, and I would rather you told me what each option costs.

(1) Progress realism, externalist. Some moral changes are improvements rather than mere changes, and the standard that makes them improvements is not constituted by whatever moral view we happen to occupy later. The abolition of chattel slavery was an improvement, and it would still have been one even if every subsequent society had come to approve of slavery again.

(2) The circle, and no principled terminus. Most of that progress has had the shape of an expanding circle: moral standing extended to beings who were previously outside it. I also hold a stronger claim, which I call *no principled terminus*: no candidate boundary feature — species membership, nationality, distance in time, a threshold of cognitive sophistication — can do genuine boundary work, because in case after case the differences that once looked decisive turned out to be differences of degree, or of accident.

(3) Subtractive progress. Some of the changes I am most confident were improvements were not inclusions at all but deletions. Honor, purity, rank, and retributive desert have largely lost their status as considerations that count in favour of or against acting. I do not want to redescribe these as expansions of the circle, because nothing was let in; something was thrown out.

(4) Genealogy, and the coming-into-view reply. I think the best causal account of why the circle widened is material rather than argumentative: literacy and print, commerce, urban density, the pacification of everyday life by states, changes in what violence pays. I try to resist the debunking conclusion with what I call the coming-into-view reply — that those same changes also brought vastly more beings' interests into view and gave us truer empirical beliefs about them. As a test case: I count the disappearance of public torture-executions as moral progress, and I think the causes were mostly state-building and shifting sensibilities rather than better arguments.

(5) Partiality. It is permissible, and sometimes required, to weight your own children and your own projects several times more heavily than the comparable interests of strangers. I hold this as a considered judgment I will not give up, and I hold that it is not the residue of an unexpanded circle.

(6) Method, and non-rivalry. Methodologically I am committed to wide reflective equilibrium, with considered judgments about cases as data; since I am a realist, I take equilibrium to be how I get at the facts rather than what makes them facts. And I hold that standing is non-rival: extending it to new beings does not diminish the standing of anyone already inside.

What I want is an audit, ordered by severity rather than a list of equally weighted worries. For each pair or cluster that genuinely conflicts, state precisely which claims conflict, which one has to move, and what moving it would cost me elsewhere in the set. Tell me which of the apparent tensions dissolve once a distinction is drawn, and what that distinction is, since I would rather not revise anything I do not have to. If one added distinction or one further commitment would hold the whole set together, say what it is and what it would commit me to beyond what I have said. And if any two of my items are not really separate commitments, say so. Assume I know the standard moves about realism, genealogy, and debunking; skip the background, and do not point me at anything to read. Keep it under about 900 words, in continuous prose, using my numbering.
