I have spent twenty-two years writing a sequence of nine novels whose central figure is a man called Gabriel Sark, and a clause in an option agreement has at last forced me to answer a question I have been avoiding since book three: what makes a fictional character in one version of a story the same character as a character in another? It is plainly not the sum of the sentences in which he appears, because I have watched that sum be replaced almost completely whilst remaining certain — certain that my readers are certain too — that it was Sark throughout. It is not simply the name either, because I have a case below in which the name stays exactly where it was and I want to say the character underneath it has been exchanged for a different one.

I set out my verdicts below as a working novelist rather than as a philosopher, and I should like a principle, or if no single principle will do, the smallest set of candidates, that captures them — or that tells me which of them I must give up. Please do not supply a bibliography or citations; I can find those myself. What I want is the shape of the account.

1. Drift. Across the nine books every concrete thing asserted about Sark in book one has been replaced or flatly contradicted: eye colour, birthplace, parentage, profession, century, even the spelling of his Christian name. Verdict: one character throughout, and the contradictions are my errors rather than evidence of two men.

2. The swap. In the galley proofs of book six I reassigned everything — scenes, memories, relationships, traits — that had belonged to Aldous Vane to Beatrix Munn, and vice versa, leaving the names attached to the same physical descriptions. Verdict: I did not rewrite either of them; I swapped their lives. The character called Vane is now living Munn's life, and readers who knew the earlier books feel this immediately, even though no sentence about either of them is new.

3. Another hand. Suppose that after my death a novelist I admire completes a tenth book from my notes: every established fact preserved, the name preserved, the prose unmistakably not mine. Verdict: not Sark. A tribute, an impersonation, a very good piece of fan work — but not the same character, and I should want the estate to resist it.

4. Translation. The French edition renames him, moves Bristol to Marseille, and refashions every sentence. Verdict: the same character, obviously, and I should be puzzled by anyone who doubted it.

5. The promotion. In revision I deleted my protagonist Ivor entirely and gave all of Ivor's scenes, decisions and plot function to a minor character, Ruth, who keeps her own name and her own remembered past. Verdict: I have not given Ivor's part to Ruth. I have deleted Ruth and dressed Ivor in her name; the thing that walks through the last third of the book wearing Ruth's name is Ivor. My readers disagree with me here, and say that Ruth is Ruth and always was.

6. Fission. In book eight I reveal that the narrator has been conflating two men. Verdict: both were present from book one. I did not create a character in book eight; the earlier books were ambiguous between them, and book eight says what they were ambiguous about.

Two of these I know to be in trouble together. One and two involve changes of the same magnitude — the whole textual basis replaced — yet I call one persistence and the other an exchange. Worse, I am fairly sure that if I had made all the changes of case one in a single revision of book two rather than across nine books, I should have said I had written a different man and given him the same name; and I can see no reason why the rate of change should matter to what a character is, any more than it matters whether the ship's planks are drawn out one at a time or all at once, or whether the old planks are fished out of the water and rebuilt into a rival vessel.

The rule of thumb I have been using is this: a character survives a change just in case the narrative role he occupies — the work he does in the plot and in the reader's attention — is continuous across it, and the change was made either by me or by someone whose licence to alter the text traces back to me and who adds nothing of their own. Please test that rule against all six cases, repair it if it can be repaired, and tell me if it can be replaced by something cleaner that overturns one of my verdicts instead. In particular I should like to know how the second clause is meant to tell the translator from the continuator, and whether the first clause survives case six, where a single role turns out, retrospectively, to have been two.

Then two cases I have not settled. In a drawer I have ninety pages of an abandoned novel whose protagonist is called Halloran; in the book now on my desk a man called Seddon has been given several of Halloran's scenes almost verbatim, including a childhood memory of a red bicycle. Is Seddon Halloran under a new name, or a different man who has inherited some scenes? And: if my estate licenses a prequel written by someone who has read only the published novels and none of my notes, is the boy in that book Sark as a child, or merely a character constructed to resemble him?

Please state the resulting principle precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause, give its verdicts on the two unsettled cases, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and what it would cost me to abandon them. Finally, since a solicitor is waiting on an answer: whatever account you settle on, what would it imply for how the words "the character Gabriel Sark" ought to be defined in a contract, and is there any account you would trust to do that work?
