I've more or less converged on the parity view about lying vs. misleading: if I deliberately get you to form a false belief by saying something literally true in a context I engineered so you'd draw the wrong conclusion, I've done the same thing to you as if I'd just asserted the false thing, and the "I didn't technically lie" move is mostly a way of keeping the upside of deceiving you while keeping your self-image (and your reputation in circles where lying is low-status) intact. I come at this from the old rationalist angle where speech is evidence-injection: what I'm choosing when I choose an utterance is the posterior I expect you to end up with, and both moves let me pick that posterior, so the difference in literal truth value looks like an implementation detail. I also think the asymmetry view gets a lot of its pull from cases that smuggle in extra differences -- the liar is usually also being blatant, dominating, obviously contemptuous of you -- and once you hold those fixed the gap definately shrinks.

So: what would count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones someone who defends the asymmetry would themselves concede as counting against them, versus which they'd dismiss as changing the subject.

The one argument that actually worries me, and which I can't tell is a real defeater or something I've already neutralized without noticing, goes like this. Misleading recruits your own reasoning: you build the false belief, using inference steps you'd endorse, and afterwards I can say "I told you the truth" and there's no apology to extract and no account to give. That looks like it could be worse, not equal -- I've made you complicit in your own deception and kept the exit. But it also cuts the other way, and this is where I lose the thread: the misleader leaves a true sentence in the shared record, which third parties can still use, while the liar pollutes the record. And there's the consent asymmetry, which is the bit that really bugs me: you can opt into being misled -- poker, haggling, "don't tell me about the surprise," a negotiation where everyone knows everyone is posturing -- but opting into being lied to seems not to work, authorization to assert falsehoods at me appears to break the practice rather than waive the wrong. That smells like assertion carrying a norm that implicature doesn't, whatever you think the norm is (knowledge, truth, justified-ness, I don't care which), and if it does, my parity claim is in trouble. Tell me whether that argument survives, and what exactly it would take for it to fail.

I'd also like the equilibrium layer, because I think that's where the answer is and I can't work out the sign of it. In a population where the loophole is widely used, hearers rationally start discounting literal content, and then: does truthful assertion become more valuable, so that misleading is free-riding on a public good it erodes (asymmetry vindicated, and my view is the thing doing the eroding), or does the record stop doing work anyway, so that the "at least the sentence was true" defense collapses and parity wins after all? Walk it out at the population level, not just for a single exchange.
