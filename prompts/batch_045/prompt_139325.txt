I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory and I'm finally drafting the chapter on the boundary problem for my thesis, the one where I have to say who exactly has to be included for a decision to count as democratically legitimate. Before it goes to my supervisor, and before I inflict it on an undergraduate reading group I'm leading next week, I'd like an audit of my own package rather than a survey of the literature, because every time I write it down I get the feeling that two of the planks are quietly doing incompatible work. Here is where I think I stand. (a) The ground of inclusion is affectedness, spelled out counterfactually: someone has a claim to a say over a decision just in case that decision, together with the enforcement apparatus behind it, foreseeably leaves their prospects for a decent life worse in expectation than they would have been under any feasible alternative the body could have adopted. I like that formulation because it keeps out people who are set back only in their preferences or only through background conditions nobody controls. (b) No bootstrapping: the constituency has to be fixed by the principle, prior to and independently of the body's own procedures, because a group that gets to decide who counts as one of its own can entrench itself against the very people it owes an answer to. (c) Case verdict: long-term residents with no route to citizenship, guest workers on renewable multi-year permits, say, must be included in the demos of the polity where they live and work; short-term visitors need not be, even when some particular decision, a sudden visa change or a new departure tax, sets them back substantially. (d) Children now, and people in the distant future, have claims to inclusion, exercised by proxies or trustees duty-bound to represent interests that are partly indeterminate. (e) On why democracy matters at all I am a coercion person rather than a self-development person: the point is to make coercive power answerable to those who have to live under it, not to give everyone the experience of participating. (f) The unit I care about is a single standing demos with an open-ended agenda, a state or something state-like; I don't want issue-by-issue constituency shuffling, since a body whose membership changed with every agenda item could not deliberate, build a record, or be held accountable over time. (g) Institutional verdict: a jointly governed transboundary regulator, a basin commission whose allocation decisions are enforced by two national legal systems and settle the livelihoods of a downstream district, can be legitimate without a directly elected assembly of its own, provided each member state's internal demos is in order.

What I want from you is the audit, not a lecture on the literature. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it: my supervisor thinks (a) and (f) cannot both be true, and a friend in the reading group thinks (c) is straightforwardly inconsistent with (a), and I suspect both of them have picked the wrong target, so please discriminate rather than flagging everything. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. For each, say precisely what would have to give, what the cheapest available revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package: in particular I want to know whether any of the fixes works only by making one plank redundant, i.e. by quietly collapsing it into another, since that would mean I have been paying for a commitment I don't really need. Then tell me which single revision cleans up the most, and which plank is load-bearing for the rest. One last thing: I have an inchoate worry I can't turn into an argument that (a)'s counterfactual test behaves oddly when it is applied to (d), and I'd like to know whether that is a real defect or just my own confusion about how the comparison class is supposed to work.
