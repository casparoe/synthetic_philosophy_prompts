I chair the Dual-Use Research Review Board of a national biomedical funding agency, and I need a decision memo I can circulate to the other six members before Tuesday's meeting. Please draft it, and please write it in American English throughout — spelling, punctuation, and idiom.

The Board's governing regulation permits a proposed project to proceed only if two conditions are jointly satisfied: (C1) the expected public-health benefit cannot be achieved by any materially safer alternative method; and (C2) the containment, personnel, and oversight plan reduces the risk of accidental or deliberate release to a level the Board judges acceptable. The regulation says nothing about how seven members' views on C1 and C2 are to be combined into a decision. Our long-standing practice has been to take a single up-or-down vote on the application as a whole and to have the chair write up the reasons afterward.

The application now before us proposes to generate and characterize mammalian-transmissible variants of a respiratory pathogen in order to test broadly protective vaccine candidates; the applicant maintains that no materially safer method yields comparable immunogenicity data and that its containment plan exceeds the applicable standard. Our members are a medical virologist, an evolutionary virologist, a biosafety officer, a bioethicist, a former agency program officer, a community representative, and an agency lawyer who also advises us on the audit standard our decisions are measured against.

Because I expected trouble, I had the clerk record last month's ballots proposition by proposition as well as on the application as a whole. Every member voted consistently, but the result was this:

- C1: 4 yes, 3 no.
- C2: 4 yes, 3 no.
- "The application should proceed": 3 yes, 4 no.

Three members accepted both conditions and voted to proceed; one accepted C1 only; one accepted C2 only; two accepted neither. So a majority accepts each of the two conditions the regulation lays down, while a different majority — built from a different coalition — rejects the application. One member tells me this pattern has a name in the literature on collective decision-making (she calls it the discursive dilemma or doctrinal paradox) and that there is a great deal written about it. I do not want a survey of that literature or any citations: I have read enough of it to know it does not settle what my board should do, and in any case I need something we can act on. Reason from the structure of this case.

Four things complicate the choice, and I want all four addressed.

First, our decisions are reviewed by the agency's Inspector General, who asks only whether the published statement of reasons shows the stated conditions to have been satisfied. Whichever procedure we adopt, the reasons we publish have to survive that review, and a statement that asserts both conditions while announcing rejection invites an awkward question.

Second, expertise is lopsided. Two members are plainly the best judges of containment, two of the necessity question, and the remaining three hold no relevant technical view at all. Deciding premise by premise effectively hands each premise to the two people who know most about it — which several members regard as a feature and which I regard as something to think hard about, since it makes a single member decisive on a premise she may be wrong about.

Third, the community representative explains that her two "no" votes are not two independent judgments. She holds that the regulation is incomplete: in her view, consultation with the communities near the two proposed sites is a further necessary condition, and the up-or-down vote is the only place on the ballot where she can register that view, since no proposition asks about it. She has said she would vote to reject even if both stated conditions were met.

Fourth, the bioethicist has said openly that if we adopt premise-by-premise voting she will vote against whichever condition she thinks is pivotal whenever she wants the project stopped, and she sees nothing improper in that. Members are under no obligation to vote sincerely, and I would like the memo to say what our procedure should assume about this.

A subcommittee member has also circulated a proposed standing rule for the Board's adoption. Under it, we would continue to decide by majority vote on the application as a whole; we would be required to record the majority's verdict on each individual condition in our statement of reasons; and where the recorded verdicts on the conditions jointly entail the opposite of the outcome we reached, we would have to state in writing which condition we are treating as unsatisfied and why, and to bring the item back for a fresh vote within twelve months — with the proviso that where the applicant's deadline does not permit a fresh vote, the application is simply rejected. I want this proposal evaluated on its merits, not treated as a footnote to the choice between premise-based and conclusion-based voting.

Two practical constraints. We are 21 days into a 60-day statutory clock; the applicant can still amend its protocol, so approving with conditions, rejecting, and inviting a revised application are all live outcomes, and any rule we adopt now will be the rule we operate under for future applications that get roughly forty minutes each. And we cannot rewrite the regulation: I take it that we may not formally replace its two conditions with a balancing test, though I would like your view on how much room is left for an all-things-considered judgment inside the up-or-down vote.

The memo should run to roughly 1,200 words or less and should contain: a header block; a short opening recommendation paragraph that states both the standing aggregation rule you want us to adopt and the disposition you recommend for this application; the reasoning for each; one paragraph laying out the strongest case for the procedure you are rejecting, stated well enough that its defenders would recognize it; and, as a separate annex, the exact wording of the statement of reasons you think we should publish for this application under your recommended rule, in about 150 words. Do not sit on the fence between procedures — give me a bottom line and a rule my members can vote on.
