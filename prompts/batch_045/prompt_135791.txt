I have come to accept illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the position, as its defenders use the term, that there are no phenomenal properties, that what introspection targets is perfectly real, and that introspection nevertheless misrepresents those states as having the features we call qualitative: intrinsicality, ineffability, the sense of acquaintance and of not being able to be wrong about it. I hold the non-eliminativist version, and I would rather not be argued out of it by someone who has mistaken it for the claim that nothing is going on.

What I want is a map of its exposure: what would have to be true, or be observed, for this to be false. Please rank the defeaters — fatal, serious, awkward, merely verbal — rather than inventory objections, and say which of them an illusionist would concede as counting against the view, as opposed to ones only its critics find forceful.

Two families worry me and I want them handled explicitly. The first is the collapse worry: that illusionism either quietly becomes realism about relational or structural qualitative properties — a quality-space view under a thinner name, which would make the dispute verbal — or else becomes eliminativism about the states themselves, leaving nothing for the illusion to be an illusion of. The second is the relocation worry: that a mechanical account of the misrepresentation leaves the original hard problem standing one level down, since explaining why there is anything it is like to be undergoing the illusion looks uncomfortably like the problem the view was introduced to dissolve. Tell me which of these is the live risk and which is a philosopher's parlour trick.

I do not trust abstract dialectic here, so please run the following probe and take it seriously as a test. Suppose a mature neuroscience allows two interventions. Call the first re-targeting: detaching the introspective system from the states it normally monitors and pointing it at arbitrary ones — another person's sensory state relayed through an interface, one's own state that never reaches report, the internal state of an artificial system. Call the second silencing: disabling that system while leaving discrimination, memory, and action with respect to the same states intact. Now suppose that under re-targeting the full package appears — the report of a determinate character, the ineffability, the acquaintance, the incorrigibility — and that under silencing the report of what-it's-like-ness vanishes while discriminative and evaluative behaviour is largely unchanged. Which of these outcomes, and which patterns of dependence in them — on the richness of the target state, on whether it is globally broadcast, on whether it has any downstream use — does illusionism need, which would merely embarrass it, and which would falsify it? And if the honest answer is that illusionism predicts all of them, say where the line falls between a theory that has made a risky prediction and a theory that has explained away every possible datum. Consider in particular what should happen if the illusion appears only for states that are themselves experiences, or if silencing fails to remove it because there is no dissociable mechanism to disable.

Then turn to what we arguably already have. Blindsight, change and inattentional blindness, confabulation and choice blindness, sensory substitution, the reports of people with unusually extended or newly acquired discrimination, interventions that systematically alter what people say about their own experience: which of these should already have moved me, in which direction, and how far?

State plainly what illusionism uniquely predicts, including about inversion and unknown-fact cases, and whether any creature in our epistemic position could in principle check it. Give me the strongest steelman of the anti-illusionist case in the terms I would find hardest to dismiss: I discount conceivability arguments and anything that bottoms out in how things seem to the speaker, and what would move me most is a case in which introspective judgement demonstrably tracks something the misrepresentation story has no room for. Make that crux explicit rather than burying it. Finally, if you conclude that illusionism is very well insulated, tell me whether that is a defect in it, given what its rivals risk — and whether the version that survives every defeater you have listed is still a substantive view or has been qualified into something I should stop holding. A thousand words that engage the probe will serve me better than two thousand that survey the literature.
