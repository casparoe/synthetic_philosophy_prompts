I dont know if this is the right place to ask this, but I've been stuck on it for a couple of weeks and my reading group is useless about it.

I want to know what a certain professional routine actually presupposes. The routine: someone proposes what they call an analysis of a concept — necessary and sufficient conditions, I think the phrase is, the idea being that you list the conditions and everything that meets all of them is an instance of the thing and nothing else is. Someone else then produces a case that meets all the conditions but obviously isnt an instance of the thing (or obviously is one but misses a condition). The first person patches the analysis with an extra clause. Everyone treats the project as still alive. Repeat for sixty years.

Two unwritten rules I've noticed where I am. First, there's a kind of budget: one extra clause per counterexample is fine, but if a single case forces you to add two, people start treating the whole analysis as in trouble, even though nobody has ever said this out loud and nobody could tell you why the number is one. Second, you are not allowed to respond to a counterexample by saying the concept just has fuzzy edges and there's no analysis to be had. That reply is permitted for a short list of sanctioned concepts and forbidden for everything else. Also, counterexamples are always introduced as it being intuitively clear that this case is or isnt an X, with no evidence offered that anyone shares the intuition, and with no one ever asking.

Here's a small example I made up, in case it helps make it concrete. Someone proposes: a person cheats iff they break a rule of some practice in order to gain an advantage within that practice. Then: an athlete who deliberately plays badly because someone paid them to throw the match — rule broken, but the advantage is outside the game. A poker player who honestly miscounts her chips, ends up with more than she is owed, notices, and says nothing — advantage within the game, maybe no rule broken, no plan. A student who copies from a textbook during a closed-book exam in full view of the proctor, not hiding it at all. Each of those seems to need a different kind of patch, and covering all three at once needs more than one new clause, which by the local rules means the analysis is in trouble — and yet everyone in that room can still tell you instantly whether any new case someone dreams up tomorrow is cheating.

Meanwhile the same people who do this will tell you, about art, or about games, that it's a family resemblance concept and that looking for a definition is a philosophical mistake. And they'll tell you that whether some clear liquid in a glass is water isn't settled by any checklist of features at all but by what the stuff is made of.

So: what view of concepts, or of what a theory of some concept is even supposed to be, does this routine presuppose? Does it strictly require the classical picture — concepts have definitions, essences, sharp boundaries — or could the same routine be run just as happily on a picture where a concept is a prototype, or a cluster of remembered cases, or a bundle of analogies? I've been reading Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences on the side, where the claim is, as near as I can tell, that all thinking is analogizing to stored examples and that categories have no fixed essence underneath. If that's even half right, does the whole routine rest on a mistake? Or can it be reinterpreted as something else doing real work?

Then the bit I really can't get straight: is it actually inconsistent to spend careers hunting definitions of knowledge while insisting that art has none, or is there a principled line between the two kinds of concept that the practice is implicitly drawing? And what would have to change in how the work is done if the classical picture were dropped — would counterexamples stop counting as refutations, and if so what would replace them as the thing a counterexample does?
