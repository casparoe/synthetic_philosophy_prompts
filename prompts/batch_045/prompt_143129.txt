I'm a PhD student in comparative politics, and I'm writing the framing section of a paper on what kind of concept democratic backsliding is: whether it picks out a real and measurable regime dynamic, or whether it functions in practice as a term of disapproval that comparativists attach to elected governments whose policies, constituencies, or civilizational self-description they happen to dislike. Instead of another literature-positioning paragraph, I want to build that section around the joint statement two well-informed, good-faith participants could sign at the end of an adversarial collaboration: one an erosion theorist, who treats backsliding as a distinctive institution-level process by which elected incumbents dismantle, incrementally and in legal form, the guarantees that keep electoral turnover a live possibility; the other a contestability skeptic, who grants that the observable markers are real but holds that the concept as deployed is not separable from the ideological commitments of those applying it, that its membership tests are applied asymmetrically across the ideological direction of the government, and that a good deal of what gets classified as erosion is better described as majoritarian re-founding that carries its own democratic warrant.

Please draft that statement, as four parts.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: what both camps accept about the phenomenon and about the four cases my paper covers (Hungary under Orbán after 2010, Poland's Constitutional Tribunal and ordinary-courts crisis of 2015-16, Turkey after the 2017 constitutional referendum, and the United States since 2020). Include agreement about what does not settle the question: not how many scholars apply the label, not the popularity of the government, not a single dramatic episode.

2. A disagreements table. Each residual disagreement should appear twice, once in the erosion theorist's own preferred wording and once in the skeptic's, such that each would sign its own column without wincing. Annotate each row with whether you take it to be a genuine disagreement, a verbal one, or an artifact of the two sides choosing different units of analysis. I also want the rows sorted by kind: disagreements about the cases, disagreements about the definition, and disagreements about what the concept is for — description, prediction, normative condemnation, or triggering a legal or supranational response such as conditionality proceedings.

3. The cruxes, and for each the case or piece of evidence both sides would accept as bearing on it, together with each side's prediction stated in advance. My candidates, which you should treat as candidates and reorganize or discard as you see fit: whether the right definition is institutional and procedural or counterfactual in outcome, i.e., whether the incumbents must actually reduce the risk of turnover or whether it suffices that their side acquires a durable structural advantage; whether democracy in the definition is thin and electoral, organized uncertainty about who governs, or thick enough to require an independent judiciary, an independent press, and a non-partisan civil service; whether intent or foreseeability matters at all, or whether only effects may count; whether the classification should be anchored to a country's own prior baseline or to a cross-national standard, which is what decides whether a high-baseline case can backslide at all; and the grain problem, that each individual institutional change taken alone has a defensible rationale, so that the concept needs some principled aggregation rule rather than a pile of anecdotes.

I also want two devices of my own tested inside this section. The first is the mirror case: hold the institutional moves fixed and invert the ideology and coalition of the government performing them, then ask whether the classification changes. If it changes, is that evidence that the concept is contaminated, or only that reasonable priors about which governments are likely to erode are doing legitimate work? The second is the performativity of the label: whether publicly classifying a government as backsliding alters the probability of the very outcomes the concept was introduced to track, by cueing elites, mobilizing oppositions, or giving incumbents a pretext, and whether that is an argument for classificatory restraint or an argument that the concept has genuine causal power. A related question belongs here too: who has standing to classify — domestic oppositions, domestic courts, supranational bodies, comparativists, or voters — and whether disagreement about standing is itself doing the work in the dispute.

4. A short closing note on what each camp would count as an observation genuinely unfavorable to its own view, and whether there is anything both agree would move the debate rather than merely restate it.

Constraints: do not adjudicate — I do not want a verdict on who is right, only the document both could sign. Do not suggest readings, authors, or sources; I need no citations and want none. Where a case is used, keep to the broad contours any comparativist would grant, since I do not want the statement to turn on a date, a vote count, or a poll. If one of my candidate cruxes dissolves under scrutiny, say so and explain why. And if at some point you cannot find a claim both camps would genuinely endorse, mark the gap instead of papering over it with a formulation vague enough to be vacuous. Around 1,000 to 1,400 words.
