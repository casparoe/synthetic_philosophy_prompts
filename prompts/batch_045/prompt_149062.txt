I'm a PhD student in computer science, working on credit assignment and self-improvement dynamics in deep reinforcement learning, and for the last two terms I've been sitting in on a philosophy department reading group on moral responsibility. Galen Strawson's Basic Argument came up last week, and I left convinced both that it is more serious than the reading group treated it and that I don't understand it. The only way I understand a claim is to rebuild it in my own vocabulary, so that is what I'm asking you for — a translation, and, more importantly, an honest account of what the translation loses. I don't need a literature survey or the state of the debate; I need the argument rebuilt in terms I can check.

The vocabulary I actually have: Markov decision processes and POMDPs; policies as parameterized functions; random initialization and optimizer dynamics; reward or loss specification; exploration noise and entropy-regularized stochastic policies; on-policy versus off-policy evaluation; credit assignment to parameters; meta-learning, including systems that learn or modify their own update rules; self-referential constructions from computability (a program that emits its own source, the recursion-theoretic result that a program can obtain and act on a description of its own code without generating an infinite regress); and fixed points of an operator applied to itself. My formal philosophy background is one seminar. If the target vocabulary lacks a term, say that rather than inventing one.

Please do the following.

1. Restate the Basic Argument premise by premise in that vocabulary. As I have it, the argument is roughly: what an agent does in a situation follows from the way the agent then is; being genuinely responsible for the action would require being responsible for being that way, at least in the relevant mental respects; and that latter responsibility is unavailable, because acquiring it would require an earlier act of self-formation performed by an agent who was already some way, which just relocates the problem. Give me the RL version of each step, name which premise is doing the real work, and say candidly whether the translation made that premise look stronger or weaker than it looks in its home idiom.

2. Handle the could-have-done-otherwise material carefully. I can think of at least three distinct things it might mean for a policy: another action has positive probability under the current policy; another action would have been produced under some reachable perturbation of the parameters or the environment; or a different initialization or update trajectory would have converged to a policy that acts differently here. Say which of these, if any, the argument must rule out, and whether Strawson's claim that the argument is indifferent to whether the underlying dynamics are deterministic comes out as obviously right, obviously wrong, or differently motivated once it is put this way.

3. Do the same for the two or three replies the reading group leaned on hardest: that responsibility might be a matter of attributability and answerability rather than of ultimate desert; that an agent can become responsible for what it is through a sustained process of deliberate self-shaping; and that the demand expressed in the second premise is simply incoherent, so the premise should be rejected rather than answered. Which of these survive the translation intact, and which only survive by changing the subject?

4. Here is the escape route I keep coming back to, and I want you to take it seriously before you dismantle it. The regress seems to assume that authorship has to be a linear chain terminating in an unmoved mover: action, then policy, then the process that produced the policy, then the process that produced that. But my field gets self-determination without a regress all the time, via self-reference: a program can act on its own source code, and a self-modifying learner can converge to a configuration that is a fixed point of its own update operator, so that the thing that produced the system is, in a nontrivial sense, the system itself. Suppose we model an agent as a temporally extended learning process that rewrites its own update rule whenever its own internal criterion certifies that the rewrite improves its long-run return, and suppose that process converges to a stable configuration that endorses — in the sense of re-selecting — the very update process that produced it. Then self-creation looks like a fixed point rather than an infinite chain, and the agent looks diachronically responsible for what it is. Reconstruct this as charitably as you can in the vocabulary above, then tell me where it breaks. In particular: does Strawson's third premise simply reapply one level up, to the architecture and the initial criterion, and if so does my argument have any answer to that, or does it just postpone the objection?

5. Give me the residue report. Which components of the original argument translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? Two things I especially want your view on. First, whether the normative layer — desert, praiseworthiness, the fact that this is supposed to be a moral conclusion rather than a causal one — has any expression in a framework whose only normative notion is a reward signal that somebody else wrote down. Second, and more suspicious: my vocabulary always comes with an external author, since someone specifies the reward, the architecture, and the initialization. Does translating the argument into RL terms therefore smuggle in a designer and beg the question against the libertarian or the robust compatibilist? For each piece of residue, say whether you think it exposes a limitation of my framework or an unclarity in the argument.

6. Finally, tell me which premise or step you think I am most likely to have misunderstood, given that I am reading this from inside a different field. Assume I would rather be corrected than reassured.
