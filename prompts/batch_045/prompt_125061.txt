Hi — I'm the only paid archivist at a county records archive (plus two rotating volunteers and a summer intern), and I make the call on maybe eight to ten offered collections a month: what we accession, what we accession with conditions, what we send somewhere else, what we let go. There's a state retention schedule that legally forces some categories to be destroyed after a set period, a county records commission that meets quarterly and mostly rubber-stamps whatever I put in front of it, and a six-person community advisory panel of volunteers that meets monthly when it meets at all. Realistically I get about half an hour per collection and no budget for oral histories.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can run on an offer, in order, on one page, that actually takes seriously the idea that an archive isn't a passive residue of the past but a machine that decides who ends up in the historical record at all — and that the deepest silences aren't the gaps in what we hold but the stuff that was never written down, or was written down by whoever had power *about* the people who didn't.

I've been messing around with two home-made distinctions and I want you to tell me wether they survive being turned into a checklist or whether they're too cute to use. First: *never-made* vs *never-sent* vs *never-theirs* — records that were never created in the first place, records that existed but never reached any archive, and records that exist but contain a person only as described by an institution that had power over them (intake forms, arrest books, a judge's case notes, truancy complaints). Second: something I'm calling the *counter-collection note* — for every collection I accession I write half a paragraph in the finding aid naming three kinds of people who were definitely present in whatever the collection documents but who left nothing in it, plus one guess about where their traces would actually be today (a church basement, the back office of a defunct ethnic newspaper, somebody's old phone). I genuinely don't know if that note is a safeguard or just a way for me to feel better, and I don't know what I'm supposed to do with it once I've written it.

So: give me the ordered questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it has to kick the decision to a person because it's a values call and not a factual one, and what gets written in the file either way. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three offers I'm roughly expecting: (a) forty boxes of papers from a juvenile-court judge who served from the 60s to the 90s — decades of case notes about mostly poor and mostly Black families, where the families' own words survive only inside his summaries; his daughter will donate them but wants the collection named for him and sealed for twenty-five years. (b) A shoebox from a tenants' union in a mostly immigrant neighborhood — flyers in three languages, some photos, a few printed screenshots of a Facebook group, no minutes, no donor agreement, and two members are scared of a name list ending up somewhere it could be used against them. (c) A set of digitized county booking photographs from the 70s through the 90s, offered to us as a "historical" collection.

Please don't soften whichever of those three comes out worst. I have an obvious emotional answer on one of them and I'd rather be told I'm wrong. Also tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do, and which direction it will misfire in over and over. No need to name theorists or cite anything — I can't chase references anyway. And tell me where your own reasoning here is most likely to break: what argument would make you say, no, don't run this at all.

End the whole thing with one line: \answer{...} containing just the short name of the single step I'm not allowed to cut if I tell you we only have time for four of them.
