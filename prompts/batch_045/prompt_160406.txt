I'm consolidating my position on the special composition question — the question of when some things compose, or add up to, a single further thing — into one chapter-length statement, and I'd like the package audited before it goes to my committee. I'm not after a survey of the literature and I don't want to be pointed at readings; I want to know whether the set of things I've committed myself to is stable. Here it is.

A. Composition is restricted, and the line is life. Some pluralities compose something and some don't; the ones that do are the ones whose members' activities jointly constitute a life (setting aside the degenerate case of a one-membered plurality). So organisms are in; statues, ships, tables, heaps, molecules, crystals, planets and galaxies are out. And I can't accept the answer that nothing ever composes anything, because I take it as a fixed datum that I exist and that I am a composite material thing.

B. But I don't think the rest of what we say should be paraphrased away. When the department administrator says the table is in the next room, what she says is true; when a chemist says the sample contains benzene molecules, that's true too; when a geologist says there is a mountain range along the coast, also true. True outright, not true in virtue of some hidden sentence about particles arranged thus-and-so. And I deny that this forces me to stock my ontology with tables, molecules and mountain ranges.

C. Composition is sharp. For any plurality, either it composes something or it doesn't — no thing that vaguely exists, no borderline case of a thing's being there. Nor am I mollified by the reply that there is a sharp cutoff we could never locate: if there's a line, I want it to be the kind of line that could in principle be found, not merely guaranteed by logic and permanently hidden from us.

D. A constraint I've come to rely on, and have started calling the Snapshot Constraint: whether the xs compose something at a time t is settled by what the xs are, and how they are related and interacting, over an arbitrarily short interval around t. What happened to them last decade, and what anyone plans to do with them next week, are irrelevant. This is, I take it, what makes replacement cases tractable: you shouldn't need an object's history to say what is in the shed.

E. I am not identical with the human animal that constitutes me. In the permanent-vegetative-state case the animal is still there and I am not; in the cerebrum-transplant case I go with the cerebrum and the animal stays behind. So two things can be in the same place at the same time, made of the same matter, and differ in how they persist.

F. Methodologically, I want one informative necessary condition — not a list, not a brute "some pluralities just do," and not whatever ordinary speakers would say offhand. My principal objection to the everything-composes answer is the problem of the many: on any cloud or mountainside there are countless slightly different pluralities of matter, each as good a candidate as the next for being the thing there, and I don't want the coincident crowd that comes with admitting all of them.

G. Finally, some case verdicts I have already written down, which I treat as constraints on the view rather than applications of it. (i) A mammalian body in profound hypothermic arrest — no metabolism, no neural activity, nothing organized at the level of the whole, for the better part of an hour — and then rewarmed and revived: one and the same organism throughout, nothing lapsing out of existence and nothing beginning. (ii) The bacteria in my gut are not parts of me, notwithstanding that nothing in my metabolism runs without them. (iii) A colonial organism — picture something siphonophore-like, though I don't want to lean on the biology — gradually divided across an afternoon into two spatially separate, self-maintaining halves: one organism at the start, two at the end, and a determinate moment of division, although I cannot tell you which moment it is and I don't think the biology settles it. (iv) A brain-dead body kept perfused for weeks, its cells alive and its organs still functioning in coordination: no organism there any more.

What I want is an audit, in this order. First, which pairs or clusters of these genuinely conflict and which only look as though they do. Second, the genuine ones ranked by how much damage they do to the package as a whole — not listed as so many problems of equal size — with a word on why each outranks or is outranked by the others. Third, for each, the specific commitment that would have to move and what moving it would cost me elsewhere in the set. And last, whether there is any version of the package in which all seven survive, and if not, which single revision puts out the most fires at once.
