Hey — I need a poem, and I need it to actually be about something, so please treat this like a real assignment rather than a creative-writing warmup. Take the role of someone who is both a philosopher of mind who has actually taught Dennett and a formalist poet who can handle fixed form, and do some quiet planning before you start writing — a short brainstorm on where the poem's turn falls and a check that your end-word rotation really is the standard sestina rotation — instead of just launching into line one. Then give me the poem, and after it a short note (a couple hundred words, tops) saying which line the turn is on and why the envoi's pairings are the ones the poem earned.

Context: I'm TAing an intro philosophy of mind course and the last section of the semester is personal identity — teleportation cases, brain transplants, Parfit, and Dennett's "Where Am I?", the story where the surgery takes the brain out, the brain ends up suspended in a lab in Houston, the body is off walking around Berkeley wired to it by radio, and the question "where am I?" gets asked and then more or less deflated rather than answered by a discovery. I want to close the final session by reading a poem instead of handing out one more worksheet, and I want it to be a sestina: six stanzas of six lines, no rhyme at all, the standard rotation of end-words, then a three-line envoi. Use these six end-words: Houston, Berkeley, vat, hand, signal, nerve. The form should be carrying the idea — the same six words keep coming back but they land at the ends of different lines each time, so "Houston" and "Berkeley" keep surfacing in new positions while the set never changes, which is basically the situation in the story. In the envoi, pair them so each line puts a place next to something that isn't a place — Houston with hand, Berkeley with nerve, vat with signal — unless you've got a better pairing, in which case use it and defend it in one sentence. A couple of my students write poetry and will absolutely notice if the rotation is off or if you quietly start rhyming.

Here's the content, and it's not quite Dennett's plot on purpose: over the poem's six stanzas I want the link quietly re-routed in stages. First the body's own eyes and ears, in the city, real. Then the lab starts substituting — a camera on a rooftop in a third city feeds the visual channel, and nothing about the experience changes, because the wiring doesn't care where a signal came from; I've been calling this the indifference of the channel, and I want the poem to make that felt rather than stated. Meanwhile the body is still being moved by the brain's output — crossing a street, shaking a hand — and that hand is the only thing in the poem that is unambiguously in one place at one time. In the last stanza or two the speaker is told the truth about the re-routing, and being told changes nothing: the reports keep arriving from wherever the channel is pointed at, and "where" still has to be answered. Which is the point — I don't want it to collapse into "so he was in a vat all along" or any other reveal. Some hard rules: no homunculus, no soul, no inner observer with privileged access to the wiring; the speaker never gets a view from outside their own stream, so the re-routing can only show up as a change in what arrives. Nothing should announce a doctrine ("I am my brain," "I am where my body is," "the self is a convenient fiction") as the poem's own verdict, and please don't quote or paraphrase Dennett's actual story — I've got the original in the packet, I'm not asking for fan fiction of it. And one more constraint I care about: the literal question "where am I?" should never appear as a sentence anywhere in the poem, but a reader should hear it behind every stanza. Aim for something that works read aloud to twenty undergrads who are tired and want to leave.
