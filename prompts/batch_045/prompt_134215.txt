I am a novelist, and the manuscript in front of me turns on a decision my narrator makes at its centre, so I have spent some months reading in formal decision theory in the hope of rendering her deliberation without cheating. When I described the scene to an economist friend, he dismissed the difficulty with what he assured me is the standard line: risk aversion is not a separate ingredient in rational choice at all, it is only diminishing marginal utility of money. Give the utility function the right curvature, he said, and everything we ordinarily call fear of risk is already inside it. I have since found the same move in popular books, nearly always drawn as a single concave curve, and in each case it is made to carry the same conclusion: there is nothing here beyond arithmetic about amounts, so a person who refuses a favourable gamble is not exhibiting a distinct attitude that could be admired or despised, and there is no room for a separate vice or virtue of risk-taking. Whether I can write my narrator as reasoning coherently but with a differently set parameter, or only as confused, depends on that, so I want the analogy audited rather than the verdict re-argued.

Two things about her seem to resist the curve. She is offered a guaranteed sixty thousand against a coin flip paying nothing or a hundred and sixty thousand, and she refuses the coin flip; that much the curve handles. What it seems not to handle is that she will surrender a large improvement in the best outcome in order to secure a small improvement in the worst, at equal expected money, and that the price she sets on a given sum when it is the floor of a gamble is not the price she sets on the same sum when it is the ceiling.

My suspicion, offered as a suspicion, is that the analogy is seductive only because both pictures are drawn as a curve, while the curves are formally different objects. Money is fungible and unordered: whatever the utility function assigns to forty thousand, it assigns wherever in a fortune that sum falls. Chances are ordered. In the risk-weighted framework I have been reading, associated with Lara Buchak, the utility of an outcome is fixed independently of attitudes to risk and a separate function transforms cumulative probabilities, so the weight an outcome receives depends on how much probability sits below it, and that lever can be turned independently of the shape of u. If I have this right, risk aversion answers to a convex weighting function but to a concave utility function, so the two curves the analogy identifies actually bend in opposite directions — in which case it is not a harmless simplification but a conflation.

I would like the correspondence table first: on the money side, wealth, the unit, the marginal unit, the agent's circumstances; on the probability side, whatever plays those roles, including rank and cumulative probability. Then the disanalogies, sorted into those that bear on the conclusion my friend draws and those that do not. Then whether that conclusion survives the audit, and a better analogy if one is available.

One further silence I cannot place. My narrator minds not only that the coin may land badly but how long it hangs in the air: a gamble resolved in an afternoon and one dragging on for three years, with the same outcomes and the same probabilities, are not the same thing to her. Is that a further attitude both expected utility and its risk-weighted rival fail to represent, or a sign that the outcomes were individuated wrongly to begin with, three years of suspense being itself one of them? And if the latter, does that rescue the analogy, or hollow it out by letting an enriched outcome space do all the work the analogy claimed to do?
