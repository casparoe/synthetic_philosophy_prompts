I have spent fifteen years as a curator of South Asian collections, and four months ago my director asked me to chair the interpretation group for a complete rewrite of our permanent galleries — around four hundred object labels, sixty panel texts and our school-facing worksheets — after a trustee described the museum as a museum of Europe with a few annexes. Our board responded by adopting a framing principle, and my job is to turn it into something my writers can actually run:

*A text should not make something count as an event in the history of the world because of its effects on, or resemblance to, a European trajectory; and it should not treat any one society as the silent standard against which others are marked as early, late, failed, missing, or about-to-be.*

That is the whole of what I was given. My writers are six regional specialists with no training in historiography, working mostly in English with one fluent Persianist and one Sinologist; they have about twenty minutes per label, a seventy-word ceiling, and no realistic access to the scholarly literature on anything outside their own region. I have a community advisory panel that meets monthly, so escalating a question to them costs four to six weeks, and I cannot escalate four hundred labels to them.

We have already tried two shortcuts and they failed. The first was: delete Europe from the sentence and see whether it still says something — but it kept flagging labels for objects that entered the collection through empire, where the European connection genuinely is the reason the object is here. The second was counting mentions of continents per panel, which told us nothing we did not know.

What I need is a decision procedure: the tests to apply in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to the panel because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to work at two levels, because I think the principle bites hardest not inside sentences but in the scaffolding — gallery titles, the period names we organise the sequence by, and which objects we chose to acquire and display at all — and my writers will apply it to individual labels. Please also tell me which distinctions inside the principle have to survive being simplified for non-specialists and which can be dropped without loss.

Three cases to run it on:

Our draft label on Indian cotton textiles, which currently asks why the subcontinent did not have an industrial revolution of its own, given that it was the world's leading producer of cloth well into the eighteenth century.

Our proposed gallery spine, which is at present: Ancient Worlds — The Age of Discovery — Revolution and Industry — The Modern World.

And a label the team is proud of, on printing: it says that movable type was in use in Korea and in China centuries before Gutenberg, which was meant as our corrective. If the procedure tells me this label is also a failure, I want to be told plainly and to understand why.

Two further things. Our collection was largely assembled under empire and our provenance research is unfinished, so for some objects the only usable sources are colonial administrative ones: the procedure has to say what a writer does when the archive itself is the problem. And please separate, in your answer, what is settled from what is genuinely contested among historians and philosophers of history who work on this — including whether the board's principle itself is defensible, whether comparison of any kind can avoid a yardstick, and whether Europe is even a coherent unit to be decentring. My writers will be told which parts they may repeat as received opinion and which they must present as one view among others, so I need that distinction marked rather than smoothed over.
