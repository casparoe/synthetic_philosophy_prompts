I'm editing a short-fiction anthology on the theme of authenticity, and the commission I keep rewriting the brief for is set roughly fifteen years from now, in the world of Old Master painting. Here is the situation I want dramatized. A small museum in Siena holds the entire surviving output of an obscure seventeenth-century painter — call him Girolamo Bassani — twenty-odd canvases, mostly nocturnes, together with his letters and account books. A commercial studio that does what it calls generative reconstruction trains a model on that corpus and produces a painting, *The Ferryman's Lantern*, which matches an entry in a 1697 inventory of a collector's estate: subject, dimensions, and even the odd detail of a lantern held at shoulder height. No such painting has ever been located. The picture is exhibited openly labeled as a reconstruction, and to everyone's surprise it moves people; conservators say it is the best Bassani they have ever stood in front of.

Then I want the story to complicate itself. Partway through, a letter surfaces in which Bassani describes painting exactly that picture during a winter he rarely mentions, and says he scraped it down in disgust rather than let it out of the studio. So the work was in fact painted by the right person, at roughly the right time, and was deliberately destroyed; the canvas now on the wall was made by no one, 350 years later, and has no causal connection to Bassani beyond the traces he left in his other paintings and his correspondence. A consortium wants to sell it at auction under a new designation — neither original nor copy nor fake, but something like *realized* — and a philosopher of art has been hired to write the catalogue entry. I'd like the story to turn on her: on what she can honestly say about the picture, and on whether she will sign the text. Along the way I want the reader to feel the pull of the thought that nothing of value has been lost, since the work is perceptually just what a genuine Bassani would be, and also the pull of the opposite thought — that a painting deliberately destroyed by its maker and then reconstituted by a machine is a violation of something, even if no one can say what.

Please write the story in the third person, in about 1,200 words, in a realist register with no speculative-fiction machinery beyond the reconstruction studio itself. The characters should argue the issue through concrete decisions — what to label the picture, whether to hang it beside the real nocturnes, what to pay for it, whether to allow it to be restored — rather than through set-piece speeches, and no character should invoke philosophers by name or settle the question with a tidy moral at the end. I'd rather the story leave me genuinely unsure what I think than have it demonstrate a thesis I could have read in an encyclopedia entry on forgery.
