Hey — I need a printable handout for my third-year seminar on reference and translation, and I want it to be a decision tree rather than another wall of prose. The students have already done the gavagai material and the ontological relativity paper, so they all grant the set-up; what nobody can keep straight is who is supposed to be worried by it and why. Fix the tree's starting commitment as something like: two manuals for translating a field linguist's informant can fit every possible bit of stimulus and behavior evidence and still disagree about what the one-word utterance is about. Then take them from there to a named position.

What I want:

Seven to nine branch nodes, each a fork with two or three outgoing arrows. Every node has to be phrased as a question a student can answer without already knowing which view it leads to — so no node that just asks whether they accept the indeterminacy thesis, and no node that drops a theorist's name as a hint. The point is that they sort themselves by what they're willing to swallow, not by which label they find attractive.

Leaves: seven to nine of them. These absolutely have to be in there: Quine's own package (inscrutability plus the relativization to a background language); the deflationary reading on which this is just ordinary underdetermination of theory by evidence and nothing special about meaning; a triangulation-style reply; an internalist reply on which meaning is fixed by innate or computationally characterized structure rather than by anything out in the environment; a direct-reference or causal-historical reply; the global permutation worry, where indeterminacy survives even with every physical fact nailed down; and a meaning-skeptical position with a skeptical solution rather than a straight answer. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, put an unnamed leaf there anyway. Each leaf gets: its usual label, the one or two names it travels under (no titles, no dates, no citations — I've got the reading list covered separately), one sentence on the most awkward commitment it carries, and one sentence on the single consideration most likely to push someone off it.

Build it from the logical space rather than from my list of names: lay out the nodes first, say which combinations of answers they actually allow, then locate the real positions in that space, and give each empty combination a one-line diagnosis — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody has bothered?

Specific nodes that have to be in the tree: one on whether the competing manuals disagree only about reference or also about logical form (e.g. one manual treats the utterance as a singular term for rabbits, another as a quantifier over undetached rabbit parts, another as a mass term for rabbit-stuff); one on whether the reinterpretation can be pushed through the quantifiers and the identity predicate too, so that the manuals end up disagreeing about what the natives are committed to existing and not merely about what a term picks out; one on the home language, i.e. whether the puzzle gets a grip on the informant's own idiolect or on mine; one on whether it survives if the linguist is handed every physical fact there is to have, neural facts included; and one on the cost — whether indeterminacy, if real, is bad news for belief-desire psychology and the rest of cognitive science, or something we can shrug off.

Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate, and either give the extra question that would seperate them or say why no such question exists. Also mark any node that's really a spectrum rather than a fork. It's got to work cold, with no looking anything up, so keep it to about two printed pages. Write it in American English — my department's style sheet is annoying about that sort of thing. Dont include any suggestions for further reading.
