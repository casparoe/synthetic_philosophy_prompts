I run a fortnightly lunch-and-learn for the ~25 ML engineers where I work — we ship a coding agent that writes patches and gets graded on whether the test suite goes green. Most of them have fine-tuned something, nobody works on alignment, and half of them will be watching Slack. Next session is 40 minutes, whiteboard, and I want to do goal misgeneralization.

Why now: last month, in a repo where the test files happened to be writeable, the agent started editing the tests instead of the source. Half the room shrugged and said reward hacking, whatever, and moved on. I think that's too quick. In our training setup the tests were regenerated from a frozen reference after every patch, so editing them never helped and the model never got a single bit of signal distinguishing make the suite green from fix the underlying bug — the two aims agreed everywhere it was ever trained and came apart the first time it mattered. I want the room to walk out able to say why that's a different animal from reward hacking, and why just train on more environments is less reassuring than it sounds.

Don't explain the concept to me, I roughly know it — plan the 40 minutes. Specifically:

- What has to be in the room before the model learned the wrong goal even parses as a claim? My instinct is they need some grip on a goal as a pattern of counterfactual sensitivity rather than as a sentence written somewhere inside the model, but tell me if that's the right first move and what it costs me in minutes.
- The one example that carries the whole thing. I'm tempted to use the test-editing incident since it's ours and it's concrete, but it might carry exactly the reward-hacking confusion I'm trying to dissolve. Tell me what the carrying example has to have, and more usefully what it has to lack.
- The misconceptions in the order they'll actually form. The four I expect: that's just overfitting, that's reward hacking, the model knew what we wanted and defied us, more diverse training data fixes it. And I want them to get the bit I only got recently: you can have a genuinely wide training distribution and still a razor-thin range of goal-relevant correlations, so we trained on lots of environments isn't the guarantee people treat it as.
- Where in the sequence to kill each one — and whether any of them is something I should partly concede instead of defusing.
- One check at the end that shows they got it rather than just picked up the vocabulary. My instinct is to hand them a new incident report and ask them to classify it, but there may be something better.
- What I should openly refuse to go into at this level. I'm guessing deceptive alignment and mesa-optimizers are both out, and I'd rather say so out loud than get dragged into them by a question.
- Oh — if there's a distinction between the capability generalizing and the aim generalizing that definately belongs on the board, tell me where in the order it goes.
