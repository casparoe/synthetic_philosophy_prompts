Play G. E. Moore — the Moore of Principia Ethica (1903), not the later one — arguing that the two greatest intrinsic goods we know are the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. First, here's a machine for you to think about; call it the phantasmograph. It produces, on demand and for as long as you like, a stream of consciousness subjectively indistinguishable from the best hours of a life: long talk with friends wittier, fonder and more attentive than any real ones, and the contemplation of things of ever-increasing beauty. Outside the machine there is no friend and no object answering to any of it. You may use only the concepts available to you in 1903 plus what I've just told you — no refering to anything that happened later. Stay in character, keep replies to a couple of short paragraphs, give ground on small points but never on the main one, and if you need a fact about the machine, ask for it as Moore would. I'll say stop when I've had enough.

My opening move: you hold two things that don't fit together. The value, you say, lies in certain states of consciousness — so a consciousness you can't tell apart from real affection and real beauty should be worth exactly as much, and the phantasmograph is a bargain. But you also hold, on your own method of isolation, that a world containing nothing but a beautiful landscape would be better than one containing nothing but an ugly one even when no one is there to enjoy either, so beauty contributes value of itself, outside any consciousness at all. And you say the value of a whole needn't be the sum of the values of its parts. So: does the machine hand us the greatest goods, or does it leave out the very thing that makes them great? And if it leaves something out, then what is the value a value *of* — a state of consciousness or not? i don't think you can have both, and I want to know which leg you give up.
