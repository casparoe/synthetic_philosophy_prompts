Hey — I'm on the admissions committee for a small grad program (around 400 files, nine readers, we have to produce a ranked top 20), and I've been trying to redesign the whole thing so we don't get stuck in an information cascade. Right now people call out their scores one at a time in a big room, and it's painfully obvious that by reader four or five everyone is just agreeing with whoever spoke first and loudest.

Here's the fix I've landed on. Scores go into a shared sheet, and instead of showing people the order things came in, I'll show each reader a running summary that's anonymized and shuffled — no names, no timestamps, no way to tell whether a given score was entered first or thirtieth. My reasoning is that a cascade is basically an artifact of knowing your place in line: you're reader number seven, you figure the six people before you knew something you don't, so you discount your own read and copy them. Take away the ladder and people will just go with their own judgment, and we'll get nine genuinely independent opinions instead of one opinion nine times.

Two other things I've decided. I'm going to drop the scores of anyone who submits in the last third of the window, since by then they're definately in a cascade and their ballot carries no information anyway — might as well count six people. And I'm treating prevention as the entire game, because once a cascade forms it's permanent; no realistic amount of new evidence is going to move nine people off it.

A friend in my department who does game theory says the shuffling is basically cosmetic, that I'm treating a rational response to a bad information environment as if it were a reasoning error, and that the readers I want to throw out are exactly the ones whose private information I most want. I dont really get any of that. What is she talking about? And is there an actual fix here, or is the whole thing hopeless?
