I sit on the grants committee of a small global-health foundation. We have one grant slot left and two finalists. Proposal A funds unconditional cash transfers to roughly 40,000 very poor households; our best estimate from well-being surveys is a modest, fairly durable gain in life satisfaction per household, of the kind people report as somewhat more comfortable and somewhat less anxious, with nothing dramatic at either end. Proposal B funds surgery for a condition causing severe, continuous, otherwise treatment-resistant pain in roughly 600 people — untreated, it sits at the top of the intensity range clinicians measure, and successful surgery largely removes it. On our standard cost-effectiveness arithmetic, which scores every outcome in a single unit, A wins by about an order of magnitude.

One member has circulated a note saying the comparison is illegitimate. Drawing on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, she argues that any state above a certain pain intensity counts lexically more than any quantity of merely pleasant states, so no number of mild improvements can offset even one case like B's and A's arithmetic advantage is beside the point. A second replies that our whole comparative apparatus requires mild and severe states to share one scale; sever it and we cannot compare two pain-relief proposals either, or say how many severe cases justify how many slightly-less-severe ones. A third notes that our only handle on intensity is a noisy self-report scale, and asks what to do with a case near the threshold, where on a lexical view a small measurement error flips the answer from nothing else matters to everything is tradeable again.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before Friday's vote. It should:

- recommend a concrete allocation — all to A, all to B, or a specified split — and give the reasons;
- state explicitly which of the contested claims the recommendation depends on, so we can see what we would be conceding by voting for it;
- give the committee a usable rule for future rounds where one option would relieve suffering above the intensity threshold and the other would not. I have been toying with a rebuttable presumption: such a round goes to the suffering-relieving proposal unless the forgone alternative would itself relieve suffering in the same band, with ties inside the band broken by numbers of people and duration. Adopt that rule, reject it as unworkable, or replace it with a better one;
- state the strongest argument available to whichever proposal you recommend against, in the form its advocate would actually put it.

Commit: I want a recommendation I can put to a vote, not a survey of the options. Where you are genuinely uncertain, say where and why in one line, and then still commit.
