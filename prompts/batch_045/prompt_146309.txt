I spent last night arguing with a friend about whether culture really evolves in a Darwinian way, and the argument ended up turning on her track record rather than on theory, so I want to cash it out on a case she has not ruled on. Her verdicts so far. Memes: a slang expression that sweeps a school and is dead within a year; a joke that is still recognisably the same joke after twenty retellings; a family recipe that gets tweaked each generation; a dance picked up from a clip and performed at a wedding; a chain message that people forward unaltered because it threatens bad luck if you break the chain. Not memes: flinching at a sudden noise; knowing that kettles are hot; a mnemonic one person invented and never passed to anyone; eye colour; and a compliance clause that every employee in a firm is required to paste into documents verbatim, with no variation permitted, which she classed as imposition rather than selection.

The new case, which I will call a schema lineage. In a large online community there is a story shape: first person, a mundane setup, one detail withheld, a short final line that reinterprets the rest. Nobody copies a token. What each poster inherits is a generative recipe, and every instance is built from scratch, by a different person, out of that recipe plus their own material. Two further features matter. Over fifteen years the recipe has shifted in ways that reliably increase how far an instance travels: the setups got shorter, the withheld detail moved later, the last line settled into a fixed rhythm. And for the last three years most instances have in fact been written by a language model prompted with a handful of earlier ones, while what anyone actually reads is whatever a ranking system puts in front of them; people read, react, and prompt again. So nothing is copied as a token, no human reproduces anything, the variation is machine-generated, and the sorting is done by an engineered filter — and yet the recipe has accumulated a genuine fit to that filter.

Would she say this is a meme, that is, a case of Darwinian cultural evolution? More usefully: what are the two or three rules that fit her old verdicts about equally well — something like heritable variation plus differential uptake being sufficient, a replicator requiring lineages of faithfully copied tokens, a variant's success needing to be its own rather than its host's — what does each predict here, which one do you think she is actually running, and why? Which of her old verdicts is the closest analogue, and what exactly is different about it. And where the rules that agree here would still diverge elsewhere, name the single variant of this case that would tell them apart.
