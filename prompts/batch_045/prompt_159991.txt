I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice, and for next week's session on the Allais cases I want to replace the usual survey-of-positions handout with something that actually does work: a decision tree that takes a student from a reaction they already have about the cases to the position in the logical space that their reaction commits them to, with the labels attached only at the leaves. My students split fairly evenly between those who think the Allais pattern is a bias to be explained away and those who think it is a datum that no independence axiom survives, and the argument between them goes nowhere because each side can redescribe the other's premises. I want them sorted by what they say about cases rather than by which name they are drawn to.

What I need from you is the instrument plus a short commentary on it.

The start state is deliberately thin: the student grants only that, looking at the standard pair of choices, they feel the pull of the safe option in the first pair and of the riskier option in the second, and they are willing to be asked further questions about cases. Everything else has to come out of their answers.

Structure. Eight to ten branch nodes, each a question about a concrete case or a concrete claim, with two or three answer options, and a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves. Constraints on the nodes: no node may mention independence, the sure-thing principle, expected utility, risk, weighting, or any named position; a student must be able to answer every node without knowing where it leads. Where a node's discriminating power depends on an empirical claim about what people actually do, mark the node as empirical and tell me what I would have to check myself. If you think one of my stipulated cases fails to discriminate what I take it to discriminate, say so and rewrite the node rather than leaving it in.

The case material should be built on the running example I use in class, with variants. The running case: a health ministry will make this decision exactly once. Programme S delivers one year of healthy life to each of a million people for certain. Programme T delivers one year to each with probability 0.89, five years to each with probability 0.10, and nothing with probability 0.01. In the paired comparison, Programme U delivers one year to each with probability 0.11 and nothing with probability 0.89; Programme V delivers five years to each with probability 0.10 and nothing with probability 0.90. The variants I want drawn on: the trembling-hand variant, in which the implementation of S carries a small independent failure probability so that nothing is literally certain; the scale variant, in which every probability is divided by a hundred and the stakes are unchanged; the delegation variant, in which the committee chooses for a population whose outcomes it never observes; the two-stage variant, in which the same options are produced by a device that resolves the branch common to the two options first; the mixture variant, in which a device that randomizes equally between S and T is offered as a third option; the repetition variant, in which the same choice structure is faced two hundred times under independent draws with one policy fixed for all of them; the stakes-domain variant, in which the same numbers describe monetary gains to the chooser rather than years of life for others; and a reflection variant, in which the options are laid out as a statewise table with the branch common to each pair visibly cancelled, and the student is asked both whether the pull survives and what its surviving reflection shows.

One caution about the nodes. Several of my students say they prefer S because additional years of health matter less than the first, or because a million people's fifth year is worth less than their first. That consideration is available to every leaf, so no node may treat it as evidence of anything; if a node is to test something in that neighbourhood, it must ask about the pair, and about why the same consideration does not settle the paired comparison in the opposite direction. I would rather you used that point to design a distractor node that catches the mistake than ignored it.

Leaves. The tree should end, wherever the logical space allows, at cells that include: a view that keeps the standard theory intact and treats the pattern as error; a view that repairs the theory by giving certainty a special role, as a first-order preference for security rather than as curvature over outcomes; views that repair it by transforming probabilities rather than by weighting outcomes, including both the rank-dependent and the cumulative descendants of prospect theory; a view that treats an attitude to risk itself as a rationally permissible parameter while preserving the rest of the structure; views that give up independence but keep betweenness, with preferences fanning out locally; views that explain the pattern by the anticipation of regret or disappointment rather than by the structure of the options; views that deny that only the reduced-form lottery over final outcomes matters, so that the process or the menu is part of what is evaluated; views on which the normative verdict varies with the domain of the choice; and quietist views on which no verdict attaches to a single unrepeatable choice and the norm governs only policies or long-run sequences. Include also, if the space allows it, the view that the datum is an artifact of unmodelled doubt about the stated probabilities or about the experimenter rather than a response to certainty as such, and keep it separate from the view that the datum is simply a mistake. Name a leaf only where you are confident an established name exists; where you are not, write unnamed and characterize the cell in a sentence. Do not supply citations, references, or reading suggestions: I know that literature, and I do not want the handout padded with it.

Commentary. After the tree and key, give me the taxonomy in about a page: the logical space generated by the nodes, the combinations of answers the tree in principle permits, the actual positions located in that space, and the combinations that are unoccupied, each diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate on case answers alone, say what kind of further thing would separate them, and note whether the separation is one I could obtain by asking another case or only by a formal or empirical result. I have been calling the pattern of answers I hear most often in my own department certainty-constrained consequentialism; if that cell turns out to be occupied by an established view, tell me which, and if it is a novel combination, say what is wrong with it. Every leaf should carry one line stating what it implies about the ministry case as originally described: one-off, decided once, outcome observed. Finally, build into the tree rather than into a footnote the distinction between the mixture-independence axiom of the von Neumann–Morgenstern framework and the common-consequence implication of Savage's sure-thing principle, and tell me which of the two the pattern actually threatens.

Aim for something that could be printed as a two-page handout plus a page of notes.
