I'm in a reading group on philosophy of psychiatry — mostly grad students, plus two clinicians who show up when they can — and the same sentence keeps turning up doing completely different jobs. There's a poster outside the student health center that says Mental illness is an illness like any other, and last week it turned up inside an argument too. Our department is circulating a draft proposal for a new diagnostic category (working name something like "socially elaborated symptom presentation") meant to cover people who show up at clinic with a symptom picture that seems to have taken shape after they spent months in online communities built around a self-diagnosed condition. One of the clinicians on the panel said we shouldn't hesitate on the category just because patients might start to act like the description once they've heard it: you don't refuse to call a broken tibia a broken tibia because the patient might limp harder after you tell them, and "mental illness is an illness like any other." My friend who does peer support work pushed back with what I took to be Hacking's looping point — you classify people, the people classified notice the classification, they change in response to it (they organize around it, their symptoms sharpen or settle into whatever shape is culturally available), and then the category has to be revised, so the kind moves around under you while you're trying to measure it. The clinician's answer was that this just makes mental illness the special spooky case again, and that that is exactly the stigmatizing move the poster is supposed to be against. I think both of them are sliding between claims and I can't tell which claim either of them needs.

So what I want is a precisification, not a ruling on who won. Can you lay out as a numbered menu the distinct precise things that sentence could be asserting — e.g. a metaphysical one about mental disorders being the same kind of kind as bodily diseases (mechanism there whether or not we've named it), an epistemic/semantic one about our terms referring or failing to refer in the same way, a methodological one about the same evidentiary bar applying (biomarkers, controlled trials, whatever), a clinical one about the same urgency and the same claim on treatment and resources, and a moral or political one about blame and about not treating these patients as a lesser grade of patient? For each: is it a platitude, is it substantive and defensible, is it probably false? And then the two bits I actually care about. First, "any other" — any other what? A fracture, hypertension (a threshold someone chose on a continuum, partly because treating above it helps), a category whose boundaries were set partly by how institutions respond to people, a category people fight to be counted in? Does the comparison class do all the work? Second, looping: does it really mark a difference in kind between psychiatric and somatic categories, or only a difference in degree, given that plenty of bodily categories come back around on the people in them too (screening changes who counts as a case, people learn illness behavior, expectation produces symptoms)? And does looping only threaten the metaphysical reading, or does it also cause trouble for the methodological one — or, weirdly, could it support some reading of the slogan, on the grounds that a category people respond to is real and clinically load-bearing in a way a mere label isn't? One more thing: if the looping story is right, then what the classified people say about themselves is partly what the kind is, and my friend also keeps quoting that disability-rights line about nothing about us without us — I'd like to know which readings of the slogan would support giving patient communities' own language real authority in setting criteria, and which would treat their input as just one more data stream.

Give me it as a list I can bring to the group on Thursday. Don't send me to papers or give me citations, we already have a syllabus we're not keeping up with; I want the readings separated out and, for each one, whether it's trivial, defensible, or false, plus your verdict on which reading the clinician needed for her argument and whether that reading actually gets her the conclusion, and which reading my friend can afford to give up without losing the anti-stigma point. Assume I know Hacking's basic idea but nothing else fancy.
