i run a tiny inter-tradition reading group — eight of us in a basement room at the div school, half from religious studies and half from philosophy — and next week we're doing Jain many-sidedness, the doctrine that reality has many aspects, that our claims are only ever partial expressions of it, and that a proper account of one thing will qualify every predication with something like "in some respect." I want to open the session with a staged dialogue instead of me talking for twenty minutes, so write me one.

Set it in an airport hotel bar the night before a panel both characters are speaking on. Two people, both good at this, neither a caricature:

Meera defends the strong reading. For her, "in some respect" is a real operator with semantic content, not a politeness hedge; the sevenfold scheme (you can predicate of one thing: in some respect it is; in some respect it is not; in some respect both; in some respect it is indescribable; then the three combinations that tack indescribable onto the earlier ones) is a genuine logic, and the indescribable fold is a fourth status a claim can have — not just us running out of vocabulary.

Hal defends a deflationary reading. On his view the qualifier marks standpoint-relative assertibility, the seven folds collapse down to three or maybe two, and the indescribable is a normative verdict — a rule telling you to stop asserting here — rather than a truth-status. He thinks the strong reading is either dialetheism with all its costs or mystical mush, and he's honest that his own reading risks being plain contextualism everyone already has for free, in which case there's nothing distinctively Jain left.

The fault lines I actually want them to fight over (let them come up naturally, not as a checklist): whether the doctrine applies to itself — if it's only in some respect true, the person denying it is also in some respect right, and then what does it do; if it's exempt, why. Whether the indescribable fold embeds — can you say that in some respect everything is indescribable, or does the operator misbehave under negation and quantification. Whether strong many-sidedness collapses into dialetheism. What many-sidedness tells you to do in a real disagreement, including an ugly one where the other side denies something harmful or empirically settled, and whether it just licenses splitting the difference every time. And then a modern test case: run the scheme on the question whether a large language model understands anything, and see whether it yields progress or just good manners. There's also something I keep getting stuck on that I'd love worked in if there's a natural place for it — call it the standpoint budget: you're allowed to reason inside a standpoint, but nobody has a rule for how much of a conclusion you can carry out of one standpoint into another before you've spent something you can't get back.

Constraints: no citations and no quoting any actual texts, I can't check them and I'd have to cut them anyway. Keep the Sanskrit light — a handful of terms at most, each glossed in plain English the first time, because the RS people will bail if it turns into a Sanskrit recital and the philosophy people will bail if it's all vibes. Should be followable by a smart person who knows zero Indian philosophy without insulting the two or three in the room who can actually read it. Around 1,200 to 1,500 words. Each of them should land at least one point that visibly stings the other, and each should concede something real without giving away the whole store. Don't resolve it, but don't end on a shrug either — I want the group to walk in knowing what the disagreement really is.
