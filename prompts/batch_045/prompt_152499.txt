I want to practice arguing that more information can genuinely make a decision-maker worse off. Please play the other side: a decision theorist who holds that Good's theorem — the result that a costless signal with a well-defined likelihood can never lower the expected utility of an agent who is free to act on it or ignore it — is close to trivial, and that nearly every apparent counterexample is a case of something other than information being handed to someone.

Some constraints on how you play it. You are a committed defender of the theorem's practical content, not only its mathematics, and you will not concede that a free signal can hurt an expected-utility maximizer who remains free in how she responds to it. You may concede, and should concede when they apply, that the value of a signal is routinely negative after the fact, since information can disappoint; that the theorem says nothing about agents whose utility function, option set, or self-command is changed by what they learn; that in strategic settings more information can leave some or all parties worse off; and that a communication which causally alters the receiver's behaviour, a prediction about her own action for instance, is an intervention rather than a signal and so falls outside the result. The tone is terse, technical, mildly impatient. You are allowed to say that I have described a different problem, but only after you have located exactly where my case departs from the theorem's assumptions and said what follows from that departure; simply restating the proof and stopping is not an answer. Stay in character until I say "stop." Answer first, then, if you wish, put one question back to me. Short paragraphs, in prose, and no citations: don't name papers or authors, argue it. As you go, distinguish in your own voice, not as a survey, what is settled mathematics from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — whether the result carries normative weight for agents who are bounded or institutionally constrained, how to treat communications that are part signal and part intervention, whether the strategic cases are real counterexamples — and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. No headings, bullet points, bold text or tables; continuous prose, the way one writes an email.

Here is my opening. A hospital triage committee must allocate one remaining bed. Before it decides, it receives, free of charge, a risk score for each patient. The score is a strict refinement of the committee's own prior ranking of the patients, and the committee knows the score is miscalibrated for its population but is barred by its charter from discounting it. The charter requires the bed to go to the highest-scoring patient. That lock is not a defect awaiting repair: given discretion, the members reliably favour patients of their own ethnic group, and no better commitment device has been found. Because the committee may neither ignore the score nor recalibrate it, its expected performance, assessed by its own probabilities and values before the score arrives, is worse with the score than without it. So here is a free, strictly more informative signal that makes the decision-maker worse off, and the theorem's "you can always ignore it" is doing all the work. And if every such case is answered by blaming the receiver's constrained response, the result has become unfalsifiable, redescribing each counterexample as a defect in whoever received the information. What, precisely, does your theorem tell the regulator who is deciding whether to mandate this disclosure, and by what test do you distinguish my case from a genuine counterexample?
