I work in quantitative risk management at a mid-size reinsurer, and the framework I actually think in is risk-weighted expected utility: I sort a prospect's outcomes worst-to-best and value it as u(x₁) + Σ_{i≥2} r(P(X ≥ xᵢ))·(u(xᵢ) − u(xᵢ₋₁)), or equivalently as the integral of u against the transformed measure r∘P. In practice I use r(p) = p^γ, with γ between about 1.5 and 3 depending on how pessimistic the reserving view is, and I keep two things carefully separate: curvature in u, which is diminishing marginal value of money, and warping in r, which is an attitude toward where in the distribution the risk sits. I also know the feature that trips up everyone who first uses this: the representation is not additive across independent prospects, so applying r to each treaty in a book and summing is not the same as aggregating the book and applying r once. I have exactly one undergraduate philosophy course behind me, so please define the philosophers' terminology in my vocabulary rather than the reverse.

The argument I want translated started at dinner with a friend who does grant-making for a foundation focused on existential risk. She defended maximizing expected value even when it tells you to pay the mugger who says that, unless you hand over your wallet, he will with probability around one in a trillion bring about an astronomically large amount of suffering. I said that expected value maximization is fanatical in the sense that worries me: it lets any sufficiently tiny probability be offset by a sufficiently enormous payoff, so that the sure, nearby, modest good always loses to the remote enormous one. She replied that there is a standard argument that any rule which is transitive, which respects stochastic dominance in a suitably strong form, and which does not simply refuse to be moved by small probabilities, turns out to be fanatical in exactly that sense — so my objection, if it is an objection, is really an objection to one of those other commitments. We went in circles.

Please restate that whole dispute in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report: which of the philosophers' claims have a clean counterpart in terms of u, r, and the decumulative weighting; which translate only after you have smuggled in something the framework does not itself contain; and which have no expression in it at all. Three things I have tried on my own, which I would like you either to confirm, repair, or demolish. First, it looks to me as though fanaticism has two faces that my framework handles differently: holding the payoff fixed and driving the probability down is damped by r (with linear u the mugger's offer is worth p^γ·M rather than p·M), whereas holding the probability fixed and driving the payoff up is untouched by r, since r(p)·u(M) still grows without bound once u is unbounded — so a pessimistic r seems to convert fanaticism into a threshold, and a mugger who knows my γ just promises more. Is that the right way to carve it? Second, a colleague proposed setting r(p) = 0 below some small ε, which kills the mugging outright; my worry is the aggregation one, that a book of many independent ε-sized chances of loss, each individually ignored, is together near-certain to produce a loss, and I cannot tell whether that worry is an instance of what the philosophers mean by stochastic dominance or something my framework has to borrow from outside. Third, it occurs to me that in my day job I would never take a stated payoff of that magnitude at face value: I would treat it as severe model uncertainty with a heavy tail, and then notice that the mean is being set entirely by the tail. If so, is part of what the philosophers are disputing really a disagreement about model uncertainty that my framework has no slot for, or is that a dodge? Finish by telling me whether the residue you find is a defect of the risk-weighted framework, or a sign that the philosophical dispute is less about attitudes toward risk than it looks.
