I maintain a large, aging pricing service at work, and over the past few years I have noticed a pattern in the way my colleagues and I argue about it. I would like to know whether the pattern is one thing or several, and whether any part of it has already been described and named. A label on its own will not help me; what I want is the carving — what exactly this is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.

Three episodes, then the questions.

First. For about two years the service produced demand estimates that beat our domain experts badly. Nobody on the team could say why. People in the business began talking about it as though it "understood" the market: they asked it what it thought before a promotion, and they were not entirely joking. Then a contractor traced the behaviour to a test fixture that had been shipped to production years earlier, interacting with a rounding rule. The behaviour was an accident. Within a week the same people had stopped attributing anything at all to the service: it had never known anything, it was a rounding artifact, it had been fooling us. What bothers me is that the discovery told us how the numbers were produced. It did not, as far as I can tell, tell us anything about whether the numbers were *good* — and they were still excellent, better than any expert's, both before and after we found the cause. Yet the retraction was total and immediate.

Second. We now use a program-synthesis tool. Last month it emitted a routine that is faster than the one our best engineer wrote and whose structure visibly mirrors the structure of the problem — it rediscovered, on its own, a decomposition that we use precisely because it corresponds to something real about the domain. The mechanism is not mysterious: it is an enormous search through a space of candidate programs with a cheap scoring function. The reaction was that there is no understanding here, only search. Again, the mechanism explains provenance. It does not seem to me to explain away the fact that the resulting routine is apt — that its structure tracks the problem's structure.

Third, and this is the part I find hardest. With our large learned models, my colleagues argue in the opposite direction from the opposite situation: we have no idea how the model does what it does, and that is offered as a reason to say it does not understand, that it is curve-fitting. So a known mechanism is evidence against understanding, and an unknown mechanism is also evidence against understanding. The verdict looks insensitive to which way the evidence points, which makes me think the verdict is fixed in advance and the mechanism story only supplies the rhetoric.

There is a fourth question that I suspect is the same question in another shape. Suppose two byte-identical binaries: one written by an engineer who understood the domain, one emitted by the search process in the second episode. Same behaviour, same internal structure, different histories. Is there a difference between them with respect to understanding? My team splits on this and I cannot tell what the disagreement is even about. It feels like the first episode again: how much does how you got here matter to what you are?

A friend who reads popular science says that Darwin and Turing each pulled off something like this — showing that the thing everyone assumed had to come first, a designer or a mind, is really a late product of processes that lack it entirely — and that this has a name. I cannot tell whether that name covers my cases. I am not trying to make a claim about evolution or about machines; I am asking about a recurring inferential move, in which an account of how a capacity arose gets treated as a refutation of the thing the capacity was supposed to be evidence for.

It is not, or not only, the god-of-the-gaps pattern: that is about concluding something from ignorance, whereas my colleagues retract even when the mechanism is fully known. And it is not a matter of bad explanations — the mechanisms they cite are good ones.

So: does this move have a name? Are the first two episodes the same phenomenon as the third, or should they be separated? What are the nearest established concepts, and how does each of them fall short of what I have described? Is there a reading on which the retraction in the first episode is simply *correct* — on which having the right kind of history is part of what understanding is, so that my discomfort is misplaced? And if nothing established fits, I would like a coined term, a definition, and boundary cases: the byte-identical pair, a human expert whose skill is largely implicit, and a system whose competence was deliberately built by someone who understood.
