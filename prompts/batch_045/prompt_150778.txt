I keep running into a difficulty of a particular shape, and I would like to know whether it has been named and carved, or whether it is one of those things everyone gestures at and nobody has separated.

The situation I work with is the familiar one: you want to get at what is good for a person, or what they really want, and you are not willing to take their present wants at face value. So you clean them up. You supply the facts they lack, subtract the influence of exhaustion, fear, low mood, addiction, misleading framing, and you reconcile the conflicts among their desires. Call the output the cleaned-up version of the person.

What I keep noticing is that the cleaning seems to work in two quite different ways, and I cannot tell whether they are two operations or one.

First case. A woman who eats meat learns, in detail and without defensiveness, what that involves. She does not conclude that she wants to eat less meat. She stops wanting meat. There is no moment at which she both wants it and judges that, all things considered, she should not; the desire itself disappears and is replaced by a different arrangement of tastes. It seems wrong to say she had an all-things-considered preference against meat that she simply did not know about.

Second case. A man has spent fifteen years on a demanding project, a small business say, which costs him his health and most of his relationships. Asked to imagine himself fully informed about the costs and deliberating well, he gives it up. But the man who gives it up is already a man changed by knowing the costs; the man who has not been changed wants to continue and also wants not to become someone who would stop. A procedure that says supply the facts and leave the agent alone, and a procedure that says let the agent keep thinking until he settles, will disagree here, and their disagreement is not about how much he knows.

The general shape: informing and reflecting are normally treated as two inputs to one idealization, but over a lot of real material they appear not to commute, and if reflection is pushed far enough there may be several resting points rather than one, with which one is reached depending on where the person started.

Adjacent things I have come across do not seem to settle it. The sour-grapes or deformed-desire phenomenon is close but reversed in a way I cannot quite state: there the deformed desire is supposed to be discounted, while here the desires that survive full information are the ones the earlier self would call deformed. Weakness of will is plainly different, since nobody is failing to do what they judge best. The phrase what you would want under ideal conditions is the very phrase I am trying to split open, because it does not say whether the ideal conditions are a filter applied to a fixed agent or a process the agent undergoes. And coherent extrapolated volition, which I have seen used in roughly this neighbourhood, looks to me like a proposal for what to do about the difficulty rather than an account of it, and it arrives attached to a different problem altogether.

So: does the thing I am pointing at have a name, and is it one phenomenon or two? If nothing established fits, say so and give me a name with a definition. Either way I want the carving rather than the label. In particular I would like the nearest concepts and, for each, the respects in which it is not this; boundary cases that decide whether the two halves belong together, such as addiction, religious conversion, and the difference between a child's wants and the adult's; and some indication of whether the distinction has any teeth, that is, whether it forces a real choice between specifying an idealization as a corrected snapshot of an agent and specifying it as the endpoint of a process the agent is allowed to move through.
