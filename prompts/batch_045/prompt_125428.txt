I should say something about who is asking, since it bears on why the question is being put this way. I am a retired experimental physical chemist — some thirty-five years of extracting unobserved quantities from noisy data — and for the past two years I have been sitting in on a reading group in philosophy of mind and cognitive science at my old department. The group has lately been working through the dispute about whether large language models understand anything, and I have noticed that the discussion keeps being closed rather than opened by a single move.

At our last meeting a member of the group, himself a retired computer scientist, ended a long silence with the following: It is just statistics. There is nothing there but statistics, and so there is no understanding. Nobody contradicted him and the conversation moved on. I would like to bring that sentence back to the next meeting properly taken apart, and I would like your help.

My difficulty is that from where I sit the sentence looks nearly vacuous. Much of my working life consisted of formally probabilistic procedures — maximum-likelihood fits, Kalman-type recursive estimation, inference to parameters that no one had ever measured directly, such as a rate constant recovered from a decay curve or an electron density from diffraction intensities where the phases are not measured at all. Had someone told me that because my estimate issued from a least-squares fit to photon counts it was therefore nothing but statistics and hence not knowledge of the rate constant, I should have thought them confused. Yet I do not think the retort is decisive, and I can see why. In those cases there was a person who set up the model, an instrument in causal contact with the sample, and a probabilistic apparatus whose job was to handle error whose structure we already understood. So something more is doing the work in that sentence than the bare word statistics, and I want to know what.

Please treat the sentence as a piece of shorthand and set out, as a menu rather than as a single preferred reading, the distinct exact claims it could be used to express — from the plainly trivial to the substantive and contestable to the straightforwardly false. For each reading I should like four things: the claim stated precisely enough that one could say what would settle it; whether it is trivial or carries real weight; whether it is plausible; and, crucially, whether it would actually support the conclusion for which it was offered, or whether it needs a further premise to get there, and if so which premise. It is this last column that I care about, because I strongly suspect that most of the available readings are either true and irrelevant or relevant and unargued.

I have some candidate readings already, but I would rather you sharpened or discarded them than confirmed them:

One reading is about machinery: that the operations the system performs are probabilistic, that nothing in it is anything other than the manipulation of distributional quantities. This strikes me as true and idle — my own instruments were like that in various respects — but I may be missing what makes it bite here.

A second is about provenance: that everything the system can do is explained by regularities in its training corpus, so that no content in its outputs derives from contact with the world the outputs are about. This seems to me the reading with the most promise, but I cannot tell whether it is a claim about the causal history of the outputs, a claim about what best explains them, or a claim about what they could be about.

A third is about explanation and prediction: that the successes of these systems are predicted by purely distributional quantities — something like cross-entropy loss or the smooth improvement of performance with scale — with no semantic property entering the best account, so that semantic attributions are idle. I do not know whether this is an argument against understanding or an argument against a particular kind of test for understanding.

A fourth is about normativity: that covariation is not the sort of thing that can be correct or incorrect in the way understanding is supposed to be, because it tracks what goes with what rather than what is true.

A fifth is about subjectivity: that a statistical procedure has no point of view, nothing that things are like for it, no stake in the outcome, and that understanding requires a subject.

A sixth, which I find hardest, is about surface versus structure: that statistics means the modelling of surface regularities as opposed to the recovery of the process that generated them. This is the reading that makes trouble for my own analogy, since a great deal of statistical inference in my former field was precisely inference to hidden states and unobserved parameters. If the force of the slogan comes from this contrast — fitting versus modelling, curve fitting versus recovering the generator — then the interesting question is not whether the system is statistical but whether a system trained to predict text thereby recovers something like the latent structure of whatever generated the text, and that seems to me a question with content. Tell me if I have located the real issue or if I have simply imported a distinction from my own discipline where it does not belong.

There is a further frame I keep reaching for and would like your verdict on: that the sentence works by sliding between levels, offering a claim about how something is computed and drawing a conclusion about what, if anything, is being computed. I have in mind the kind of threefold distinction — what is computed, by what procedure, in what physical substrate — that used to be drawn in vision research. If that is the right diagnosis, say so and say what the conclusion at the upper level would have to be established by instead. If it is a red herring or a piece of borrowed jargon that obscures more than it reveals, say that too.

Two related matters. First, the same thought is often put as It is just curve fitting, or as autocomplete, or by way of the stochastic-parrot charge. I would like to know whether these are the same claim in different clothes or whether each adds something the others lack; my suspicion is that curve fitting smuggles in the surface-versus-structure contrast and that autocomplete smuggles in something about the localness of the task, but I would like that made explicit and corrected where wrong. Second, and most useful of all for my purposes: which reading is my colleague most likely to have meant, and what is the single question I could put to him at the next meeting that would force him to say which one it is, without its sounding like a challenge?

A list I can take with me is the deliverable I have in mind: each reading on its own line, with its verdict and with an explicit note on whether it reaches the conclusion. Where a reading fails to reach it, please state the missing premise in one sentence, since that is what I should most like to have in front of me when the silence comes round again.
