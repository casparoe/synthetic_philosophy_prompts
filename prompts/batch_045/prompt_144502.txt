I run my city's rationality meetup and I've got a 90-minute slot in two weeks on takeoff speeds. Crowd is maybe a dozen: two ML engineers, a biologist, a lawyer, and a handful of people who read the Sequences back in 2014 and have been quietly lurking ever since -- so they've all absorbed the foom-versus-gradualism fight as a vibe without ever having the actual crux handed to them. Don't write the talk, I want the skeleton.

My sense is that fast-vs-slow is the wrong axis and I want to teach something I've been calling veto points: the number of moments at which the people building the thing could still have collectively said no. The counterintuitive bit I want them to actually feel is that a takeoff that takes fifteen years can have fewer veto points than one that takes fifteen months, because every individual step is defensible under competitive pressure and there's never a moment where 'stop' is the live option.

So: what has to be in place before that even makes sense to them? I'm guessing capability-level-is-not-rate-of-improvement and capability-is-not-deployment both have to land first, but tell me if I've got the depedency order wrong or if something else needs to come before either. What's the one concrete scenario to build the whole session around? I was going to use a toy research loop with doubling times, but I worry that makes the slow case invisible, and it also can't be robotics or they'll smuggle in 'the physical world is slow so the AI is slow'. Tell me what the example has to have and what it has to lack.

Then: the misconceptions to expect, and exactly where in the arc to cut each one off. I'm anticipating 'slow takeoff means we get decades to react' and 'fast takeoff means one model wakes up and takes over', but you probably know others. I also want them to get the difference between capability-gradualism and diffusion-gradualism, including the overhang case -- systems much better than anything actually deployed, waiting on adoption friction to clear -- but I'm not sure where that goes or whether it'll just muddle things for this crowd, so tell me if it belongs. And what should I explicitly refuse to get into at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing?

Last thing: one question I can ask in the last ten minutes that separates someone who has the ratio idea from someone who's just memorised continuous-good-discontinuous-bad.
