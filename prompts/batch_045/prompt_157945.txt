ive been trying to write down my actual position on the problem of old evidence for a reading group in phil sci, and the more I write the more I suspect im holding things that dont fit together. here's the bundle as it currently stands:

1. Bayesianism is the right account of rational belief change: credences, probabilism, update by conditionalization.
2. The problem of old evidence is a real problem, not a pseudoproblem. Paradigm case: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion. Roughly 43 arcseconds per century that Newtonian theory couldn't account for, noted by Le Verrier back in the mid-nineteenth century; in 1915 general relativity got it, and that genuinely counted in GR's favour. But if your credence in the datum is already 1, then P(H given E) = P(H), so on the machinery there's no confirmation to be had.
3. I dont like any of the standard repairs:
 - the counterfactual or ur-prior move (go back to the credence function you'd have had before you learned E and update from there). Two objections: it asks you to reason from a credence state that contradicts what you actually know, and it needs a prior over general relativity at a time when nobody could even formulate the theory, so the prior isn't so much as statable.
 - the move where what you really learn is that H entails E. Entailment facts aren't the sort of thing you can hold a nontrivial credence in and then learn.
4. But I want to keep the verdict: old evidence really can confirm, and the scientists were right to count the perihelion advance for GR.
5. Confirmation is objective. It's a relation between evidence and hypothesis that holds or fails independently of anyone's psychology, and you can find out later that some old datum had been confirming a theory all along.
6. Doxastic attitudes are one-dimensional — degrees of belief and nothing else. No extra attitude of acceptance, or of taking something to be explained, doing work alongside credence.
7. Explanatoriness is evidentially relevant in its own right, and I dont think a Bayesian gets to absorb that by saying the explanatory considerations were already packed into the likelihoods or the prior.
8. Methodological: if a formalism can't reproduce an evidential judgment that working scientists actually make and treat as binding, that's a mark against the formalism, not against the practice.
9. I'm fine with idealization — logical omniscience, unbounded agents, no computational limits. I dont attack Bayesianism on nobody could actually do this grounds.
10. My own attempt at a repair, which I'll call the partition-shift view: what's new in 1915 isn't the datum, it's the menu. Confirmation by old evidence gets measured against the partition of live alternatives at the time — GR earns credit because it accounts for the datum without the ad hoc auxiliaries the Newtonian account needed, and in doing so knocks rivals out. So it's elimination inside a shifting space of alternatives, not probability-raising on a fixed one.

Two things people have said to me that I can't shake: a colleage says my favourite example is actually the one case where the standard machinery is fine, because what Einstein established in 1915 was the derivation, not the observation. And someone else says (3) and (9) can't both be mine — I'm rejecting a move as unrealizable while signing up to logical omniscience.

What I want is an audit, not a survey. Go through the ten and rank the tensions by how bad they actually are; say which ones are only apparent and what makes them dissolve; and for each real one say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me. Then commit: if I can only keep nine, tell me which one goes and why — don't hand me a menu and leave me to choose. And if the partition-shift thing in (10) just collapses back into one of the moves I rejected in (3), say that plainly rather than being polite about it.
