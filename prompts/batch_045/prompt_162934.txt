Hey — I run a tiny forecasting reading group (eight people, pub, very bad lighting) and we've got a split that has gone absolutely nowhere for about a year. Half of us think AI takeoff is discontinuous: the "foom" picture, where once systems can do AI research themselves the thing goes from roughly human-level to wildly superhuman quickly, with few or no usable warning shots. The other half think it's continuous: the same kind of smooth capability-vs-scale-and-investment curves we've been watching for years, no special regime change at the point of recursive self-improvement, plenty of visible intermediate steps. Instead of another hour of this I want the document they should have written.

So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed hard-takeoff person and a committed gradualist/continuist. Steelman both. The foom side gets the compute-overhang and recursive-amplification arguments, the regime-change point, and the distinction between capability and deployment. The gradualist gets the scaling-regularity point, diffusion and integration lags, and diminishing returns to throwing more of the same at it. Don't adjudicate — I don't want a verdict on who's right, I want a text both could sign with the residual disagreement made exact.

What I want in it:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section, roughly 8–10 claims, that both would genuinely sign — not platitudes. Things like: the disagreement is about the shape of a curve inside a specific window rather than about whether AI eventually gets very capable; "takeoff speed" doesn't mean anything until you fix an x-axis (calendar time? compute? cumulative research effort?) and a y-axis; benchmark scores are a bad proxy for the thing at issue; the right centre of the window is the point where systems do a substantial fraction of AI R&D subtasks; smooth scaling so far is real but its relevance past that point is exactly what's contested; and — this one I care about — that a very fast takeoff in research capability is compatible with a slow, boring-looking ramp in the outside world, because deployment is gated by liability, regulation and integration time. Call that last possibility "quiet takeoff" if it helps.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the crux; the foom side's own wording of its position, phrased so the foom person would accept it; the gradualist's own wording, likewise; and whether the disagreement is factual, conceptual, or about reference classes. Cruxes I'd guess at — replace or add if you can do better:
   - serial vs parallel: whether the binding constraint on AI-research progress is the depth of a single chain of insight (so extra copies buy little) or throughput of experiments and candidate ideas (so a thousand copies buy a lot);
   - the one-run jump: whether a lab sitting on a large compute overhang could get most of the way to vastly superhuman capability inside a single training or fine-tuning cycle, or whether each increment needs feedback from a world still mostly run by humans and institutions;
   - the y-axis: whether takeoff should be measured in AI-research capability, in broad economic substitutability, or in something else, and whether those can come apart by years;
   - whether the smooth curves of recent years are evidence about the recursive regime at all, or only about the pre-recursive one — i.e. whether "recursive self-improvement" is a genuine regime change or just more of the same automation that's already been added to the pipeline.

3. Pre-registered tests: for each crux, one concrete case or measurement both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Two I definitely want:
   - The sealed lab. A well-resourced lab keeps everything internal for two years from the point where its systems can do about half of a fixed battery of AI-R&D subtasks, running at flat (not growing) compute, and afterwards releases the full weekly internal log of performance on the held-out half of that battery plus the compute used. Both sides commit beforehand to the shape they expect — roughly log-linear with a slowly drifting slope, versus a knee followed by a much steeper segment — and, more importantly, to what shape would make them concede.
   - The thousand-copies test. Hold total compute fixed and compare one long serial run against a thousand shorter parallel instances on a real research task with verifiable answers (find and fix a stated bug in a large codebase; beat a published result by a stated margin). Both sides say what result would move them and roughly how far.
   Add a third if you can think of one that isn't just "wait and see what happens".

4. Where a chunk of the dispute is really about words rather than the world, say which chunk and why — I'd have thought the y-axis one is the big offender, but maybe thats just the pub talking.

Also: I want calibration, not diplomacy. Mark each agreed claim as either "both actually confident" or "signed but one side is hedging", naming the hedging side and what it would want to add. Give every prediction a rough number, or at least a "would surprise me / wouldn't" tag. And if you think quiet takeoff makes the whole foom-vs-continuous question unobservable from outside for years, say that too — that's a finding, not a failure.

One last thing: if any of this can't be done honestly, say so plainly in a short note before the document rather than producing a tidy consensus nobody believes. If the two views as I've set them up don't really conflict, or if "takeoff speed" isn't one quantity, or if the sealed-lab scenario is uninformative because no lab would ever behave that way, I'd much rather be told that than handed a fake joint statement. Keep the whole thing under about 1200 words so I can paste it into the group doc.
