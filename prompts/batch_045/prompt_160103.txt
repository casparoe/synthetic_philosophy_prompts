I teach epistemology and philosophy of science, and about a year ago a colleague in the religion department dragged me into a reading group on classical Indian epistemology — we worked through the Nyāya material on the pramāṇas, the conditions on a good hetu (that it actually be present in the subject, present in cases that have the property to be proved, and absent in cases that lack it), the business of vyāpti and the upādhi that wrecks it, the way the school handles identification-by-similarity with that stock example of the man who's told a gavaya is cow-like and later spots one in the forest, and the way it treats knowledge from what someone says as a source in its own right rather than as inference in disguise. It's made me want to change how I do something I actually do all the time: I edit the "notes and discussion" section of a mid-ranking philosophy journal, which means I triage somewhere between thirty and fifty short submissions a term, at maybe fifteen to twenty minutes each, deciding whether a note goes out to a referee, goes back for restructuring, or gets desk-rejected. Ideally a postgraduate editorial assistant with no Sanskrit and no training in the theory should be able to run most of it, with only the flagged judgment calls coming to me, and I sign off on anything that isn't a clean pass.

So: turn that material into a procedure my assistants could actually run — questions in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, clear stopping points, and explicit markers at the places where the question is one of judgement or values and has to be handed to a human. Keep what the theory really says: don't collapse testimony into inference, don't let a similarity do the work of a pervasion, keep the novelty condition on genuine knowledge (that a cognition only counts if it tells you something you didn't already have) doing real work against papers that argue for what everyone already grants, and keep the requirement that a pervasion be checked against the possibility of an adventitious condition rather than just asserted. Two complications that the material obviously doesn't cover, and which I want handled rather than dodged. First: we're now getting submissions whose evidence base is a pattern someone found by running a statistical model over a corpus — nobody perceived anything, there's no identifiable speaker who knows the thing and isn't trying to mislead us, and the pervasion claim depends on steps nobody can inspect, so I don't know which pramāṇa it's supposed to be an instance of or what the procedure should demand of it. Second: I've been using a private rule of thumb for years, that a note is allowed at most one inference whose pervasion the reader has to check for themselves and everything else should be either directly observable or traceable to a named source, and I want you to tell me honestly whether that rule survives the translation or is just a prejudice I happen to like.

Then run the procedure on three real shapes of submission, and please don't soften the results. (1) A 2,500-word note on the ethics of AI companions whose central premiss is that "the empirical literature has now settled" that users form durable attachments to chatbots — no named finding, no named researchers, just a gesture at a consensus. (2) A paper arguing that a certain kind of corporate actor can bear obligations, on the ground that the case is "structurally just like" the case of a person acting through an agent, which everyone in the literature already accepts. (3) A note resting on one large correlational study: people scoring high on a trait are more likely to do X, therefore the trait disposes them to X. The third is very close to something I published last year and the second is the exact shape of the paper I'm drafting at the moment, so if the procedure says these come back for restructuring, say so plainly and say what restructuring would actually involve. I'd also like a short closing section on what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, which kinds of paper it systematically disfavours, and which verdicts should never be left to it. One page of procedure plus the worked cases, in British English (my co-editor is a stickler about it and the section's style sheet is British), and assume no Sanskrit from the people running it — gloss any term you can't avoid keeping.
