I run an eight-week undergraduate reading group on the problem of evil, and I have a scheduling problem that has turned into a philosophical one: we read Ursula K. Le Guin's 1973 story about the utopian city of Omelas in week two, and we are not getting to Leibniz and the best-of-all-possible-worlds claim until week six. By then the group will have settled into reading the story as a utilitarian parable — the scapegoat, whether one may do evil that good may come, negative responsibility, and the usual comparison with Williams's Jim and Pedro case — and I would rather not hand them another Pangloss joke either. So I want a Leibnizian reading of the story, written as something I could photocopy and hand out in week six, that treats both sides seriously and does not simply reduce the fiction to an illustration of a thesis.

Two distinctions I have been using in my own notes, which I would like you to work with, correct, or discard. First, I have been separating three different objections a citizen of Omelas might have: the participatory one, that her happiness is causally sustained by the child's misery; the spectatorial one, that she knows about the misery and stays to enjoy the festival anyway; and the authorizational one, that the arrangement is ratified rather than merely tolerated. I think Leibniz's own machinery discriminates sharply between these, and I want to know which of them the theodicy can actually absorb and which it cannot. Second, and more important: Leibniz distinguishes, as I understand him, between evils that are unavoidable concomitants of a package of goods — a matter of what is composible with what — and evils that function as means to goods, and he is at pains to say that God permits the former without ever willing evil as a means. The story insists that the prosperity of Omelas would wither if the child were brought into the light, but it never says whether that dependence is causal, metaphysical, or simply brute. So the sharpest question I can put to the text is whether Omelas is even the sort of world optimism could bless: a world in which the suffering is a structural concomitant of the good, or one in which it is the price of it. I would also like you to address a worry from the other direction: if for any possible world there is a better one, optimism fails without any particular evil being to blame, and I want to know whether that no-maximum worry is visible in the story at all, or whether it is a problem Leibniz has that Le Guin does not.

One constraint on the reading, and one request about its shape. The constraint: the group is secular and largely hostile to theodicy as a genre, so I want the strongest version of the Leibnizian reading that does not lean on God, a fall, a felix culpa, or post-mortem compensation — call it structural optimism, the claim that value attaches to whole worlds as maxima and that no local evil can be excised without global loss. If you think the reading collapses once those props are removed, say so and explain why, since that is itself useful for the session. The shape: three or four cruxes — the child in the basement, the festival, the citizen who is told and weeps and then goes on, the ones who walk away toward a destination the narrator cannot describe; pick others if you prefer — with, for each, what the Leibnizian lens makes visible and where the lens has to admit the story is a genuine counterexample rather than an instance. Close with what you think the story contributes to the argument that the argument on its own does not; my own guess is that it is something about the phenomenology of refusing a benefit you have already received, and about whether goods taken at that price are the same goods, but tell me if that is wrong. Aim for roughly eight hundred words, do not quote the story or Leibniz at me from memory, and do correct me if I have misstated any of his machinery.
