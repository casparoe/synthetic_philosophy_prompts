I've been reading about the norms of assertion and I want to test the debate on a real case: Pons and Fleischmann's cold fusion announcement in 1989. Facts as I'll stipulate them: two electrochemists at the University of Utah announced at a press conference, before the details were properly out in a journal, that their heavy-water cell had produced excess heat they took to be nuclear, and reported small amounts of neutrons and tritium alongside it; the radiation they reported was orders of magnitude below anything that could accompany that much heat; nobody could replicate it; it became the modern cautionary tale. Assume too that part of why they went public when they did was fear of being scooped by a rival group.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a survey. Take the knowledge norm, the truth norm, and the belief / reasonable-belief norms in turn, and for each say: what exactly is the act being assessed (the press conference as a whole? an individual sentence in it? the months of follow-up?), who counts as the speaker (the two of them, the lab, the university's press office — do these norms even have anything to say about an institution asserting?), what the norm says they should have done instead (sit on it, publish first, hedge, wait for replication), and which of those options were genuinely open to them. I know this literature's usual test cases (the creationist teacher, the selfless assertion) and dont need them rehersed except where this case differs from them.

Then two things I can't find discussed. First: a friend argued to me that a scientific announcement isn't an assertion at all but a seperate speech act — call it a proffer, where you put a finding into the public record for others to test, and the governing norm is something like: proffer only if the result is worth other people's time and you've done due diligence. Does that move earn anything here, or does it just relabel the problem? Second, what does the case do to the standard norms — does the fact that Pons and Fleischmann were blamed rather than excused support the knowledge norm, or is the blame better explained as blame for bad inquiry rather than for bad asserting? And if it's the latter, is that an anomaly for the whole debate: a sign that these are norms for flat assertion between individuals and that this case was never in their domain?
