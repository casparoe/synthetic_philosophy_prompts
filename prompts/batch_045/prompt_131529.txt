I work on a small team that fits a reward model from the behaviour of a single client — call him H — for a semi-autonomous home-assistance system. The model is then acted on without checking back each time, so a bad inference is not a harmless line in a document; it is a week of H's life arranged around a guess. Our inference lead keeps a casebook of the calls she has made whenever observed behaviour had to be turned into a preference. The background assumption is the standard one in inverse reinforcement learning: any stretch of behaviour is equally well explained by a different reward, a different belief about how the world works, a different planning ability, or a different feasible set, and the casebook exists to say which of those we adjust.

Her recorded calls so far:

1. H says he wants more exercise but never takes the stairs; the stairwell door has been broken for months. Call: no inference against exercise. The option was not genuinely in the feasible set.

2. H avoids the 6:15 bus on Sundays because he believes it does not run then; it does. Call: infer a preference for arriving on time, not a preference for the slower route. The behaviour is explained by a false belief about the dynamics rather than by the reward.

3. H walks past an identical and closer coffee cart to buy from one particular barista. Call: the object of the preference is that barista's coffee, not coffee. We do not substitute an objective equivalent for the thing he actually tracks.

4. H takes the certain option over a higher-expected-value gamble even when the stakes are trivial. Call: that is risk aversion, part of the reward, not a distortion to be divided out.

5. H says he wants to save and spends heavily on the day his pension lands. Call: model two reward components with a present-bias term rather than force a single consistent utility function over him.

6. H always takes whichever item is listed first on the screen. Call: no preference inferred. Behaviour that does not covary with any payoff-relevant feature of the options is an artefact of the interface, not evidence.

7. H refuses a transfusion his clinician recommends, on religious grounds. Call: infer a genuine preference against transfusion, and do not correct it the way we corrected the bus timetable. The correction policy in call 2 was restricted to descriptive beliefs about how the world works; we do not adjudicate whether a religious commitment is true.

8. H books the earliest available appointment and then reports that the wait spoilt his morning. On one occasion the system booked a later slot by mistake; he said the day had gone better and asked to keep it. Call: the preference is the one his retrospective reports reveal, not the one his choices reveal.

9. H has lived for years in a flat whose windows face a wall and says he prefers no direct sun. Call: no inference. Where the apparent preference may itself have been shaped by a constraint we can now remove, we abstain and gather evidence by making the option genuinely available.

The new case. H has declined a rollator that would let him get to the garden and, more to the point, to his grandson's graduation. His stated reason is that once he starts using one of those he will never walk again. He did use a walker for six weeks after a hip fracture and describes it as the worst six weeks of his life, though that was during acute post-surgical pain. He has also put the refusal in different terms to different people: to his daughter as a prediction about his legs, to his physiotherapist in the language of what he is and is not willing to be.

I have to write this call up myself. So: what rules are the nine calls jointly consistent with? I can see at least three, differing over whether an identity-laden refusal that also contains a false empirical prediction should be treated as a belief to be corrected, a commitment to be respected, or grounds for abstention and a reversible trial. Tell me what each predicts here and which one you would record. Say which precedent is the closest analogue among 2, 7, 8 and 9, and what turns on treating it as that one rather than another. Name the further observation that would separate the rules from each other, bearing in mind that on the commitment reading a good experience with the rollator will not move him, so a trial is not automatically evidence for anything. Finally, state what principle we would be adopting under each option, and what the two kinds of error look like from H's side, given that we act on the model without asking him.
