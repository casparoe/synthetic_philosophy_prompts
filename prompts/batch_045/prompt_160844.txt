I'm a journalist writing about the current boom in prestige dramatizations of real lives — a limited series that invents its subject's private conversations wholesale, a biopic that folds three colleagues into one composite character, a docudrama that gives a real and well-documented figure a fabricated motive for a decision she in fact made. The more ethical commentary I read, the more I find myself holding what I'll call the surrogate-interest view: the dead are not the kind of thing that can be wronged or benefited, so whatever we owe concerning them — including whatever we owe in dramatizing them — is owed to the living: to descendants who still live inside the story, to communities that organize themselves around a memory, to a public with an interest in an accurate record. Tell me what would count against it. I want the cases, intuitions and adjoining commitments it stands or falls with — the fabricated atrocity pinned on someone dead three centuries, the deathbed promise kept long after everyone who witnessed it is gone, the last surviving eyewitness, the invention no viewer could ever detect and that costs no living person anything — ranked from fatal to merely awkward, together with which of them a defender should concede rather than manage. I especially want to know whether the move I keep making in my own drafts has insulated the view past the point of risk: that we can still respect the interests of the person she was before dying, since her ante-mortem desires reached forward into precisely these post-mortem events — the prospective-interest move, as I've been calling it. If it has, say whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply what a true view looks like once stated carefully. For what it's worth, the undetectable-invention case is the one I would personally find hardest to shrug off.
