I'm applying for a nine-month dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal that does more than announce a topic. My area is Dewey's experimentalism: the claim, running from his writings on the logic of inquiry into his political theory, that the pattern of intelligent problem-solving, an indeterminate situation, the formulation of a genuine problem, hypotheses, and the test by consequences, is also the pattern that collective decisions ought to follow, so that democracy is not a procedure for counting antecedent preferences but the social form inquiry takes when the consequences of an action fall on people other than the agents. Assume the reader already knows this material; I don't want any of the two pages spent explaining it.

Here is the half-formed idea I need turned into a research question, and it came out of an argument I had at a workshop last spring between a political theorist and a machine-learning researcher who clearly meant different things by participation. Every contemporary institutional attempt to bring the public into technically mediated decisions, citizens' assemblies on climate or on AI governance, deliberative polling, decentralized fact-checking on social platforms, public comment periods attached to model or system deployments, is described by its designers as democratic deliberation, but my suspicion is that most of these are aggregative mechanisms wearing the vocabulary of inquiry, and that Dewey's apparatus supplies a principled way to say which is which. The test I keep coming back to: a mechanism counts as inquiry only if it can revise the ends-in-view that its participants arrived with, that is, reconstitute what the problem is taken to be, rather than optimize means toward ends treated as fixed inputs; call this the reframing condition. I then want two failure modes kept apart, the case where reframing is blocked because the agenda and the option space are fixed in advance by experts, and the case where reframing is permitted but nothing in the design lets the revised ends feed back into the decision that gets made. The proposal should also face two problems I cannot solve on my own: first, if democratic inquiry is to govern the design of institutions, it must also govern the design of the institutions that do the governing, which looks regressive, and I want to know whether the view can supply its own stopping rule or has to import one; second, Dewey's own account has a public constituting itself by recognizing the consequences it suffers, yet in cases like AI deployment or germline editing the affected parties are in part future or unidentified, and I want to know whether the view needs a proxy for the absent public that it cannot generate from its own resources.

What I want from the proposal, concretely: a precise statement of the question, stated so that it admits a determinate answer rather than a free-floating verdict; an account of why the two obvious literatures, the standard treatment of Dewey on democracy as a mode of associated living and the mainstream theory of deliberative democracy, each leave that question open; a month-by-month method for nine months, including what I would actually work through and which of the above cases I would use as test instances; a statement of what success would look like, which I take to be a usable diagnostic that someone designing such a mechanism could apply to it, and, just as important and honestly, what would count as the project failing; and an argument for why this approach beats the alternatives, in particular why not simply evaluate these mechanisms empirically by their outcomes, and why not run the whole inquiry through a discourse-ethics framework instead. Close by telling me what to cut to make nine months realistic: my committee has one political theorist who suspects the whole thing is normative hand-waving and one computer scientist who suspects it is, at best, a spec sheet, so the proposal has to be legible to both without the philosophical claim being watered down to accommodate either. Don't give me a bibliography or a literature review organized around citations; name the positions and arguments you think I have to engage and I will track down the references myself.
