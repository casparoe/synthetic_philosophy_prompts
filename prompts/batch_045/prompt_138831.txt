Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a representation realist and a representation deflationist about trained neural networks. The realist holds, roughly, that in a trained network there is a decomposition of its internal state into a manageable number of causally efficacious variables; that these variables have content; and that current interpretability methods can, at least sometimes, be said to discover rather than impose them. The deflationist holds, roughly, that any such decomposition is one among many equally well-behaved ones; that what gets called a feature is a direction a method found convenient under a sparsity penalty plus a story a human found memorable; and that the genuine causal-explanatory structure of the network lives at the level of the whole computation, so that feature talk is either a harmless shorthand or a systematic source of false confidence. Produce the document both of them could sign after a year of good-faith argument.

Fix the following shared setup before you begin, and state it in a short preamble: both parties work with the same 1-billion-parameter decoder-only transformer trained on a mixed text corpus, and with the same unsupervised dictionary-learning pipeline (a sparse autoencoder) fitted to the residual-stream activations at one middle layer. They have jointly studied three example recovered directions that they agree to use as running examples: one that seems to track the language a passage is written in, one that seems to track something like sentiment or politeness, and one that seems to track references to a particular kind of named entity. They also agree to treat as a separate, cleaner test case a small transformer trained on a synthetic language generated by a known grammar over a known set of latent variables, so that in that case the right answer about which variables the network tracks is available by construction.

The deliverable has five parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. Include claims about what the pipeline does and does not establish on its own; about what stands in need of explanation; about what the familiar arguments in this vicinity rule out regardless of where you start; and about what a representation claim would have to deliver in order to be worth making. Every claim must be substantive: it should be possible for a competent partisan on either side to deny it. Discard any candidate that reduces to a definition, to a methodological platitude, or to the observation that the question is hard.

Second, a disagreements table. Give each residual disagreement a row with: the disputed claim in neutral language; the realist formulation, written so that a realist would sign it without wincing; the deflationist formulation, likewise; and a note on why the two wordings differ. Separate the disagreement into its component sub-questions rather than leaving it as one block — at minimum, whether a privileged decomposition exists at all, whether the candidate variables carry content, whether the causal role attributed to them is the one they actually play, and whether our methods converge on whatever is there. Say explicitly which of these are live for these two parties and which they turn out not to be fighting about.

Third, a crux table: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, and, for each, a concrete test, case, or argument that both parties accept in advance as bearing on it, together with each party's prediction written down before the test is run. Consider, among others you think better:

(a) The change-of-basis argument. Given any invertible linear map on a layer's activation space, one can insert it into the upstream weights and its inverse into the downstream weights, leaving the network's input-output function and all of its internal causal structure intact. Ask what follows about whether any particular decomposition, as opposed to the subspaces it spans or the linear functionals it induces, can be the one the network uses.

(b) The illegible-ground-truth control. In the synthetic case, make the true latent variables deliberately illegible to humans — for instance, let them be random linear functionals of the input, or dense and mutually entangled rather than sparse and independent, or more numerous than the available dimensions. If the pipeline recovers them, that is evidence it tracks structure in the network rather than structure a human can name; if it fails, the deflationist's legibility worry gains weight. Give each side's prediction and say what each would infer from either outcome.

(c) The untrained-network control, together with an assessment of whether it is well designed. Applying the identical pipeline to the same architecture with random weights is the obvious control, but the activation statistics differ from those of a trained network in ways that make a null result ambiguous. Propose the control both parties would actually accept, and say what result each predicts.

(d) Cross-model transplant and far-transfer steering. Take a direction recovered from one network and use it, without refitting, to steer a second network of the same architecture trained on different data from a different initialization; and separately, take a direction identified on one input distribution and test whether steering along it produces the predicted behaviour in a context as far as possible from the one in which it was found. Say what pattern of results each side predicts and what pattern each would count as adverse.

(e) A compression or out-of-distribution prediction criterion: whether a decomposition's usefulness for predicting the network's behaviour on inputs unlike those used to fit it tracks something that deserves the name of correctness, and how one would tell.

If you conclude that one of these is not in fact diagnostic, say so in the table and explain what test would be, rather than padding the row.

Fourth, a short retraction-conditions section: what each party would accept as counting against its own view, stated strongly enough that the other party would recognise it as a real concession rather than a tautology.

Fifth, a closing section identifying which parts of the dispute are verbal and why, and which are not — and, for the parts that are not, one sentence each on what the collaboration commits to doing next, including how the pre-registered predictions would be adjudicated.

Constraints. Do not cite, reference, or name any paper, author, or empirical result; I will fill in citations, and I would rather you not invent findings. Where a crux turns on how an experiment would come out, write it as a prediction to be tested, not as a settled fact. Define every technical term you use — residual stream, direction, superposition, steering, linear probe, dictionary — well enough for a philosopher of science who has never read the machine-learning side. Write for an audience of philosophers and machine-learning researchers who do not know each other's literatures. Aim for roughly two thousand words plus the tables. The register is that of a document both parties will put their names to: no verdict, no rhetorical point-scoring, no suggestion that the truth must lie in the middle, and no claim included merely because both sides happen to assert it.
