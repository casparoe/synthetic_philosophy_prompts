I'm a historian of labor movements, finishing a monograph on municipal general strikes in interwar Europe, and a referee's report on the manuscript has backed me into a methodological corner I can't work my way out of. My central case is a transport strike that I argue was broken primarily by the national federation's refusal to release strike funds; the referee says the outcome was "overdetermined," because the funds were nearly exhausted anyway, the city government had already signed a replacement labor agreement with a non-union contractor, and the strike committee had lost the confidence of one of the two key affiliates, and any one of these would have ended the strike on its own, so my causal claim can't carry the weight I put on it. I have been going back and forth on this for months. A lawyer friend tells me tort law runs into something structurally similar with its "substantial factor" test, which suggests I am not the first person to trip over this, but I cannot find the concept in my own field's methodological writing.

What I keep noticing, in my manuscript and in everyone else's, is this. Whether the strike would still have collapsed without the funding decision depends entirely on how much detail I build into the phrase "the strike." Stated thinly — the work stoppage ended without the workers' demands being met — the collapse survives the removal of any one of my three factors, and the case really does look overdetermined. Stated thickly — a stoppage that held for eleven days, was directed by an elected rank-and-file committee, and ended when one affiliate's membership voted to return — the collapse survives the removal of none of them, and the overdetermination evaporates. Nobody says which grain is the correct one, and a single chapter will often use two grains within a page of each other without remarking on the switch. For my own use I have started distinguishing sliding vertically (adding or subtracting detail from the description) from sliding horizontally (whether features such as the timing, the mechanism, or the identity of the actors count as part of the event at all), and the two kinds of slide seem to do different damage. Two things make me think this is not just overdetermination: overdetermination is supposed to be a matter of several processes each sufficient for the very same event, whereas here what is in question is what makes it the same event; and when I described the pattern to a colleague, she said it was just the familiar point that why-questions come with a contrast class, but in my cases the question stays fixed — why did the strike collapse? — and only the outcome term moves, which feels like a different and worse problem. A sociologist I asked mentioned "equifinality," but as I understand it that assumes a fixed endpoint, and a fixed endpoint is precisely what I don't have.

So: does this have a name, in historiography, in the philosophy of causation, or anywhere else, and if it doesn't, what would you call it and how would you define it so that it is not merely overdetermination, not merely the contrast-sensitivity of explanation, and not merely the platitude that large outcomes have many routes to them? I am not after a reading list or citations — I can find those myself — I want the carving: what exactly the move consists in; whether my vertical/horizontal distinction is the right axis to cut along or whether there is a better one; what the boundary cases are, in particular when a re-description is a legitimate sharpening of what stands in need of explanation and when it is a way of insulating a favored cause from a counterfactual; and finally whether the phenomenon, if it is real, vindicates or destroys my referee's objection — whether the permanent availability of a thinner description means that "your case is overdetermined" can be said of anything and therefore says nothing, or whether the thin description is in fact the right level at which to make causal claims about large events, in which case I should cut the chapter.
