For a paper I am writing on whether the criteria Hume sets out for credible miracle-testimony are individually necessary but jointly insufficient, I need a scenario that makes the failure of additivity vivid. Please design one for me.

What the case has to do: three or more attested events, each of which — considered on its own, before the others are on the table — satisfies every one of Hume's tests for credible testimony (witnesses who are numerous; of good sense, education and learning; of undoubted integrity; reporting an event performed publicly, in a noted part of the world), and each of which, taken alone, would support the truth of some contested religious claim. But the set must be jointly unreportable: once all three are on the table, the rational response is to reject all of them, even though nothing has been shown wrong with any one of them individually.

Constraints:

- The witnesses for each event must be religiously neutral outsiders with no stake in the claim the event is later taken to support — attending physicians, court or colonial recorders, journalists, inspectors. So the collapse cannot be explained by Hume's own way out, that miracle reports are accredited by the credulous and the interested.
- The claims supported must be genuinely incompatible, not merely different interpretations of the same occurrence. The case must not be soluble by saying that all the events happened and only the theology disagrees.
- The three events must be similar enough that noticing the pattern is what defeats them, but distinct enough that no shared cause — one epidemic, one storm, one fraud, one mistranslation — explains them away as a single ordinary episode. That tension is the crux; tell me explicitly how you resolve it.
- Realistic and non-science-fictional; set in the twentieth century or later; no stipulation that anyone is lying or self-deceived; and no appeal to the premise that we antecedently know miracles do not occur.

After the scenario, do the work: apply Hume's tests to one of the events in isolation and show that they return a verdict of belief; then apply them to the set and show the verdict flipping to rejection, naming precisely which step in the reasoning changes when the second and third events are added. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the argument if that feature is deleted. Then give the strongest reply available to someone who insists the tests are additive: what would they say about your scenario, and is that reply one I should take seriously?

Finally, say where your own construction is most likely to fail, and what would convince you that no case meeting these specifications can be built at all.
