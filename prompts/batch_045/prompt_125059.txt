I'm working through a survey handout on epistemic externalism that was circulated for a graduate seminar I'm sitting in on, and one passage moves much faster than I can follow. I've copied it out below.

"Externalists hold that a belief counts as knowledge when it is produced by a process that reliably tracks the truth; nothing about the subject's own reasons for trusting the process is required. The problem of easy knowledge — also called the bootstrapping problem — turns that permissiveness against them. Suppose you are shown a card along with a device that prints THE CARD IS RED. The device is in fact perfectly reliable, but you have no independent evidence of this; all you have is its printouts. Still, you may read the printout and infer from it that the card is red, and infer from that that the device said something true, and from a few hundred such episodes that the device is reliable. Nothing in process reliabilism blocks any step, yet the upshot is that you have come to know that your source is reliable on the basis of that very source. Worse, had a trickster substituted a device that prints at random, the reasoning would have looked exactly the same to you from the inside — so reliabilism cannot mark the difference between the good case and the bad one. That is the refutation: any theory that lets the output of a source be converted into knowledge of that source's own reliability is untenable. And the moral is not parochial, since the argument needs only that a theory permit basic knowledge — beliefs that are non-inferentially justified and rest on nothing further — so it tells equally against internalist evidentialism. The internalist, however, can sidestep it by demanding accessible reasons, which is why the stable position here is internalism. Externalists have essentially one reply left: deny the closure principle, the thesis that knowledge is closed under known entailment. Cut the chain of entailments and the argument never gets off the ground."

I don't trust my own reaction, which is that the conclusion is louder than the argument: this looks like a reductio of a package of commitments rather than a direct hit on reliabilism, so it isn't obvious to me that the externalist is the one who has to pay. Three things in particular strike me as possible sleights of hand, and I'd like to know whether I'm right about any of them. First, the handout seems to use "basic knowledge" as if it were the foundationalist's notion of a basic belief, and then leans on that reading to generalize the argument. Second, it presents bootstrapping and easy knowledge as one argument with two names, whereas I've heard that they are different arguments, one of which does not need the repetition at all. Third, the trickster device is said to show that reliabilism "cannot mark the difference" between the cases, but from a reliabilist standpoint the difference is just the actual reliability of the process — indistinguishability from the inside looks like a different complaint.

There is also a variant I constructed while reading, and I can't tell whether it is a real instance of the problem or a degenerate toy. Suppose the device does not report on the card but on itself: pointed at any card, it reliably prints THIS DEVICE IS RELIABLE, and it is reliable. The proposition printed is true, and the process that produces my belief in it is reliable, so a simple process reliabilist seems committed to saying I know that the device is reliable — in a single step, without any inference from its other outputs. Is this genuinely the same problem, is it a special case that is easier or harder for the externalist, or does it dissolve into some other difficulty (the generality problem, say, or the question whether a process can be reliable in virtue of producing one particular true belief about itself)? And would the answer change if there were two such self-certifying devices, each untrusted, whose reports agreed?

What I want is an accuracy audit I can act on, claim by claim: which sentences a specialist would accept as stated, which are acceptable simplifications, which are genuine distortions, and which distinctions the literature keeps apart that the handout collapses. Then, for the corrected version of the argument, tell me what it actually establishes and whether the internalist conclusion follows — my own suspicion is that the handout's last move, the appeal to denying closure, is the weakest part, since giving up closure seems to cost more than the disease, but I can't see what an externalist gives up instead, and I don't trust my sense of the available options. Please also settle the status of my self-certifying-device variant and of the two-device version.

Finally, be explicit about the epistemic status of what you tell me: separate what is settled in this literature from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and flag places where you are giving your own assessment rather than reporting agreement. Don't present a contested response as the consensus, and if you are unsure whether some reply is actually defended by anyone, say so rather than filling the gap with a plausible-sounding position.
