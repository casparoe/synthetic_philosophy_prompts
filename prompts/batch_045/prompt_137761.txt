Before retiring I spent a little over thirty years in experimental physical chemistry, largely on irreversible processes in condensed phases, and for the past two years I have been working through decision theory with a small reading group of philosophers and economists. We have come to the money-pump arguments for diachronic coherence, and the group has settled on a way of describing them that I find persuasive but cannot quite trust. I would like you to audit it rather than endorse it.

The analogy, as the group uses it: an agent whose preferences violate the coherence axioms is like a system with a non-conservative force field, or equivalently like a market quoting mutually inconsistent exchange rates. In both cases there exists a closed path along which the system can be driven round and round with a net yield each circuit — a perpetual motion machine of the first kind on the physical side, a pure arbitrage loop on the market side. The pump is the closed path; the money extracted is the work; the person proposing the sequence of trades is the arbitrageur; and the coherence requirement is the requirement that no closed path have nonzero circulation, that is, the existence of a potential function, or of a single consistent system of prices. Friction corresponds to any cost of trading, any reluctance to reopen a settled question, any noise in the agent's dispositions.

The work the analogy is being made to do is this. The group concludes that diachronic coherence — no cycles in preference over a sequence of trades, no violations of independence across choices made at different times — is a genuine requirement of practical rationality and not merely a modelling convenience, and further that the normative force is structural: the agent is not being blamed for false beliefs or bad luck but for being the kind of thing that can be drained. One member draws a stronger corollary, that the magnitude of the incoherence is measured by the magnitude of the exploitable loop, so that a small violation is a small irrationality, and that once friction is restored the argument collapses entirely, since real arbitrage loops close in microseconds and real agents are nothing like frictionless.

I would like four things.

First, an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the state, the closed path, the work extracted, the potential function, the arbitrageur, transaction costs, and the no-arbitrage equilibrium; and where the target side has no counterpart, say so rather than forcing one.

Second, a sorting of the disanalogies into those that bear on the intended use and those that are inert. In particular I want you to consider: (a) In the physical and market cases the loop is traversed by states, whereas in the agent case it is traversed by choices made at different times by something that remembers and can form plans. Does the pump require the agent to be myopic — to re-decide each trade on the spot — and what becomes of the argument if the agent is capable of resolute choice, adopting a plan at the first node and declining an individually tempting trade at the second? (b) A system with nonzero circulation is defective only relative to a design purpose, and a market with inconsistent prices violates no norm at all; it is merely an opportunity for someone else. What converts exploitable into criticizable in the agent's case? (c) Physical and market loops are run by an external party with better information or faster computation. The pump seems to require an exploiter who knows the agent's dispositions better than the agent does and who can present what is in effect the same exchange again without the agent recognising it as the same. How much survives if the agent is transparent to herself? (d) Hysteresis and path-dependence are ubiquitous and entirely respectable features of physical systems: the state of a magnetic material depends on the history of the applied field, and nothing is thereby defective. Why is the agent's path-dependence not simply hysteresis? Which feature of the physical case — a potential function, a conserved quantity, an underlying state — is doing the normative work, and does the agent case supply one?

Third, a distinction I cannot get straight on my own. The perpetual motion gloss suggests a loop that can be run indefinitely, but I suspect that is available only for cyclic preferences, where the agent can be brought back to a holding it strictly disprefers to what it started with, and not for the sequential-choice violations associated with the Allais pattern, where the extraction looks more like a single exothermic reaction: bounded, and finished once the agent's situation has changed. Is that right? What structural feature makes a pump iterable rather than one-shot, and does the difference matter normatively — is being drainable once as serious as being drainable without limit?

Fourth, a verdict on the coda. Does the analogy license the corollary about small violations and friction, or does it undercut it — for instance, because in the physical case friction does not show that the field was conservative, only that the machine will not run for long, and because the market analogue treats unexploited opportunities as a failure of the other traders rather than a property of the prices? And if the analogy cannot carry the conclusion the group wants, tell me what it can carry, and whether there is a better comparison available.

Please write for a reader who is comfortable with vector calculus and elementary thermodynamics but has no more philosophy than the above implies; define any term of art you rely on. I am not asking for references or further reading, and I would be grateful if you would flag, rather than assert, any step that turns on a technical result I should check independently.
