I sit on the dual-use review committee at my research institute. We review proposed work whose downside, in the branch of possibility where the optimistic risk assessment is wrong, would be severe and not confined to the people who chose to take the risk. I want help with Thursday's docket item, and I want it in two parts: a prediction of what the committee will do, and a recommendation of what it should do if coherence with our own record is the goal. What I care about is the reasoning that licenses either answer, not the answer itself.

The framework I have in mind is the unilateralist's curse, roughly: when some action is available unilaterally to each of many agents, when it is worth taking if the world is in one state and catastrophic if it is in another, and when each agent decides on the basis of a noisy judgment of its own, then the chance that the action is taken grows with the number of agents who can take it, while the chance that it is taken *correctly* does not grow with them. Each agent's own expected-value calculation therefore systematically overstates the case for acting, because it prices the upside it would capture and not the share of the downside borne by everyone else. The standard moral is supposed to be some kind of conformity or deference: do not act on your own judgment alone when the structure is like this.

Our record, in the form I would defend it:

1. Approved unanimously, 2019. A transmissibility study on a seasonal influenza strain in ferrets. Exactly one lab in the country held the containment rating for it; worst case was a contained escape, serious but not global; the risk assessment was commissioned by us from an outside group. Minute: single possible actor, bounded downside, ordinary review suffices.

2. Rejected four to one, 2021. An experiment of the same class with the same per-experiment risk estimate, but by then the technique had appeared in a widely read methods paper and roughly forty labs worldwide could run it within the year. The assessment was thorough, but it had been commissioned and paid for by the applicant's own funder. Minute: our decision binds one group, and the risk is set by the least careful group that can now do this.

3. Approved unanimously with conditions, 2022. Work on a pathogen with pandemic potential at a single national high-containment facility. Catastrophic worst case, unconditionally severe; no other group in the world could have done it that year; an independent external panel assessed it. Conditions concerned reporting cadence and withholding the protocol from publication.

4. Rejected three to two, 2023. A protocol in the same risk class whose only risk assessment was the applicant's; our own staff could not finish an independent review before the funder's deadline; the group intended to publish the protocol, after which many labs could copy it; and the group declined a delay. Minute: we have no estimate except the applicant's, and no one to coordinate with.

5. Approved four to one with conditions, 2024. Open publication of a model together with a fine-tuning recipe that measurably lowers the cost of a sensitive capability. Independent third-party evaluations found no uplift. Approval was conditional on a six-month delay and on release being folded into a coordinated, simultaneous disclosure with other institutes. Minute: the objection was to unilateralism, not to the work; coordination cured it.

6. Rejected four to one, 2024. The same shape as item five, except that the evaluations were run by the applicant's own collaborators using the applicant's benchmark suite, the group refused any delay, and no sector forum was available to coordinate with. Minute: coordination was the condition, and it is not on offer.

Thursday's item:

A lab proposes to publish open weights for a mid-sized model from which it has removed refusal behaviour, together with the fine-tuning recipe and the evaluation harness. An independent evaluator, funded by a foundation with no stake in the outcome, found no dangerous-capability uplift — but the evaluator had no alternative to the applicant's own benchmark suite, so its error is probably correlated with the applicant's rather than independent of it. The recipe is arguably already in the air: two other groups have said publicly they could reproduce it within weeks, so the release may add less capability to the world than it adds convenience and an artifact for others to build on. Downstream, dozens of groups could fine-tune the released weights further, and a meaningful subset of them sit outside every coordination forum we can reach. The lab will not delay: the release date is fixed, and it has told us it will release whether or not we endorse. So we cannot prevent the action. What we can withhold is endorsement — and our decisions are published in redacted form, and two other review bodies have told us they read them when setting their own policy.

What I want from you:

State, precisely enough to apply, the two or three rules that fit all six precedents. I can see at least three candidates — one keyed to how many agents could take the action, one keyed to whether the risk estimate comes from a party with a stake in approval, one keyed to whether the action can be converted from a unilateral one into a coordinated one — but I want them stated so that they actually discriminate, and I want to know whether any of them is really the same rule as another in different words.

Say what each predicts about Thursday's item, where they diverge, and which divergence is doing the work. Give me your best guess of the committee's verdict with the reasons for preferring it and some indication of how confident you are.

Then the recommendation: if the committee wants to be consistent with itself, what should it decide, which precedent is the closest analog, and where our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would we be adopting under each of the available choices. Be explicit about which choice adds a new principle to our record rather than applying an old one.

Two complications I want handled rather than gestured at. First, correlated evidence: the curse's force is supposed to come from the independence of agents' errors, so tell me whether an institutionally independent evaluation that shares the applicant's benchmarks counts as independent in the sense the argument needs, and what our record has already committed us to on that. Second, the reflexive case: our own decision looks like it is itself subject to the curse — a permissive decision, published, may move other review bodies and thereby increase the number of agents willing to take actions of this kind. Tell me whether a committee applying the unilateralist's curse should treat its own published permission as the kind of diffusing action it rejects in applicants, and what follows either way.

Finally, name the one variation on this case, or the one further case, that would best tell the surviving rules apart — and say plainly where the curse framework is silent here: what it gives us no guidance on, and what we would have to decide on grounds it does not supply.
