Our neuroethics working group is writing the theoretical preamble to a protocol for detecting awareness in patients who show no behavioural response at the bedside — patients diagnosed as unresponsive, some of whom nonetheless produce willful, sustained brain changes when asked in the scanner to imagine moving a hand, and about whom we have to decide whether analgesia is indicated, whether a sedated patient was aware during a procedure, and eventually whether the same criterion can be extended to infants, to animals in veterinary and laboratory settings, and to depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring. The committee is provisionally committed to phrasing the criterion in higher-order terms: a patient is consciously in a mental state only if the patient is, in the right way, aware of being in it. Before I sign off on that, I want the specification it would have to meet.

Write me the desiderata for a higher-order theory of consciousness that is to be good enough to underwrite a test of this kind. Adequacy is relative to a purpose, so take the purpose to be fixed: the theory has to yield an operationalisable verdict, from evidence available in a patient who cannot speak, move reliably, or follow commands outside the scanner, about whether that patient is now consciously undergoing some experience, and if so something about what kind.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it and mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Here is the raw material I think belongs on the list, which you should correct, split, merge, or throw out rather than simply reproduce:

- It must not count a higher-order state as sufficient when the first-order state it represents is absent — the targetless or misrepresenting higher-order state, as in a subject who is convinced she is seeing something that is not there at all, or who judges that she is in pain while undergoing no such state.
- It must not count mere unconscious self-monitoring as sufficient: error-detection, conflict-monitoring, and confidence-like signals can plausibly operate without any experience, and if a criterion counts those, it counts thermostats and it will produce false positives in exactly the patients we care about.
- It must distinguish whole-organism consciousness — being awake, responsive, an organism with a point of view — from a particular mental state's being conscious, since a patient can be awake and yet undergoing no experience of anything, and the protocol needs the second notion.
- It must make the here-and-now verdict authoritative rather than the dispositional one: a patient who would report an experience if prompted, or who recalls one on waking from anaesthesia, is a different case from a patient having it now, and the protocol needs the difference to be real.
- It must say how finely the higher-order state has to represent its target — whether it suffices that the subject be aware of being in some state or two, or whether the higher-order state must single out the specific character of the experience — because the neural markers we can actually measure are coarse and aggregate.
- It must earn its keep against a first-order view: if the criterion ends up being that the state is globally available, reportable, or accessible to downstream systems, then the higher-order apparatus is doing no work the access theorist did not already have, and the preamble should say so plainly.
- It must be usable without the subject's report at all, not merely without speech: several of the patients will fail every command-following task, so any requirement that the higher-order state be verified by what the subject does with it is self-defeating here.
- It must be able to be wrong on the evidence we have, rather than fitted post hoc to whatever the patient later reports.
- It must say something, even if only by implication, about whether the detected state suffices for suffering, since the clinical point of the verdict is largely analgesia and prognostication.
- It must give consistent verdicts on infants, on non-human animals, and on the split-brain and blindsight-type dissociations, or explain why it is entitled to stay silent about them.

Three things I want specifically. First: which of these are genuine desiderata on a theory of consciousness, and which are demands of the clinic that a theory of consciousness is entitled to ignore — in particular, whether it is legitimate to demand that a theory be shaped by the asymmetry in the cost of its errors, given that a false negative here may mean untreated pain or a premature decision about life-sustaining treatment while a false positive means little. Second: tell me whether some subset of the surviving requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. My own suspicion is that the demand for a report-independent marker and the demand that the criterion exclude targetless higher-order states cannot both be met, since telling a veridical higher-order state from an empty one seems to require exactly the kind of access to the first-order state that the protocol cannot get; but I also suspect the coarse-marker demand and the content-specificity demand pull apart, and I would rather hear about a tension I have not noticed. If the list is itself the interesting result, say so and say what follows for the protocol. Third: name the single requirement that does most to narrow the field, and why.

Then, in a few lines only — I will do the detailed scoring myself — say how the three main families fare: actualist views on which the higher-order state is a distinct occurrent thought about one's own state; inner-sense views on which it is a quasi-perceptual monitoring of one's own mental goings-on; and dispositionalist or interpretivist views on which it suffices that the subject be disposed to judge, or be interpretable as judging, that she is in the state. I want a verdict on whether any of them can underwrite the test we are proposing, not a survey of the literature; no citations and no reading list, since I am working from the structure of the positions rather than the texts.
