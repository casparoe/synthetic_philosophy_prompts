I'm applying to a philosophy summer programme, and as part of the application I have to write a short statement of my own philosophical commitments on a topic of my choosing and then defend it in an interview. I chose moral luck, partly because of something that happened in my town last year: one student's older brother was driving down a familiar street, glanced at his phone for a second, and hit a child who ran out between two parked cars; another guy I know did exactly the same thing on the same street the same week and nothing happened at all. Everyone around me reacted to the two of them in completely different ways, and I couldn't tell whether that reaction was just irrational or whether there was something in it worth saving, so the readings my teacher gave me (the usual Nagel and Williams essays on luck) felt like they were about something real. Here is roughly where I've landed, and I'd like it stress-tested before I send it in:

(1) I accept something like the control principle: two agents can't differ in how blameworthy they are, or in what they deserve, unless they differ in something that was up to them. (2) Even so, I think the law should punish attempted murder less severely than a completed murder even in cases where the two agents were exactly as careless and came exactly as close to killing — not because it deters anyone, but because there is something about an actual death that has to be answered for, and the outcome seems to me to matter to the response. (3) At the same time, I'm fairly hard-line that punishment should track what people deserve and not what would be useful; I really dislike the idea of sentencing someone more harshly because it satisfies the public or sets an example. (4) I think the driver who hit the child is not more blameworthy than the one who didn't, and is not a worse person, but there is still something he has to do that the other one doesn't — showing up, saying he is sorry, helping the family in practical ways. I've started calling this answerability and I want it to be a separate thing from blameworthiness. (5) I also think people can be properly blamed for who they are — for being cruel, cowardly or callous — even though no one chooses their temperament, because those traits are the person himself rather than something that merely happened to him. (6) And I think the honest upshot of all of this is that the problem of moral luck has no solution: once you see how much of what we blame people for is a matter of luck, there is no way to make the practice add up — although I also think that this conclusion should change nothing at all about how we treat each other in ordinary life.

Which of these actually conflict? I'd rather have the conflicts ranked by how serious they are than get a list in which everything is either fine or equally broken, and for each real conflict I want to know which commitment would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. And if some pairs only look like they clash but are in fact compatible, please say which those are and why, because I don't want to drop a view I actually believe for no reason.
