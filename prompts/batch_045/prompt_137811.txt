My dad died about six months ago and left me and my sister Ruth twenty grand each. Ruth is the devout one — Mass every week, the whole thing — and I'm the family's resident consequentialist, the sort of person who gives to bednet charities and thinks money should go where it does the most good per pound.

She wants her twenty grand to go to an endowment for Masses for Dad's soul and to the parish roof. I think that's worth approximately nothing and that her share should go to the bednets. She thinks the bednets are nice but trivial next to the state of Dad's soul and to my seven-year-old growing up with no faith at all.

Her husband Marcus has been reading about something he calls moral trade — his gloss is that when two people disagree about what's actually valuable, they can still both come out ahead if each does the thing that is cheap for them but precious by the other one's lights, because each of them can do something the other one can't. His proposal: Ruth's twenty grand goes to my charity, and in exchange I commit to a year of taking my son to Mass and letting him do the children's stuff. She can't manufacture my attendance; I can't move her money. Win-win, he says, and he's at about 0.9 that it's the right thing for me to do. Ruth is at maybe 0.15, because her tradition apparently has a name for buying and selling spiritual things and she thinks this lands uncomfortably close to it. I'm at roughly 0.4 and I definately can't put my finger on why I feel queasy, which is why I'm asking.

So: your credence that going through with this is the right thing for me to do — permissible, and better by my own best lights than either refusing or just letting everyone spend their own money their own way. I want a single number and the decomposition behind it, and if you think I've cut it up wrongly, cut it differently. The sub-questions I keep circling:

- whether the whole move of treating moral disagreement as an opportunity for exchange is kosher at all, or whether you should just act on your own view and let other people act on theirs.
- whether the thing I'd be selling even exists. If I'm sitting in that pew not believing a word of it and doing it purely as part of a deal, does that deliver anything by Ruth's own lights, or does the value on her view come from the willing? If it delivers nothing, I'm taking her money for nothing, and I don't think she just gets to stipulate that it counts.
- the integrity cost to me: pretending to worship, in front of my own kid, for money. A genuine cost that I'm undercounting because it doesn't appear anywhere in my ledger, or sentimental squeamishness?
- exploitation: if I'm confident it's worthless, is taking twenty grand for it a species of fraud? Does it change anything that she knows exactly what I believe?
- the one that's been loosing me sleep. I'm not certain Ruth is wrong. I'd put maybe 10% on her broad picture being roughly right, and if it is, what I'd be selling is worth something enormous — Dad, my son, forever. But then the thing I'm selling is already very valuable by my own lights, so the surplus the trade is supposed to create is much smaller than Marcus thinks, and there's a real chance I'd be flogging something precious well below its price. And if her stakes really are infinite rather than just very large, I'm not sure the comparison holds together at all.
- sequencing: I'd be performing over a year, she'd be paying up front, and all she can really verify is my body in a pew. Does the enforcement problem sink it, or not?

Then combine them into one number, tell me which of the three of us — Marcus at 0.9, Ruth at 0.15, me at 0.4 — is most likely wrong about which sub-question, and name the single piece of evidence or argument that would move your number the furthest. No sitting on the fence, please. And British English throughout.
