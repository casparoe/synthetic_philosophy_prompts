I am a PhD student in comparative moral philosophy, and the chapter I am currently drafting concerns the structure of role-based reasons in classical Confucian ethics. I keep collapsing two accounts that I think should be kept apart, and I would like a careful comparison of them, worked out against the test cases I have been using.

Call the first the **constitutive account**. On it, the reasons a person has in virtue of occupying a role are fixed by the internal point or norm of that role as the role is constituted by *li* within a given form of life: to ask what a father owes his son is to ask what it is to be a father, and the only criticism of a role-demand available from within the practice is the charge that it is inconsistent with the role's own point. Call the second the **historical account**. On it, role reasons are debts incurred by a history of asymmetrical giving and receiving between these particular individuals: filial obligation is a matter of having been cared for, and its content, weight, and expiry track what was actually received, by whom, and at what cost to the giver.

I want the comparison run through five pressure points, in each case saying not merely what each account predicts but which prediction is more plausible and why.

1. **Role fission.** One person bears two roles toward the same individual — the man who is both a boy's father and his teacher, or the elder brother who raised a younger sibling and is now dependent on him. Does the constitutive account have to say how two role-norms compose into a single practical requirement, and can it do so without an ordering principle that is not itself role-relative? Does the historical account have to say whether debts to the same creditor aggregate, or whether the second relation discharges rather than adds to the first?

2. **The empty role.** A man abandons his infant son and reappears at seventy expecting to be supported and mourned. Here role-occupancy and history of care come apart completely. Which account can better explain the pull of both verdicts — that something is owed and that it is not the full thing — and at what cost to its own simplicity?

3. **The outsider.** Consider the famous case of the child about to fall into a well: someone with whom the agent has no role and from whom he has received nothing. Whichever account you think handles this best, say explicitly whether it needs a supplementary principle (a developmental or extension thesis, something like the family being the root from which wider concern grows), and whether adding that principle damages the account's distinctiveness.

4. **Novelty and corruption.** Roles that have no place in the classical five relationships — colleague in a large bureaucracy, fellow citizen in a modern state — and roles whose internal norms are themselves corrupt, as where a household's practices require a son to participate in graft. I want your judgment on which account has the resources to extend to new roles and which has the resources to condemn an existing one, and whether the appearance that one of them is structurally conservative is a real defect or merely a cost of taking roles seriously.

5. **Directionality.** Parental obligation to children and filial obligation to parents are strikingly asymmetric. One account explains this easily, the other must treat it as a brute difference in role content. Is that explanatory ease a point in the first account's favour, or does it come from a feature of that account that fails elsewhere?

Please argue structurally rather than textually: I do not need citations, passages reconstructed, or a survey of the secondary literature, and I would rather you not spend words rehearsing the familiar framing of role ethics as an alternative to virtue ethics or to principle-based ethics. What I want is the comparison itself and a verdict: which account you would defend, where exactly the other one fails, and what would have to be shown — by which of the five cases — for you to change your mind. Around 900 to 1,100 words, prose rather than headings, though you may name the five cases as you take them up.
