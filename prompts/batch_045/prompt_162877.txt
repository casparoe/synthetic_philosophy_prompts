Hey, I'm on the board of the Northvale Transit Authority and we've got one last chunk of capital money, about $14m, with two projects fighting over it. Project A is a frequency upgrade on three outer-suburb bus routes, cutting headways from 20 minutes to 10, serving mostly shift workers and people without cars, with small gains per rider but a lot of riders. Project B is a Riverside express link out to the airport, serving mostly higher-income commuters and air travellers, fewer riders but large time savings. Our staff economist ran a standard benefit-cost analysis using willingness-to-pay and B wins by a mile, and half the board is furious because, as one member put it, WTP just lets money vote twice: you value things more because you have more money. I dont want to overrule the economist on vibes, but "there's no way to compare how much a thing matters to different people, so let's use the metric that tracks ability to pay" doesn't feel like much of an answer either.

So a subgroup floated what they're calling the common-rung method: don't ask people what they'd pay, ask them to place the change on a scale whose rungs are fixed by concrete reference situations everyone is supposed to share — rung 0 is "I'd have to turn down a shift I want", rung 50 is "no real change to how I get around", rung 100 is "I could reliably get to a job interview on time" — then add up rung-gains across riders, with extra weight on people starting lower down. They piloted it and now A comes out ahead, though the margin is thin and the error bars overlap, and my brother-in-law, who does survey work, keeps warning me that answers to hypothetical scale questions drift with question order. The awkward bit: the pilot also found that Line 7 riders and Riverside riders seem to be reading the rungs differently, with airport commuters treating "reliably get to a job interview" as basically the rung they're already on while Line 7 riders treat it as a real stretch, so it isn't clear the rungs are doing the interpersonal work the method needs them to do.

Could you draft the memo I'd circulate to the board before the March 12 vote? Recommend one concrete course of action with a timeline — fund B on the staff numbers, fund A on the rung numbers, split the money, defer pending a better instrument, whatever you actually think is right — and don't duck the economist's strongest point, which I take to be that stated scales are cheap to game while revealed tradeoffs at least tie to behaviour. I'd also like an honest verdict on whether a board like ours should be picking a welfare metric at all, or whether the right answer is some decision rule that never requires comparing one person's gain against another's in the first place. Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion, and lead with the recommendation itself since I'll be reading this on my phone in the parking lot.
