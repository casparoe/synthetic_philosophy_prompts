I'm a high school student in a philosophy reading group, and I got into an argument with the teacher about skills. Her position is that whenever someone knows how to do something — ride a bike, solve a certain kind of problem, calm down a friend — what they really have is knowledge of a fact, held in a special practical way: roughly, they know, of some way of doing it, that it is a way for them to do it. I think knowing how is a different kind of thing altogether: an ability rather than a piece of information.

So I want a number. What is your credence that her view is right, understood as the claim that every genuine case of knowing how to do something is at bottom a case of knowing that something is the case? Please fix whatever you need to fix for that claim to have a truth value, and say what you would count as settling it, rather than answering that it depends on what knowledge means. Then decompose the number: how much rides on whether skill can come apart from any statable fact, as I think it does when a coach can still teach a movement she can no longer perform, or when an expert's account of what she is doing is wrong in detail; how much on whether the practical way of thinking of the fact is a real explanation or only a label for the problem; how much on whether an ability can be stored, lost through disuse, and damaged by being made explicit — I have noticed that being asked to say exactly what I am doing while I do it often makes me worse at it, and that seems like it should be evidence about what knowing how is; how much on whether something with no body, trained only on text, could know how to do anything; and whatever other terms you think belong. Give a number and one line of justification for each term, then the combination. Finally, tell me which single term you are least confident in and what I could go and actually observe, without a university library, that would move it most.
