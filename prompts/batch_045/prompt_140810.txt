I'm helping a small research group — philosophers and decision theorists, two years of funding, no empirical ML capacity — set its agenda on acausal trade: exchange or cooperation between agents whose actions are correlated without any causal influence running between them, whether through shared algorithm, shared structure, mutual prediction, or relations of logical implication. The question we ultimately care about is whether an agent, or the designer of one, should ever give acausal-trade considerations real weight in a decision procedure, and if so how much and under what conditions.

Which open questions in this area should we prioritize, and in what order? I want a defended agenda rather than a survey: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable in two years with conceptual tools alone, which are neglected relative to their leverage, and which you would drop on the grounds that their answer wouldn't change much downstream whatever it turned out to be.

Three problems I keep returning to, for whatever they are worth — please don't feel obliged to rank all three highly, and say so if you think the real crux is elsewhere:

1. Degraded correlation. The clean cases assume that one's own action and the counterparty's are perfectly correlated. Suppose instead the correlation is partial — because of implementation noise or private randomness, asymmetric uncertainty about which world each party occupies, or limits on how accurately either can simulate or prove facts about the other. The gains from trade then attenuate, and it looks as though there should be thresholds below which trade becomes worthless or actively harmful. Is there a principled account of how the value of an acausal trade varies with the degree of correlation, and does the answer depend on what the correlation is grounded in — shared code, shared deliberation procedure, or bare logical implication?

2. Counterparty measure. If one trades with types rather than tokens, how are counterparties of uncertain number to be weighted, and what happens when expected value is dominated by speculative, remote counterparties capable of offering enormous payoffs, so that the entire practice becomes hostage to one's prior over them?

3. Legibility as a design choice. Whether it is instrumentally rational to make oneself or one's successors more predictable, more transparently committed, or more cheaply simulable by unknown counterparties — and whether that recommendation, if it holds, is stable when other agents are making the same choice.

Say explicitly what would move a question up or down the list within two years, and what a negative result would look like: what would justify the group concluding that acausal trade deserves near-zero weight in practice.
