I work on moral responsibility and I have been going in circles on the Frankfurt-case literature for about three years. I want a diagnosis of the dispute, not a verdict in it.

The state of play as I see it. Frankfurt-style examples are supposed to show that an agent can be morally responsible even though she could not have done otherwise, and hence that the principle of alternative possibilities is false. Every move in the resulting exchange now has a standard answer. The flicker reply: the agent can still do something else — produce the prior sign, or fail to produce it — so some alternative survives. The dilemma objection: if the sign is something the agent controls, the flicker is real and the example misfires; if the sign is involuntary, the example builds in determinism and begs the question against the incompatibilist. The robustness requirement: a surviving alternative must be one that could bear the weight of an ascription of responsibility, and being able to blush differently is not one. Blockage cases with no intervener at all: the agent's own settled psychology guarantees the action, so no counterfactual device is needed. Then the split between guidance control and regulative control, with leeway held to be required only for the latter; and the source-theoretic fallback, which concedes the cases but denies responsibility on the ground that the will itself was formed by factors the agent did not control. Over all of this, survey work on what ordinary people say about such cases, claimed by both sides. As far as I can tell, none of it has moved anybody in fifty years: every consideration is on the table, everyone has heard all of them, and nobody converts.

The case I carry around as a probe. Call it the Deliberative Lockout. A device is fitted to a person of thoroughly settled character. It does not wait for a sign of wavering. It detects the onset of practical deliberation about whether to perform a certain action, and if any deliberation occurs it overrides her and produces the action by direct stimulation. She never deliberates: she acts at once, wholeheartedly, for reasons she already has. The device is causally inert in the actual sequence; every event in that sequence is exactly what it would have been had the device never been fitted. Still, she could not have done otherwise, and she could not even have begun to consider doing otherwise. There is no intervener in the actual history and no flicker, not even a flicker of deliberation. When I try it on people, verdicts track antecedent allegiance, but the reasons given no longer mention alternatives: one side says the action issued from her own reasons-responsive mechanism; the other says she was never given a chance to register and respond to a reason against it. Both remarks are counterfactual, but about different counterfactuals.

So here is my hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than confirmed. The debate is not about alternative possibilities at all. Both sides have quietly become modalists who disagree about which pattern of counterfactual sensitivity responsibility requires — and they disagree about that because they have different targets, attributability versus accountability, under a single word. The rival I cannot rule out is that the disagreement is about what sort of concept moral responsibility is: a folk concept whose content is fixed by ordinary practice and settleable only by empirical work on ordinary attributions, or a normative notion to be engineered, in which case no argument was ever going to move anyone and the apparent deadlock is a symptom, not a puzzle.

Which is it: verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, or a partly empirical question in disguise? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually argues rather than in a taxonomy of positions. Then tell me whether the Lockout case is a genuine probe or a disguised way of stipulating an answer, and, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, empirical evidence, abandonment of the question, or nothing at all.
