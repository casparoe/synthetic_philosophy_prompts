I'm in a longtermism reading group (we did the astronomical-waste paper and Ord's Precipice in the last couple of weeks) and I got into an argument with a friend who works on biosecurity policy about the recombinant-DNA moratorium and the Asilomar conference in February 1975. His line is that Asilomar is the clean historical precedent for a pause on a dangerous technology: a handful of scientists, mostly Paul Berg and Maxine Singer, got everyone to stop voluntarily after the 1974 NAS committee asked for a halt, ~140 people met at Asilomar, they came out with tiered containment guidelines matched to estimated risk, research resumed, nothing bad happened, everyone cites it approvingly. My worry is that this is a feel-good story that longtermists have absorbed without ever running it through the actual machinery of strong longtermism. So: do that. I don't need the history narrated back to me — I want the mapping.

Concretely. Set up the decision properly first: who the actors are (a small self-selected community of molecular biologists, plus the NAS and later the NIH — no state, no firms, no real enforcement), what the option set actually was (carry on; pause with containment tiers; restrict certain experiments; try to ban), and above all what the outcome variable is supposed to be on a strong-longtermist axiology — not lab infections in 1975 but effects on the shape and size of the far future. Then note the mismatch I keep getting stuck on: the risk they were actually worried about was a lab escape or an engineered E. coli colonizing someone's gut and maybe causing cancer, which is nowhere near an existential risk. Does that mismatch mean Asilomar is the wrong precedent entirely, or can you argue that what generalises is the decision procedure under deep uncertainty rather than the size of the risk? Bring in the unilateralist's curse and the compliance problem — how many labs were really bound, and what does it do to the analogy that this was a tight, norm-governed, reputationally exposed community rather than states and companies?

Two distinctions I invented while arguing and want used, or told why they're bad. First, delay versus deflection: a pause can just push a capability later in time, or it can change the order in which capabilities arrive relative to each other and to safety work — roughly, differential technological development vs pure waiting. Which one was Asilomar, and which one should a longtermist have wanted, given that recombinant DNA was also a big input to biotech speed? Relatedly, how much does astronomical waste bite here — is the delay in biotech itself supposed to be astronomically costly via growth and earlier space settlement, and how much of that rests on assumptions I should be told are load-bearing? Second, what I've been calling precautionary capital: the credibility, institutional muscle memory and public licence available to people in future centuries who may need to pause things repeatedly. Asilomar spent some of that on risks that mostly fizzled. On a longtermist view, does that make it a bad investment, or can you argue a well-run fizzled pause manufacture more capital than it burns — and how would we ever tell?

Finally, run it back the other way: does the episode support the hinge-of-history / time-of-perils framing, or does a case where the technology turned out mostly fine and the risk receded actually undercut it, or is it simply outside what the view was built to say? Please be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than squeezing an answer out of it — e.g. about how substitutable biotech speed is for other paths to the same long-run goods. One caution: some of the downstream stuff people cite (the NIH guidelines hardening into permanent oversight, later delays to agricultural biotech and things like Golden Rice) is contested, so flag how much of your conclusion depends on those empirical claims rather than leaning on them. Call it 800–1000 words, with a short mapping section at the top, then the case-through-theory direction, then the theory-tested-by-case direction.
