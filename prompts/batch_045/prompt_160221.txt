I'm taking an advanced undergraduate seminar on contemporary political philosophy, and my term paper this term is on whether the original position can actually deliver the difference principle. I keep running into one analogy, used in opposite directions by two people in the seminar, and I'd like a proper audit of it rather than a verdict by slogan.

The analogy: choosing principles of justice behind the veil of ignorance is like buying insurance. You don't know whether you'll end up in the worst-off position, so you choose principles that guarantee you a decent floor, and that is supposed to be why the difference principle falls out. One person uses it to make the difference principle look like common sense: everyone buys fire insurance, so of course a rational chooser behind the veil would insure against landing at the bottom. Another uses it to attack the same principle: insurance-buying is a risk-averse response to known probabilities, and behind the veil there are no probabilities at all, so Rawlsians are smuggling in an arbitrarily extreme risk aversion; if instead you read the veil as an equal chance of occupying each position, you should maximize average utility, which is roughly the Harsanyi-style objection.

What I'd like, then, is the mapping made explicit and then stress-tested. Please give me the correspondence table: in the insurance case, what plays the role of the insured, the premium, the policy, the insurer, the pool, the actuarial probabilities, the deductible or coinsurance rate, and the moral hazard? And in the original position, what are the targets of each?

Then the places it breaks. Here are the ones I've been turning over, but please add the ones I've missed and, more importantly, tell me which of them actually matter for the work the analogy is being asked to do:

- Ordinary insurance works by pooling many independent risks, so the law of large numbers does the heavy lifting. Behind the veil there is one draw of the whole basic structure, and everyone's fate within it is perfectly correlated, so there's no diversification gain to be had: the policy is just a redistributive transfer.
- Insurance presupposes a baseline endowment that you are protecting (the house) and an outside option (go uninsured). Behind the veil there is no pre-social entitlement and no exit from society.
- Real policies are almost never full coverage: they carry deductibles and coinsurance, and premiums are loaded above the actuarially fair rate because of moral hazard and administrative cost. If deductibles map onto the inequalities the difference principle permits, and premium loading maps onto the incentive effects that shrink the social product, the analogy starts to look like it supports the difference principle's actual structure — a floor plus incentive-compatible inequality — rather than either strict equality or bare maximin. But I worry this is too convenient, since in the social case the size of the loading is itself downstream of the principles being chosen.
- The information asymmetry runs backwards. Normally the buyer knows her own risk type and the insurer doesn't; behind the veil the chooser is the one who knows nothing, so she occupies the insurer's epistemic position while making the buyer's decision.
- Insurance is bought by a self-interested chooser for her own case, whereas the veil is often read as a device for impartially choosing principles for everyone. Our seminar distinguishes a thin reading (the veil as a heuristic for impartiality) from a thick reading (the veil as a decision problem under ignorance with a specified decision rule), and I suspect the analogy quietly converts one into the other.

So: where does the mapping genuinely hold, where does it break, which features of ordinary insurance does it import that the original position lacks, and does either conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table — the friendly one (the difference principle is just insurance-buying) or the hostile one (maximin behind the veil is unmotivated risk aversion)? If the insurance analogy can't carry either, is there a better comparison available — something closer to a deductible-and-coinsurance contract, a mutual aid pool with no external reinsurer, an annuity, or a lottery — that captures what the original position is actually doing?

Please answer in American English, and please don't spend time on citations or a reading list; I have the syllabus and can track down the texts myself.
