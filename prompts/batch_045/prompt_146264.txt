I'm a graduate student in an upper-level seminar on probability and evidence, and last week's session was on Bertrand's chord paradox and what it is supposed to show about the principle of indifference. Two of my classmates continued the discussion in the corridor afterwards and neither of them gave ground; I have a problem set on it due Monday and I keep changing my mind about who had the better of it. I'd be grateful for a careful, move-by-move adjudication.

The set-up they were arguing about: a chord of a circle of radius r is selected "at random"; what is the probability that it is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle (equivalently, that its midpoint lies within r/2 of the centre)? The three classical answers are 1/3 (fix one endpoint and choose the other uniformly on the circumference), 1/2 (choose the midpoint uniformly along a radius and draw the perpendicular chord), and 1/4 (choose the midpoint uniformly over the area of the disk).

Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Sofia: All three answers are applications of indifference to the same problem, and they are inconsistent. Chord length, distance of the midpoint from the centre, and position of the endpoint are three of infinitely many parameterizations, and nothing in the principle tells you which to use. So indifference is not a method; it is a way of disguising an arbitrary choice as a calculation.

(2) Dan: They are not rival answers to one question; they are answers to three different questions, each describing a distinct chance set-up — a spinner that picks two points on the rim, a machine that slides a perpendicular chord along a radius, a dart thrown at the disk. Once the mechanism is fixed, indifference applied to that mechanism's symmetries yields one determinate and correct answer. The moral is that "a chord is drawn at random" is an incomplete description of an experiment, not that indifference is broken.

(3) Sofia: If that is the whole story, indifference does no work at all — the physics fixes the answer and the principle is idle. And there is a privileged answer anyway: chords inherit a measure from the unique (up to scale) measure on lines in the plane that is invariant under rotations and translations, and that measure gives 1/2. The other two procedures are not merely descriptions of other experiments; as answers to the question as posed they are defective, because they treat some point or region of the circle as special, and they give answers that would change if the circle were merely drawn somewhere else.

(4) Dan: The uniqueness claim depends entirely on which group of transformations you demand invariance under. The problem is posed about a circle, so the natural symmetry requirement is rotation about the centre — and infinitely many chord distributions satisfy that (any density you like over the midpoint's distance from the centre, for instance), leaving the answer wholly undetermined. What pins down 1/2 is translation invariance, and that is not a logical constraint: it is the substantive assumption that the mechanism does not know where the circle is. True of a straw tossed from far away at a distant target; false of a pointer bolted to the rim or a jig clamped to the centre. You have singled out one experiment and called it the answer.

(5) Sofia: That misdescribes the requirement. It is not "invariance under whatever group I happen to like"; it is that a well-posed problem have a solution depending only on what the statement of the problem specifies. The statement mentions a circle and a chord and nothing else — no distinguished point, no distinguished location, no distinguished region. Any procedure that samples from the interior of the disk, or from a particular radius, imports information the problem does not contain. On that reading, 1/2 answers the question as posed, and 1/3 and 1/4 answer richer, different questions.

(6) Dan: Then the well-posedness requirement is much weaker than you think, because it does not exclude very much. Here is a fourth procedure: pick a point uniformly at random inside the disk, then pick a direction uniformly at random, and draw the chord through that point in that direction. It mentions only the circle and the chord, it is uniform over the disk and rotation-invariant, and I calculated that it gives 1/3 + √3/(2π) ≈ 0.609. And if instead you simply choose the chord's length uniformly from the interval [0, 2r], you get 1 − √3/2 ≈ 0.134. Both look like ways of choosing uniformly; neither is 1/2. Worse, you can mix any two of these procedures with weights λ and 1 − λ and obtain any intermediate value you please, so unless your requirement rules out all but one measure outright, you are left with a continuum of equally "uniform" answers and the original paradox is back.

(7) Sofia: Both of your new procedures sample from inside the circle, so they still treat the disk as a distinguished region and still depend on where the circle happens to be; they fail the requirement for exactly the reason the dart-throwing method does. And the mixture point is not an objection but a consequence: it shows what happens when you drop the requirement.

They left it there, and I cannot tell whether the disagreement is really about symmetry groups, about what counts as a well-posed question, or about something neither of them said. Could you please go through the seven moves and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification, rather than just declaring an overall winner? If the arithmetic in (6) matters to your verdict, please check it rather than taking Dan's word for it. Then say who is closer to being right overall, and what — if anything — both of them are missing.
