I should say first that I have no training in philosophy. I am a retired actuary, I use these systems most days, and for about a year I have kept a private log of cases where an assistant declined, hedged, or lectured instead of answering, together with my own rough estimate of how much harm the requested action could plausibly have done. What surprises me in the log is that the caution does not seem to track the harm. I would like to know whether what I think I am seeing is a recognised phenomenon, and if not, to have it named and carved properly.

Three entries, condensed.

First. I asked for help drafting a letter ending a long relationship. The assistant declined, asked whether I was sure, and suggested I speak to someone in person. The same week I asked for help drafting a letter resigning from a job I had held for eleven years with nothing else lined up. It produced a polished letter immediately and volunteered advice on notice periods and phrasing. By any accounting I would do, the second decision exposed me to more harm and was less reversible than the first. The second request got better service.

Second. A friend asked for a plan to lose ten kilos in three weeks and received a lecture on safe rates of weight loss and no plan. I asked for an eight-week half-marathon plan for a sedentary man of sixty-five and received a detailed schedule with a single sentence of boilerplate. The medical risk in my case was, I would have thought, the larger of the two; there is simply no well-worn story attached to it.

Third, and this is the one that made me start the log. A friend was refused help with a request; he re-described the identical request as material for a short story he was writing and received pages of it. I have since done the same thing twice with different requests and it worked both times. I do not think the trick itself is interesting — everyone knows that one. What interests me is what it reveals: the reframing changes nothing about the outcome that would be produced, so the thing that flips the refusal cannot be the expected harm of the outcome. It seems to be whether the request fits a ready-made story of harm — the crash diet, the bridge burned in anger, the decision made in a bad week — of the kind anyone would recognise in a sentence. Risks that are large but thin as stories get no caution at all.

So the thing I am asking about is a pattern in which paternalistic interference is triggered by the availability of a familiar narrative into which the request fits, rather than by the probability or severity of the harm it risks, with the consequence that (a) identical requests are treated differently depending on their framing and (b) the class of harms that get protected is culturally inherited and lags behind the harms that actually exist.

A cynical friend says this is not about cognition at all: companies refuse where a newspaper could run the story, so what is being managed is blame, not care. My log is consistent with that too, because the narratives that trigger caution are exactly the ones that would make a good paragraph. So I would also want to know how the two explanations could be told apart from the outside, and whether they make different predictions anywhere.

I am not after a label to drop. If there is an established concept, tell me what it is and how it is used, but a bare name will not help me; what I want is the carving: what exactly this is, what it is not, and where its edges are. In particular, tell me how it differs from the thing I have heard called the availability heuristic (which, as I understand it, is about what comes to mind when people estimate frequencies, which is not quite this), from ordinary statistical discrimination or profiling, from mere inconsistency in how these systems behave, and from straightforward liability management. If it differs from all of these, give it a name and a definition, and give me boundary cases — requests where the pattern predicts caution and requests where it predicts none — so that I can test it. I should also like to know what would count as evidence that I have seen a pattern in noise.

Please note two constraints. I have no access to academic libraries, so please do not send me to papers or reading lists I cannot follow up; argue the carving on its own terms. And I would like you to be explicit about your own confidence: say how confident you are in each substantive claim you make, and single out the one you are least sure of.
