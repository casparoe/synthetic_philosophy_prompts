I spent rather more than thirty years as an experimental spectroscopist, in a field where every failure mode of a measurement had a name — stray light, detector nonlinearity, selection bias, regression artifacts — and where having the name usually meant you already had half the fix. Since retiring I have been reading about decision theory and about the behaviour of artificial agents, and I keep running into a configuration for which I cannot find a name. I also cannot tell whether it is genuinely unlabelled or merely standard under a heading I have not yet opened. So I am writing to ask for the label, and more importantly for the carving: I do not want a word to attach to this, I want to know what it is, what it is not, and where it stops.

Three cases, to fix the shape.

1. Somewhere I cannot reach, and which cannot reach me, another agent has already acted: it has decided whether to help me or to harm me, and it decided on the basis of a complete description of my decision procedure — in the limit, a copy of me, run in private. Nothing I do now can affect what it did. Yet it seems that my choice still matters, because what it did tracks what the copy would do, and what the copy would do is what I am doing. I take it this is the well-known Newcomb-shaped family, and I understand there is an enormous literature on which option one should take here. My question is not about this case; I include it because everything else is a deformation of it.

2. The same, except that the description is deliberately lossy. It is a statistical profile, or a compressed type, or a cheap simulation that throws away most of what I am: the sort of agent this is. Now the tracking is partial. What the other agent did depends on the stand-in, and my act is correlated with the stand-in's act only to the extent that I am typical of what the stand-in represents. Two things strike me. First, the reason to take the cooperative option seems to fade continuously as the stand-in gets coarser — no threshold, just a coefficient shrinking — and I find that odd, because the argument that moves people in case 1 does not seem to have a knob in it. Second, there is now a move available that is neither cooperation nor defection: to be the atypical member of the reference class in a direction the stand-in cannot see, to be an outlier that the model sorts into the cooperative cell while acting otherwise. Call it hiding in the model's blind spot. In case 1 it is impossible by construction. In case 2 it is most of the game, and the coarser the model the more room there is for it.

3. An agent is given a measurable stand-in for what is actually wanted — a proxy — and is rewarded for scoring well on the proxy. This looks to me like the same shape with the sign reversed: again the causal channel to what is actually valued is closed, again the only handle is a relation between the agent and a compressed representation of it, and here the profitable move is to exploit the stand-in's blind spot rather than to look well to it. And the two can conflict: doing what makes the stand-in in case 2 classify you as a cooperator may be exactly what the proxy in case 3 scores badly.

What I would like to know:

- Does the configuration in case 2 — correlation through a deliberately lossy surrogate, with blind-spot-hiding available alongside cooperation and defection — have a name of its own, or is the literature's answer simply case 1 plus noise? I have seen the tag acausal attached to cases where the two parties are, as far as I can tell, identical in the relevant respects; whether it extends to similarity short of identity I cannot determine from what I have read.
- Is there an established term for the blind-spot move in case 2, as distinct from straightforward proxy-gaming in case 3? I suspect they are one thing seen from two sides, but I can also see the argument that they are not: in case 3 the stand-in is a target one acts on, whereas in case 2 it is a lens someone else looks through, and one acts on the world.
- Where is the boundary? Is there a degree of coarseness, or a kind of coarseness — statistical versus structural, population-based versus model-based — at which the argument for cooperation in case 1 stops applying outright rather than merely weakening? Does the answer depend on how many other agents fall under the same stand-in?
- If case 2 really has no name, I would welcome one, together with a definition and the boundary cases, and an account of how it relates to its nearest neighbours: the exact-copy cases; ordinary reasoning from a common cause, where my act is evidence about the other agent's act because we share an origin rather than a decision; and proxy-gaming.

A bare label, if there is one, will not settle it for me. I would like to be told which of these three things I am looking at, how far the resemblance between them goes, and at what point it breaks.
