I am writing a chapter for a collection on freedom in modern political thought, and I want to use Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go as a case rather than as decoration. Please read the novel through the trichotomy of negative liberty (freedom as the absence of interference by others), positive liberty (freedom as self-direction or self-realization, being governed by one's own reasons and projects rather than by alien forces), and republican liberty (freedom as non-domination: not standing under the arbitrary power of another, where arbitrary means a power that need not track your interests and that you have no standing to contest). Take each view at its strongest, not as a slogan.

The puzzle I want handled is this. On a negative-liberty reading the students at Hailsham are, for long stretches, strikingly free: little coercion, a sheltered childhood, room to live more or less as they like, and an interference that arrives only later and briefly, at the donations. On a republican reading they are never free at all: from birth they stand under a power that fixes their fate, that they cannot contest, and that no amount of kindness diminishes. On a positive-liberty reading something more disturbing is true, since their deepest projects — the art, the search for a possible, the hope of deferral — are not self-authored in the required sense, and one of them is harvested by the regime and displayed as evidence that they have inner lives at all.

I want four questions addressed in particular.

1. Is non-domination a temporally extended property? Can someone be unfree now in virtue of what will be done to them later, at a time when nothing is being done to them and when they do not yet know? Say how each of the three views handles this, and whether the novel's structure — a narration written from inside a fate that is already sealed — is itself an argument about it.

2. Domination without an acknowledging dominator. Republican theory standardly models domination as a relation to an agent who holds the power and can be called to account. Here the power is diffuse: a public that wants the donations and prefers not to look at them, institutions that close, benefactors who would rather not know. Is there such a thing as domination by an unacknowledging collective, and can a theory built on the master-and-slave case describe it?

3. Can the tracking condition be satisfied from the inside? The guardians do, in a sense, attend to the students' interests; Hailsham exists to make them better off. But their docility, their failure to protest, and even the content of their hopes are themselves products of the institution. If a power shapes the interests it then tracks, and is praised for tracking them, does that count as non-arbitrary? This seems to me a problem for republicanism that the literature does not discuss.

4. Where the reading fails. Which scenes resist all three concepts, and what does the novel know about freedom that none of them says — about the value of being allowed to finish something, about being given time, or about what Ruth is actually asking for when she wants the deferral to be real?

Write it as criticism, roughly a thousand words, with a section on each question and a closing paragraph on what, if anything, the novel adds to the debate that argument alone does not. Do not reduce the book to an illustration and do not stretch the concepts to fit it; where the fit is bad, say so and explain why.
