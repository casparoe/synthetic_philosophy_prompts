been reading about peer disagreement on and off for years (mostly via the usual blogs, I never actually took a grad epstemology class) and I keep feeling like both camps are quietly cheating, so I want to see the fight staged properly. Write me a dialogue between two people, both sharp and both well-read, one conciliationist, one steadfast, stuck on a long drive home after a meetup. Neither is a strawman: the conciliationist is not a pushover who drifts to 50% on everything, and the steadfast one is not just an egotist who defines peers as people who already agree with him. Real back and forth, each responding to what the other actually said, and by the end each of them should have conceded one specific thing (not the whole debate, just one point). No "you're both right in your own way" wrap-up, and no citations or name-checking of papers — I can't look any of that up anyway.

The case I want them gnawing on — I call it the broken-record case, if it has a proper name in the literature I don't know it: two forecasters who've done hundreds of calibration questions side by side and whose overall scores are basically indistinguishable. But on one class of question, call it type X — say, "will this kind of startup still be alive in three years" — they have disagreed, always in the same direction, about forty times across eight years, and on those forty questions they've also been about equally accurate. Today they disagree about a fresh instance of X.

The stuff I actually want them to fight over, not glide past:

- Is forty same-direction disagreements forty pieces of higher-order evidence, or basically one piece wearing a trenchcoat? What pattern in the track record would even settle which it is — what would each of them need to see in the data to switch readings?
- Does the bare fact of disagreement screen off the object-level evidence, or does it just get added to the pile alongside it? Make the steadfast one say what he'd do upon learning he'd been wrong on 38 of the 40.
- Whether "peer" is doing hidden work: if you only conciliate toward people whose disagreement you take seriously, and you decide whose disagreement to take seriously using the very same reasoning that produced the disputed belief, isn't the whole thing circular? And does the steadfast view have its own version of that problem?
- Self-application: they also disagree about conciliationism itself, and both of them know that. Whatever rule each proposes, run it on that disagreement and see what it does to their confidence in the rule.

Toy Bayes and actual numbers are welcome — I'd rather have one small worked example than a tour of the dialectic. And keep it sounding like two people talking, not two conference abstracts.
