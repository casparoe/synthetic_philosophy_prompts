Next Thursday I'm presenting to the county's Algorithmic Accountability Working Group and I'm mildly terrified, so what I need is a proper Q&A prep sheet, not a polished defense of my view.

Context on how I got here: I do outreach for a food bank, and I got drafted onto this committee after I complained — loudly, during public comment, not my finest hour — about how the new benefits-triage assistant handled my dad's recertification. It closed his case as "abandoned" because he didn't upload a document within ten days. My dad is 74 and does not do document uploads. So this isn't abstract for me, which is both why I care and why I'm probably the wrong person to be calm about it.

The room. Ninety minutes, fifteen for me and the rest for them:

- Anita, a political philosopher at the state university, works on liberal neutrality and public reason. Her instinct is that "whose values should the AI serve" is a malformed question — you don't add up conceptions of the good, you ask what terms everyone could reasonably accept, and anything else is the county taking sides.
- Priya and Sam, the two ML people from the vendor who actually built the triage model. Not hostile; they think in reward models, eval suites, distribution shift. They will be polite and then quietly tell me my proposal isn't implementable.
- Marcus, deputy county counsel. Administrative law, equal treatment, the record, what happens when someone appeals.
- Denise, runs legal aid, sees claimants every day, deeply suspicious of anything that sounds like efficiency.
- Ray, a county supervisor who wants no headlines and will ask the question that is really about money.

My thesis in one line: stop trying to aggregate values into a single utility function — aggregate *authority* instead. Three moving parts:

1. Each stakeholder group gets a small "patch": a narrow, explicitly listed class of decisions where their ranking is final and never traded off against anybody else's. (Ours: claimants get final say over the assistant's tone and over whether it nags them; caseworkers get final say over what gets flagged for a home visit; the county gets final say over anything touching fraud exposure.)
2. Outside every patch, the tiebreak is a rotating lottery: one stakeholder group's ordering governs that session, drawn at random, logged, and shown to whoever was affected.
3. The whole patch map sunsets every eighteen months and has to be renegotiated from scratch, on the theory that who gets which patch is the thing that will actually be fought over and shouldn't be locked in.

Two anchors I'll use: whether the assistant may say "you look like you might qualify for emergency benefits" when an application is incomplete, and the voice question — warm and chatty versus flat and transactional. Neither is a factual disagreement, and neither feels like it should be settled by a coin flip, which is sort of the point and also sort of the problem.

What I want from you: the six or eight objections I'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll come, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — Anita's version should sound like Anita's, Priya's like an engineer's, Marcus's like a lawyer's — ordered by how much damage they'd do. For each: a roughly two-minute reply I can give out loud without being technical, what I should concede up front rather than be dragged into conceding, and where my reply is soft.

Also two things separately. First: the weakest joint in my own position that I should raise myself, before someone else does, rather than hope they miss. (I think it's that the lottery decides a lot more than I'm admitting, and "randomness is fair under pluralism" is doing way more work than the patches are.) Second: the question I'm hoping nobody asks. I have a feeling it's from Denise, and that it's about whether I'm on this committee under false pretenses — whether I'd still be proposing a rotating lottery if the draw handed my family's patch to somebody who thinks the county owes people nothing.
