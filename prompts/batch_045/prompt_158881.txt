I run a small reading group — mostly working programmers, one cognitive scientist, several longtime readers of the same blogs I read — and we are doing a single session on whether dreams are experiences, starting from Malcolm's and Dennett's classic arguments. Rather than a reading list, I want to hand out a decision tree that takes someone from an initial hunch to a named position on whether, during sleep, there is something it is like for the sleeper.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of views: first the questions and the answer combinations they permit, then the actual positions located in that space. The branch nodes should be concrete cases a reader can answer without knowing where the answer leads — for instance: a lucid dreamer who executes a prearranged pattern of eye movements during REM and later reports having formed the intention; a sleeper who, woken from REM, says they were definitely dreaming but can bring back no content at all; an odor or a name presented in the room during sleep that turns up in the report; a report taken at 3 a.m. versus the same person's account of that dream the next morning; someone who denies ever dreaming yet produces a report when awakened during REM.

Leaves must include, at minimum: dreams as hallucinatory perceptual experience later remembered fallibly; dreaming as imagination rather than perception; a Dennett-style view on which something goes on during sleep but there is no determinate fact about the order or duration of the content; a Malcolm-style view on which a report of the form I dreamt that p does not describe anything that went on in sleep; and a view on which the question never gets going because phenomenal consciousness is not the sort of thing there is a fact about. Six to eight branch questions, six to eight leaves, each leaf carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment — especially what the view must say about dream science, which treats reports as data about sleep states. Flag pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, and for each coherent combination of answers that nobody actually occupies, say briefly whether it is empty because it is unstable, unattractive, or merely unclaimed. Assume an audience that mistrusts introspection and would rather dissolve a question than answer it, so no branch should simply ask whether one trusts one's own introspection.
