Two settled features of American federalism sit side by side and, as far as I can tell, pull in opposite directions. I would like to know what theory of federalism could make them parts of a single doctrine rather than two unrelated rules, and what that theory commits us to.

The first is that Congress may not simply order a state government to work for it. It cannot require a state legislature to enact and enforce a federal regulatory program, and it cannot conscript state executive officers — police, sheriffs, licensing officials — into carrying out federal duties such as conducting background checks on gun purchasers. There is a narrow and seldom-discussed exception for state courts, which can be made to hear federal claims. What Congress may do instead is regulate private individuals directly, or displace state law in the field altogether. And here is the part that puzzles me: outright preemption, which cancels state law and replaces it with federal law, is permitted, while requiring the state's own machinery to carry out the same federal policy is forbidden. Preemption looks like the more drastic intrusion; it is the permitted one.

The second is that Congress routinely obtains the very state action it is forbidden to command, by paying for it. Conditions attached to federal highway funds — a minimum drinking age, prescribed rules for licensing drivers — are standard practice and have been upheld even where Congress could not have regulated the subject directly. The condition is formally an offer the state may decline. The limits are loose: the condition must be related to the purpose of the spending, must be stated clearly enough that a state knows what it is accepting, and must not be so large that inducement turns into compulsion. That last line was held to be crossed when a federal program threatened to withdraw all of a state's existing Medicaid funding unless the state expanded the program.

In Germany the ordinary arrangement is close to the reverse of the American one. The federation legislates, and the Länder execute federal law as a matter of their own concern, with the Länder holding a consent veto, through the Bundesrat, over federal legislation that touches their administrative interests. What American doctrine forbids as conscripting the states, German federalism installs as the default — and pairs it with a formal state voice in making the federal law the states will have to administer.

I also want the reconstructed theory tested on two cases for which I do not think the current doctrine has a settled answer. First: Congress offers a state a large block grant on condition that the state legislature place a specified federal model bill on its agenda, hold a recorded floor vote on it within sixty days, and transmit the result, the roll call, and a written statement of reasons to a federal agency. Enactment is not required. Is this a permissible condition on spending, or a forbidden conscription of the state legislature? Second: a federal regulator, acting under a valid statute, requires state agencies to compile and transmit to it specified data about their own licensing decisions, and requires state officials to notify it within a fixed period whenever they take a specified action, even though Congress could not compel the licensing decisions themselves. Is the reporting duty different in kind, and if so, why?

So, concretely, what I want is this. Reconstruct the implicit theory in its strongest form: the most natural candidate is that the doctrine protects states as governments with a claim to clear political accountability, on the ground that voters must be able to see whom to blame, and that conscription blurs attribution by making federal policy wear the state's face while preemption keeps the lines of responsibility straight. Say which features of the practice that theory explains and which it leaves untouched. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and mark the places where several rival theories would license exactly the same outcomes, so that the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then test the two halves against each other: if the accountability story is right, does it not also condemn conditional spending, where a state legislature enacts a drinking age it would never otherwise have enacted and its voters can only blame the legislature? Does the requirement that a spending condition be related to the federal program sit comfortably with a consent-based view at all, or does it betray a different view — that federalism marks out reserved spheres, so that even a willing state cannot sell access to a domain that is not the federal government's to buy into? Say whether the German arrangement shows the accountability theory to be one option among several, redeemable by giving states a formal voice in federal lawmaking, and whether adding such a voice would make the American pair cohere.

Finally, suppose we replaced the implicit theory with an explicit subsidiarity test of the sort the European Union writes into its treaties: act at the higher level only so far as the objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved lower down and can be better achieved centrally, with the principle policed largely through national parliaments rather than through courts. What would have to change in American doctrine? Would courts have to make comparative empirical findings about which level handles a given problem better, and would preemption, conscription, and spending conditions then collapse into a single inquiry? If so, is that a reason to think the rules we actually have were never really about efficacy at all?
