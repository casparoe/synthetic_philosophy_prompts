I am writing the opening chapter of a thesis on counterfactual history and the origins of the Final Solution, and before I build a three-year argument on top of it I would like my own position audited. Assume I know the standard literature and do not want a survey of it; what I want is a consistency check. Here is where I have landed, stated as flatly as I can.

(1) I reject any version of the German special-path thesis. I do not think the Nazi seizure of power was written into the structure of German society or the German state, and I think the collapse of Weimar was highly contingent: the timing of the Depression, the failure of the grand coalition, the presidential system, the behaviour of the conservative elites around Hindenburg, and a run of miscalculations that no one had reason to expect. Different accidents in 1930–32 and you get some other authoritarian or conservative-authoritarian outcome, not the Third Reich.

(2) Consistently with that, I think the seizure of power itself is best explained structurally rather than through Hitler's personal qualities. The electoral breakthrough was produced by the disintegration of the bourgeois party system and the panic of the Protestant middle-class milieu; the appointment was made by elites who thought they could use him. He was the beneficiary of a crisis he did not author.

(3) I nonetheless think that without Hitler there would have been no Nazi Germany, but for a deflationary reason: the movement's only genuine political asset was his plebiscitary appeal to the electorate, and it was precisely that mass following which made him worth bargaining for. Take him away and the party has no asset that the elites need to borrow, and the crisis resolves in some other authoritarian direction.

(4) On the origins of the Final Solution I am a structuralist, or functionalist. I hold that there was no single decision and no master plan; that the policy emerged through cumulative radicalisation in a polycratic regime of competing agencies; that the critical junctures — the invasion of the Soviet Union, the failure of deportation and reservation schemes, the improvisations of the occupation administrations in the East — generated the escalation locally and then fed it upwards; and that the mechanism by which radicalisation propagated was broadly the one Ian Kershaw captured with the idea of working towards the Führer, in which initiatives came from below in the expectation of approval rather than from orders given above.

(5) I also hold that the Holocaust is historically singular. Unlike other genocides it was not instrumental: it aimed at the total destruction of a people, it had no end that could be negotiated, and it continued to be resourced when doing so was plainly damaging to the war effort.

(6) And I hold that this singularity is explained by the content of Nazi ideology — a redemptive antisemitism in which the Jew figures as a metaphysical threat to the national body — and not by generic features of modern bureaucracy, nor by local rational-choice explanations of individual killing decisions in terms of food supply, housing or labour allocation. Those accounts, I think, get the texture of the events right and the explanation wrong.

(7) Finally, a case verdict. Suppose Hitler had died in the summer of 1940 and been succeeded by another senior figure from the party leadership, with the war in the East still to come. My instinct is that you would still have gotten a war of conquest and a catastrophic anti-Jewish policy — expulsion, ghettoisation, forced labour, mass death by starvation and shooting on a colossal scale — but almost certainly not the industrialised extermination camps: no Auschwitz as we know it.

What I want from you is an audit. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are, order the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each one say precisely what would have to be given up and what that would cost me. Two specific things I suspect but cannot localise: that my structuralism in (4) may be person-insensitive in a way that makes my verdict in (7) unavailable to me, and that the singularity claim in (5) and (6) may quietly reintroduce exactly the kind of top-down explanation that (4) was meant to rule out. But do not take my word for where the problems are — if the real fault line is elsewhere, say so. Finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most, and tell me honestly what I would lose by making it.
