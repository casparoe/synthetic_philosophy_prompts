I'm on the internal safety review group at a lab that ships coding agents. Every week we get one or two proposals for a safeguard — a monitor, a sandbox, a tripwire, a human-approval gate — and we have maybe two days each, the person signing off is the safety lead who is smart but not a specialist in that particular control, and the recommendation goes to a deployment committee that won't read any theory. If we get it wrong one way we block a release for nothing, the other way a bad agent goes out to real users. The thing we keep relitigating badly is: does this safeguard actually need a worst-case guarantee, or is average-case evidence over realistic deployments enough?

Please turn that into a decision procedure we can run in a couple of hours: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it has to hand off to a person because it's a values call about how much tail risk we eat.

Two wrinkles specific to our setting that it has to handle. First, much of our validation data is partly generated by the models we're evaluating — synthetic transcripts, agent rollouts, model-written test suites — so the "deployment distribution" we'd be averaging over isn't independent of the thing being tested. Second, we've started using an intermediate category we call conditional worst-case: worst-case over a set of scenarios we enumerate, average-case over which scenario we're in. Half our arguments are about whether a proposal is honestly that or just average-case with a scenario list bolted on, so give us an actual test for the difference.

Then run the procedure on three real-shaped cases: an LLM monitor for data exfiltration with a 0.1% false-negative rate on 10k red-team transcripts; a syscall-allowlist sandbox with no network access, presented to us as a hard worst-case control; and a tripwire that only works if the model doesn't notice its in an evaluation. Include at least one where the procedure comes back no and we're going to find that annoying, and say plainly what the procedure can't tell us.
