hey — I'm in this little reading group on AI governance (mostly engineers, one philosopher who keeps rolling his eyes) and we keep circling back to the same move. Someone will say: look, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem is basically just the liar paradox in a costume — you build a sentence that says of itself that it can't be proved, and lo and behold the system can neither prove it nor refute it. Then they run with it to get three conclusions in a row:

1. any formal specification of an AI's goals or constraints will have cases it can't settle, so no amount of rule-writing closes the gap, and rule-writing was always a fool's errand;
2. no system can fully audit or verify itself, so there has to be a human, or something outside the system, in the loop — permanently, not just for now;
3. and this is the one that sets off my philosopher friend — human judgement is different in kind, because when we're shown the Gödel sentence for a system we can just see that it's true, so our grip on truth outruns any machine's grip on proof.

What I want is an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on Gödel.

First: what actually maps onto what. Give me the correspondences explicitly — what plays the liar sentence, what plays truth, what plays provability, what plays the system, and where the arithmetization step (coding syntax into numbers) sits in the mapping. I want to see whether the mapping is even doing what people think it's doing, or whether it's mostly the word self-reference doing the work.

Second: where it breaks. My sense is that at least three things are off, but I can't tell which of them matter for the use we're putting it to and which are just tidiness:
- the liar sentence has no stable truth value and blows up if you take it on board, whereas the Gödel sentence is (given consistency) perfectly true and merely undecided — so the analogy imports a whiff of paradox that isn't there;
- the analogy makes it look like the problem is semantically self-referential sentences, when the construction is syntactic and depends on a coding scheme you could have chosen differently — so there isn't one canonical gap, there's a family of them, and none of them is about anything but numbers;
- it leaves out the side condition entirely: an inconsistent system proves everything, so the theorem only bites under an assumption of consistency, which is itself the thing the system can't establish.

Third, the part I actually care about: does the chain of conclusions survive, one at a time? My worry about (2) is that being unable to prove one's own consistency isn't the same as being unable to audit oneself in any operational sense — a system can check lots of things about itself and just not certify global consistency. My worry about (3) is that seeing the Gödel sentence as true seems to require already knowing the system is consistent, which is exactly the move we're congratulating ourselves on being able to make from outside — and which we can't obviously make about ourselves. And for (1): is this gap even the same kind of gap as the open texture of a norm like don't manipulate your users? Someone in the group pointed out that there are perfectly complete, decidable theories — arithmetic without multiplication, the first-order theory of the reals — which suggests the theorem is about crossing a threshold of expressive strength, not about rule systems having holes as such. Is that right, and does it sink (1) as stated, or does (1) survive for different reasons and the Gödel story is just decoration?

Last thing: my housemate says the liar-paradox framing is the wrong picture anyway, and offers the blind spot in your eye instead — there's literally a hole in the visual field where the optic nerve exits, but you never notice it, because something fills it in. Is that better or worse? What does it import that the liar picture doesn't, and does it import anything false — e.g. does it suggest the system papers over the gap, or that the gap is harmless, or that it's located somewhere in particular rather than showing up all over the place? And if both pictures are bad, give me a better one, or tell me plainly that no analogy is doing real work here and the argument has to be made directly.

Please skip the usual throat-clearing about how Gödel is widely misunderstood by popular writers — I've heard that bit a hundred times and it's not what I'm asking. Assume I know the rough shape of the theorem and go straight at the mapping and the load-bearing bits.
