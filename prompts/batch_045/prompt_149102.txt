I TA for a survey course on 19th century philosophy and this week we did Kierkegaard on the press and "the public" — the complaint that the press and anonymity and chatter grind everyone down into leveling, that everybody has an opinion about everything and nobody is anybody, that a real public (a jury, a parish, a union local) can act while the phantom public can only talk, and that you have to become a single individual before anything real can happen to you. Half my study group thinks it's just 1840s grumpiness with a noble savage bolted onto it, the other half thinks it predicted social media better than anything else in the canon. I'm in the middle and I'd rather make the case by making something than by arguing it.

So write me a product-launch announcement — the upbeat, first-person-plural, "we've been listening to your feedback" blog post a tech company puts out — in which a social platform (invent a name, don't use a real one) announces that it is sunsetting individual accounts and migrating all its users into one shared account called THE PUBLIC. Around 700 words, formatted as a changelog with numbered sections and short fake subheads, maybe a small FAQ at the end. The joke has to come from the product copy being, straight-faced, a perfect description of the thing Kierkegaard was describing: give me features that do the leveling on purpose and describe it as liberation. Anonymity pitched as safety because no one is exposed and therefore no one can be held responsible; something like a "Parity Engine" that quietly throttles any user who is starting to be someone, since distinction reads as unfairness to everyone else; a metric — call it Conversation Volume — that counts talk about a thing and is dragged down by anyone who actually goes and does the thing; a throwaway line about legacy "publics" (a book club, a picket line, a jury) being allowed to stay, with the note that these can still do things, which is exactly why they aren't scalable; a reaction set reduced to indiscriminate applause and indiscriminate condemnation, available on every post at all times, so nobody ever has to take a position about anything in particular; a publisher partnership paragraph about how, now that no one is the author, no one can be sued, and because everyone is the author, everyone reads.

Keep it deadpan: the voice should sound genuinely delighted, the relief of no longer having to be anyone should be the actual selling point, and nobody in the piece should ever explain the philosophy, name Kierkegaard, or wink at the reader. A reader who doesn't know the material should just feel that something has gone badly wrong; a reader who does should be able to point at the specific line where it happens. Don't put invented quotations in quotation marks and don't attribute made-up sentences to real texts — paraphrase, and if you're hazy on some detail stay at the level of the general idea rather than guessing at Danish words or chapter titles. I'd like it to close on an invitation to send in questions, now that there's no one left who could answer them.
