I spent a little over forty years in ecosystem modelling — population and nutrient dynamics mostly, with a long detour into resilience and regime shifts — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group and reading a good deal of archaeology. I have ended up with a set of views about societal "collapse" that I suspect do not all fit together, and I would like an audit before I inflict them on anyone. Please answer in American English; I intend to circulate what you give me.

Here, I think, is where I have landed.

(a) I have been using a working definition of my own: a polity undergoes collapse at a time t if, within roughly two generations of t, the number of distinct administrative tiers separating primary producers from the topmost decision-makers falls by at least half, and the polity does not regain its earlier depth within five generations. I like it because it gives me a threshold, a timescale, and a recovery clause, and because it seems to me to be measurable in principle rather than a matter of impression.

(b) On that definition, or on anything in its neighbourhood, I am confident that the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE is a genuine case: the palace economies and their record-keeping disappear within a couple of generations, and literacy vanishes from the Aegean for centuries. I am nearly as confident that Rapa Nui is not a case — the popular ecocide story strikes me as a just-so story, and what looks like decline when you stand near the statues looks more like relocation and reorganization once you take the whole island, and not the quarry, as the unit. And I think "the Maya collapse" is a misnomer for the Terminal Classic: the southern lowland cities were abandoned, but nothing that deserves to be called a civilization ended there.

(c) I also think the word "collapse" is irredeemably evaluative in ordinary use. It presupposes both that losing complexity is a way of going badly and that a society is the sort of thing that can go badly. So I want the descriptive content split off and captured by something like (a), and the remainder dropped.

(d) Relatedly: societies are not organisms and not agents. They have no welfare, no projects, no lifespan; individuals do. So a society cannot fail, and I distrust every narrative in which a society overshoots its limits, destroys itself, or commits suicide.

(e) I think the explanations are bottom-up. What happened in any of these episodes is that households, local elites, and garrisons re-optimized under changed payoffs — a trade route shifting, a cheaper metal, a pathogen, a new fiscal arrangement — and the collapse of a polity is a bookkeeping description of many small decisions, not the name of a system-level cause.

(f) But I also think a predictive science of these transitions would be worth having. The ecological notion of a regime shift — a system with alternative configurations and a threshold between them, where the approach to the threshold shows up in the variance before it shows up in the mean — seems to me to transfer, with the relevant state variable being the scale over which people can be made to coordinate. And I think the overshoot and carrying-capacity models I was trained on should be set aside: they are close to unfalsifiable on the available evidence, and I have never seen one whose parameters were earned rather than fitted.

(g) Finally, a methodological worry I take seriously: the record is visibility-biased. Monumental, literate, stone-building polities are enormously over-represented relative to ones whose architecture was perishable and whose coordination was oral, and a disappearance from the record may as easily be a change in what got deposited as a change in what was there. So I distrust any claim about how common collapse is or how large the losses were.

What I want is not a survey and not a reading list; I can find those myself, and in any case I am asking you to work on the package as stated. Please take the case descriptions in (b) as I have given them, and correct them only if one of them is inconsistent with something else I have said.

The question is which of these commitments actually conflict, and how badly. For each pair that genuinely pulls against the others, tell me what would have to give and what giving it would cost me, and name the specific commitment that has to move. Rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging all of them as equally bad. I strongly suspect that at least one apparent conflict dissolves once the terms are fixed — that, say, denying that societies have welfare is quite compatible with explaining and predicting complexity loss — and I would like that one identified as a pseudo-problem rather than lumped in with the real ones. If the definition in (a) is doing something illegitimate — smuggling in a unit of analysis I have no right to, given (d) and (g), or a threshold that cannot in principle be measured on the evidence I say we have — say so plainly. And if one of the seven is idle, doing no work in the set at all, say which.

I have no favoured revision and I would rather not be asked which of these I would least like to part with; that is the question I am trying to answer. If one single revision would clean up more than the others, tell me what it is and what it would leave me unable to say. Please write in American English.
