To stop this from being a pure slogan fight, assume the following two hypothetical results so we are not arguing about who read which survey:

Setup A, the sliding-window test. Someone takes every familiar generational finding — millennials are more likely to do X, Gen Z does less Y — and reruns the whole thing with every boundary shifted five years earlier and five years later, and also with 12-year and 22-year windows instead of the usual roughly 15. Suppose the effect sizes barely budge and the same qualitative story survives in almost every case.

Setup B, the big dataset. A huge preregistered cross-national longitudinal dataset lands, and it turns out that (i) within any single birth year the spread on all the traits that get called generational is enormous, and between-cohort differences account for under 3 percent of the variance; (ii) once you control for age, survey year, country-level period shocks, and the attitudes of respondents parents, the residual cohort effect is essentially zero; but (iii) if instead you group people by whether they were 13 to 19 during some specific datable event — a recession, a war, the diffusion of smartphones, a pandemic — you get large, stable differences that survive all of those controls.

Now the bit my roommate and I keep talking past each other on. Call a membership cohort a set of people born between two dates, and an exposure cohort a set of people who underwent some specified thing during a developmental window. It seems to me that Setup B shows the real causal structure lives in exposure cohorts and that membership cohorts are just a noisy proxy for them, but I genuinely cannot tell whether that makes Millennial unreal or merely badly measured. My roommate says a proxy that reliably tracks a real kind is still latching onto something real, so the labels are fine.
