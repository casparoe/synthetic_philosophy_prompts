Hi — I work on the flood risk team at a regional water agency (about forty people, one big river basin). A few times a month we put out a seasonal flood-risk estimate for a particular reach of river, and those numbers drive real decisions: whether to pre-position pumps and temporary barriers, whether to pre-buy flood easements from landowners, whether to issue an early warning that shuts down a couple of industrial sites for a week. Each decision has a rough threshold — we pre-position above about 15% chance of exceeding the levee crest, we buy easements above about 30% — and those thresholds were set by our board years ago and arn't mine to change.

Here's the problem. Our numbers are fake-precise. We run four or five hydrological models; they disagree substantially, especially in dry years; and my two senior hydrologists will honestly hand you different intervals if you ask them separately. Right now the reporting software forces one number per reach, so somebody averages, we ship it, and everyone downstream — emergency management, the county, the press — treats it as a probability. I've been reading a bit about imprecise probabilities, credal sets, decision rules that aren't just maximise expected utility, and it seems like it was written for exactly our situation. But everything I can find is either formal epistemology or a decision-theory paper that never says what to actually put in the memo.

So: turn it into a procedure my team can run. Constraints, and I mean them: it has to be executable in about half an hour per estimate by someone with an engineering-stats background and no philosophy; the output has to fit on a single page for the duty officer; and the duty officer signs off, not me, so anything that's really a values call has to be flagged as theirs rather than smuggled in as method.

What I need in it:

- how to get from "four models and two people disagree" to one interval we can defend. Do we take the union of their ranges? The intersection? What do we do when the intersection is empty, because that happens maybe a quarter of the time and right now we just quietly average.
- whether it matters that some of the disagreement is between models and some of it is between humans, and if so how the aggregation differs. I have a feeling these are different animals but I can't say why.
- one decision rule we actually apply, picked off the menu the literature offers. I've run across the "best worst case" one, something called maximality or E-admissibility, and interval dominance — give me a plain-english statement of each, but then commit to one for us, and say why that one fits a public agency that has to justify itself in a hearing after the fact when the levee did or didn't overtop.
- what to do when the interval straddles a threshold. This is most of the time. Do we act, do we wait, do we buy more information, and if we buy information what kind.
- what the one-pager says when the honest output is that we don't know enough to act. The board hates that answer and I think sometimes it's the right one, so I need it written in a form the duty officer can actually sign.

Then run the whole thing on two or three cases. One of them should be a case where the interval gets *wider* after we condition on new monitoring data — I gather this is a known phenomenon with a name, something like dilation? Our director will absolutely refuse to sign a page that says we got less certain after spending money to get more certain, so tell me how to write that up honestly without getting the procedure thrown out. And one should be the case where the procedure tells me not to do the thing I want: I've been pushing for three years to pre-buy the easements on the Milburn bend, I think it's obviously right, and I want to see whether the procedure licenses it or kills it.

One more wrinkle I have to live with. The board just circulated a draft directive saying every published estimate must carry a "swing" figure alongside it: how much the probability would move if the single most contested modelling assumption were resolved one way or the other. I have to comply. I can't tell whether the swing figure is the same object as the interval, a different object that sits next to it, or whether it quietly drags a point estimate back in through the side door and wrecks the whole approach. Tell me where it belongs in the procedure, or tell me it doesn't.

Be straight with me about which steps are judgment wearing a lab coat, and about what imprecise probabilities can't do for us — I'd rather hand the director a short honest method than a long one that looks rigorous and isn't.
