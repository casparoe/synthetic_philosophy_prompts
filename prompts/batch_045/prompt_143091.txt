Write a scene, in continuous prose, in which two invented discussants — Mira Voss and Anil Rahn — argue about where the meaning of a message is located. They should use, and keep returning to, the three-way vocabulary that Douglas Hofstadter introduces in Gödel, Escher, Bach: the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message, where the frame message is whatever makes an object announce itself as a message that demands decoding, the outer message is whatever must be grasped before the object can be decoded at all, and the inner message is what the object thereby turns out to say. The two of them should not spend time on what Hofstadter himself thought; the question is what they think.

The case they are arguing over is stipulated here, so nothing needs to be looked up and no sources should be cited. Call it the Kotuy object, an invented find: a cylinder of fused silica about the length of a thumb, recovered from a sealed clay vault. A spiral track on its surface, readable optically, carries a sequence of roughly four million marks drawn from an alphabet of exactly four symbols. The sequence is not random: it segments into recurrent blocks whose frequency distribution is approximately Zipfian, and one short block recurs at perfectly regular intervals, like punctuation. Inside the cylinder, machined to nanometre tolerance, sits a rotor with sixty-four cams whose profiles can be read as a lookup table taking triples of symbols to single symbols — that is, as a fragment of just the machine one would need in order to read the track. Two complications matter. First, the cam profiles are indexed, and the index is written on the track itself in the four-symbol code: the reader is needed to read the track, and the track is needed to assemble the reader. In practice the researchers broke in after two years by exploiting the Zipfian structure and guessing a mapping, which then paid for itself. Second, the rotor can be seated in either of two orientations, and nothing on the object marks which is correct. In one orientation the decoded output is a table of the gaps between successive primes, carried out to a point far beyond anything the receivers had computed. In the other it is a sequence of operations for manufacturing a copy of the cylinder and sealing it in clay. Both outputs are internally coherent, both are exactly the sort of thing any receiver would call meaningful, and they do not say the same thing. Note finally that the object's manufacture is not in dispute — its isotope ratios do not occur naturally and its tolerances are beyond any known natural process — but nothing establishes that anyone ever addressed it to anybody, and it may have been a toy, a devotional item, an apprentice's exercise, or a working part of some machine.

Voss holds that meaning is there to be recovered: the frame message is a fact about the object rather than a courtesy extended to it, the bootstrapping shows that the object carries the seeds of its own decoding, and while the two orientations show that the object may contain more than one inner message, the set of admissible inner messages is fixed by the object's own constraints and not by anything the receivers bring. Rahn holds that the inner message is not simply there: the frame message establishes manufacture but not address, the bootstrapping worked only because the receivers supplied a great deal — a preference for Zipfian structure, for simple mappings, for output that looks mathematical — and the unbroken tie between the two orientations is not a temporary embarrassment but the normal situation, since constraints never single out one reading by themselves.

Both of them should be equally competent and neither should be a strawman: each must state the other's strongest point in a form the other would accept, and each must make at least one genuine concession that is not a capitulation. In particular, Voss must answer what it is about the object that makes the successful decoding feel like discovery rather than invention, and must say what kind of further evidence would legitimately settle the tie between the orientations. Rahn must answer why some readings are plainly inadmissible — why the cam profiles cannot be read as, say, a shopping list — and why it seems to be the object rather than the receiver's whim that is doing the work. The disagreement should be worked out entirely on the cylinder; do not settle it by invoking the standard set pieces by name, such as radical translation, the Chinese Room, or rule-following scepticism, and do not refer to any author, book, or paper apart from using Hofstadter's three terms. The scene should end either by isolating precisely the single claim on which the two still diverge, or by drawing a distinction that genuinely dissolves part of the dispute; it should not end in the observation that both are partly right.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, between eight hundred and a thousand words: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no script layout with speaker names on separate lines, and no bracketed stage directions. Fold what each of them says into the sentences of the scene, the way one would in a short story or a letter.
