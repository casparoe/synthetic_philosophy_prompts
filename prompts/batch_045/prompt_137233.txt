Hey — I TA for an intro philosophy of language / cog sci crossover class, and we just ran a session on Hofstadter's essay ranting about how shallow statistical machine translation is; I got into a long arguement with a friend who works in NLP afterwards and we didn't resolve anything. So I want a number, not a tour of the positions. Set aside what today's systems can actually do and ask about principle: what's your credence that a system trained only on text — huge amounts of monolingual text plus parallel corpora, no sensorimotor contact with the world, nothing at stake for it, no being the kind of thing that has plans — could nonetheless come to understand the meanings of the texts it translates, in the sense that its errors would be human-shaped errors (a wrong guess at what the author meant, a stylistic misjudgment, a bit of world knowlege it happened to lack) rather than that distinctive flavour of error that seems to reveal that nothing inside it ever represented what the sentences were about? Give me the number, then take it apart: how much rides on whether understanding requires grounding or embodiment at all, how much on whether the thing actually missing is world knowledge rather than a mechanism for deploying it, how much on whether the relevant sort of world model is in principle recoverable from text statistics alone, how much on whether there has to be a subject with purposes behind the representation, and how much on whether "statistical" versus "understands" marks a real distinction at all rather than this being the Chinese Room in a new jacket. Rough probability and one line of justification per term, say how you're combining them, and tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to and what would move it most.

A few things I'd like you to actually engage with rather than talk around. First, a distinction I keep coming back to and can't quite make work: it seems to me failures come in at least three kinds — not knowing some relevant fact; never having represented the distinction that needed drawing in the first place; and representing the meaning perfectly well while attributing the wrong intention to the author, i.e. quietly repairing a sentence so it says something sensible when the author was being ironic, or was mistaken about what the reader knew. My sense is that Hofstadter's complaint is really about the second and third kinds, and that a system could pass every test we have for the first kind and still be shallow in his sense. Is that the right way to carve it, and does carving it that way change your number? Second, a test I half-invented and half trust: take a short paragraph in language A that turns on an ambiguous word or a pronoun, where the disambiguating information is never stated in the text but follows from some mundane fact about the physical world — which of two objects is heavier, what happens to a thing left in the sun — translate it into B and back, and ask whether a shallow system should be expected to pick the statistically likelier reading while a human picks the right one, and whether getting it right for the wrong reason should count as understanding at all. Third, the control case that makes me doubt myself: a human translator working from a language they learned entirely out of books and dictionaries, who has never spoken to a native speaker and has never seen the objects half the words name. My intuition is that they still understand, and when I try to say what they've got that the system lacks I end up mumbling — so either my intuition is doing all the work here or there's a real difference I can't articulate. And finally: be honest about how much of your number is just inherited from your view on the Chinese Room and on whether syntax could ever suffice for semantics, and how much is genuinely about translation. If you think the question is partly verbal, say which reading gets which number.
