I teach a six-week module on persistence in an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics seminar. The session I need help with is the fourth: sixteen students, seventy-five minutes, a seminar room with a whiteboard, movable chairs, and nothing else. No AV, no prep budget beyond what I can print or carry in a bag, and no real money for stakes.

By this point the students have worked through the standard pressure points in the abstract: the problem of temporary intrinsics, the objection that a thing wholly present at each of several times would have to be wholly present at many places at once, the embarrassment of how many things a perdurantist must count in an ordinary room, and the way the Ship of Theseus seems to dissolve under one picture and become a forced choice under the other. The difficulty is that all of it stays notional. They can reconstruct the arguments and still feel, reasonably, that nothing is at stake: whichever picture they adopt, the world looks exactly the same afterwards, and the discussion decays into a canvass of who finds what intuitive.

So I want a session in which they build something, and the cost of the picture they have built shows up in the thing they built rather than in anything I say. The intended arc is this. First, under pressure from a case rather than from a definition, they should arrive at something recognisably perdurantist on their own: they posit different bits of the thing at different times, because that is the only way to stop the thing from being both bent and straight, or the only way to make sense of a thing that has a part at one time and not at another. Second, I want that home-made picture to be shown unstable, so that the natural repair pushes them toward a view I have been toying with and have never managed to get students to take seriously when it is stated on the page. Call it chunk perdurantism: a persisting thing does have temporal parts, but not arbitrary or instantaneous ones — its temporal parts are exactly the maximal intervals across which its intrinsic qualitative profile is constant, so that a thing is a finite train of homogeneous blocks rather than a continuum of slices, and each block can be bent, or straight, or four-legged, simpliciter. Third, I want the repair to fail on them in a way they can feel, and the failure I have in mind is continuous change: for something that is changing throughout — rotating, cooling, wearing down — there are no maximal intervals of qualitative constancy, so either the thing has no temporal parts at all or the grain of its articulation has to be fixed by something extrinsic to the thing, and every candidate they reach for will look arbitrary in a way they did not expect.

I also want one diagnostic case built in somewhere, to be used at the point where I need each picture to commit itself: a ship dismantled at one time, its planks numbered and stored in a warehouse for a season during which nothing ship-shaped exists anywhere, then reassembled from the same planks in the same arrangement. Both perdurantist variants have a distinctive thing to say about it, and the endurantist alternatives have to choose between intermittent existence and a plain denial of identity. The activity should force that commitment to be made in public and in a form we can point at afterwards.

What I need from you is a runnable design: the setup and materials, the rules or roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the exact instructions I give at each stage. Please keep it honest about the pedagogy. I want the students to do something — assemble, trade, bid, sort, commit to a written verdict — and to run into the problem before it is named; no lecture up front, no show-of-hands intuition polls, and the words endurantism, perdurantism and chunk perdurantism should not be uttered until the last ten minutes. The debrief should map specific moments in the session onto specific moves in the argument, including what each picture must say about the dismantled ship. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the shortcuts students will take, the way a group will try to vote its way out, the version of the activity where everyone sees the trick in the first five minutes — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which part of the dispute the activity cannot stage and which I will have to supply in the debrief myself: I suspect the real cost of these views is metaphysical rather than bookkeeping, and that a game can only ever stage the bookkeeping. If you think part of my intended arc is a bad idea, say so and say what to put in its place.
