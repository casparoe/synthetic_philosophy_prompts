What I want is the joint statement that would come out of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis:

Camp A, call them the constitutivists: the thesis isn't a contingent claim that could have turned out false. "Effective procedure" / "algorithm" / "mechanical method" is a concept with enough structure in it that, once you try to make it precise, the pre-theoretic notion pins down exactly the Turing-computable functions; the famous convergence of independently motivated formalisms — Turing machines, general recursive functions, the lambda calculus — is what a successful conceptual analysis looks like, and no experiment could overturn it, only leave us wanting a new word for something else.

Camp B, call them the physicalists: whatever is true about the analysis of our concept, the live question is the physical version of the thesis — that no physical process can compute anything beyond the Turing-computable — and that is contingent, empirical, and in principle refutable by a working hypercomputer. The convergence of formalisms is evidence about the world's computational structure only given that our concept was shaped by the world, and that assumption is the interesting part.

For the cruxes, work with these three cases I've made up rather than the usual examples. Use mine, don't swap in your own:

(i) The Halting Box: a sealed, mass-produced physical device. You feed it the description of an arbitrary Turing machine and an input; within a second it prints HALTS or DOESN'T. Over three years it's been tried on ten million cases, including machines whose status was only settled years later by exhaustive simulation or by an independently discovered proof, and it's never been wrong and never failed to answer. Nobody can open it: any attempt to probe it's interior permanently converts it into a box that prints random bits. Labs in six countries have reproducible units. Crux: does the existence of the Halting Box refute the Church–Turing thesis, and if yes, which one, and if no, what exactly has it refuted?

(ii) The two boxes: as in (i), except that a year later somebody works out that the box's answers track not halting behaviour but an unrelated physical quantity that happened to correlate perfectly with halting status across the tested range — its reliability was, in a sense still to be spelled out, coincidental rather than systematic. Each camp has to say whether that changes the epistemic lesson of (i), and what "systematic rather than coincidental" would even have to mean here. I suspect one crux is whether a process has to be surveyable or explainable to count as computing anything at all — call it the surveyability clause, and say which camp, if either, is committed to it.

(iii) The lambda-star formalisms: suppose someone proposes a new candidate characterisation of "effective procedure", independently motivated and not gerrymandered, which permits exactly one infinitary rule — a single limit step over the machine's infinite history, so that once, and only once, the machine may read off whether some specified cell has stabilised. These machines decide the halting problem. Does being independently motivated earn this formalism a place in the convergence argument, and if not, what has to be missing from its motivation for excluding it not to be question-begging? Camp A has to say what distinguishes it from the general recursive functions; Camp B has to say what they'd conclude if the physical world turned out to run on these things.
