I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the separateness of persons objection this term, and I keep running into the same move from an economist in my department — he's a genuinely good philosopher of the social sciences, not a cartoon utilitarian — and I want to know whether the thing he leans on is actually load-bearing. His line, usually delivered over coffee, is that the objection only looks forceful because we fetishise the boundaries between organisms. On his picture, aggregating well-being accross persons is exactly like aggregating it accross times within a single life: prudence and benevolence are the same operation, just with more columns in the spreadsheet, and the only reason the two feel different is that in one case we happen to run the calculation from the inside. He uses that to license two conclusions: (i) that cost-benefit analysis in real policy settings — a transport appraisal, a hospital budget, a pandemic triage protocol — is prudence written large and needs no extra moral warrant beyond what any sane person already grants for their own life, and (ii) that Rawls's insistence on the distinctness of persons and Nozick's crack about there being no social entity with a good of its own are rhetoric dressed up as argument. He also gestures at Harsanyi's veil-of-ignorance result as the formal version of the same thought.

Here is where my seminar made it harder than I expected. We spent a session varying the *degree* of subject-fusion, and the analogy seems to move with it in a way I can't get a grip on. Four set-ups: (A) one person across an ordinary life — prudential trade-off, nobody objects. (B) a pair of people joined in such a way that every affective state is shared at full intensity and there is one decision procedure between them. (C) the same pair only partially fused: each feels the other's pains and pleasures at half intensity, and they still deliberate separately. (D) the reverse direction — one human life whose earlier and later stages share no memory, no projects, no psychological continuity at all, Parfit-ish fission without the branching, so that prudence across that life starts to look like one stage of a person making a sacrifice for a stranger. So what I want is an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on utilitarianism. Give me the correspondence table first: in the intrapersonal case, what plays the role of the planner, the earlier self, the later self, the discount rate, compensation, and the boundary of the unit — and what does each map onto in the interpersonal case, including who plays the planner there (and note that in the target the planner is always somebody with their own stakes, which has no analogue in the source).

Then the disanalogies, sorted rather than listed: which ones actually block the inference to (i) and (ii) and which are noise — e.g. whether the single stream of consciousness and shared memory in the source matter for the metaphysics but not for the policy conclusion, or the other way round. Then run the cases: does B behave like A or like the ordinary many-person case? Does C give me a scalar version of the objection, with separateness coming in degrees rather than on/off — and if so, is that trouble for the standard Rawlsian framing, which wants distinctness to be a hard moral fact, or trouble for my colleague, or both? And D: if the analogy is meant to borrow the self-evidence of prudence, and prudence across a discontinuous life is itself just one stage benefiting another, hasn't it lost the thing it was borrowing — or is that just a change of subject? Finally, if the analogy fails, is there a better one that does the work he wants without the fusion metaphysics? I've been toying with a parent dividing things among several children — one decision-maker, several distinct beneficiaries, no identity needed, aggregation of a sort clearly happening — but I suspect it quietly imports something about the parent's own stake or about the children's claims that he isn't entitled to. Say what that is, and say whether any analogy can get you from trade-offs within a life are fine to trade-offs between lives are fine without smuggling the conclusion. Don't bother with citations or a reading list — I've read the standard stuff, I just want the mapping and its breaking points.
