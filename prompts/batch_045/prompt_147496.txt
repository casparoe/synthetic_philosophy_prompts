I'm a retired physicist — solid state, mostly symmetry and phase transitions, which is why I keep seing the same shape in everything — and I've got into philosophy far too late in life to be careful about it. My daughter gets married in June and I'm doing the father-of-the-bride toast, and I want to build it around Buridan's ass.

The problem is that the toast everyone expects says something like of all the people in the world you two chose each other, and I've come to think that's false in an interesting way: when something matters that much there usually isn't a reason that singles one person out, and pretending there is one is vanity. But I obviously can't stand up and say it was arbitrary, and I'm not going to call it a coin flip either. The thing I've been chewing on, which I haven't found in any of the books and suspect is either wrong or already has a name: what breaks a tie like that isn't a reason at all, it's an event, and the event doesn't discover a value that was already sitting there — it makes one. Afterwards there are reasons (habits, history, a shared kitchen) that didn't exist beforehand, and the ass doesn't starve becuase it picked the wrong pile, it starves because it thinks walking has to be justified in advance.

About forty people: half my old physics crowd, who will groan at anything sentimental, and half my daughter's friends and the groom's family, who've never heard of Buridan and will hear there was no reason as an insult. The groom's grandmother is devout and needs to hear commitment said like it means something. Four minutes please. And the bit I keep failing at: it can't land as a consolation prize — I don't want to sound like marriage is what you settle for once the reasons run out.
