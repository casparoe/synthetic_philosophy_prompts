I'm a philosopher, which is apparently why my college's investment committee has asked me to draft the written opinion it will circulate to the full board of trustees before its vote in six weeks; I would be grateful for your help composing it.

In 1911 an alumna left the college a restricted endowment directing the trustees to apply its income to the instruction and material assistance of students of the College in each successive generation. The board has always read that as a perpetual charge and has spent only real income, preserving the principal intact. A petition from a majority of the faculty and roughly a third of the students asks instead that we spend down about a quarter of the real principal over the next decade: half on decarbonizing the campus, half on need-based aid reserved for applicants from regions already suffering serious climate disruption. Their argument is that a perpetual charge is a charge to benefit each generation rather than to hoard, that the students we are teaching now will live most of their lives after 2070, and that a dollar spent on mitigation today does more for the students of 2120 than a dollar carefully preserved for them.

I want the opinion to face three complications rather than wave them away. First, our general counsel thinks the restriction genuinely ambiguous, but a spend-down is also irreversible, and several trustees treat that irreversibility as the objection rather than the obstacle: on their view a perpetual trust is not a transfer to the future but a way of keeping each generation's options open and bequeathing the next one an authority equal to our own. Second, the beneficiaries the petition names do not yet exist, and whether they ever enrol depends on admissions decisions we have not made, so it is not obvious that declining to spend down leaves anyone worse off. Third, a compromise has been proposed that I find harder to dismiss than I expected: adopt the spend-down, but treat the principal as borrowed from the future and commit the college to restoring it, with interest, out of later gifts, on the theory that what matters is not the stock of assets held but whether the college sustains a relation of reciprocal giving across time.

Please write roughly eight hundred to nine hundred words recommending one course of action, with a timetable and, if you like, a trigger or a sunset provision, and give the strongest version of the case against whatever you recommend. Treat the donor's restriction as a live moral claim rather than only a legal one, and say plainly where the petition's talk of benefiting future students goes wrong, if you think it does. Write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullets, bold type, or tables, and cite nothing.
