I convene a small reading group — six of us, in the back room of a pub in Bristol, about half of us philosophers by training and half not — and next month we are giving one session to whether 'better than' is transitive, and specifically to the continuum-style cases that are supposed to show it isn't. I would like to open the session with a poem rather than a handout, and I have a fairly exact idea of what I am after.

The form should be a pantoum of six quatrains (seven, if the drift genuinely needs the extra link), rhymed, in a steady metre that reads well aloud. The reason for the pantoum is that its rules are the argument: lines 2 and 4 of each quatrain must reappear as lines 1 and 3 of the next, so that consecutive stanzas share half their lines — that shared half is the part of the world the speaker is holding fixed while one small thing changes — and the closing quatrain must bring back lines 1 and 3 of the opening quatrain (as its own second and fourth lines, in either order), so the poem ends on words it began with and the reader is forced to hold the first world and the last world together at once. One of the returning lines should be a short evaluative phrase — something in the shape of 'and no one would call it pain', or 'the smaller number wins' — that is comfortably true where it first appears and has become doubtful, or plainly false, by the time it comes back; the drift should register as a change in the sense of an unchanged line, not as something the speaker says about the drift.

The case itself: a scale of pain from 1 (an itch you would have forgotten by lunchtime) to 100 (the worst pain you can imagine), and a series of worlds, each lasting a single minute. In the first, a million people each feel intensity 1. Then a hundred thousand at 2; ten thousand at 5; a thousand at 12; a hundred at 30; ten at 60; and at last one person at 100. The speaker should accept each step on the strength of a single consideration — that it is better for fewer people to be in pain at all, even if each remaining person's pain is somewhat worse — and the steps should grow harder to accept as they go without any single one of them being an obviously bad trade: if a reader can point to the link where the argument goes wrong, the poem has failed, and the unease has to be distributed along the whole chain. Give the turn to the final link or to the closing return — the moment the reader notices what is left, one person at the limit of agony, and that most of us, asked directly, would rather have the first world back: a million forgotten itches than one unbearable minute, even though we have just conceded, a step at a time, that the last world is better.

Two last constraints. First, no jargon and no name-dropping: the words 'transitive', 'intransitive', 'utility' and 'aggregation' should not appear, and neither should any philosopher's name; someone who has never opened a philosophy book should be able to follow the shape of the problem and simply enjoy the poem, while someone who knows the literature should recognise exactly which argument is being staged and be able to say afterwards which of the four commitments it forces us to choose between — the step-by-step verdicts, the move from those verdicts to a verdict about the endpoints, the verdict about the endpoints themselves, or the assumption that there is one relation 'better than' here rather than several respects each returning its own verdict. I would rather the poem not settle which of those should go; leave the reader holding all four and unable to keep them. Second, no notes, no epigraph, no prefatory explanation — just the poem, with a title if it wants one.
