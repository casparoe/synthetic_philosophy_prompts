I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice under strategic uncertainty, and we have just spent a fractious session on a problem I would rather define from scratch than inherit from any particular literature. Call it the commitment race. Two agents are about to play Chicken: each prefers that the other swerve, each prefers mutual swerving to mutual straight-driving, and each can, before the moment of decision, take an irrevocable step — discard the steering wheel, sign a binding contract, hand control to a program — that removes its own ability to swerve. Whoever commits first, and is seen to have committed, wins; the other is left with nothing better than swerving. But both agents know this, so both have reason to commit as early and as visibly as possible, and the result is a race in which each side spends real resources on speed and on publicity, and in which a badly synchronized race ends with both wheels discarded and both cars in the ditch — an outcome each would have paid to avoid beforehand. Among agents who know each other to be consequentialist reasoners of comparable power, the race is the whole game, and any question about what to do inside it is downstream of whether to enter it at all.

My students and I could not agree on what we were asking for, so before we look at any proposed solution I want a specification: what would an adequate normative account of commitment races have to deliver? Please treat the account as a design specification rather than a purely evaluative theory — something that could in principle be handed to an engineer building agents that really will meet each other in these situations and must act without knowing the other side's architecture, reaction speed, or decision theory. That purpose, I take it, already makes some requirements non-negotiable which a purely evaluative theory could set aside: implementability under that kind of ignorance, tolerable performance when the other party is not a consequentialist at all or cannot commit in the first place, and no reliance on a common prior or on perfect prediction of the other side.

The heart of what I am asking for is the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and I want the cases to include at least these, because these are the ones that split the seminar: the symmetric race with simultaneous arrival; the fast-hands asymmetry, in which one agent can finalize and display a commitment strictly faster and the slower agent knows it will lose; the blind commitment, where an agent can commit but cannot make the commitment visible; the sealed-escrow variant, in which a trusted third party accepts commitments and releases them simultaneously, which may dissolve the race or may merely relocate it; partial commitment, where an agent can bind itself to swerve with some probability or below some threshold rather than outright; the mirror pair, two agents running near-identical decision procedures, so that either one's effective commitment is predictably the other's as well; meta-commitment, binding oneself now not to enter commitment races later; iterated races in which reputations for speed or for refusal to race become the object of competition; and the spectator race, where winning buys the right to exploit a third party who has no commitment technology at all. Alongside the cases, I want the distinctions preserved rather than collapsed: winning a race versus the race being worth entering, having the capacity to commit versus exercising it, and committing about a first-order action versus committing about one's own future commitment policy.

Please give me the desiderata as a numbered list, each with its motivating case in a sentence or two, marked as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. Flag any subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, with a sketch of the argument for the incompatibility — that part interests me most, since if the list is impossible I would rather know now. Tell me which single requirement does the most exclusionary work, and if a requirement obviously sinks one of the familiar strategies — always commit first, never commit, commit to a conditional policy, lean on the logical or causal links between the agents' decision procedures — say so in a clause; I do not need a scorecard or any discussion of who has defended what. Reason from the cases rather than from the literature: no citations, footnotes, or reading suggestions, since I will be reading this without library access. Around eight hundred words, in American English.
