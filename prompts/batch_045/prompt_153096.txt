I teach a graduate seminar in social choice theory. My students have worked through the standard statement and proof of Arrow's theorem (unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, collective rationality ⇒ dictatorship), the decisiveness-coalition machinery behind it, the standard domain restrictions (single-peakedness, value restriction), and the familiar scoring rules. I am writing the final exam — three questions in three hours — and I want one 25-mark question on Arrow's theorem that cannot be answered by restating the theorem or by running the decisiveness-coalition argument again from memory. It should separate students who can take Arrow's conditions apart, check their hypotheses, recombine them with unfamiliar conditions, and diagnose an escape route, from students who have only memorised the labels.

The angle I want to develop is this. IIA is usually motivated by the slogan that the social ranking of x and y should depend only on how individuals rank x against y. A natural-looking retreat is to let the social comparison of x and y depend also on the rank distance between them in each voter's ordering — that is, on how many alternatives the voter ranks strictly between x and y, together with the direction. This is still purely ordinal information, but it is finer than the bare pairwise ranking, and Borda's rule runs on exactly this information: it is anonymous, neutral, Paretian, non-dictatorial, and always delivers a weak order. So at first sight the retreat escapes Arrow. I want students to see exactly why the escape is genuine when the agenda is held fixed, and why it collapses the moment the rule is required to be coherent across agendas of different sizes.

Please produce the following three things.

1. The exam question itself, self-contained, so that a candidate could answer it with nothing in front of them but the paper. Set it in a variable-agenda framework: a universe of alternatives with at least three members; every finite agenda of size at least two is admissible; profiles are profiles of strict linear orders; the rule assigns to each agenda-and-profile a complete and transitive social relation on that agenda. State Arrow's theorem explicitly as a result candidates may cite, while requiring them to verify its hypotheses when they use it. Stipulate two conditions under names of your own choosing (they are invented for the exam; say so): a rank-intensity independence condition of the kind described above, and a deletion condition saying that removing an unchosen alternative from the agenda never changes the social comparison among the alternatives that remain — note that this is unconditional, and in particular holds even for alternatives that some voters rank between the two alternatives being compared. Then present, in the voice of a hypothetical colleague, the claim that Borda's rule refutes the pessimism of Arrow's theorem because it satisfies rank-intensity independence together with weak Pareto, anonymity, neutrality and non-dictatorship while always producing a weak order. Ask the candidate to: (a) show that IIA implies rank-intensity independence but not conversely, by deriving the identity linking Borda score differences to signed rank distances and then exhibiting an explicit small profile on which Borda violates IIA; (b) prove that the deletion condition forces, on every agenda, the social comparison of any pair to depend only on individual rankings of that pair; (c) conclude that any rule satisfying weak Pareto and the deletion condition is dictatorial, taking care to show that it is the same voter who dictates on every agenda, and saying explicitly where the availability of all agendas — including two-element ones — is doing work; (d) write a short referee-style paragraph on the colleague's claim, addressing what it establishes, what it does not, whether cross-agenda coherence is a legitimate requirement, and whether Borda's sensitivity to the agenda is an idiosyncrasy of Borda or something forced.

2. A model answer of roughly 800 to 1,000 words: the computation in (a) with the witness profile, the proof in (b), the closing argument in (c) including the step that identifies the dictator across agendas, and a verdict in (d) that is genuinely committal rather than a list of considerations.

3. A marking scheme organised around the specific moves a top answer must make, with marks attached (25 total), each accompanied by a one-line note on the mistake that should cost marks at that point. Include an explicit note on what should earn no credit — in particular, reproducing the standard proof of Arrow's theorem instead of using it, or announcing that the deletion condition "just is" IIA without giving the reduction that shows it.

For your own orientation when writing the model answer: the load-bearing computation is that the difference in Borda scores between x and y equals the sum over voters of the signed rank distance, so Borda's pairwise verdicts are a function of the rank-distance vector alone; a two-voter, three-alternative example suffices both to break IIA (keep each voter's ranking of x against y fixed while moving the third alternative from between them to outside them, which flips the social verdict) and to show that deleting an alternative can turn a strict social preference into indifference. The proof in (b) should delete alternatives one at a time down to the two-element agenda, then observe that two profiles on the same agenda which agree on the pair must have the same restriction to that agenda and therefore the same social comparison there — this is where the two-element agendas must be in the domain. For (c), the dictator obtained on one agenda must be shown to dictate on every other agenda by extending profiles up to a common super-agenda and deleting back down; candidates who conclude only that each agenda has its own dictator have missed the point. The punchline for (d) is that the contrapositive of (c) applies to Borda itself: no rule that is Paretian, weak-order-valued and non-dictatorial can be deletion-robust, so Borda's agenda-sensitivity is the price of everything else it buys, not a defect peculiar to it.

Please keep the whole thing free of citations, references and attributions beyond Arrow's theorem itself; a candidate should not need to look anything up. Write the question in a polished, formal register, at a level appropriate for a PhD-level field exam or an advanced master's seminar.
