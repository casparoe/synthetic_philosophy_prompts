ok so we went over the Sapir-Whorf thing in class and everyone keeps saying "language shapes thought" like its one single claim, but it feels like its secretly two different claims and people switch between them whenever its convenient. I want you to compare these two versions:

Version A, the strong one: the words and grammar your language actually has decide which thoughts you are even able to have. No word or grammatical habit for some distinction, you can't think that distinction, full stop.

Version B, which I kind of made up so tell me if its dumb or if someone already thought of it: language doesn't set the limits of whats thinkable, it sets whats *cheap*. Having a ready-made word or a grammar rule that forces you to mark something every time you talk makes that thing automatic to notice and easy to hold in your head, while stuff your language ignores is still totally thinkable, you just have to build it by hand each time, like doing arithmetic without a calculator.

What I actually want you to get into:

1. Could anyone even tell these apart with an experiment? The usual kind of result people cite is basically "speakers of a language that splits up this category are faster / more accurate at some task with it" — but that fits both versions equally well, so what result would back A but not B?
2. Which version deals better with two awkward facts: bilingual people sometimes saying they feel like a slightly different person depending on which language they're in, and some people reporting that they often think with no words in their head at all?
3. Does B actually stay a separate option, or does it quietly collapse back into A once you push on what "able to think" even means?

Don't just sit on the fence at the end, pick the one you think is closer to right and say why. And pls keep it readable, I don't need any sources or names of papers.
