My younger sister is having her first child, and I've been asked to give the toast at the naming on Saturday: about three minutes, in a village hall, to roughly sixty people split almost exactly down the middle. On one side is my mother's large, devout, extremely fertile family, for whom a baby is simply good news and a bigger family is a bigger blessing; on the other are a dozen of my sister's friends from graduate school, several of whom work on population ethics and will be listening with their arms folded. I've drafted the thing four times and every version has come out either as a position paper or as a sentiment my sister would call false, so I'd like you to write it.

The problem is that she and her husband spent years arguing themselves into two commitments I have to respect. First, the person-affecting asymmetry: they deny that a child is benefited by being brought into existence, because if she had never existed there would have been no one who was worse off for it — which rules out, for them, every version of the "we did this for her, we're giving her the gift of life, think of the wonderful life she'll have" line, which is of course the warmest thing my relatives want to hear. (They'd add that if they had waited three years it would have been a different child anyway, so "this child rather than no child" was never the choice on the table.) Second, they are not pronatalists, and they don't want the birth rate invoked: they hold that a larger population isn't better in itself, that the total-value view which seems to imply otherwise collapses into the repugnant conclusion, and that the civilizational-replacement and demographic-decline talk now surrounding all of this is embarrassing. What they do believe is something they worked out for themselves and that I find hard to argue with, and it's the one thing I think a toast could be built on: call it the stewardship view. What matters about a new person, on this account, isn't that there is one more of us; it's that some of the things that exist — a practice, a place, a body of knowledge — can only continue by being handed to somebody, and a birth is the moment a family gets to make that handover. The handover in this case is real: the orchard my great-grandfather planted, and the way of pruning it that only two people still alive properly know. My sister has also asked that nobody say anything about the world the child is inheriting — no climate, no "it'll all be better by then" — since she thinks the hopeful and the grim versions both turn a baby into an argument about something else.

So: write me the toast. It has to be something a person would actually say out loud at a party rather than a lecture, and warm enough that my grandmother doesn't go home worried that I've gone strange, but written so that my sister's friends can't catch it doing either of the two things she takes to be false — treating existence as something done for the child's sake, or treating the arrival as a demographic good. Don't explain the philosophy in the toast; there should be no "some people think that..." in it, and the constraints should show up in what the toast does and leaves out rather than in anything it says. And if you think the three demands can't all be met at once — warmth for a room of believers, plus both of my sister's commitments — then tell me plainly which constraint has to give and why, and only then write the toast that breaks it.
