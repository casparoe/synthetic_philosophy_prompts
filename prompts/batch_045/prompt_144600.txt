I'm in my forth year, and I'm applying for a one-semester internal dissertation fellowship: four months of funding, two-page proposal, and the committee is one philosopher, one political scientist and one econ person, so it has to be legible outside a seminar room while still being a real philosophy project rather than a summary of one. The chapter it's for is on Kavka's toxin puzzle, and I've become convinced everyone is fighting about the wrong thing. The standard dialectic, as I see it: one camp says of course you can form the intention, by getting yourself into the right state — self-manipulation, commitment devices, whatever; the other says no, intention is partly belief-like, it's constrained by what you believe about your own future action, and since you know you won't drink, you can't intend to. And then the payoff is supposed to be that the answer tells us something about deterrent threats, where you have to intend to do something that would be pointless if you ever had to do it. Fine. But both camps take for granted that the subject is *certain* she won't drink, and I think that's load-bearing in a way nobody has checked.

Here's the half-formed bit. Run a version where the billionaire's machine can't read intentions directly, only your confidence that you'll drink, and the payout is proportional to that number rather than all-or-nothing at midnight. Now the subject isn't at certainty; she's somewhere on a curve, and she has some inkling that she's the sort of person who sometimes follows through. The question I can't get straight: does intention degrade smoothly as self-predictive confidence falls, or is there a cliff — a threshold below which trying to A just stops being intending to A? If there's a cliff, the original puzzle is the limit case at confidence zero and the interesting work is locating the cliff and saying why it's there; if there's no cliff, the 'you can't intend what you believe you won't do' camp owes us an account of what weaker state is doing the work in ordinary action. And then the deterrence application looks different, because real deterrent credibility is a matter of the target's estimate, not the threatener's private state: maybe intention turns out to be doing less work in that story than the literature assumes, and what's needed is a public signal.
