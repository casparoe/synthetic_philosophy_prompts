I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on moral status, and I am writing this year's final. I want one essay question, answerable in 90 minutes and capped at 1,200 words, worth 20 marks, on sentience as a matter of degree — but not a question that can be answered by rehearsing the familiar move from the capacity to suffer being what matters to the conclusion that insects therefore matter a little and humans a lot.

The device I have used all term, and which the question should exploit, is a hypothetical calibrated sentience index S: suppose a future neuroscience produces a reliable, interpersonally comparable scalar measure of a creature's capacity for valenced experience at a time. Its reliability is to be granted outright — I want the epistemics of measurement ruled out as a topic, because the point is that even a perfect measure of one quantity leaves the shape of moral status entirely open. Assume it reads 1.0 for a typical adult human, roughly 0.02 for a honeybee, and exactly 0 for a domestic thermostat.

The question should force students to do three things, and to see that they are three things rather than one. First, state what function maps S — plus whatever else must be added — onto moral status, and defend the shape of that function: is any threshold defensible, given that small differences in S seem not to permit large differences in status, or does that continuity demand itself collapse under a sorites-style argument? Second, notice that S is one number while the thing we care about does not look like one number: intensity, duration, temporal depth (whether the creature remembers and anticipates), and the unity or fragmentation of its experience can all come apart, and once they do, the question how much this being matters is not well posed until one specifies what is being decided — inflicting pain, killing, using, replacing. Third, handle the thermostat: give the further condition that excludes it (a subject, a point of view, something it matters to, valence that is intrinsic rather than merely informational), and then face the awkward consequence that this condition looks all-or-nothing, so that gradualism about sentience appears to smuggle a cliff back in at the bottom. Finally, students should say whether, on their own account, very many low-S creatures can sum to outweigh one high-S creature, and explain why that is or is not a separate question from the one about degrees.

Please provide: (a) the question itself, worded as it would appear on the exam paper; (b) a model answer of approximately 700 words; (c) a 20-mark scheme organised around five must-hit moves, with the marks attached to each and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong — including the predictable mistake of treating uncertainty about insect sentience, or expected-value reasoning over huge populations, as an answer to the question about degrees; and (d) a short statement of what separates an A from a B, naming the specific move the A answer makes that a competent, well-organised B answer does not.
