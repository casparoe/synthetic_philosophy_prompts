I write historical fiction, and the manuscript I am finishing reaches its climax in the Palace of Justice at Nuremberg in 1946: my protagonist is a young American lawyer on the prosecution staff who, in the scene I keep rewriting, has to justify to himself why these twenty-odd men and not the tens of thousands of people arguably as implicated as they were. I know the outline well enough — the main tribunal prosecuting a deliberately representative sample, the further proceedings under the occupation law that followed, the ex post facto objection to crimes against humanity and to conspiracy, superior orders rejected as a complete defence but taken in mitigation, sentences running from death through prison terms to acquittal, and the serious alternative that some Allied officials preferred, of simply shooting the captured leadership without trial. Correct any of that I have got wrong rather than building on it.

What I want is the case read through the retributivism-versus-consequentialism dispute, and then the dispute read back through the case. Use the standard distinction between the general justifying aim of punishment and the principles of its distribution, and tell me which combination best describes what the tribunal was actually doing: consequentialist aim with retributive distribution, the reverse, or something that dissolves into a didactic, record-making, nation-rebuilding purpose that neither theory was built to name. Give me the disciplined mapping — who the relevant parties are for each theory (the defendants, the German public, future aggressors, the victims, the victors themselves), what each theory says the tribunal owed each of them, and — importantly — where the theory is silent rather than where you can make it speak.

Then there is the argument I invented for my protagonist's law clerk, which I have not been able to break and which I suspect is doing less work than it appears to. Call it transferred desert: if the victors knowingly punish a token sample, the punishment is not answering the desert of the men in the dock but standing in for the desert of a population, and everyone in the courtroom knows it. He concludes that this is fatal to retributivism, which cannot explain why one deserving person hangs while ten thousand equally deserving ones go home and farm, and harmless to consequentialism, which needs only the spectacle, not the coverage. Test two claims in particular. First, whether the retributivist who replies that scarcity of enforcement resources is no injustice to the person punished has conceded the clerk's point or dissolved it. Second, whether retributivism in a sample case is committed to what I will call ratcheting — inflating the severity of the sample's sentences so that the total suffering imposed approximates what the whole population deserved — and whether that commitment is a reductio of the theory, a defensible feature, or evidence that the question was ill-formed. And if the clerk is wrong about retributivism, is he also wrong about consequentialism? Exemplary punishment of a sample looks like using the defendants for the sake of others, which is exactly the charge consequentialism is supposed to be vulnerable to.

Flag where you are unsure of the record or where historians contest it, and tell me plainly if my clerk's dilemma is a genuine one or merely courtroom rhetoric that would not survive a seminar. I also want a verdict on what the episode does to the theories: does Nuremberg confirm the retributivist's suspicion that consequentialism licenses spectacle punishment, does it confirm the consequentialist's suspicion that retributive language is decoration on forward-looking state-building, or does it mostly show that both theories were designed for ordinary crime and that mass atrocity is a case neither anticipated? Please answer in under 900 words, in four or five short labelled sections of no more than a paragraph each, with no bullet lists.
