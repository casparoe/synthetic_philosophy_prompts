I've got a view about everyday conventions that I keep defending at meetups, and I want to know how exposed it actually is before I build more on top of it. Rough version: conventions like queue order, tip rates, whether you wait for the walk signal, which side you stand on an escalator, generate real moral obligations for visitors to a place — not just prudential reasons or etiquette. But the content of the obligation is fixed by local expectations, not by what the convention is nominally for, and what you owe is *conspicuous* compliance: you have to be seen going along, because the thing noncompliance damages is the shared expectation itself, not any particular persons interests. I basically think of conventions as Schelling points with normative glue smeared on them. Call it the expectation-maintenance account. There's one bit I especially like: locals are permitted to break their own convention (they've got standing to renegotiate it) while visitors aren't — call that the guests license asymmetry or whatever.

So what would count against this? Skip the obvious stuff — "cultural relativism," "who says you have to follow local norms" — I can field that. What I want is a map of where the view is actually at risk:

- The case it can't describe, or describes badly. I'm most worried about the invisible tourist: crossing against the light at 4am with nobody around, or skipping a tip at a place you'll never return to where nobody compares notes. My account seems to say there's no duty, since no expectation gets damaged. If that's actually fine, say why; if it's a reductio, walk me there.
- The collapse risks, both directions. Where does this stop being moral philosophy and become "don't be rude to the locals" with extra steps? And where does it stop being a story about conventions and turn out to be an instance of a general duty not to free-ride on a cooperative scheme? Which collapse is more likely, and which feature of a case decides it.
- A discriminating case: something my view predicts that the plain free-riding account doesn't, or vice versa, with an honest note about which way I'd have to find my intuitions going for it to count against me.
- The awkward-but-not-fatal stuff, ranked separately: conventions that are themselves bad (a norm of paying off officials, a norm about who gets served first), visitor-only conventions (a queue at a tourist site with no locals in it), conventions in flux (a place where half the locals have stopped tipping), and the "opting out by announcement" move — if I loudly announce I'm not playing, have I discharged the duty?
- What a defender would concede rather than fight. If I concede as much as I probably should, does anything interesting survive?
- And whether I've insulated it. When I get pushed I start saying the duty is only pro tanto and the relevant expectation is that of a reasonable local, and I want to know if that's legitimate specification or whether I've qualified the thing into having no risk at all.

The thing that would actually move me: an argument that expectation-maintenance can't distinguish being morally required from being strongly expected without secretly importing a harm or unfairness premise that's doing all the real work. If that lands I'd probably drop the view, so tell me how close such an argument is to succeeding. Rank everything fatal down to merely embarrassing, and if there's a real-world pattern you think already counts either way, describe the shape of it — I don't want dates or studies, just the shape. Be straight with me if the honest answer is that the view is well insulated and that this is okay because it's mostly a claim about what we owe strangers in shared spaces rather than an empirical claim.
