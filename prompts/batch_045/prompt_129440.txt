I teach a third-year seminar on the foundations of strategic reasoning, and I am assembling a first-week self-diagnostic handout for my students: "Which account of play in the centipede game are you actually committed to?" They will have had one prior course — dominance, iterated deletion, Nash equilibrium, backward induction in games of perfect information, mixed strategies — but no epistemic game theory, no type spaces, and no literature. The deliverable is a classification instrument: seven or eight diagnostic questions, drawn as a flowchart in prose, with a scoring path that ends in named positions.

The game. Fix the six-node centipede: two players alternate; the pot starts small and is multiplied by a constant factor at each pass; at each node the mover either takes the larger share and ends the game or passes the move to the other player; taking is forced at the last node. Choose the numbers yourself, print them, and make sure the usual monotonicity holds — at every node, taking now beats passing if the next mover takes at the next node.

Questions must be diagnostics, not label preferences. Each node should be a concrete case, or a claim about a concrete case, that the competing accounts answer differently, phrased so that a student who has never heard of the debate can answer it in a sentence. No node may ask whether people are rational in general, or which named position the student finds attractive; the labels appear only in the key. The following cases must appear somewhere in the tree: the decision at the first node; the decision after learning that the other player has just passed; the same decision after learning that the other player has just passed at a node where passing was very expensive for them; a variant in which both players are publicly read, before play, a statement about how the other reasons; and a variant in which all payoffs are multiplied by twenty with the structure unchanged.

Build the logical space first, then place the positions in it. Lay out the premises whose conjunction makes the intuitive answer ("pass early, take somewhere in the middle") unavailable: that both players maximize expected monetary payoff; that each believes the other does; that this holds to arbitrarily high order; that the payoff structure is mutually known; and that each player's confidence in the other's rationality survives the counterfactual supposition that the player himself deviated — the robustness condition on which the standard "common knowledge of rationality yields the backward-induction outcome" theorem depends. Present the tree as a sequence of "which premise do you give up?" questions, and show which combinations of answers are jointly satisfiable. The combination in which nothing is given up must be marked as impossible, with a short explanation the student can read: they must surrender something, and the instrument's job is to show them what.

Required leaves, each named in the key by its central claim in your own words (attach the traditional label only where you are confident, and no bibliography or further reading):

- the proof-is-right view, on which backward induction is the correct answer and the experimental data simply record error;
- the tremble view, on which a small probability that the other player is not a payoff maximizer, or that the game will not end as expected, makes early passing rational;
- the counterfactual view, on which the robustness condition does the work and should be rejected: a player may be fully convinced of the other's rationality while refusing to say what he would believe at a node reachable only through irrational play;
- the belief-dependence view, on which the players' beliefs about each other are correlated or mutually dependent in a way that defeats the independence the backward-induction argument needs;
- the mis-specification view, on which the subjects are not playing the centipede at all, because they care about the other's payoff, or about equality, or about not being the first to take;
- the collective view, on which the unit of agency is the pair and the reasoning is "what should we do?", not "what should I do, given what you will do?";
- a quietist leaf for the student who holds that the normative question and the predictive question are simply different questions, and that the data can settle neither.

Add one further leaf that I want tested rather than assumed. A manuscript circulating in my department argues that the data are explained by what its author calls counterfactual blindness: the objects of choice are not strategies but belief-revision policies, rationality constrains only the policy an agent actually runs, and a policy is never evaluated at nodes it does not reach, so the question "what would you believe if you had deviated?" has no determinate answer and the backward-induction argument never gets off the ground. Locate this leaf in the space. If it is genuinely distinct from the counterfactual view and from the collective view, say what further case would separate the three. If it collapses into one of them, say which, say what extra assumption would pry them apart, and keep it in the tree only as a marked variant.

Two further constraints on the key. First, suppose for the sake of the exercise that our own in-class replication with two hundred and forty undergraduates produced these results: the median taking node is three, about one in eight students takes immediately, roughly seven in ten students who are told that the other player has just passed will pass in turn, and multiplying all payoffs by twenty moves the median taking node earlier but not to the first node. State which leaves these results discriminate between and which leaves are empirically indistinguishable given them; I expect at least one pair to be unfalsifiable by these data, and I want that flagged rather than hidden. Second, identify any two leaves the instrument cannot separate at all, and supply the one follow-up question that would separate them, or explain why no question about cases could.

Finally, run the finished instrument on three answer sheets and report the leaf each student lands on.

Student A takes at the first node, says that anyone who passes is making an error, and says that she finds the error understandable.

Student B passes at the first node, says she would take if she were moved to again later, and says that on being told the other player has just passed she would take immediately.

Student C passes at the first node, says she would pass again if given the chance, insists that this is what maximizing requires of her and not a departure from it, and reacts to the suggestion that she must be relying on the other's irrationality by denying that the question "what would I believe if I had taken?" has any content.

For at least one of the three, I want you to show that the leaf the student reaches is not the label she would give herself, and to say what the handout should tell her. Keep the whole handout under twelve hundred words of student-facing prose.
