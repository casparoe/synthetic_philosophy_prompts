hey — I'm in this little reading group, half philosophers half physicists, and we've gotten stuck on something I can't get straight. One of the physicists keeps leaning on this analogy: he says consciousness is like mass. Mass is a basic property, every particle has some of it, big things have more of it, and the mass of a composite is more or less the sum of the masses of its parts, so there's no mysterious emergence to worry about. He says the panic over the combination problem is misplaced — if micro-experiences add up the way masses do, panpsychism predicts ordinary human consciousness just fine — and that the real upshot is moral: suffering might be as physically pervasive as mass is, so fundamental physics turns out to be an ethical subject after all.

I half believe him and half think the analogy is carrying the whole conclusion. Could you audit it properly? I'd like the correspondence table — what plays the role of rest mass, of the sum, of binding energy, of relativistic frame-dependence, of whatever measurement reveals the quantity — and then the places the mapping breaks, sorted by whether they actually matter for the job he's using it for. Two things I keep chewing on: mass is a thoroughly dispositional, structural property (it's defined by what it does to trajectories and to the metric), whereas the whole point of the Russellian version of panpsychism is that consciousness is the intrinsic, categorical nature that physics's purely structural description leaves out — doesn't the analogy quietly import into the target exactly the feature the target was introduced to lack? And is summing even the right model: bound systems weigh less than their separate parts, and in general relativity there isn't a clean local mass–energy density to add up, so what happens to "add up the micro-experiences" once that's on the table?

Then the ethics, which is where I really lose the thread. We invented a probably-silly principle in the group and I can't decide how silly it is: call it valence additivity — the disvalue of a composite's experience is the arithmetic sum of the disvalues of the micro-experiences composing it, so the moral cost of any physical process can just be read off the ledger at the micro-level. Is that principle even coherent, what would the mass analogy predict about it, and does my friend's conclusion (we might owe something to a star, or to whatever's going on inside a particle collider, and physics needs an ethics module) survive once the disanalogies are out — especially given that suffering, unlike mass, seems to need a subject it's for, and that nobody thinks a single molecule has a temperature even though the gas does? If mass is a bad model, is temperature (or something else) a better one — a macro property that's real, not a sum, and not had by the parts? Please write in British English, and feel free to tell me where I've mis-stated the physics.
