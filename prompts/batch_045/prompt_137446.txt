You are a philosopher of science who has also sat on interdisciplinary grant panels: someone equally at home with Dennett's vocabulary of cranes and skyhooks, with the contrast between good and greedy reductionism, and with the mundane business of turning a vague worry into something a reviewer can write down in a form that survives an appeal. Think carefully before you answer. Begin with a short brainstorm — a dozen lines or so — of the ways a proposal that is not a skyhook nevertheless looks like one, and the ways a real skyhook gets past busy reviewers, and let that shape the procedure you go on to write.

I chair the review panel for a public funder's high-risk, cross-disciplinary scheme. Each round we read around forty proposals. Each gets roughly twenty minutes from one reviewer who is a specialist in one of the fields involved and therefore a non-specialist in the other, and the panel must then defend, in writing, both the declines and the awards, to applicants who will push back hard. Our recurring problem is not fraud or sloppiness; it is that a large fraction of the ambitious proposals buy their explanatory power with something they have not paid for. Some posit an explainer that is exactly as mysterious as the thing to be explained, or one whose operation would require an exemption from the very regularities it is invoked to explain. Others are honest but impatient: they jump straight from a high-level phenomenon to a low-level mechanism, skipping the intermediate levels where the real work was always going to be, so that the phenomenon becomes indescribable at every level except the bottom one. We have no shared way of separating these from proposals that are merely unfamiliar, or from proposals that have earned the right to leave a mechanism unspecified.

I want a decision procedure my reviewers can actually run in twenty minutes. It should be ordered tests, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a stop-rule that says when to decline without going further, a stop-rule that says when to recommend funding without going further, and explicit escalation points where the call passes to an outside referee or to the panel because it is a judgement about risk appetite rather than a matter of fact. It must be usable by a reviewer who has never heard the word "skyhook": gloss each step in plain language. Give me a small set of verdict categories with wording that could go into a letter, and mark which steps are genuine findings and which are judgement calls wearing a procedure's clothing.

Four distinctions I have found useful in our discussions must survive into the procedure, and I want you to sharpen or replace them where they are wrong:

A dormant crane is not a skyhook. A proposal may leave a mechanism unidentified provided it says what kind of thing the mechanism is, what level it operates at, and how we would tell if it were absent. A skyhook is different: it is an explainer whose own operation would need resources, exemptions, or powers that the proposal has not budgeted for and cannot point to.

Explanations must balance their books. Any apparent increase in order, specificity, or functional fit has to be paid for out of something the proposal can identify — a reservoir, a population, a selection process, a timescale. A mechanism that imports organisation without saying where it came from is borrowing a crane, not building one.

There is a mirror test for greed as well as for skyhooks. If the property the proposal invokes at its preferred level is just as much in need of explanation as the phenomenon it was brought in to explain, nothing has been gained, and the proposal should be told so in those terms.

And there is a ladder test for reductionism. Ask the applicant to restate the explanation one level up, and then one level above that. A good reductionism has to be able to descend the ladder, deriving the higher-level pattern rather than gesturing at it. A reductionism that can only be stated at the bottom rung, or that must quietly reintroduce higher-level vocabulary — function, information, representation, purpose — to do its work, is greedy: it is spending currency it refuses to acknowledge.

Apply the finished procedure to four proposals, in this order, and show the reasoning at each step rather than only the verdict.

One: a well-written, genuinely exciting proposal on the origins of life, which explains the emergence of biological organisation by positing a law-like intrinsic tendency of matter to complexify, and treats the appearance of design as the surface expression of that tendency. This is the panel's favourite going into the round.

Two: a proposal on the persistence of promise-keeping in a large anonymous society, which proposes to explain it directly in terms of individual neurochemistry and reward circuitry, on the grounds that the institutional and strategic stories are, in its words, merely descriptive.

Three: a molecular biology proposal which argues that an observed class of folding error is buffered by some as-yet-unidentified chaperone-like mechanism, and asks for money to run a screen to find it. The mechanism is not named; the kind of thing it is, and the detection method, are.

Four: a proposal that a well-replicated but puzzling cognitive phenomenon depends on a quantum-level mechanism sustained in warm, wet neural tissue. The panel's instinct, off the record, is that this is theatre. I want to know whether the procedure agrees, or whether its distaste is doing the work.

At least one of these applications must return a verdict I will not like, and I want you to say which and why you think I will find it unwelcome. Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire: what kinds of proposal it will systematically punish, how a genuine revolutionary would look to it, and at which steps a reviewer's ignorance is most likely to be laundered into a finding.
