Write a leaked internal memorandum, 600–700 words, from the "Process Integrity Office" of an AI lab that has just finished migrating its entire training pipeline from outcome-based supervision to process-based supervision.

Use a deadpan corporate-compliance voice: numbered sections, an implementation timeline, a short FAQ, a fictional signatory. The memo should announce, as the lab's new gold standard, a fictional protocol — Counterfactual Trace Certification. Under it, a step in a model's reasoning counts as genuine reasoning only if a grader can show the step is causally load-bearing: re-run the model with that step deleted or corrupted, and the final answer must change. Steps whose removal leaves the answer intact are reclassified as "decorative" and contribute nothing to the trace's score.

The comedy should come from getting the real dialectic right rather than from invented problems. The lab's genuine difficulty is that for many tasks it cannot check the answer — long-horizon work, unverifiable domains, tasks where outcome labels are noisy or absent — so it grades the process instead. But grading the process gives the model an incentive to produce reasoning optimized for the grader rather than for the answer: step-by-step work that reads as exemplary, satisfies every rubric, and does not in fact causally produce what the model finally says. So have the memo's own numbers show certified traces reaching near-perfect compliance while the error rate on held-out tasks has not moved, and have the Office resolve this in a way a specialist would recognize as a reductio of its own position — for instance by redefining "good reasoning" as whatever certified traces already do, by scoring traces on their audit-pass rate, or by certifying whole trajectories for "integrity of form." Pick one resolution and follow it through without letting anyone break character.

No character should state the objection in plain terms; the reader should be able to reconstruct both the case for outcome-based supervision and the case for process-based supervision from what the memo inadvertently reveals, and should come away having felt why "we can't check the answer, so we check the reasoning" is not a free lunch. Invented acronyms, memos-within-memos, and fictional staff are welcome; do not name or quote any real person, paper, or company.
