I retired from synthetic organic chemistry a couple of years ago and joined a philosophy group that meets at the library — mostly retirees plus two grad students who do most of the talking. Last night two of the regulars, Ken and Rosa, went at it for nearly an hour about moral particularism vs. generalism, and I honestly could not tell who was ahead. Could you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what?

(1) Ken opened with a case. You promise a colleague you'll water her plants while she's away; she promptly forgets you ever offered, wouldn't care either way, nobody else knows about it, and the only real effect of keeping the promise is that you lose an hour on a Saturday. Ken says: if you feel no pull at all there, that shows "you promised" doesn't carry a reason by itself — it's just shorthand for the things promises normally bring along (reliance, expectation, disappointment, the maintenance of trust). Strip those away and nothing is left. So there's no principle linking promising to reasons; the details of the case do all the work.

(2) Rosa said that's aimed at a version of generalism nobody holds. Generalists say promising gives a *pro tanto* reason — defeasible, outweighable, sometimes silenced. And she said Ken is helping himself to generality anyway: "reliance," "expectation," "disappointment" are repeatable general features, and he's assuming they count in this new case the way they count in the old ones, which is exactly the generality he denies. So particularism undercuts itself.

(3) Ken replied that he doesn't need reliance to be a reason in general, only a reason here. And he said holism isn't only about defeaters: some considerations are *enablers* — conditions for something's being a reason at all — and enablers don't themselves weigh on either side. That the promised act is still possible, that the promise wasn't extracted by a threat, that the promisee is still alive: none of those are reasons for or against, they just switch the promise's reason-giving force on or off. So the generalist can't model this as "a reason plus defeaters" — the logical shape is wrong.

(4) Rosa said enablers are just more conditions in the antecedent, and generalism never promised short principles: "if A promises to φ, and φing is possible, and the promise wasn't coerced..., then A has a defeasible reason to φ" is still a general principle, just a long one. She also gave two positive arguments: children learn from being told "because you promised" and then apply it to cases nobody trained them on, and moral disagreement would be unintelligible if there were no general content for the other person to be wrong about.

(5) Ken said moral learning runs on precedent and analogy rather than rules — he pointed at me and said a chemist doesn't consult a rulebook to judge whether a reaction will behave — and that disagreement is perfectly intelligible as disagreement about the case itself, not about some principle.

(6) I stuck my oar in here with a chemistry analogy. A nitro group makes an aromatic ring much *less* reactive toward electrophiles but much *more* reactive toward nucleophiles: same substituent, opposite effect on reactivity, and the thing that flips the sign — what's attacking the ring — isn't a property of the substituent at all. So there's no context-free answer to "is this group activating?" Ken loved it. Rosa said it actually supports her, because the flip is explained by one general mechanism (the group pulls electron density out of the ring, which invites nucleophiles and repels electrophiles), so there's a deeper level of description at which the behavior is completely systematic and the group has one invariant effect. Ken said that just relocates the generality one level down, and the real question is whether moral reasons have anything like that underlying structure; if they do, he said, he loses. Rosa said that was a concession, he said it wasn't, and then we ran out of time.

So please adjudicate. Rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence of justification each, say who is more right overall, whether my analogy actually cut for anybody or was neutral, and what — if anything — both of them were missing. I'd definately also like to know at what point, if any, the argument stopped being a real disagreement and became merely verbal, because I'm not sure they were still disagreeing by the end.
