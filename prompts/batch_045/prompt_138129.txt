I've been going in circles on the special composition question — what it takes for some things to add up to one further thing — ever since I picked up, from years of reading the rationalist blogs, the habit of saying that reality has no joints and only our concepts do. That habit has started to feel like a way of changing the subject. The gloss I'm playing with instead is that a composition criterion isn't a discovery about which arrangements of matter really are objects, but a lossy compression scheme: a rule about which clusters of simples your language gets to name with a single singular term, so that the three standard answers — every plurality composes something, no plurality does, and only a plurality caught up in a life does — are three different tolerances for representational loss, chosen for what you want to be able to say cheaply. I'd like a poem that puts pressure on that gloss rather than illustrating it.

Write me a sestina on the special composition question with the end-words part, whole, edge, many, life, one. The conceit I want is that the fixed inventory of six words returning in rotated order is the same move the question itself makes: one stock of simples rearranged into different candidate objects. The form should be doing the arguing, not decorating it — I should be able to tell from how the rotation lands in each stanza which of the three answers the poem is currently inside, whether it's the permissive one that costs nothing to assert, the restrictive one that makes most of the stanzas quietly stop working, or the austere one that leaves the six words with nothing to be parts of. Let edge carry the boundary worry, the sense that there may be no fact of the matter where the cat ends and its shed hairs begin. In the three-line envoi all six words return, two to a line; treat that as the one place where a genuinely composite thing gets to exist, and commit to whether it does. It should be right enough that someone who actually knows this literature recognises the positions and not just the vocabulary.

Close with a few sentences, not a checklist, on where you had to cheat: which answer the sestina is secretly rigged in favour of, whether the compression gloss survives being said out loud in verse, and one claim about composition the form forced you to make that you wouldn't defend in prose. Take a definite line — if you think my gloss is empty or wrong, write the poem against it and say why.
