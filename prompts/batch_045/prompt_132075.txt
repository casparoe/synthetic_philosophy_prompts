I've been arguing with a friend — she's the sort of person who invokes Epicurus at dinner — about a view she calls tranquilism: the only thing that prudentially benefits a person is the absence of craving, the only thing that prudentially harms her is suffering, and everything else we call good in a life (achievement, knowledge, love, intensity of pleasure beyond the stilling of an urge) matters only instrumentally, by way of warding off craving or pain. To get past our usual stalemate I want to pin down one comparison and get a number on it. Case Q: from age 25 to 85 a woman lives under a continuous infusion — call it the quiescence protocol — that prevents new appetitive cravings from forming and dissolves old ones as they arise, while leaving occurrent sensory pleasures fully intact; her cognitive and agential capacities are unimpaired but largely unused. She gardens, reads, sees her friends, feels the warmth of an afternoon, reports neither boredom nor any sense that something is missing, and when asked whether she wants more from her life says no and appears to mean it. Case A: someone who over the same sixty years finishes a decade-long research program, raises two children, loses a marriage, endures ordinary amounts of frustration and anxiety plus one stretch of genuine despair, and at 85 says the difficulties were worth it. The proposition I want your credence on is this: Q's life is at least as good for Q as A's life is for A. Mine sits at about 0.35; my friend's at about 0.7.

Don't give me a survey of theories of welfare; give me your number and the decomposition that produces it. The terms I think belong, though you should add or replace any you think matter more: whether the quiescence protocol describes a psychologically possible state at all, or whether boredom, restlessness, or a background drive for stimulation and novelty would re-emerge (and, if they did, whether that would count as craving); whether absence of craving is a single natural state or a gerrymandered disjunction, and whether the view can be stated at all without collapsing into hedonism or into some desire-satisfaction theory once you ask what the craving was a craving for; the narrow-versus-strict reading, where I'll say a person is in clear-eyed wanting when she forms and acts on goals with no felt urgency, no dread of non-fulfillment and no restless pull — on the strict reading any conative state is craving and Q's life is not tranquil at all, on the narrow reading only the felt-urgency kind counts and she may garden and read as freely as she likes, so give me a number for each reading and tell me which one you take to be the defensible formulation; whether intact-but-unused capacities, or the absence of difficulty, cost anything prudentially; whether the pull toward A is better explained by something other than welfare — meaning, narrative shape, or the fact that we as judges still have cravings and cannot picture her state from inside, which would be a bias term rather than a reason; and how much of your uncertainty is really semantic indeterminacy about whether at least as good for her has a determinate truth value here, as opposed to substantive disagreement. Assign a rough probability and one line of justification to each, say whether it pushes your final number up or down, and then show how they combine into the headline figure.

Then tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, which term you are least confident about, and where you think my 0.35 and my friend's 0.7 actually diverge — which sub-question one of us must be resolving differently.
